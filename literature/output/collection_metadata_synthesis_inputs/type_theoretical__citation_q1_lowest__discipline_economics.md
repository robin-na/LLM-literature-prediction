- source: Alventosa, A., & Olcina, G. (2021). Rich and Poor: On the Emergence of a Sanctioning Institution. *Journal of Institutional and Theoretical Economics-zeitschrift Fur Die Gesamte Staatswissenschaft*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The model demonstrates that centralized sanctioning (pool punishment) can transform a public-goods game from full free-riding (inefficient) to full or partial contribution (higher efficiency), depending on the punishment technology and wealth distribution. If the sanctioning institution is strong enough to induce all social classes to contribute, efficiency is maximized and the outcome is socially optimal. If only some classes contribute, efficiency is improved relative to no punishment, but there is a welfare loss compared to the full-contribution case. The government type (which social class it represents) affects whether the institution is implemented and whether the implementation is efficient. Poor-class governments are more likely to implement the institution efficiently when full contribution is possible. In partial-contribution cases, both over- and underimplementation can occur, leading to inefficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support that enabling centralized punishment in a public-goods game increases efficiency (group payoff, welfare) compared to the control (no punishment), especially when the punishment technology is strong enough to induce full contribution. The effect is moderated by the marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment effectiveness (probability and fine), and the wealth distribution. The model also highlights that the identity of the government (which class it represents) can lead to over- or underimplementation of punishment, affecting efficiency. For prediction, the presence of centralized punishment, high punishment effectiveness, and a government representing lower-wealth classes are associated with larger efficiency gains. In cases where only partial contribution is achieved, efficiency gains are smaller and may be offset by implementation costs or misaligned government incentives.

- source: Huang, S. T., Dong, Y. L., & Zhang, B. Y. (2024). THE OPTIMAL INCENTIVE IN PROMOTING COOPERATION: PUNISH THE WORST AND DO NOT ONLY REWARD THE BEST. *Singapore Economic Review*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper provides a theoretical analysis of public goods games with institutional incentives, showing that: (1) Punishing only the lowest contributor (relative punishment with high selectivity) is the most effective way to sustain full cooperation and maximize group welfare for a given punishment budget. (2) Rewarding only the highest contributor is not effective; to promote cooperation, the reward must be distributed such that even lower contributors have a chance to receive it. (3) When both reward and punishment are available, group welfare is maximized by using as much reward as possible and only the minimal punishment needed to sustain full cooperation. (4) The paper gives explicit formulas for the minimum incentive size (punishment and/or reward) required to achieve full cooperation, and shows that, above this threshold, the cooperative Nash equilibrium is globally stable and group efficiency is maximized.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is highly informative for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It provides explicit theoretical conditions for when punishment (and/or reward) will lead to full cooperation and maximal group welfare, as a function of the number of players, the marginal per-capita return, and the size and targeting of incentives. The results suggest that, for a given game design, introducing punishment targeted at the lowest contributor will sharply increase efficiency if the punishment is just above the critical threshold. The paper also clarifies that the efficiency gain from punishment is maximized when the punishment is minimal but sufficient, and that reward should be used as much as possible if available. These findings can be directly mapped to the prediction task, especially for settings where the control game (no punishment) has low efficiency and the treatment introduces targeted punishment.

- source: Tanimoto, J. (2018). Evolutionary Game Theory. *Evolutionary Games with Sociophysics: Analysis of Traffic Flow and Epidemics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that: (1) Costly punishment (including jealous sanctions) can promote cooperation in public goods and prisoner's dilemma games, especially in spatially structured populations, but this often comes at the expense of group efficiency due to the cost of punishment. (2) The efficiency of punishment (cost-to-impact ratio) is a key moderator: higher efficiency (lower cost per unit impact) generally leads to more cooperation and sometimes higher group payoff, but in heterogeneous networks, even inefficient punishment can promote cooperation by targeting key defectors. (3) The structure of the game (e.g., network topology, update rule, dilemma strength) and the type of sanctioning mechanism (peer vs. institutional, jealous vs. standard) strongly affect both cooperation and efficiency outcomes. (4) In some cases, adding punishment or more information (e.g., indirect reciprocity) can actually reduce cooperation or efficiency, especially if the information is noisy or the sanctioning is misapplied. (5) The introduction of additional strategies (e.g., costly loners) does not always relax the social dilemma or improve efficiency, and may result in all-defector equilibria.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, this paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that punishment can increase cooperation rates, but its effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is mixed and context-dependent. Key moderators include the cost and effectiveness of punishment, the network structure, and the type of dilemma (Chicken vs. Stag Hunt vs. PD). In many cases, especially with costly punishment, group efficiency may decrease even as cooperation increases, due to the resource drain from sanctioning. Efficient punishment (high impact per cost) is more likely to improve both cooperation and efficiency. The paper also highlights that the presence of noise, network heterogeneity, and the specific sanctioning rule (e.g., jealousy-based) can lead to non-intuitive outcomes, including cases where punishment backfires. Thus, when using game design dimensions and control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should account for the cost-effectiveness of punishment and the possibility of negative efficiency effects, especially in heterogeneous or noisy environments.

  findings: The paper finds that introducing and increasing the fraction of defectors who are punished in an optional public good game (with loner option) increases the equilibrium level of cooperation and reduces the share of loners. When the punishment fraction surpasses a threshold (d1), the full cooperation equilibrium becomes stable and can be globally attractive, leading to maximal group efficiency. The model gives explicit formulas for the thresholds and equilibrium outcomes as functions of the game design parameters (player count, MPCR, etc.). For the prediction task, this paper supports the expectation that enabling punishment (with sufficient effectiveness) will increase efficiency relative to the control, and that the magnitude of the effect depends on the punishment fraction and cost. The results are most directly applicable to settings with optional participation and random punishment, but the qualitative implication that punishment increases efficiency is robust.

For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that enabling institutional punishment (via tax) is likely to increase cooperation rates, and that institutional reward may be even more effective. The comment also observes that the effects of punishment on contribution are sensitive to the details of the punishment structure, and that the presence of a constitutional convention stage (where subjects choose the institutional mechanism) adds further complexity. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that careful attention must be paid to the specific design dimensions of the game, as small changes can have large effects on outcomes.

- source: Dong, Y. L., Gavrilets, S., Qin, C. Z., & Zhang, B. Y. (2024). Kinship can hinder cooperation in heterogeneous populations. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The paper finds that in repeated public goods and multi-player PD games with heterogeneous cost-benefit ratios, increasing genetic relatedness does not always increase cooperation or efficiency. For low relatedness, direct reciprocity (punishment via GRIM) can sustain cooperation and high efficiency. For high relatedness, cooperation is a dominant strategy and efficiency is high. However, for intermediate relatedness, some individuals become unconditional cooperators (AllC), which undermines the effectiveness of punishment and allows others to defect (AllD), making full cooperation and maximal efficiency unsustainable. This results in a non-monotonic, U-shaped relationship between relatedness and efficiency. The findings are supported by both subgame perfect equilibrium analysis and agent-based evolutionary simulations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in repeated public goods games with heterogeneity in cost-benefit ratios, the effect of mechanisms analogous to punishment (i.e., direct reciprocity via GRIM) on efficiency depends critically on the population structure and relatedness. When relatedness is low, punishment can sustain high efficiency; when relatedness is high, cooperation is self-sustaining. However, at intermediate relatedness, the threat of punishment is undermined, and efficiency drops. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of heterogeneity and the degree of relatedness (or analogous parameters) can cause non-monotonic effects of punishment on efficiency, and that punishment is most effective at low relatedness. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in repeated PGG-like games with heterogeneity, but less so for one-shot games or games with explicit costly punishment mechanisms.

- source: Sugaya, T., & Wolitzky, A. (2023). Bad apples in symmetric repeated games. *Theoretical Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in large repeated public goods or similar games, the possibility of sustaining cooperation and high efficiency depends critically on the ability to punish defectors specifically. If the only available incentives are rewards or withdrawal of provision (i.e., voluntary mechanisms), then cooperation collapses and efficiency is low when there are even a few commitment types (bad apples) who always defect. Involuntary punishment (such as fines) that can be targeted at defectors restores the possibility of cooperation and high efficiency. The main technical result is that if a 'population dominant' action exists (i.e., defecting yields a higher payoff than the population average, regardless of others' actions), then equilibrium efficiency converges to the inefficient outcome as the population grows. If no such action exists, and punishment can be targeted, then high efficiency is possible. The results are general and apply to a wide class of symmetric repeated games, including public goods games with or without transfers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods and related games. It implies that enabling targeted, involuntary punishment (e.g., fines for non-contributors) is necessary for sustaining high efficiency in large groups with possible commitment types. If only rewards or voluntary mechanisms are available, efficiency will be low in the presence of even a small fraction of defectors. The paper's results are most directly applicable to settings where the game is symmetric, the population is large, and there is a possibility of commitment types. For the prediction task, the presence or absence of targeted punishment (punishment_tech), the nature of the incentive (reward_exists, punishment_cost), and the ability to distinguish defectors are critical moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) will be low in large groups with commitment types unless involuntary punishment is available; enabling such punishment can dramatically increase efficiency.
