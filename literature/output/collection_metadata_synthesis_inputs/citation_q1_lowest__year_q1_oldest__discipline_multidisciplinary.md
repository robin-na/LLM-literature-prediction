- source: Kodaka, F., Takahashi, H., Yamada, M., Takano, H., Nakayama, K., Ito, H., & Suhara, T. (2012). Effect of Cooperation Level of Group on Punishment for Non-Cooperators: A Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging Study. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that the degree of punishment assigned to a non-cooperator is significantly higher when the rest of the group is highly cooperative compared to when the group is less cooperative. This is reflected both in behavioral choices and in increased activation in the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and left anterior cingulate cortex. The results suggest that the social context of group cooperation modulates punitive behavior, but the study does not address efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the social context—specifically, the overall cooperation level of the group—modulates the severity of punishment assigned to non-cooperators. However, it does not provide any data on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. Its main relevance is in highlighting that punishment behavior is context-dependent, which may be important for interpreting or modeling punishment effects in environments where group cooperation levels vary.

- source: Bshary, A., & Bshary, R. (2012). Individual Differences in Foraging Strategies of Parasitic Sabre-Tooth Blennies. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The study finds that individual blennies differ markedly in their foraging strategies, including how they respond to punishment by victim fish. Some blennies are more likely to switch victim species after being punished, while others are not. Laboratory experiments show that punishment can reduce the likelihood of attack or cause switching, but these effects are highly individual-specific. The paper discusses how only certain patterns of blenny response to punishment (specifically, switching to another species) would generate a public good for the victim group, while other patterns would not. However, the study does not measure group efficiency or payoff directly.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, mechanistic insight into how punishment might or might not generate public goods in a natural system, depending on the behavioral rules of the punished party. However, it does not provide quantitative evidence on efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it manipulate game design dimensions in a way that would allow direct prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. Its main value for prediction is in highlighting that the effect of punishment on public good provision (and thus efficiency) may depend on the behavioral heterogeneity of the punished agents and the structure of the interaction (e.g., whether punishment causes switching to other species or not).

- source: Szilagyi, M. N., & Somogyi, I. (2010). Agent-Based Simulation of an N-Person Game with Parabolic Payoff Functions. *Complexity*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing
  findings: The study finds that the final proportion of cooperators depends strongly on the shape and relative position of the parabolic payoff functions, the agent update rule, and the definition of neighborhood (local vs. global). For some parameterizations, stable intermediate levels of cooperation emerge; in others, the system converges to all-cooperation or all-defection. The results are often unpredictable except in special cases (e.g., greedy or conformist agents with global neighborhoods). The paper does not report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes, nor does it include explicit punishment or reward mechanisms as in standard public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The game studied is adjacent to PGGs in that it is an N-person game with binary choices and payoff externalities, but it lacks explicit punishment/reward actions and does not report efficiency or group payoff. The findings may be useful for understanding how nonlinear payoff structures and local interactions affect cooperation rates, but they do not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment interventions.
