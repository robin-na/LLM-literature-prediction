- source: Suleiman, R., & Samid, Y. (2021). Punishment Strategies across Societies: Conventional Wisdoms Reconsidered. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Punishment in public goods games increases group earnings and contributions, but the efficiency gains are moderate and highly variable across groups and societies. The main mechanism is not the deterrence of free riders, but rather an increase in contributions by cooperators and strong reciprocators, often in anticipation of punishment. The fraction of strong reciprocators among punishers is a strong predictor of both group cooperation and group earnings. Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare and does not explain low efficiency; instead, the presence of 'norm-keepers' who punish both high and low contributors is associated with lower group efficiency. The effect of punishment is more positive in groups and societies with more strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends on the mix of punishment strategies in the group, which is in turn influenced by societal or cultural context. For prediction, the presence of punishment generally increases efficiency, but the magnitude is moderated by the prevalence of strong reciprocators versus norm-keepers. Game design dimensions such as player count (4), number of rounds (6 per phase), MPCR (0.4), punishment cost (1 MU per point), and punishment effectiveness (3 MU per point) are clearly specified and match standard PGG designs. The control (no-punishment) phase provides a baseline for efficiency, and the treatment (punishment) phase shows a moderate increase in group earnings. The findings suggest that, all else equal, enabling punishment will increase efficiency, but the effect will be larger in groups with more strong reciprocators and smaller in groups with more norm-keepers. The paper also shows that the anticipation of punishment can have a strong effect on contributions and efficiency, even before punishment is actually applied.

- source: Cobo-Reyes, R., Katz, G., Markussen, T., & Meraglia, S. (2022). Voting on sanctioning institutions in open and closed communities: experimental evidence. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment compares public goods games with endogenous institutional choice (formal vs. informal sanctions) in closed (fixed group) and open (migration allowed) societies. Formal sanctions always yield higher average payoffs and contributions than informal sanctions, but the gap is much larger in open societies. In closed societies, informal sanctions can sustain high cooperation and efficiency if initial group cooperation is high, but this is undermined when migration is allowed. The popularity and effectiveness of informal sanctions are fragile to group openness. The main efficiency outcome is average individual payoff per period, which is significantly higher under formal sanctions than informal sanctions in open societies (MT: FS=65.59, IS=60.63), but not significantly different in closed societies (NMT: FS=68.29, IS=67.59).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment (specifically, formal/centralized punishment) is larger in open societies (where group membership is endogenous) than in closed societies. When predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, the presence of migration or endogenous group formation is a key moderator: punishment is more effective at raising efficiency when groups are open. The marginal per-capita return (MPCR), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness are all specified, allowing for precise mapping to prediction dimensions. Control (no-punishment) efficiency is not directly reported, but the comparison between informal and formal sanctions provides a strong basis for predicting efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. The results are most informative for settings with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and the specific punishment/reward technologies described.

- source: Pancotto, F., Righi, S., & Takács, K. (2023). Voluntary play increases cooperation in the presence of punishment: a lab in the field experiment. *Theory and Decision*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The experiments show that punishment increases contributions in public goods games, both when participation is compulsory and when it is optional. The effect is strongest when participation is compulsory and punishment is available. Optional participation (opt-out) alone does not significantly increase contributions compared to compulsory participation. Loners (those who opt out) contribute less when forced to participate, but are not responsible for anti-social punishment. Instead, low contributors (free-riders) are the main source of anti-social punishment. The study does not directly report efficiency or group payoff, but the contribution data suggest that punishment increases group welfare, especially in compulsory settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in discrete public goods games with 4 players, 1:3 punishment technology, and no communication, enabling punishment increases contributions, especially when participation is compulsory. While efficiency is not directly reported, the increase in contributions strongly suggests that efficiency would also increase with punishment, particularly in compulsory games. The design dimensions are well specified, allowing for mapping to prediction tasks, but the lack of direct efficiency or group payoff data means that predictions must be inferred from contribution outcomes. The results are most informative for settings with similar group size, discrete contributions, and punishment technology.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: Unanimity voting increases contributions but leads to frequent rejections, resulting in lower overall efficiency compared to majority voting or automatic provision. Majority voting does not significantly differ from automatic provision in terms of efficiency or profits. The mechanism (voting rule) is the primary driver of these outcomes, not individual voting behavior. The study provides direct evidence on how institutional design (voting rules) affects efficiency in public good games, but does not test punishment or sanctioning per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is directly informative for predicting efficiency in public good games with voting mechanisms but does not provide evidence on the effects of punishment or sanctioning. The findings suggest that mechanisms that safeguard against exploitation (like unanimity voting) can increase contributions but may reduce efficiency due to coordination failures or excessive vetoes. For prediction tasks involving punishment, this paper is only indirectly relevant, as it shows how institutional safeguards affect efficiency, but not how explicit punishment changes outcomes.

- source: Grund, C., Harbring, C., Thommes, K., & Tilkes, K. R. (2020). Decisions on Extending Group Membership-Evidence from a Public Good Experiment. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment finds that temporary group members contribute marginally more when the possibility of group membership extension is decided endogenously by a group member, compared to an exogenous random draw. However, this effect is weak and does not translate into significantly higher overall group contributions. Permanent members' contributions are not significantly affected by the extension mechanism. When a temporary member is replaced (especially exogenously), the newcomer initially contributes more than other group members, but this effect diminishes quickly. The study discusses monetary incentives and payoffs, but the main focus is on contribution behavior rather than efficiency or group payoff. There is no direct analysis of how the extension or replacement mechanisms affect group efficiency relative to the social optimum.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence for the prediction task. It shows that mechanisms resembling social sanctions (replacement or exclusion) can have incentive effects on contribution behavior, but these effects are modest and short-lived. The design does not include explicit costly punishment or reward mechanisms, and efficiency is not directly measured. For prediction, the results suggest that changing the group membership mechanism (endogenous vs. exogenous extension) has limited impact on overall group efficiency, and that replacement (as a form of exclusion) may temporarily boost newcomer contributions. However, these effects are primarily on contribution rates, not on efficiency or total group payoff. The study is most informative for understanding how exclusion or replacement (as a weak form of sanction) affects cooperation, rather than for predicting the efficiency impact of explicit punishment mechanisms.

- source: Peng, H. C. (2022). Effects of majority-vote reward mechanism on cooperation: a public good experimental study. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The majority-vote reward mechanism significantly increases group efficiency and cooperation compared to both peer reward and no reward, in both homogeneous and heterogeneous MPCR settings. Peer reward increases efficiency and cooperation only in homogeneous groups. In heterogeneous groups, only the majority-vote reward mechanism leads to a significant increase in efficiency. The efficiency gains are due to higher contributions and more willingness to pay reward costs under the majority-vote mechanism. The paper provides direct evidence on efficiency outcomes (Table 6), showing that the majority-vote reward mechanism achieves the highest efficiency among the tested mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with 4 players, 6 rounds per condition, continuous contribution, no chat, and full information, adding a majority-vote reward mechanism (with a 1:3 cost-to-reward ratio) increases efficiency relative to the control (no reward) and peer reward conditions. Peer reward also increases efficiency in homogeneous groups, but not in heterogeneous groups. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar design dimensions, especially regarding the presence and type of reward mechanism, MPCR homogeneity, and the absence of punishment. The results suggest that, in the absence of punishment, majority-vote reward is a robust way to increase efficiency, especially when group members have heterogeneous returns from the public good.

- source: Nax, H. H., Balietti, S., Murphy, R. O., & Helbing, D. (2018). Adding noise to the institution: an experimental welfare investigation of the contribution-based grouping mechanism. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that, in a repeated public goods game with 16 players (4 groups of 4), introducing a contribution-based grouping mechanism (meritocratic matching) with low or moderate noise leads to much higher efficiency (average total payoff) than random matching (no meritocracy). The efficiency gains are robust across different levels of noise, with the highest efficiency observed under perfect meritocracy (no noise), but even substantial gains at moderate noise levels. The control (no meritocracy, i.e., random matching) produces the lowest efficiency, consistent with standard PGG decay. The study also finds that increased meritocracy reduces payoff inequality, contrary to theoretical expectations. The results are robust to different measures of inequality and are observed both in lab and online settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with grouping based on contributions, increasing the precision of the grouping mechanism (i.e., reducing noise in meritocratic matching) leads to large increases in efficiency relative to random matching. For prediction tasks, the presence of a contribution-based grouping mechanism (even if noisy) should be expected to substantially increase efficiency compared to standard PGG controls. The effect is robust across a range of noise levels, and the efficiency gains are not offset by increased inequality. However, the study does not involve punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, so its direct applicability to punishment-enabled games is limited. The findings are most relevant for predicting the effect of sorting/grouping mechanisms, not punishment.

- source: Kanitsar, G. (2021). Self-Governance in Generalized Exchange. A Laboratory Experiment on the Structural Embeddedness of Peer Punishment. *Games*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Peer punishment increases cooperation and group earnings (efficiency) in public good structures only when punishment is costless; costly punishment does not improve efficiency. In generalized exchange, peer punishment (both costly and costless) does not increase cooperation or group earnings. The effectiveness of punishment is thus highly contingent on the network structure: dense sanctioning networks (public good) allow punishment to deter defection and improve efficiency, while sparse networks (generalized exchange) do not. The cost-impact ratio of punishment is important, but its effect is moderated by the structural environment. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency gains from peer punishment in public goods do not transfer to generalized exchange settings.
  prediction_guidance: For prediction tasks, this paper provides strong evidence that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on the network structure. In standard public good games, costless punishment can substantially increase efficiency, but costly punishment may not. In generalized exchange (circular, sparse punishment network), enabling punishment—even if costless—does not improve efficiency. Thus, when predicting the efficiency effect of punishment, it is crucial to account for the density of the punishment network and whether the game is a standard public good or a generalized exchange. Control efficiency is not a reliable predictor of treatment efficiency when moving from public good to generalized exchange structures.

  findings: The paper finds that adding an altruistic punishment option (students can sacrifice their own points to remove points from a random over-consumer) increases the rate at which groups successfully avoid the 'tragedy of the commons' (i.e., more classes stay under the 10% threshold and receive points) compared to prior years without punishment. However, the study does not directly measure or report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare. The main findings are that the punishment option increases cooperation (fewer students choose the selfish option), and that students report positive attitudes and increased understanding of social dilemmas.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect evidence that introducing a punishment mechanism in a threshold public goods dilemma can increase group cooperation and the likelihood of group success (i.e., receiving the reward). However, because the outcome is binary (group gets points or not) and not a continuous efficiency or payoff measure, the findings are only adjacent to the prediction task. The evidence suggests that punishment can improve group outcomes in similar all-or-nothing, threshold-based social dilemmas, but does not provide quantitative estimates of efficiency change. The design is also classroom-based and not a standard repeated PGG, so caution is warranted in generalizing to other settings.

- source: Buso, I. M., Di Cagno, D., Gueth, W., & Spadoni, L. (2025). Voluntary Partnerships for Equally Sharing Contribution Costs. *B E Journal of Theoretical Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: Voluntary cost-sharing partnerships in a PGG-like environment significantly increase average contributions and prevent the typical decay of cooperation over repeated rounds. Partners contribute more than outsiders, and the presence of the partnership mechanism (whether endogenous or exogenous) leads to higher group contributions and payoffs than the standard PGG. The effect is strongest for larger partnerships. The mechanism does not involve punishment or reward, but rather a self-commitment to cost-sharing. Efficiency (in terms of group payoff relative to the social optimum) is not directly reported, but the data show that average contributions are about 50% of the endowment with partnerships, compared to about 32% in the standard PGG. The voluntary nature of partnership formation has a modest additional effect, especially for small partnerships.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that institutional mechanisms allowing voluntary cost-sharing partnerships can substantially increase group contributions and payoffs in PGG-like settings, even in the absence of explicit punishment or reward. For prediction tasks, the presence of such a partnership mechanism should be expected to yield higher efficiency than standard PGGs with similar MPCR and group size. The effect is robust to whether partnership formation is endogenous or exogenous, except for very small partnerships. The findings are most directly relevant for settings where cost-sharing or coalition formation is possible, but do not directly inform about punishment effects. The results suggest that, in the absence of punishment, other institutional mechanisms can also enhance efficiency, and that the design dimensions (player count, MPCR, endogenous/exogenous group formation) interact with the mechanism's effectiveness.

- source: Becchetti, L., Conzo, P., & Degli Antoni, G. (2015). Public disclosure of players' conduct and common resources harvesting: experimental evidence from a Nairobi slum. *Social Choice and Welfare*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment finds that, in a repeated CPRG with fixed groups and no punishment, providing public feedback on individual withdrawals and payoffs (vs. private feedback only) increases average withdrawal rates by about 12 percentage points (from 62.7% to 74.3%), significantly reducing group efficiency and total payoff. The divergence between conditions grows over rounds. The effect is robust to controls for individual and group characteristics. The authors attribute the effect to conditional cooperation/conformity: when information is public, individuals tend to match the group's average behavior, but this leads to convergence at higher withdrawal (lower cooperation) levels. The results support Ostrom's hypothesis that, without formal sanctions, monitoring alone can undermine cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods or common pool resource games without punishment, increasing transparency or monitoring (public feedback on individual actions and payoffs) can reduce efficiency and group payoff, especially in low social capital environments. For the downstream prediction task, this suggests that, when punishment is not available, enabling public information about individual behavior is likely to decrease efficiency relative to a private information baseline. The findings are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with and without public feedback, but do not inform the effect of punishment, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in either condition. The results highlight that monitoring alone is not a substitute for punishment in sustaining cooperation.

- source: Li, Y. Z., Luo, J., Niu, H., & Ye, H. (2023). When punishers might be loved: fourth-party choices and third-party punishment in a delegation game. *Theory and Decision*.

  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: Across three lab experiments, the paper shows: (1) Helpers are more trusted and preferred as partners in trust games, but punishers are more likely to be offered higher amounts in ultimatum games due to their deterrence signal, resulting in higher actual payoffs for punishers. (2) In a delegation game, when the proposer's knowledge of the delegate's punishment history is made explicit, fourth-party players prefer to select punishers as their delegates, and this choice leads to higher expected payoffs for both the delegate and the principal. (3) The effect is contingent on the visibility of the punishment signal; when the proposer's information is limited, helpers are preferred. Thus, punishment increases efficiency/payoff in environments where its deterrence is visible and relevant.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in games adjacent to public goods games (third-party punishment, ultimatum, and delegation games), enabling punishment increases the efficiency (payoff) of punishers and those who select them as delegates, especially when the punishment signal is visible to others. The effect is context-dependent: punishment is less effective in trust-based settings but more effective in settings where deterrence is valuable and observable. For prediction tasks, the presence of a visible punishment mechanism, the structure of the game (ultimatum/delegation vs. trust), and the information available to other players are key moderators. Control efficiency (without punishment) is lower than with punishment in deterrence-relevant settings.

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment used a 2x2 design (punishment/no punishment x justification/no justification) in a three-player trust game (two senders, one responder, repeated stranger matching, 6 rounds). The opportunity for senders to punish responders (at a cost: 1 point spent reduces responder's payoff by 3) did not significantly increase the amounts sent or returned, nor did it improve group efficiency.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a trust-game-like environment with costly punishment (punishment cost 1:3), enabling punishment does not increase group efficiency and may reduce it due to the cost of punishment. The design is adjacent to public goods games (not a standard PGG, but a trust game with two senders and one responder, continuous contributions, no communication, 6 rounds, known horizon, no rewards). The findings suggest that, for similar game designs, the predicted efficiency with punishment enabled should be lower than or similar to the control (no punishment), unless the punishment is costless or much more effective. The paper also shows that justification requirements can increase contributions, but this does not translate into higher efficiency when punishment is present. Thus, for prediction, enabling costly punishment in similar trust or PGG-adjacent games is likely to reduce or not improve efficiency relative to control.
