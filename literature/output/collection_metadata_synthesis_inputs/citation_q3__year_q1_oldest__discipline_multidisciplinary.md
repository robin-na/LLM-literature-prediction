- source: García, J., & Traulsen, A. (2012). The Structure of Mutations and the Evolution of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in evolutionary models of optional public goods games with punishment, the assumed structure of mutations (i.e., how likely it is for one strategy to mutate into another) has a major impact on the stationary distribution of strategies. With uniform mutations, populations spend most of the time in cooperative, punishing states, leading to high efficiency. When mutations from abstaining (loner) strategies to social (cooperating, punishing, or defecting) strategies are made less likely, or when a bitwise mutation structure is used, the prevalence of cooperation and punishment drops, and abstention can become the most common outcome, reducing group efficiency. The effect of punishment on efficiency is therefore not robust to changes in the mutation kernel, and predictions about the benefits of punishment depend critically on this modeling choice.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the mutation structure in evolutionary models. If the mutation process allows easy movement between all strategies (uniform kernel), punishment can sustain high efficiency. If the mutation process is more constrained (e.g., bitwise or risk-averse kernels), punishment is less effective, and efficiency can be much lower due to the dominance of abstention. For prediction tasks, this means that unless the mutation structure is well justified and matches the real-world process, predictions about the efficiency effects of punishment may be unreliable. The paper also provides explicit parameterizations (e.g., group size n=5, population N=100, r=3, c=1, punishment cost γ=0.3, fine β=1, loner payoff σ=1) for the theoretical models, which can inform parameter mapping.

- source: Fehl, K., Sommerfeld, R. D., Semmann, D., Krambeck, H. J., & Milinski, M. (2012). I Dare You to Punish Me-Vendettas in Games of Cooperation. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In public goods games with multiple rounds of punishment, participants often retaliate and escalate into vendettas, incurring high costs. Although cooperation increases, the costs of punishment and counter-punishment reduce overall earnings, especially for those involved in vendettas. The negative impact on payoffs is clear: participants involved in vendettas or retaliation earn significantly less than those who abstain. In contrast, in dyadic games, punishment and vendettas are rare, and most participants act cooperatively, so efficiency losses from punishment are minimal. The study highlights that while punishment can increase cooperation, it can also lead to costly vendettas that undermine group efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling repeated, peer-based punishment in public goods games can increase cooperation but at the cost of reduced efficiency due to costly vendettas. The negative effect on payoffs is especially pronounced for those who engage in cycles of retaliation. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, even if control efficiency is moderate, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and can even reduce it if vendettas are likely. The design dimensions most relevant here are the number of punishment rounds, the ability to identify punishers, and the group vs. dyad structure. The findings are most directly applicable to settings with repeated, open punishment opportunities and identifiable punishers.

- source: Smaldino, P. E., & Lubell, M. (2011). An Institutional Mechanism for Assortment in an Ecology of Games. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr
  findings: The paper finds that capacity constraints (limits on the number of players per game) are a simple and effective institutional mechanism for promoting positive assortment of cooperators in an ecology of public goods games. When group size is limited, cooperators can cluster together and achieve higher payoffs relative to defectors, even without punishment or reputation. Budget constraints (limits on the number of games per player) are less effective. The model does not include punishment, but discusses it as a more complex and potentially costly alternative. The main outcome is the relative cooperator payoff, not group efficiency or total welfare.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as it does not model punishment or sanctions. However, it is relevant for understanding how institutional design (e.g., group size limits) can promote cooperation and higher payoffs without the need for punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper suggests that capacity constraints can increase the relative payoff of cooperators, but does not inform the effect of punishment on efficiency. It is not directly useful for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency in the context of punishment.

- source: Pan, X. S., & Houser, D. (2011). Competition for Trophies Triggers Male Generosity. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The study finds that in a standard linear public goods game, the introduction of a unique and displayable reward (a 'trophy' mug) leads to significantly higher contributions, especially among males, compared to both a baseline (no reward) and a control reward (ice-cream) that is not unique or displayable. The effect is driven by male competitiveness for the trophy. Female behavior is less affected by the trophy. The paper does not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism, nor does it report efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings as outcomes. The main outcomes are behavioral: contribution rates, approval points, and star rewards.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or payoff in public goods games. It is relevant for understanding the effects of non-pecuniary, status-based rewards (trophies) on cooperation, especially among males, but does not inform the prediction of efficiency changes due to punishment. The game design dimensions are well described, but the absence of punishment and lack of efficiency or payoff analysis means this paper should not be used to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Seip, E. C., van Dijk, W. W., & Rotteveel, M. (2009). On Hotheads and Dirty Harries The Primacy of Anger in Altruistic Punishment. *Values, Empathy, and Fairness Across Social Barriers*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds
  findings: The main finding is that anger, rather than the perception of unfairness, is the primary driver of altruistic punishment in social dilemma games. Experimental evidence from a one-shot, three-person public goods game with punishment shows that lower contributions from others evoke more anger and lead to harsher punishment, and that the effect of others' contributions on punishment is fully mediated by experienced anger. Priming participants with anger increases their tendency to punish defectors. The paper does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, so it does not directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods games. However, it does suggest that the presence and intensity of punishment are strongly influenced by emotional responses (especially anger) to defection or low contribution. For prediction tasks, this implies that punishment is likely to be used when players experience anger at norm violations, but the paper does not address whether this increases or decreases group efficiency. The findings are most relevant for understanding when and why punishment is deployed, not its payoff consequences.

- source: Brown, S. P., & Taddei, F. (2007). The Durability of Public Goods Changes the Dynamics and Nature of Social Dilemmas. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=none | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: none
  findings: The paper finds that when the public good is durable (i.e., changes slowly relative to the frequency of cooperators), the dynamics of cooperation and group payoff can be substantially altered compared to classic models. For example, durable public goods can lead to multi-generational oscillations in cooperation and payoff, shift the thresholds for invasion and stability of cooperation, and even allow for transient increases in cooperation in settings where defection would otherwise dominate. The explicit modeling of public good dynamics reveals that group payoff and efficiency are not solely determined by current cooperation rates, but also by the history and durability of the public good. However, the paper does not address punishment, sanctions, or any intervention mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper is useful for understanding how the dynamics and durability of the public good itself can affect efficiency and group payoff in public-goods-game-like environments. It highlights that efficiency is not always a simple function of current cooperation rates, especially when the public good is durable or slow to change. However, because the paper does not include punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. It is most relevant for understanding baseline dynamics and for considering how lagged or durable public goods might interact with other interventions in future models.

- source: Brüne, M., Scheele, D., Heinisch, C., Tas, C., Wischniewski, J., & Güntürkün, O. (2012). Empathy Moderates the Effect of Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation of the Right Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex on Costly Punishment. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=weak
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that inhibiting the right DLPFC via rTMS increases the amount of costly punishment assigned by third-party observers in a Dictator Game with punishment option. This effect is significantly moderated by individual differences in empathy: the increase in punishment is stronger for individuals with lower empathy scores. No significant effect is found for left DLPFC stimulation. The study does not report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare, and does not use a public goods game or a direct variant, but rather a third-party punishment Dictator Game. The findings are relevant for understanding the neural and psychological mechanisms underlying costly punishment, but do not provide direct evidence about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or their close variants. It does, however, provide informative evidence about the psychological and neural mechanisms that may underlie costly punishment, showing that punishment can be increased by disrupting cognitive control (right DLPFC) and that this effect is moderated by empathy. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency or group payoff, this paper is only weakly relevant, as it does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes, nor does it use a PGG or close variant. It may be useful for understanding individual differences in punishment behavior, but not for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes.

- source: Helbing, D., & Johansson, A. (2010). Cooperation, Norms, and Revolutions: A Unified Game-Theoretical Approach. *Plos One*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that the dynamics and outcomes of cooperation and norm establishment in multi-population games depend critically on the payoff structure (game type), the relative power of populations, and initial conditions. In multi-population prisoner's dilemma games, defection dominates regardless of power. In stag hunt games, shared norms can emerge, often set by the more powerful or initially larger group, but sometimes by the weaker group depending on initial conditions. In snowdrift games, polarization and abrupt 'revolutionary' transitions can occur. Costly punishment can transform a prisoner's dilemma into a stag hunt, supporting norm establishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, focusing instead on behavioral equilibria and norm dynamics.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical insight into how punishment, payoff structure, and group power shape the emergence of cooperation and norms in environments related to, but not identical with, public goods games. It suggests that punishment can promote norm establishment by changing the effective game structure, but does not provide quantitative predictions about efficiency or group payoff. For prediction tasks focused on efficiency, this paper is only indirectly informative: it supports the idea that punishment can shift equilibria toward more cooperative outcomes in some settings, but does not quantify efficiency gains or losses. Its main value is in highlighting the importance of game structure and group asymmetries as moderators of punishment effects.

- source: Mancini, A., Betti, V., Panasiti, M. S., Pavone, E. F., & Aglioti, S. M. (2011). Suffering Makes You Egoist: Acute Pain Increases Acceptance Rates and Reduces Fairness during a Bilateral Ultimatum Game. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The study finds that acute pain leads to higher acceptance rates of unfair offers and lower fairness in offers made, suggesting that pain induces more self-interested behavior in both roles of the Ultimatum Game. The effect is specific to pain (not just heat) and is most pronounced for moderately unfair offers. The intensity of pain predicts acceptance rates for these offers. However, the study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or any direct payoff-based outcomes, and does not include punishment or sanctioning mechanisms.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It uses the Ultimatum Game, which is adjacent but not equivalent to PGGs, and focuses on the effect of pain (a state manipulation) rather than institutional punishment. The outcomes are behavioral (acceptance, offer size) rather than payoff-based. Thus, its relevance for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like games is limited.

- source: Panasiti, M. S., Pavone, E. F., Merla, A., & Aglioti, S. M. (2011). Situational and Dispositional Determinants of Intentional Deceiving. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=none | punishment=none | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that participants are more likely to lie for self-gain when facing an unfavorable outcome, but this tendency is reduced when their reputation is at risk (i.e., when the opponent will know about their lies). The presence of the opponent in the same room does not significantly affect lying rates. Personality traits, particularly reward dependence and social desirability, predict the degree to which reputation risk reduces lying. Manipulativeness and moral disengagement are associated with less sensitivity to reputation risk. The study does not involve punishment, sanctions, or public goods game structures, and does not report on efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games or similar environments. It does not manipulate or analyze punishment, sanctions, or efficiency/welfare outcomes. Its findings are limited to the behavioral determinants of lying under reputation risk and personality variation in a dyadic deception game. It should not be used to inform predictions about punishment effects on efficiency in PGG-like settings.
