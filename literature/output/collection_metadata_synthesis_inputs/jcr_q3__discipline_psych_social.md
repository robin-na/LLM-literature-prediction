- source: Kuwabara, K., & Yu, S. Y. (2017). Costly Punishment Increases Prosocial Punishment by Designated Punishers: Power and Legitimacy in Public Goods Games. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment shows that in a public goods game with three players per group and six rounds, making punishment costly (1 MU per point, 3 MU impact per point) increases efficiency and earnings, especially when punishment is centralized in a designated punisher. Costly designated punishment leads to higher prosocial punishment, higher cooperation, and higher group earnings than costless designated punishment. Costless peer punishment leads to excessive punishment and the lowest efficiency. The effect of costly punishment on efficiency is mediated by increased legitimacy and more targeted (prosocial) punishment. The findings suggest that the cost of punishment is not just an economic parameter but also a social signal that shapes legitimacy and the effectiveness of enforcement.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with a designated punisher, enabling costly punishment (vs. costless) increases efficiency and group earnings. The effect is less pronounced for peer punishment, where costless punishment leads to excessive, inefficient punishment. For prediction tasks, the presence of a designated punisher, the cost of punishment, and whether punishment is costly or costless are key moderators of the efficiency effect. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling costly punishment (especially with a designated punisher) will increase efficiency, while enabling costless punishment (especially with peer punishment) may decrease efficiency. The results are directly relevant for mapping game design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

- source: Fischer, S., Grechenig, K., & Meier, N. (2016). Monopolizing Sanctioning Power under Noise Eliminates Perverse Punishment But Does Not Increase Cooperation. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that centralizing sanctioning power (i.e., having a single authority punish) eliminates perverse punishment (punishment of cooperators) but does not increase overall cooperation or efficiency compared to decentralized peer-to-peer punishment, except under intermediate noise where decentralized punishment leads to more punishment and lower efficiency early in the experiment. Under perfect information and high noise, efficiency and contributions do not differ significantly between institutions. Noise in information reduces efficiency in both institutions. The design holds punishment cost, effectiveness, and externalities constant across treatments, isolating the effect of centralization per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public goods game with 5 players, 30 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, no default, MPCR 0.4, and punishment cost/effectiveness held constant, enabling punishment (either centralized or decentralized) does not reliably increase efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) when information is perfect or highly noisy. Under intermediate noise, decentralized punishment can reduce efficiency due to excessive punishment, but this effect is not robust in the long run. The results suggest that the efficiency effect of punishment depends on the information environment and the punishment institution, but centralization alone does not guarantee higher efficiency. For prediction, the presence of punishment does not necessarily increase efficiency unless the information environment and institution interact in specific ways.

- source: Andrighetto, G., Brandts, J., Conte, R., Sabater-Mir, J., Solaz, H., Székely, A., & Villatoro, D. (2016). Counter-Punishment, Communication, and Cooperation among Partners. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that in a repeated public goods game with fixed groups and fixed identifiers, allowing communication (either normative messages at the punishment stage or free-form messages at the counter-punishment stage) significantly increases both contributions and group earnings compared to a baseline with no communication. The negative effect of counter-punishment on efficiency, observed in prior work, is neutralized when communication is possible. There are no significant differences in efficiency between the different types of communication, and combining both types does not yield additional benefits. Punishment and counter-punishment levels are generally low, and there are no significant differences in punishment levels across treatments. The key moderator of efficiency is the presence of communication, not the presence of counter-punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in repeated public goods games with punishment and counter-punishment, enabling communication (either as normative messages or accountability messages) substantially increases efficiency relative to the no-communication baseline. For prediction tasks, the presence or absence of communication is a critical moderator of the efficiency impact of punishment/counter-punishment. When communication is possible, the efficiency loss from counter-punishment is eliminated. The game design dimensions most relevant for prediction are the presence of communication, the punishment/counter-punishment structure, and the information structure (fixed identifiers, group feedback). Control efficiency (no communication, with punishment and counter-punishment) is lower than in any treatment with communication.

- source: Fatas, E., & Mateu, G. (2015). Antisocial punishment in two social dilemmas. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab experiment in Spain, groups of four played two types of public goods games: a standard linear VCM and a weakest-link minimum game (WLM), each for 20 rounds without punishment and then 20 rounds with punishment. In the VCM, punishment led to only moderate increases in public good provision and did not increase group efficiency, due to substantial antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors). In the WLM, punishment led to a dramatic increase in public good provision and group efficiency, with antisocial punishment being rare and most groups earning more with punishment than without. The effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is thus highly dependent on the production technology of the public good, with complementarity (WLM) supporting positive effects of punishment even in a culture with high antisocial punishment in linear games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games is highly sensitive to the production function: in linear VCMs, punishment may not improve efficiency in cultures with high antisocial punishment, but in weakest-link (complementary) games, punishment can substantially increase efficiency. For prediction, the game structure (linear vs. weakest-link) is a critical moderator. Control efficiency (without punishment) is similar across games, but the treatment effect of punishment is much more positive in the WLM. The paper also provides parameter values for player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and information structure, which can be used to inform predictions in similar designs.

- source: Sparks, A., Burleigh, T., & Barclay, P. (2024). Expressed disapproval does not sustain long-term cooperation as effectively as costly punishment. *Evolutionary Human Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a 40-round, 4-player public goods game, the presence of costly punishment (where players could pay to reduce others' earnings) led to a sustained increase in contributions and, over time, group earnings. Costless public disapproval (where players could assign disapproval points with no material consequence) produced only a temporary increase in contributions, which then declined to control levels. Group earnings in the costly punishment condition started lower (due to punishment costs) but increased over time, eventually matching or slightly exceeding the control and disapproval conditions by the end of the game. The study provides strong evidence that only punishment with tangible consequences can maintain long-term cooperation and group efficiency; costless disapproval is not sufficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard repeated public goods game increases long-run group efficiency (as measured by group earnings) compared to a control game without punishment. The effect is robust over 40 rounds, with punishment leading to higher contributions and, eventually, higher or equal group earnings despite the cost of punishment. Costless disapproval (without material consequences) does not sustain efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that, holding other design dimensions constant, enabling costly punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially in longer games. The specific game parameters (4 players, 40 rounds, MPCR 0.4, no chat, continuous contributions, punishment cost and tech specified) make this study highly informative for similar designs.

- source: Pfattheicher, S., Böhm, R., & Kesberg, R. (2018). The Advantage of Democratic Peer Punishment in Sustaining Cooperation within Groups. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Democratic peer punishment in a repeated public goods game leads to higher cooperation and higher total group payoff than standard peer punishment, primarily by reducing costly and antisocial punishment. Over six rounds, both punishment systems are less efficient than no-punishment, but democratic peer punishment's efficiency increases over time and may eventually exceed the no-punishment baseline. Psychological outcomes (fairness, satisfaction, trust) are also highest in the democratic punishment condition. The study provides direct evidence on how the structure of punishment (democratic vs. standard) moderates the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the design of the punishment mechanism (specifically, introducing a democratic voting step for punishment execution) can substantially moderate the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods games. When predicting the effect of enabling punishment, it is important to distinguish between standard peer punishment and democratic peer punishment: standard peer punishment increases cooperation but reduces efficiency due to high punishment costs, while democratic peer punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency relative to standard peer punishment, and may eventually outperform no-punishment in efficiency if the game is long enough. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 6 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and a punishment cost/impact ratio of 1:3. The results suggest that, all else equal, democratic peer punishment is more likely to yield positive or neutral efficiency effects compared to standard peer punishment, especially as the number of rounds increases.

- source: Dorrough, A., Glöckner, A., & Lee, B. (2017). Race for Power in Public Good Games with Unequal, Unstable Punishment Power. *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Across three lab experiments, the authors show that in standard public good games with punishment, group efficiency is higher when punishment power is equal and stable. When punishment power is both unequal (one strong punisher, three weak) and unstable (the strong role can change based on performance or prosociality), groups experience more punishment, similar or lower contributions, and significantly lower efficiency (average round win). The negative effect on efficiency is robust to whether the strong role is assigned based on selfish or prosocial behavior. The detrimental effect is not due to inequality alone, but to the combination of inequality and instability, which triggers a 'race for power' and resource waste.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public good games with punishment, introducing instability in the assignment of punishment power (especially when combined with inequality in punishment effectiveness) reduces group efficiency compared to stable, equal punishment power. For prediction, if the control (punishment disabled) efficiency is known, enabling punishment with equal, stable power is likely to increase efficiency, but enabling punishment with unequal, unstable power is likely to decrease efficiency relative to the equal, stable punishment case. The effect is driven by increased competitive punishment rather than increased cooperation. The findings are directly relevant for settings with 4 players, 10 rounds, continuous contributions, no chat, and full feedback on others' actions.

- source: Gintis, H. (2003). Solving the puzzle of prosociality. *Rationality and Society*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games, the introduction of costly punishment by strong reciprocators can sustain high levels of cooperation and group efficiency, even when cooperation would otherwise unravel due to self-interest. Theoretical results show that with sufficient oblique cultural transmission and a non-trivial fraction of punishers, full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) can be a stable equilibrium. The paper also reviews experimental evidence showing that when punishment is allowed, group payoffs and cooperation rates are significantly higher than when punishment is not allowed. The model further predicts that the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining efficiency depends on the cost and impact of punishment, the fraction of punishers, and the strength of cultural transmission.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and empirical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in public goods games increases group efficiency relative to control games without punishment, provided that punishment is not prohibitively costly and that a sufficient fraction of players are willing to punish defectors. The model and supporting evidence suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across a range of game parameters, including group size and number of rounds, as long as punishment is effective and not too costly. The paper also highlights the importance of cultural and institutional context (e.g., norm internalization, oblique transmission) in sustaining prosocial behavior and efficiency. For prediction tasks, this paper supports a positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially when punishment is not too costly and is effective at deterring defection.

- source: Bednar, J. (2006). Is full compliance possible? Conditions for shirking with imperfect monitoring and continuous action spaces. *Journal of Theoretical Politics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games with continuous contribution choices and imperfect monitoring (where only a noisy group-level signal is observed), punishment regimes can sustain some compliance but cannot eliminate shirking. As a result, group efficiency is always below the full-cooperation level. The equilibrium level of compliance (and thus efficiency) is determined by the tradeoff between the marginal benefit of shirking and the marginal expected cost (via increased probability of triggering group punishment). Full compliance (and thus maximal efficiency) is only possible under perfect monitoring or very special functional forms. The paper also shows that increasing the severity of punishment can increase compliance (and efficiency) up to a point, but cannot fully eliminate shirking unless punishment is extreme or monitoring is perfect.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in repeated public goods games with continuous action spaces and imperfect monitoring, enabling punishment will increase compliance and efficiency relative to no punishment, but will not achieve full efficiency. The equilibrium efficiency with punishment will be strictly less than the full-cooperation benchmark, and the gap depends on the monitoring technology, the severity and structure of punishment, and the marginal per-capita return. The results suggest that, for prediction, one should expect a positive but incomplete effect of punishment on efficiency, with the effect size moderated by the informativeness of monitoring and the cost/impact of punishment. The paper also provides comparative statics: more severe punishment and more informative monitoring increase efficiency, but diminishing returns and practical limits apply. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in settings with imperfect monitoring and continuous contributions.

- source: Prétôt, L., Taylor, Q., & Mcauliffe, K. (2024). Children cooperate more with in-group members than with out-group members in an iterated face-to-face Prisoner's Dilemma Game. *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in linear public goods games (CPR settings), cooperation and high efficiency can be sustained if and only if the group collectively invests a sufficient fraction of its resources into monitoring and sanctioning rule violations. When monitoring is funded unilaterally (peer punishment), self-interested agents will not invest enough, leading to a second-order free-rider problem and low efficiency. When monitoring is funded collectively from the common-pool resource (institutional punishment), there exists a threshold investment (beta) above which full cooperation and high efficiency are stable equilibria. The paper provides explicit formulas for the minimum investment required and shows that the effectiveness of monitoring (punishment technology), the cost of monitoring, and the severity of sanctions all moderate the efficiency gains from punishment. The model also predicts that initial 'free' monitoring (by some agents ignoring costs) may be needed to escape the no-cooperation equilibrium, but once cooperation is established, monitoring can be fully incentivized and efficient.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, quantitative guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-like games, conditional on game design dimensions. It shows that the presence and structure of punishment (especially whether it is institutionally organized and collectively funded) are critical moderators of efficiency. The explicit inequalities derived in the paper allow one to predict whether enabling punishment will increase efficiency, given the marginal per-capita return, punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, and the fraction of resources invested in monitoring. The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling institutionally organized punishment with sufficient investment will reliably increase efficiency, while peer punishment may not. The results are most directly applicable to settings where monitoring and sanctioning are collective, and less so to purely peer-based punishment. The paper is highly relevant for mapping from design dimensions to expected efficiency outcomes.

Third-party reward alone has little effect on cooperation. The combination of punishment and reward (TP/R) leads to the highest average cooperation, though the difference with punishment alone is not statistically significant. Both punishment and TP/R treatments maintain high cooperation throughout the game, while cooperation declines in the baseline and reward-only treatments. In terms of payoffs, third-party interventions (especially punishment and TP/R) change the earnings of PGG-players, generally improving the alignment between contribution and payoff. The paper provides evidence that third-party sanctions, especially those involving punishment, can increase group earnings and improve the outcome of cooperation.

The effect is even more pronounced when both punishment and reward are available, suggesting a complementary effect. The game design dimensions (e.g., player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are clearly specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in similar lab PGGs. The findings support the prediction that, holding control efficiency constant, enabling punishment (especially with a 3:1 punishment tech and moderate cost) will increase efficiency. Reward alone is much less effective. The results are most applicable to lab PGGs with 4 players, 10 rounds, no chat, and full information about others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Harrell, A., & Wolff, T. (2023). Cooperation in Networked Collective-Action Groups: Information Access and Norm Enforcement in Groups of Different Sizes. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that punishment opportunities in public goods games (PGGs) increase cooperation and, over time, group earnings, especially in large and densely connected groups. In information-only conditions (no punishment), dense networks increase cooperation in small groups but not in large groups, where dense information can actually decrease cooperation. When punishment is enabled, dense networks increase cooperation in both small and large groups, but the effect is especially strong in large groups. Although punishment is initially costly and reduces earnings, the resulting increase in cooperation leads to higher group profits in later rounds. The study provides direct evidence that the efficiency gains from punishment depend on both group size and network density.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling punishment in PGGs increases group efficiency (as measured by group earnings/profits) over time, particularly in large and densely connected groups. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment should be expected to have a more positive effect on efficiency in large groups with dense networks than in small or sparsely connected groups. The effect is less pronounced or even negative in large groups without punishment, and in small groups, dense information alone can suffice to sustain cooperation. The findings suggest that the interaction between group size, network density, and punishment availability is critical for predicting efficiency outcomes.

For low enhancement factors, distributing punishment/reward equally is optimal for maximizing cooperation. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency) depends on the distribution of punishment, the enhancement factor (analogous to MPCR), and the network structure. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited to inferring that higher cooperation rates (under optimal punishment schemes) may translate to higher efficiency, especially when the enhancement factor is high. The findings are most relevant for settings with network heterogeneity and institutional (not peer) punishment.

For prediction tasks, this suggests that the timing of sanctioning opportunities (ex ante vs. ex post) is a critical moderator of the behavioral use of punishment, which could in turn affect efficiency if actual group payoffs were measured.
