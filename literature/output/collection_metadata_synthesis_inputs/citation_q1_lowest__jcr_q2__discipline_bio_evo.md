- source: Levy, D. A. (2022). Optimizing the social utility of judicial punishment: An evolutionary biology and neuroscience perspective. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_punishment_id
  findings: The paper argues that the human tendency to punish non-cooperators, even at a personal cost, is rooted in evolutionary and neural mechanisms. It reviews evidence from public goods games and other economic experiments showing that punishment can increase cooperation and, in some cases, average group profit. The author discusses the adaptive value of punishment, the neural reward mechanisms involved, and the importance of institutionalizing punishment to maintain social order and cooperation. The paper also highlights complexities and limitations, such as the costliness of punishment, the potential for retaliation, and the need for centralized enforcement in large societies.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and review-based support for the idea that punishment can increase cooperation in public goods games and, under some conditions, improve group outcomes. It references empirical studies (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, Gächter et al., 2008) that find punishment can increase average profit, but also notes limitations and boundary conditions (e.g., costliness, retaliation, institutional context). For the prediction task, this paper supports the general mechanism by which punishment can improve efficiency, but does not provide direct quantitative evidence or dimension-specific effect sizes. It is most useful for contextualizing why and when punishment might be effective, rather than for precise prediction.

- source: Li, J. B., Liu, X. L., Yin, X. L., Li, S. Q., Wang, P. C., Niu, X. F., & Zhu, C. K. (2018). Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation of the Right Lateral Prefrontal Cortex Changes a priori Normative Beliefs in Voluntary Cooperation. *Frontiers in Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=none | payoff=none
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that modulating rLPFC activity via tDCS causally shifts participants' a priori normative beliefs about cooperation in a public goods game. Anodal stimulation increases, and cathodal stimulation decreases, both the perceived normative standard (norm.belief) and the expected contributions of others (pg.belief). These effects are robust across player types (high and low endowment) and are statistically significant. However, the study does not measure or report actual contribution behavior, group payoffs, or efficiency, nor does it include any punishment or sanction mechanism.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide evidence relevant to predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It uses a standard linear PGG design but does not include punishment, nor does it report any efficiency, payoff, or contribution outcomes. The only outcomes are beliefs about norms and others' contributions. Therefore, it does not inform the downstream prediction task of how punishment affects efficiency, given game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- source: Chen, S., Shi, J. C., Yang, X. L., Ye, H., & Luo, J. (2019). Modulating Activity in the Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex Changes Punishment in the 3-Player Prisoner's Dilemma: A Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation Study. *Frontiers in Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that modulating DLPFC activity via tDCS can significantly decrease the amount of punishment assigned by cooperators to defectors (both in second-party and third-party punishment), depending on the stimulation mode. One bilateral stimulation (left anodal/right cathodal) increases cooperation rates. However, the study does not report group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare as outcomes. The findings are relevant for understanding neural mechanisms of punishment and cooperation, but do not provide direct evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff in public goods or prisoner's dilemma games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency or group payoff, as it does not report these outcomes. It does, however, provide detailed evidence on how punishment behavior and cooperation rates are affected by neural modulation in a 3-player prisoner's dilemma with punishment. For the prediction task, this paper suggests that punishment behavior can be modulated by factors unrelated to game design (e.g., neural stimulation), and that cooperation can be increased by certain interventions. However, without efficiency or payoff data, the paper's direct value for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions is limited.

- source: dos Santos, M., Braithwaite, V. A., & Wedekind, C. (2014). Exposure to superfluous information reduces cooperation and increases antisocial punishment in reputation-based interactions. *Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In reputation-based helping games (without punishment), cognitive disturbance (superfluous words) reduces cooperation and lowers group payoffs, indicating that efficiency is sensitive to cognitive constraints. In reputation-based punishment games, disturbance increases antisocial punishment and reduces punishment of defectors, but does not significantly change overall cooperation or mean final payoffs. The link between reputation and payoff is weakened under disturbance. The study highlights that experimental outcomes in reputation and punishment games are highly sensitive to environmental and cognitive factors, and that the expected efficiency gains from punishment or reputation mechanisms may not materialize under cognitive load.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the efficiency effects of punishment and reputation mechanisms in indirect reciprocity games are sensitive to cognitive context. For prediction tasks, it suggests that efficiency gains from punishment (or reputation) may be undermined or nullified if cognitive load is high, even when game design parameters are otherwise favorable. The results also show that the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency: the effect depends on how punishment is used (e.g., antisocial vs. prosocial punishment) and on whether players can process reputational information. When using control efficiency to predict treatment efficiency, one should consider that cognitive or informational disturbances can reduce or eliminate the positive effects of punishment on efficiency.

- source: Güney, S., & Newell, B. R. (2013). Fairness overrides reputation: the importance of fairness considerations in altruistic cooperation. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_n_rounds
  findings: The experiments show that rejection of unfair offers (costly punishment) in the Mini Ultimatum Game is primarily driven by fairness considerations, particularly the perceived intentions of the proposer. When intentions are not assessable, outcome fairness still motivates some punishment, but to a lesser degree. The opportunity for reputation building and future interaction (i.e., repeated, non-anonymous play) does not significantly increase costly punishment when fairness considerations are strong, but can increase punishment when intentions are ambiguous. The study does not analyze or report on efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the psychological drivers of costly punishment in ultimatum-like bargaining games are primarily fairness-based, not reputation-based, and that the presence of repeated interaction or reputation opportunities does not necessarily increase punishment or change behavioral outcomes when fairness motives are strong. However, because the study does not report on efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods or similar games. The findings are most relevant for understanding when and why punishment occurs, not its aggregate payoff consequences.

- source: Cisarovsky, G., Bshary, A., Bouzelboudjen, M., & Bshary, R. (2012). Spatial Group Structure as Potential Mechanism to Maintain Cooperation in Fish Shoals of Unrelated Individuals. *Ethology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=observational | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=adjacent | punishment=close | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count
  findings: The paper finds that anthias in natural shoals have partially non-overlapping home ranges, and blennies (parasites) show site fidelity, attacking more frequently in certain areas. Individuals more exposed to the blenny may benefit more from punishing (chasing) the blenny, making punishment potentially self-serving rather than purely altruistic. This spatial structuring could help maintain the public good of punishment even in large, unrelated groups. However, the study does not measure or report efficiency, group payoff, or direct fitness outcomes; it infers potential payoff implications from spatial and behavioral patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect, contextual evidence that spatial structure and local exposure can moderate the effectiveness and self-serving nature of punishment in public-goods-like dilemmas in natural systems. However, it does not provide quantitative or experimental data on efficiency or group payoff, nor does it manipulate game design dimensions in a controlled way. For prediction tasks, the main value is in highlighting that spatial structure and heterogeneity in exposure can affect the distribution and potential benefits of punishment, but the paper does not provide direct input for predicting efficiency changes due to punishment in standard PGG designs.
