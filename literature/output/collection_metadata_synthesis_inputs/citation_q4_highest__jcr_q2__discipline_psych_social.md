- source: Reuben, E., & Riedl, A. (2009). Public Goods Provision and Sanctioning in Privileged Groups. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: This experiment compares standard (normal) and privileged public goods groups, with and without the option for decentralized costly punishment. In normal groups (all members have the same marginal per-capita return), punishment substantially increases contributions and group earnings, especially in later rounds. In privileged groups (one member has a high marginal return, others low), punishment increases contributions and earnings only slightly, and it increases inequality. The main reason is that punishment is less targeted and less effective in privileged groups: high-benefit members punish in an unstructured way, and low-benefit members respond weakly to punishment. As a result, the efficiency gains from punishment are much smaller in privileged groups than in normal groups.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency depends strongly on group composition, specifically on whether the group is homogeneous (normal) or contains privileged members (heterogeneous marginal returns). For standard linear PGGs with 3 players, 10 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR, punishment increases efficiency, especially in later rounds. However, if the group is privileged (one high-benefit, two low-benefit), the efficiency gain from punishment is much smaller, and inequality increases. Thus, when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, it is crucial to account for heterogeneity in marginal returns. Control efficiency (without punishment) is higher in privileged groups, but the treatment effect of punishment is much weaker than in normal groups.

- source: Decker, T., Stiehler, A., & Strobel, M. (2003). A comparison of punishment rules in repeated public good games - An experimental study. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=mixed | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study finds that collective punishment rules generally induce more punishment activity and higher contributions than the individual rule. The most severe collective rule (maxi) achieves the highest contributions but not the highest efficiency, as the costs of punishment offset the gains from increased cooperation. Efficiency (group profit after punishment costs) does not differ significantly between rules, and the relationship between punishment severity and efficiency is reversed compared to the relationship with contributions. Emotional punishment, especially in the most severe rule, can damage efficiency. Justice (as measured by profit-contribution correlation and variance) improves with more severe collective rules. Participants are more likely to support collective rules if they expect higher profits and if the rule requires a higher degree of consent (e.g., unanimity).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that, in a standard repeated linear public good game with punishment, enabling punishment increases contributions but does not guarantee higher efficiency or group profit. The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the severity and structure of the punishment rule: more severe rules can increase cooperation but may reduce efficiency if punishment costs are high, especially due to emotional punishment. For prediction, this suggests that the relationship between control efficiency and treatment efficiency with punishment is non-monotonic and depends on the balance between increased cooperation and punishment costs. Game design dimensions such as punishment cost, punishment technology, and the collective/individual nature of punishment are critical moderators. The paper's results caution against assuming that more severe or more available punishment will always improve efficiency.

- source: Fehr, E., Fischbacher, U., & Gächter, S. (2002). Strong reciprocity, human cooperation, and the enforcement of social norms. *Human Nature-an Interdisciplinary Biosocial Perspective*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: In repeated public goods games with and without punishment, the introduction of a costly punishment option leads to a dramatic increase in cooperation and a 40% increase in aggregate monetary payoff, even after subtracting the costs of punishment. Without punishment, cooperation and efficiency unravel over time, with most subjects defecting by the end. With punishment, cooperation is sustained or even increases, and group earnings approach the social optimum. The effect is observed both in stable groups (partner design) and in randomly re-matched groups (stranger design), though the effect is stronger in stable groups. The paper also shows that punishment is targeted at defectors and is proportional to the degree of free-riding. The findings are robust across cultures and with high monetary stakes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-quality evidence that enabling costly punishment in a standard public goods game with known MPCR, group size, and round structure leads to a large, positive effect on group efficiency compared to the same game without punishment. The effect is robust to group composition (fixed or random), and the paper provides enough detail on the game design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information structure) to inform predictions for similar designs. The findings support the prediction that, controlling for control-game efficiency, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency in public goods game-like environments, especially when punishment is sufficiently effective and observable.

- source: Choi, J. K., & Ahn, T. K. (2013). Strategic reward and altruistic punishment support cooperation in a public goods game experiment. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In a lab public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds per phase, and a marginal per-capita return (MPCR) of 0.5, both voluntary punishment and voluntary reward options significantly increase average group contributions compared to baseline. In the partner condition (fixed groups), average contributions rise from 8.17 (punishment baseline) to 12.51 (punishment with punishment enabled), and from 9.38 (reward baseline) to 12.31 (reward with reward enabled). In the stranger condition (random rematching), punishment sustains higher contributions (11.38) than reward (9.21). The effect of punishment on cooperation is robust to group reshuffling, while reward is much less effective when strategic reciprocity is not possible. The paper does not directly report efficiency, but the increase in group contribution implies higher group payoff and thus higher efficiency when punishment is enabled.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard linear public goods game with 3 players, 10 rounds, no communication, and MPCR=0.5, enabling voluntary punishment increases group contribution (and thus group payoff/efficiency) compared to a baseline with no punishment. The effect is robust to random rematching (stranger condition), where punishment remains effective but reward does not. The paper does not report efficiency as a percentage of the social optimum, but the group contribution data can be used to estimate efficiency. For prediction, the findings suggest that, under these design parameters, enabling punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, especially when group membership is not fixed. The absence of direct efficiency reporting is a limitation, but the contribution data is a strong proxy.

- source: Sylwester, K., Hellmann, B., & Bryson, J. J. (2013). Homo Homini Lupus? Explaining Antisocial Punishment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper reviews and interprets evidence that antisocial punishment—punishing cooperators at a cost to oneself—is a widespread phenomenon in public goods games, especially in certain cultural and ecological contexts. It argues that antisocial punishment is often driven by status competition, aggression, or negative reciprocity, and is more prevalent where the cost of punishment is low, legal institutions are weak, and social competition is high. The review notes that antisocial punishment can undermine the cooperation-enhancing effects of punishment, and that its prevalence varies widely across cultures. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative evidence or predictions about efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment; instead, it focuses on behavioral mechanisms and cross-cultural patterns.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides important theoretical and contextual background for understanding when and why punishment may fail to increase efficiency in public goods games, especially due to antisocial punishment. It highlights that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly context-dependent and can be negative in environments with high antisocial punishment. However, because the paper is a review and does not report new or meta-analyzed efficiency or payoff data, it cannot be used directly for quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes. It is most useful for identifying moderators (e.g., punishment cost, anonymity, cultural context) that should be considered when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, and for warning that punishment can sometimes reduce, rather than increase, group welfare.

- source: PARKS, C. D., & HULBERT, L. G. (1995). HIGH AND LOW TRUSTERS RESPONSES TO FEAR IN A PAYOFF MATRIX. *Journal of Conflict Resolution*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The study finds that in both public goods and resource dilemma games, the presence of fear (risk of zero payoff for cooperation) interacts with individual trust to affect cooperation rates. High trusters cooperate more than low trusters when fear is present, but this difference disappears when fear is absent. Removing fear increases cooperation among low trusters in resource dilemmas, but decreases cooperation among high trusters in public goods games. The paper does not experimentally manipulate or measure punishment, but discusses its theoretical relevance. No direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes are reported; all results are in terms of cooperation frequency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games. It is useful for understanding how trust and fear interact to affect cooperation rates, but does not manipulate or measure punishment, nor does it report efficiency or group payoff outcomes. The discussion suggests that punishment may be more relevant in no-fear situations, but this is not tested. Thus, the paper offers only indirect, contextual support for prediction tasks involving punishment and efficiency.

- source: Sonnemans, J., van Dijk, F., & van Winden, F. (2006). On the dynamics of social ties structures in groups. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.

  findings: The experiment shows that in repeated public good games, social ties between group members develop and are influenced by the relative contributions of each member. Groups with more positive mutual ties tend to have higher average contributions, while groups with more negative or neutral ties have lower contributions. Social value orientation and affective responses play a significant role in the formation of these ties. The study provides evidence that group dynamics and interpersonal attitudes can lead to substantial variation in cooperation, even when incentives and endowments are held constant. However, the paper does not study punishment, sanctions, or their effects on efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper does not provide direct evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, as no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present in the design. It does, however, highlight the importance of social ties and group dynamics in driving contribution behavior, which may be relevant for understanding baseline cooperation levels in the absence of punishment. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is mainly useful for understanding control (no-punishment) environments and the role of social preferences, but it does not inform the effect of enabling punishment.

It does, however, provide evidence that public disclosure (a form of social image incentive) has only a small and statistically insignificant effect on average contributions in highly collectivist developing country contexts. For the downstream prediction task, this paper is not directly informative about the efficiency effects of punishment, but it does suggest that non-monetary social incentives (like disclosure) may have limited impact on contributions and, by extension, on efficiency in similar settings.

- source: Henrich, J., & Henrich, N. (2006). Culture, evolution and the puzzle of human cooperation. *Cognitive Systems Research*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper argues that punishment, especially when combined with cultural learning mechanisms (such as conformist and prestige-biased transmission), can stabilize costly cooperative norms in human groups. It reviews theoretical models showing that the effectiveness of punishment in maintaining cooperation depends on the cost of punishing relative to the cost of being punished, and that social norms enforced by punishment can persist even when the cooperative act is individually costly. The paper also discusses how group-level selection can favor groups with more effective cooperative norms, and how cultural evolution allows for rapid adaptation of cooperation strategies. However, the paper does not provide empirical or quantitative findings on efficiency or group payoff, focusing instead on the mechanisms and evolutionary logic.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the idea that punishment can stabilize cooperation in public goods-like environments, especially when punishment is effective and not too costly relative to the cost of being punished. It highlights the importance of social norms, cultural learning, and group structure in moderating the effects of punishment. However, it does not provide empirical or quantitative evidence on efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot be used directly to predict the magnitude of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled. Its value for prediction lies in its mechanistic insights and identification of key moderators (e.g., punishment cost/effectiveness, group size, norm strength), but not in providing direct outcome estimates.
