# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game (PGG) environments is **broad and deep**, with a heavy empirical foundation (lab and some field experiments) complemented by robust theoretical contributions. The set includes multiple high-quality lab PGG experiments varying key design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, punishment cost, feedback, communication) and provides extensive direct evidence on efficiency and payoff-related outcomes. There is strong representation of standard linear PGGs with and without punishment, a variety of peer and centralized punishment mechanisms, as well as studies covering contextual moderators (e.g., heterogeneity, endogenous institution choice, monitoring technology, feedback, social norms, cultural context). Several key theoretical models provide mechanistic and comparative-static predictions. Field experiments and studies on related environments (e.g., CPR games, trust/investment games) offer additional, though sometimes indirect, insights.

# 2) Task Relevance

### (a) `pgg_or_variant`
- **Relevance:** _exact_ for the core of the evidence base; most payoff-measuring studies are in standard or linear PGGs (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007; Arechar et al., 2018).
- _close_ for evidence from CPR, contest, trust, or weakest-link games that share essential features but may differ in specifics (e.g., Casari & Plott, 2003; Abbink et al., 2010).
- Several theory papers provide _exact_ or _close_ predictions for PGGs, often discussing parameterized environments.

### (b) `punishment_or_sanctions`
- **Relevance:** _exact_ in nearly all empirical studies directly addressing costly punishment, both peer and institutional (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sutter et al., 2010).
- Some key adjacent variants include exclusion (ostracism) or legal/centralized sanctions (_close_).
- Quality theory literature offers _exact_ or _close_ relevance with comparisons between punishment, monitoring, and reward dynamics.

### (c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
- **Relevance:** _exact_ for many studies that report group efficiency (ratio of total group payoff to fully cooperative optimum) or direct measures of earnings/welfare/surplus (e.g., Sefton et al., 2007; Arechar et al., 2018).
- _close_ or _adjacent_ for studies that primarily report contribution rates or frequencies but discuss, estimate, or allow inference about efficiency (e.g., Choi & Ahn, 2013). For some, efficiency must be inferred from detailed contribution and punishment data.
- A few behavioral studies report only non-payoff outcomes or discuss efficiency only tangentially.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related (Primary for Prediction):**
- **Efficiency** (group payoff as share of maximum)
- **Group earnings/welfare/total coins generated**
- **Surplus over baseline or Nash equilibrium**

**Non-payoff behavioral (Secondary/Moderating):**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**, **punishment frequency**, **return rates**
- **Norm compliance, anti-social punishment, retaliation**
- **Voting on rules/institution choice, group formation, prosocial preferences**

Importantly, although efficiency and contribution rates often move together, they can diverge when punishment is costly, misdirected, or when there is heterogeneity in responses. Studies carefully highlight cases where high cooperation does **not** translate to high efficiency due to the deadweight cost of punishment (e.g., Nikiforakis et al., 2010; Anderson & Putterman, 2006).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**1. Enabling Punishment Frequently Increases Efficiency, Conditional on Punishment Cost/Effectiveness**
- In standard PGGs, introducing costly punishment reliably increases group efficiency above baseline (no-punishment) levels, provided punishment is moderately to highly effective and not misused (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007; Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020). The typical efficiency gain ranges from 10% to 30% in the best-documented experiments, with larger gains when punishment is highly leveraged (impact >> cost).
- **Initial efficiency costs** from active punishment are common, but as cooperation stabilizes and punishment use drops, **efficiency rises** (Sefton et al., 2007).
- Institutional details—such as consensus rules, targeted punishment, and endogenous/voted-in enforcement—greatly improve the efficiency payoff by minimizing anti-social or excessive punishment (Casari & Luini, 2009; Ertan et al., 2009).

**2. Moderators That Weaken or Reverse the Efficiency Effect:**
- **Costliness of Punishment:** Cheap punishment may increase cooperation but reduce net efficiency due to overuse; expensive punishment is used less and may support higher efficiency (Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Kube & Traxler, 2011).
- **Monitoring Quality:** Noisy/imperfect monitoring or feedback can diminish or even reverse efficiency gains; only strong punishment restores efficiency to baseline under high noise (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; ABREU et al., 1991; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007).
- **Anti-social Punishment and Feuds:** In some contexts (notably Russia, India, or when counter-punishment is possible), punishment is directed at cooperators or used retaliatorily, lowering efficiency below the control (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Fehr et al., 2008; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Nikiforakis, 2008).
- **Counter-punishment (Feud) Opportunities:** Multiple rounds, identifiability, or possibilities for retaliatory punishment sharply reduce or eliminate the positive efficiency effect (Nikiforakis & Engelmann, 2011).
- **Institutional Design:** Consensus/filtered punishment mechanisms (punish low contributors only, require multiple punishers) deliver the highest efficiency; unfiltered open punishment can reduce efficiency (Casari & Luini, 2009; Ertan et al., 2009).
- **Endogeneity:** Voted/voluntary institution choice amplifies efficiency gains for punishment (Sutter et al., 2010; Markussen et al., 2014; Tyran & Feld, 2006).

**3. Structural Design Moderators:**
- **Player Count:** Smaller groups support stronger efficiency effects; as group size increases, effectiveness of punishment may decline unless monitoring/punishment is scalable (Carpenter, 2007; Wolitzky, 2013).
- **Rounds/Repetition:** Repeated games allow threats of future punishment to sustain cooperation and efficiency; with known endgames or in one-shot settings, effects are weaker but can still be positive if norm compliance is high (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Fudenberg & Pathak, 2010).
- **Communication:** Absence of chat leaves punishment as the primary tool; if chat is enabled, efficiency gains from communication can match or exceed those from punishment, and the marginal gain from adding punishment may be small (Bochet et al., 2006; Bochet & Putterman, 2009).

**4. Heterogeneity and Normative Conflict:**
- **Group heterogeneity** (in endowment, valuation, or productivity) weakens or destabilizes the positive efficiency effect, partly due to disagreements over norms and increased conflict (Nikiforakis et al., 2012; Kölle, 2015; Tan, 2008).
- In highly cooperative or homogeneous groups, the marginal impact of enabling punishment is often negligible or unnecessary, as high efficiency is already achieved (Gächter & Thöni, 2005).

**5. Centralized, Delegated, or Ostracism-based Institutions:**
- Centralized and voted-in punishment mechanisms tend to dominate peer-to-peer punishment in terms of efficiency, because they minimize the direct cost of punishment and reduce anti-social or retaliatory punishment (Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Kosfeld et al., 2009).
- Ostracism/exclusion as punishment offers large and robust efficiency gains if well-targeted (Cinyabuguma et al., 2005; Maier-Rigaud et al., 2010).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How the Literature Should Inform Prediction:**

- **Direct Inference:** In canonical designs (small fixed groups, 10–20 rounds, continuous linear PGG, no chat, known rounds, anonymous), the literature supports a substantial positive adjustment to control efficiency when punishment is enabled, with a typical efficiency gain in the 10–30% range if punishment is effective and not misused (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007; Arechar et al., 2018).
- **Moderator Adjustments:** The magnitude and even the sign of the efficiency gain should be adjusted downward or made negative if:
    - Punishment is cheap and easily overused (risk of overpunishment).
    - Monitoring is noisy or feedback is incomplete.
    - Retaliation, feuds, or counter-punishment are possible.
    - Anti-social punishment is common (in some cultures/contexts).
    - Group is highly heterogeneous and/or norms are not clear/common.
    - The punishment institution is unfiltered/unconstrained (anyone can punish anyone for any reason).
- **Institutional Features are Key:** Prediction should incorporate features such as whether punishment is peer or centralized, the cost-to-impact ratio (`punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`), whether communication (`chat`) is enabled, and whether institutions are endogenously chosen.
- **Control Efficiency as "Floor":** If control efficiency is high (near-optimal), enabling punishment may have little further effect; if low, the ceiling for improvement is higher, but only if institutional and behavioral moderators permit.
- **Design-specific Interactions:** The precise structure of feedback and information (`show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`), as well as the provision of summary statistics to participants, can make a substantial difference and must be mapped to empirical evidence on these moderators (Nikiforakis, 2010; Wolitzky, 2013).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed Dimensions:**
    - `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return), `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: all heavily discussed and manipulated, with clear effects on efficiency outcomes.
    - `all_or_nothing` (binary vs. continuous contribution): less variation, but some studies (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012) report on this.
    - `chat`: robustly established as a strong positive moderator for efficiency, often more effective than punishment and interacting with it (Bochet et al., 2006).
    - `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`: the effects of round structure, feedback, and identifiability are well evidenced (Nikiforakis, 2010; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007).
    - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: studies directly compare reward, punishment, and combined mechanisms (Sefton et al., 2007; Sutter et al., 2010).
    - **Endogeneity of institution choice** (not parameterized but well discussed) is a strong moderator.
- **Indirectly/Contextually Informed:**
    - `default_contrib`: Contribution framing is rarely the treatment of interest, but may interact with conditional cooperation and default effects.
    - Network structure is sometimes manipulated (Carpenter et al., 2012), but typically not parameterized within PGGs.
- **Effectively Missing:**
    - Some context-dependent moderators (e.g., cultural norms, local trust, prior exposure) are only indirectly addressed.
    - Few papers (if any) explicitly vary `show_punishment_id` as a treatment, though identifiability/anonymity is sometimes compared.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sensitivity to Social Context:** Efficiency effects of punishment are highly context-sensitive—cultural, normative, and emotional moderators can lead to negative effects in some settings (e.g., antisocial punishment in Russia, India, and some southern European contexts) (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Fehr et al., 2008).
- **Overreliance on Lab Settings:** Most direct evidence comes from lab environments; external validity to field or naturally occurring groups is plausible but not always certain.
- **Edge Cases and Interactions:** Several critical game dimensions (e.g., feedback structure, consensus rules, institution choice mechanisms) interact in ways that can override general trends; evidence is mixed or insufficient in high heterogeneity groups, very large groups, or highly dynamic environments.
- **Contribution vs. Payoff Confounds:** Several studies measure and find strong effects on contributions, but the translation to efficiency is imperfect. Behavioral improvements are sometimes outweighed by increased costs of punishment.
- **No Universal Positive Effect:** The literature documents clear cases where punishment either reduces efficiency or has a negligible effect, particularly when costly, when monitoring is poor, when counter-punishment is possible, or when applied inappropriately.
- **Sparse Evidence for Some Dimensions:** Some design variables (default contributions, specific forms of observable summaries) are lightly or inconsistently treated.
- **Limited Evidence on Long-Run/Delayed Effects:** Most studies focus on short- to medium-term lab interactions—a few exceptions explore dynamic or long-run adaptation (Gächter et al., 2017).
- **Measurement Limitations in Non-Payoff Studies:** Many adjacent studies report only behavioral or norm compliance effects, which should not be conflated with efficiency gains.

---

**In summary**, the literature robustly supports the prediction that enabling punishment generally increases efficiency under standard laboratory public goods game designs, especially when punishment is effective, targeted, and not excessively costly. However, efficiency gains are far from universal and are heavily moderated by punishment cost/tech, monitoring quality, group composition/homogeneity, feedback and information structure, possibility of retaliation, communication, and institutional details. The prediction task should incorporate these moderators, and caution is warranted in applying lab-based results to highly heterogeneous or field contexts. The literature is especially informative for core game design parameters, but less so for dimensions rarely manipulated or context-specific moderators.
