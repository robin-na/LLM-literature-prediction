# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set includes a mix of **empirical experimental studies** (mainly laboratory-based, with one field experiment) and **theoretical works**. Roughly half the papers present laboratory experiments using public goods games (PGGs) or closely related social dilemma paradigms; the remainder develop theoretical frameworks for punishment, cooperation, or efficiency in repeated and/or incomplete-information environments. The empirical papers are generally more focused on PGGs, while theory contributions expand to adjacent structures (stag hunts, general cooperation games).

On the dimensions of **empirical vs. theory** and **narrowness vs. breadth**:
- **Empirical and PGG-focused**: Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015), Drouvelis et al. (2015), Martinsson et al. (2015), Noussair et al. (2015), Becchetti et al. (2015).
- **Theory, covering adjacent structures**: Voelkl (2015), Blonski & Spagnolo (2015), Forges et al. (2016), Beraldo & Sugden (2016), Golman (2016), Thöni (2014).
- **Hybrid and broader context**: Liu & Riyanto (2017), Leibbrandt & López-Pérez (2014).

Overall, **experimental coverage of standard PGG environments with explicit peer punishment and measured payoff/efficiency is limited**. Several papers discuss non-PGG games, consider only behavioral (non-payoff) outcomes, or focus on mechanisms rather than direct efficiency measurements.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes**:
- **Directly reported efficiency/group payoff**: Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) (experimental; main outcome), Voelkl (2015) (theory), Becchetti et al. (2015) (lab CPRG).
- **Indirect/adjacent payoff outcomes**: Noussair et al. (2015) report behavioral proxies (catch, effort), suggesting implications for efficiency (not analyzed explicitly); Forges et al. (2016) theorize about efficient outcomes as feasible payoffs.
- **Baseline-only or context**: Some studies (e.g., Martinsson et al., Drouvelis et al.) supply control contribution/earnings data but do not report on punishment or efficiency effects.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**:
- **Contribution rates, norm compliance, and punishment behavior**: Many behavioral studies (Thöni, Drouvelis et al., Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, Golman, Liu & Riyanto) focus on contributions, punishment frequency, or approval/disapproval, without directly linking to payoff or efficiency.

**Distinction**:
- The **majority of outcome measures in the set are behavioral** (e.g., cooperation/contribution rates, punishment usage), **not direct efficiency or group payoff metrics**. When efficiency is discussed, it is sometimes inferred from behavior, not directly measured.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical Evidence (Payoff/Efficiency Focused):

- **Benefit of Peer Punishment Depends on Mechanism Structure**:  
  Enabling peer punishment in repeated linear PGGs **can increase efficiency** when punishment is structured to limit retaliation and keep costs low (e.g., single anonymous stage per round: "Standard Punishment") (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015). In richer punishment environments (multiple stages, fixed IDs, full information, potential for retaliatory feuding), the **efficiency gains disappear**: higher contributions are seen, but offset by high punishment costs and within-group conflict. Thus, **punishment costs and the possibility of retaliation critically moderate whether punishment helps or hurts efficiency**.

- **Field vs. Laboratory Settings**:  
  In a field experiment with a common-pool resource (CPRG), **adding peer punishment or reward opportunities did **not** increase cooperation or its behavioral proxies nor improve group outcomes** (Noussair et al., 2015). This cautions against direct extrapolation from lab-based PGGs to field or real-world social dilemmas.

- **Transparency/Monitoring Without Punishment**:  
  Making individual behavior public increases conditional conformity but, **in the absence of a punishment mechanism, this transparency can actually decrease efficiency and group payoff** by accelerating convergence to less cooperative behavior (Becchetti et al., 2015).

## Theoretical and Mechanism-Focused Evidence:

- **Sufficiency and Cost of Punishment**:  
  Theory suggests that **effective and strong (costly for defectors) punishment stabilizes cooperative equilibria and maximizes group efficiency**, provided that the threat is credible and costs to punishers do not themselves outweigh cooperation gains (Voelkl, 2015; Forges et al., 2016).

- **Role of Antisocial Punishment**:  
  Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators or for reasons other than norm enforcement) can **undermine the positive effects of punishment on efficiency**, especially in subject pools or designs where such motives are prevalent (Thöni, 2014). The paper, however, **does not quantify efficiency effects**.

- **Interaction with Communication and Social Image**:  
  Communication can act as a substitute or moderator for punishment. When chat is enabled or approval/disapproval is possible, individuals may prefer costless signaling over costly sanctions, potentially altering the net efficiency effect of enabling monetary punishment (Golman, 2016; Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014).

- **Game Structure and Patience**:  
  The effectiveness of punishment in supporting efficient cooperation depends on game structure (PD, stag hunt, PGG) and the patience/discounting of players (Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should this literature inform prediction of treatment efficiency** (enabling punishment) **from design plus control efficiency?**

- **Control efficiency matters, but mechanism structure is critical**: The likely gain in efficiency from enabling punishment depends crucially not only on baseline (control) efficiency, but on punishment design. **Punishment mechanisms that allow for high costs, ambiguous targets, retaliation, or repeated feuding can completely nullify or even reverse the efficiency benefit** (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

- **Dimension-level guidance**:
  - *Punishment Cost/Tech/ID Disclosure*: **Lower-cost, single-stage, anonymous punishment increases the chance of efficiency gains**; richer or more complex punishment tech (multiple stages, fixed IDs, full transparency) makes negative or null net effects more likely.
  - *Player Count/MPCR/Number of Rounds*: These factors are sometimes reported, but not systematically varied in a way that allows for strong predictions about how they interact with punishment's effect.
  - *Communication (Chat), Reward Options*: Communication may moderate or substitute for punishment; if chat or non-monetary approval/disapproval is possible, the net effect of enabling peer punishment may be dampened.
  - *Transparency of Other Outcomes*: Monitoring without sanctions can reduce efficiency; its interaction with punishment is less well-documented.
  - *Reward Exists/Cost/Tech*: These are less well-investigated and mostly absent from empirical setups with efficiency measurement.

- **When empirical data is available for games closely matching the design of interest**, (e.g., Engelmann & Nikiforakis's Standard Punishment), **these data points should be heavily weighted** in prediction. In their absence, caution is warranted, especially when design features diverge (e.g., by enabling retaliation or rich punishment technology).

- **Limit extrapolation to field or structurally different environments**: Laboratory evidence does not reliably generalize to field populations or CPRGs, especially with regard to efficiency implications of punishment.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions**:
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `all_or_nothing`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Directly manipulated or modeled in several key studies (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Voelkl, 2015; some theory).
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Occasionally manipulated as monitoring/feedback conditions (Becchetti et al., 2015).

**Indirectly Informed Dimensions**:
- `chat`: Discussed and sometimes included, mostly as context (Golman, 2016; Drouvelis et al., 2015); rarely analyzed for its interaction with punishment efficiency.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Modeled/theorized more than empirically tested in relation to efficiency; present in a few setups as a comparison to punishment (Noussair et al., 2015; Liu & Riyanto, 2017).

**Only Contextually Discussed or Missing**:
- `default_contrib`: Framing (opt-in vs. opt-out) is specified but rarely varied for focusing on efficiency with/without punishment.
- `show_punishment_id`: Occasionally referenced as subdimension of punishment technology (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015), but not systematically studied.
- *Combinations*: The full combinatorial interaction of design dimensions is largely unstudied.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Few direct experimental estimates of efficiency change due to enabling punishment** in canonical PGGs. Most payoff-based findings come from a single or small number of setups with lab populations and restricted parameter ranges.
- **Generalizability is limited**: Mechanisms shown to increase efficiency in labs may not do so in field or real-world contexts (Noussair et al., 2015), and vice versa.
- **Non-payoff outcomes dominate**: Most behavioral studies focus on contributions, punishment behavior, or related proxies; **payoff or efficiency changes must often be inferred rather than measured directly**.
- **Limited coverage of design space**: Many theoretically important dimensions (reward tech, chat, opt-in framing, visibility of punishment identity) are rarely manipulated orthogonally. The impact of multiple interacting design features on efficiency is underexplored.
- **Mechanism arguments may overstate positive effects** unless antisocial punishment, retaliation, or high punishment costs are explicitly accounted for (Thöni, 2014; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Heterogeneous subject pools and environmental context**: Results from university labs or specific field sites (e.g., Nairobi slum) may not represent broader populations.
- **Absence of quantitative 'calibration'**: While qualitative direction-of-effect insights are provided, the literature rarely supplies enough variation in design and outcomes to build fine-grained quantitative prediction models for efficiency as a function of all 14 design parameters and baseline efficiency.

---

**In summary**:  
The literature provides moderate, but **sharp and design-sensitive, guidance** on the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments: **efficiency gains depend on careful mechanism design** (especially sans retaliation and excessive punishment costs). The empirical evidence is strongest for simple, anonymous, single-stage punishment. Theoretical models add detail about necessary conditions for efficiency but lack empirical specificity. **For dimensions not directly studied—especially in field/complex environments—predictions should be made with caution.**
