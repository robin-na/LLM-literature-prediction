# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of three studies with a mix of theory and empirical approaches. One paper (Levy, 2022) is a theoretical review drawing extensively on prior empirical and experimental literature in public goods games (PGGs), focusing on the mechanisms and adaptive value of punishment. The second paper (Miraghaie et al., 2022) is an experimental neuroscience study using related but distinct economic games (Dictator and Ultimatum Games) to examine neural correlates of fairness and punishment behavior. The third paper (Smith et al., 2022) is an observational field study on sharing preferences among Hadza hunter-gatherers, examining indirect social dynamics with no explicit punishment or efficiency measurement.

For the downstream task—predicting efficiency under peer punishment in PGG-like designs, conditioned on design features and control efficiency—this evidence base is primarily theoretical and conceptual for punishment effects in PGGs (Levy, 2022), and only contextually relevant or adjacent in the other two papers. Direct empirical, quantitative evidence linking the 14 design dimensions to group efficiency under punishment is sparse to absent. The breadth of this set is therefore limited for quantitative or fine-grained prediction, although it provides useful context for why and when punishment may have efficiency effects.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Levy (2022): exact**—explicit focus on PGGs and variants.
- **Miraghaie et al. (2022): adjacent**—studies economic games (DG, UG) that share punishment/fairness elements but lack key features of PGGs (group provisioning, repeated rounds).
- **Smith et al. (2022): adjacent**—uses an anonymous sharing game with emergent social preferences; related to cooperation but not a formal PGG.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Levy (2022): exact**—focuses directly on mechanisms, costs, and structures of punishment.
- **Miraghaie et al. (2022): adjacent**—studies rejection of unfair offers as a form of "punishment," but the context, mechanism, and group outcome structure differ from PGGs.
- **Smith et al. (2022): none**—does not involve punishment or sanction mechanisms in design or analysis.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Levy (2022): adjacent**—mainly reviews non-payoff cooperation outcomes but references empirical work showing some cases where group profit rises with punishment. No original experimental payoff results reported.
- **Miraghaie et al. (2022): adjacent**—focuses on neural and behavioral patterns of fairness/punishment; does not report, compare, or analyze efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
- **Smith et al. (2022): none**—no analysis of efficiency, group payoff, or related aggregate outcomes.

Overall, only one paper has high task relevance; the others are adjacent or non-overlapping with the central prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (efficiency, group payoff, welfare, surplus, total earnings):  
  - **Levy (2022):** Theoretical review references prior studies (e.g., Fehr & Gächter) showing punishment may sometimes increase group profit, but its main evidence and emphasis focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes such as cooperation rates and norm compliance. Presentations of specific efficiency metrics or direct payoff comparisons are indirect or absent.
  - **Miraghaie et al. (2022)** and **Smith et al. (2022):** Do not measure or report efficiency, welfare, or group payoffs.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (contribution rate, cooperation, punishment frequency, norm compliance, neural signals):  
  - All papers address these. Levy (2022) discusses cooperation and norm maintenance; Miraghaie et al. (2022) investigates neural correlates of fairness/inequity responses; Smith et al. (2022) examines sharing preferences and partner choice.

- **Summary:** The measured outcomes most central to prediction—group efficiency and related payoffs under peer punishment—are only discussed in secondary or reviewed results from the literature, not measured in original experiments in this set.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment can, under some conditions, increase cooperation and yield higher group payoffs in PGGs** relative to controls without punishment (Levy, 2022). This is mainly derived from reviewed experimental evidence (e.g., Fehr & Gächter; Gächter et al., 2008), not novel data.
- **Boundary conditions and complexity are repeatedly emphasized:** Efficiency gains from peer punishment are not universal; they are contingent on punishment not being overly costly, not leading to retaliation cycles, and possibly on the institutional environment (Levy, 2022).
- **Mechanistic Evidence:** Neural and individual-difference mechanisms (Miraghaie et al., 2022) show variation in the tendency toward costly or spiteful punishment, but the direct impact on group efficiency is not measured.
- **No direct findings on efficiency from the other two papers.** Adjacent behavioral and neural findings (Miraghaie et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2022) inform on when individuals might punish or cooperate, but the effect on group payoff—especially in the context of the full 14-dimension design space—is not established.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

The literature in this paper set provides only high-level, qualitative support for the mechanism by which peer punishment may raise efficiency in public goods game environments, **conditional on certain design features** (notably, manageable punishment cost, transparency of actions, and appropriateness of institutional support) (Levy, 2022). 

**Direct, quantitative prediction of efficiency with punishment enabled—using design dimensions plus control efficiency—is not empirically addressed.** Theoretical claims indicate that, in settings where punishment is not prohibitively costly and retaliation is limited, efficiency can rise relative to baseline; but the effect size and specific interactive implications across the 14 design features are not detailed. 

**If the goal is to predict efficiency increases from peer punishment, this literature encourages caution:**  
- Expect a positive effect only in some scenarios (low punishment cost, low risk of counter-punishment, transparent environments).  
- High punishment cost, potential for retaliatory spirals, or lack of clear social feedback mechanisms may limit or reverse this effect (Levy, 2022).  
- No quantitative mapping from the configuration dimensions to predicted efficiency is available in this set.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**
- *punishment_cost,* *punishment_tech,* *show_punishment_id* (Levy, 2022)—Theory and review emphasize how punishment costs, technological implementation, and visibility impact the likelihood and impact of punishment, as well as potential for efficiency gains or losses.
- *player_count,* *num_rounds,* *all_or_nothing,* *chat* (Miraghaie et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2022)—These are present in experimental designs but only inform behavioral phenomena, not payoff/efficiency consequences.

**Indirectly informed / contextually discussed:**  
- *show_other_summaries,* *show_n_rounds,* *default_contrib,* *mpcr* (parameters frequently alluded to in background or referenced studies, but not the focus of the main analyses).
- *reward_exists,* *reward_cost,* *reward_tech* (discussed as institutional/structural factors in Levy, 2022, but not experimentally probed here).

**Effectively missing:**  
- No paper in this set provides original, task-specific empirical data mapping any combination of the 14 dimensions to changes in efficiency due to punishment.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Quantitative Empirical Evidence is Lacking:** No study in this set experimentally compares efficiency (or group payoff) in PGGs with versus without punishment across systematically varied design parameters.
- **Payoff/Efficiency Reporting is Low:** Outcomes are predominantly non-payoff behavioral (cooperation, punishment rates, neural signals) rather than efficiency.
- **Limited Coverage of Prediction Dimensions:** Only a handful of the 14 design dimensions are directly or even indirectly addressed; several (especially interaction effects) are missing from empirical coverage.
- **Contextual Transferability is Uncertain:** Findings from adjacent game types (e.g., Dictator/Ultimatum games, Hadza sharing) may not map onto PGG outcomes and design sensitivities.
- **Task Relevance is Narrow:** Only one paper (Levy, 2022) is both theoretical and specifically PGG/punishment focused; empirical coverage on the core task (effect on efficiency) is via referenced, not original, results.

**In summary:**  
The literature set provides conceptual support for the view that peer punishment can (in some configurations) raise group efficiency in public goods games, and highlights important contextual conditions that moderate this effect. However, there is little to no direct, empirical grounding in this set for predicting the magnitude of efficiency change as a function of concrete design features and baseline efficiency. Greater empirical coverage, including original efficiency measurements under systematic variation of design parameters, would be required for precise downstream prediction.
