# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of 35 papers, spanning a mix of empirical (lab and field experiments, as well as observational studies) and theoretical (modeling, simulation, and conceptual argument) research. The set is relatively broad in its coverage of punishment, cooperation, and related social dilemma environments, including both public goods games (PGG) and adjacent game forms (prisoner's dilemma, dictator/ultimatum games, networked PGGs, and real-world analogues). However, only a subset of the papers report direct, empirical evidence on efficiency or payoff-related outcomes as affected by punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. Theoretical and simulation results are well represented, and most work is contemporary, but there is a notable reliance on adjacent or indirectly related setups and outcomes in many papers. Overall, the evidence base is solid for conceptual and mechanistic understanding and for qualitative guidance, but is relatively narrow for quantitative or highly specific prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *Exact:* Several papers focus directly on laboratory PGGs or mathematically equivalent variants (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Wang et al., 2011; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Rosas, 2008).
- *Close/Adjacent:* Many others study closely related settings (e.g., networked PGGs, binary PGGs, prisoner's dilemma, emission target games, economic or real-world analogues).
- *Weak/None:* A few papers are situated in quite different environments (e.g., ultimatum or dictator games, real-life forums or policing).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *Exact:* A substantial subset manipulates or theorizes about explicit, costly punishment, including both peer and institutional forms. Some detail specific punishment technologies (peer, pool, exclusion, group-penalty, tax/fines; e.g., Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Wang et al., 2011).
- *Close/Adjacent:* Others discuss mechanisms analogous to punishment (reputation, avoidance, social exclusion) or focus on reward, norms, or metapunishment. Some only address punishment in a conceptual or evolutionary context.
- *Weak/None:* A few only discuss punishment tangentially or not at all.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *Exact:* A moderate number of papers directly measure or model group efficiency, average payoff, or welfare outcomes (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Wang et al., 2011; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012).
- *Close/Adjacent:* Several papers measure closely related outcomes (e.g., group “success ratio,” emission target attainment, “total wealth”) or calculate payoffs in the course of theory/modeling without presenting them as primary outcomes.
- *Weak/None:* Many others focus primarily on behavioral outcomes (contribution/cooperation rates, norm adherence, punishment frequency, etc.), with efficiency only implied, not reported.

*Summary:*  
The set offers high task relevance at the conceptual and mechanistic level and moderate to strong direct relevance for exact PGGs with explicit punishment. Direct empirical coverage of efficiency outcomes is only modest and limited in the range of parameter manipulation, making quantitative prediction challenging.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, welfare):**  
- **Direct measurements:** Group efficiency (actual/optimal payoff ratio), average total earning/welfare, and explicit payoff calculations are presented in several core papers (e.g., Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Wang et al., 2011; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Heller & Sieberg, 2010; Nuño et al., 2010; Langlois & Langlois, 2004; Thomadsen & Bhardwaj, 2011).
- **Indirect/closely related:** Studies use group “quality,” “success ratio,” “wealth,” emission compliance, or duration in cooperative states as proxies (Fatas et al., 2010; Brick & Visser, 2010; Galan et al., 2011). Some provide theoretical efficiency boundaries or comparative statics.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (NOT efficiency):**  
- **Frequently measured:** Mean contribution rates, cooperation rates, punishment/reward frequency, norm compliance, rejection rates, willingness to punish, retaliation, network adaptation.
- **Role in the literature:** These outcomes often inform mechanism discussions or are used to infer efficiency effects but are distinct and not always predictive of payoff-based efficiency, especially when costly punishment or misapplied punishment is present (Goette & Huffman, 2007; Shutters, 2012; Zhang et al., 2010; Varga et al., 2010).

*Explicit distinction between these outcome types is made in several theory papers and is important for interpreting the findings, as increased cooperation does not always translate into increased efficiency if punishment expenditures are high or misdirected.*

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical and theoretical results converge on several points:**

- **Punishment Usually Increases Efficiency When Designed Well:** Introduction of punishment, particularly externally imposed, strong, and well-structured mechanisms, significantly increases group efficiency in standard repeated PGGs, especially when baseline/cooperation is low (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Wang et al., 2011; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Heller & Sieberg, 2010).

- **But Punishment Can Lower Efficiency if Poorly Designed:** Costly punishment, if excessive or misapplied (e.g., punishing cooperators, mutual retaliation, "self-punishing" schemes), can reduce group efficiency, especially in short games or with high punishment cost (Frey & Rusch, 2012; Langlois & Langlois, 2004; Rosas, 2008).

- **Threshold and Nonlinear Effects:** There are often threshold or phase transition effects—punishment must be sufficiently strong, widely applicable (in networked settings, high “coverage”), and cost-effective to induce a large jump in efficiency (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012). Too weak, too costly, or too local punishment may have little to no effect.

- **Longer Repetition Strengthens Punishment’s Benefits:** Efficiency gains from punishment emerge over longer repeated games as initial punishment expenditures decline with stabilized cooperation (Frey & Rusch, 2012).

- **Network Structure and Institution Type Moderate Effects:** Peer vs. pool punishment, network clustering, interaction structure, and possibilities for retaliation or corruption all critically shape outcomes (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Galan et al., 2011; Duéñez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012).

- **Optimal Punishment Level:** There exists an optimal amount of punishment expenditure—too little fails to deter defection; too much wastes resources and can decrease efficiency (Nuño et al., 2010).

- **Punishment vs. Reward:** Peer reward can, in theory, sustain high cooperation and efficiency, but in dynamic, anonymous, or high-noise environments, peer punishment is generally more robust (Wang et al., 2011; Schmitt & Marwell, 1970).

- **Individual Differences and Group Composition:** Efficiency gains from punishment are strongest in groups dominated by defectors; automatically cooperative groups may not benefit and can even suffer from unnecessary or misapplied punishment (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Rosas, 2008).

- **Punishment Technology Matters:** Methods such as exclusion, redress (compensation to victims), taxation/fines, or centralized vs. decentralized enforcement yield different efficiency impacts (Rosas, 2008; Langlois & Langlois, 2004; Brick & Visser, 2010).

- **Contextual and Behavioral Moderators:** Communication, social comparison, reputation, “forgiveness” windows, institutional stability, and even affective responses (anger, satisfaction in punishing) moderate the efficiency impact but are less well studied quantitatively in relation to efficiency.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Best-informed setting:** When predicting treatment (with punishment) efficiency from a known control (no-punishment) efficiency, the most reliable evidence supports a *substantial positive effect* of punishment on efficiency in classic repeated PGG designs *if*: (a) punishment is strong and externally imposed; (b) punishment cost is not excessive; (c) punishment is applied with high coverage and efficacy; (d) the game features repeated, stable groups.

- **Adjustment for design dimensions:**  
   - **Short or one-shot games, high punishment cost, low MPCR, or weak punishment technology → minimal or negative efficiency impact.**
   - **Long repeated games or games with partner matching, high punishment magnitude, and sufficient network coverage → large efficiency gains.**
   - **If the control efficiency is already high, punishment may add little or even decrease efficiency if misapplied.**
   - **Enabling punishment in dynamic, anonymous environments is only effective if augmented by entry fees or additional mechanisms (Wang et al., 2011).**

- **Cautions:**  
   - **Punishment is not always beneficial:** If mutual or self-punishing phases are likely (due to noise or revenge cycles), or if group voting fails to implement strong institutions, the efficiency gain may be negligible or negative (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Langlois & Langlois, 2004).
   - **Mechanisms such as redress, exclusion, or institutional punishment can further increase efficiency and reduce negative side effects.**
   - **Behavioral outcomes are not proxies for efficiency unless punishment costs and retaliation rates are accounted for.**

- **At parameter boundaries:** Predicting high efficiency gains from punishment is inappropriate if the design falls into poorly covered empirical scenarios (e.g., short, high-noise, high-cost punishment games, or network structures with low punishment reach).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed Dimensions:**
   - `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `reward_exists`—extensively modeled and manipulated, clearly shown to moderate efficiency impact of punishment.
   - `all_or_nothing`, `chat`—directly manipulated in some experimental designs and regarded as moderators.

- **Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
   - `default_contrib` (contribution framing), `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `reward_magnitude`—less central, discussed primarily in contrast to punishment or as additional interventions.
   - `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`—occasionally manipulated, particularly in studies on information, observability, and transparency, but direct impact on efficiency only modeled in theory (e.g., signal vs. noise).

- **Sparse or Effectively Missing:**
   - `show_punishment_id`—seldom directly tested; mostly discussed as a context for psychological or reputational mechanisms.
   - `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `default_contrib`—some simulation and conceptual discussion, little direct empirical evidence.
   - Specific implementations of “punishment_tech” (e.g., peer vs. pool, exclusion vs. monetary) are a point of theoretical discussion but are rarely varied within the same empirical study.

- **Other moderators (partially covered):**
   - Group structure (partner vs. stranger matching, networked vs. well-mixed), institutional design (centralized vs. decentralized punishment), and exogenous vs. endogenous institution setting are important but not always mapped directly onto the 14 prediction dimensions.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical Range:** Most direct empirical evidence is limited to small group sizes (typically 4-5), moderate numbers of rounds, and excludes many combinations of dimensions (e.g., reward and punishment together, rich institutional features).
- **Quantitative Coverage:** Few papers provide explicit quantitative mappings from design dimensions to treatment efficiency; most evidence is qualitative or comparative.
- **Efficiency vs. Behavioral Outcomes:** Many papers infer efficiency effects from cooperation rates or behavioral proxies, which is unreliable when punishment is costly or misapplied.
- **Institutional Complexity:** Real-world and simulated studies suggest the importance of nuanced institutional factors (e.g., coverage, exclusion, redress, voting), but rarely operationalize these for prediction in standard PGGs.
- **Interaction Effects and Nonlinearities:** Several theoretical models predict threshold or non-monotonic effects (e.g., excess punishment reduces efficiency), but empirical confirmation across parameter space is sparse.
- **Contextual Dependence:** Network structure, partner stability, voluntary participation, reputational mechanisms, and individual differences all affect punishment’s impact, but their interaction with the supplied prediction dimensions is underresolved.
- **Control Game Baseline:** Several studies suggest that the effect of punishment is strongest when control game efficiency is low, but the scaling of effect magnitude with control efficiency is not precisely calibrated.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature set provides robust qualitative guidance: introducing well-designed, costly punishment mechanisms to public goods games with low baseline efficiency generally produces substantial efficiency gains, especially in repeated, stable group contexts where punishment is not excessively costly, is effective, and is broadly applicable. However, the effect is heavily moderated by game design, institution type, and behavioral adaptation; misapplied or costly punishment can be counterproductive. Quantitative prediction of treatment efficiency relative to control is best supported when design dimensions match empirically studied cases; outside those, uncertainty increases rapidly due to data gaps, nonlinearity, and context dependence.
