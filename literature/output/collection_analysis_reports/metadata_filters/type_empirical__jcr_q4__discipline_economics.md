# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is strongly empirical, with the overwhelming majority being laboratory or field experiments (mostly lab, with a few field studies). The set is broad in terms of institutional and environmental variation: it covers standard linear public goods games (PGG), various close or adjacent game forms (generalized exchange, trust, bargaining, team production, coordination, and CPR games), and a wide range of punishment and reward mechanisms (peer, centralized, ex-ante, ex-post, formal/informal, visible/invisible, costly/costless, coordinated/uncoordinated, and discretionary/discriminatory). Most studies focus on the short- and medium-run effects of punishment, with group size, rounds, and marginal per-capita return (MPCR) typically in standard experimental ranges.

The set includes both direct PGG studies and several adjacent game forms offering indirect or contextual insights about punishment, efficiency, or design features. There are a few contributions primarily investigating the behavioral or psychological underpinning of punishment but without direct payoff or efficiency analysis.

Overall, this evidence base is suitable for an in-depth synthesis for the downstream prediction task in standard and some variant PGG environments with and without punishment enabled. However, direct and quantitative efficiency findings are dominant only in a subset of PGG-focused papers; payoff-based evidence is less common in PGG-adjacent studies.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *exact:* Strong evidence from classic linear PGGs and close institutional variants (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Carpenter et al., 2012; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- *close/adjacent:* Numerous studies with games structurally similar but not fully matching the standard PGG (e.g., team investment/coordination, trust, CPR, bargaining).
- *weak/none:* A minority of studies focus mainly on behavioral phenomena or motivations, omitting discussion of PGG or close variants.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *exact:* Many studies directly manipulate costly punishment or formal/informal sanctioning (peer or centralized).
- *adjacent:* Some rely on exclusion, ostracism, withdrawal of cooperation, or social feedback mechanisms as proxies for sanction.
- *none:* Papers on rewards only, priming, or institutionless control conditions do not contribute to this dimension.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *exact/close:* About half of the relevant studies directly measure and report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus.
- *adjacent:* Some report only on related behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates) but infer efficiency changes based on standard payoff structure.
- *weak/none:* Several studies provide only behavioral, motivational, psychological, or attitudinal results, not directly measuring efficiency or group payoff.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes:**
- Many key studies measure *efficiency* (group total payoff vs. social optimum) directly and report net effects after accounting for the cost of punishment/reward (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Carpenter et al., 2012; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; others).
- Some studies infer efficiency from reported experimental earnings or group profits, even if not labeled as efficiency (e.g., with or without cost accounting).

**Non-Payoff (Behavioral) Outcomes:**
- Several studies focus solely on *cooperation rates, contribution levels, punishment assigned, norm compliance,* and *punishment frequency* (Pancotto et al., 2023; Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016; Grund et al., 2020; Drouvelis et al., 2015).
- Many reporting behavioral outcomes explicitly clarify that such measures are not equivalent to efficiency or group payoff, and in some cases, behavioral improvements do not correspond to efficiency gains (e.g., increased contributions but offset by punishment cost).

**Distinction:**  
There is strong attention in the PGG-focused studies to distinguishing efficiency from contribution rates or cooperation levels, highlighting the sometimes divergent implications of punishment for these outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment Often Increases Contributions, but Effects on Efficiency Are Mixed**:  
  - Peer punishment typically increases contributions (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Pancotto et al., 2023), but does **not always increase efficiency** because the costs of punishment can outweigh its cooperation benefits (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Abbink et al., 2004).
- **Context Moderates Punishment’s Impact on Efficiency**:  
  - Efficiency gains from punishment are highly contingent on design features:
    - **Network structure / Punishment network**: In *complete* or *well-connected* networks, punishment is more likely to be efficiency-enhancing; in *directed* or *disconnected* networks, punishment increases costs and reduces efficiency (Carpenter et al., 2012; Kanitsar, 2021).
    - **Centralized vs. Peer Punishment**: Centralized (formal) punishment often increases efficiency more reliably, especially when combined with open/migratory groups (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021).
    - **Cost and Effectiveness of Punishment**: Higher punishment cost and effectiveness ratio can reduce or enhance net efficiency, depending on use intensity (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Kanitsar, 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - **Possibility of Retaliation or “Rich” Punishment Environment**: Allowing for retaliation or multi-stage punishment can nullify or reverse efficiency gains (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - **Antisocial or Norm-Keeper Punishment**: The prevalence of norm-keeper types or antisocial punishers (punishing cooperators) can prevent or erode efficiency gains (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
    - **Information and Visibility**: Visibility of punishment, identity, and public feedback can have variable effects, sometimes increasing, sometimes undermining efficiency depending on context (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Carpenter et al., 2012).
- **Reward Mechanisms**:  
  - When available, reward often increases efficiency more than punishment, as it raises contributions without incurring a direct cost that exceeds gains (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Peng, 2022). Majority-vote reward has particularly strong efficiency gains (Peng, 2022).
- **Institutional Choice and Self-Organization**:  
  - The popularity and sustainability of informal sanction institutions are fragile to group openness (migration); formal sanctions are more robust in open settings (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
- **Control Efficiency as Baseline**:  
  - All key studies report or reference comparison to control efficiency, often using a control phase or baseline as the predictor for punishment-enabled efficiency.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **General Rule:** *Enabling peer punishment in a standard linear PGG does not guarantee an efficiency increase relative to the punishment-disabled control; the direction and magnitude of the effect depend critically on both the punishment technology and other design features.*
  - **If control efficiency is low,** enabling punishment may increase efficiency, but only if the cost of punishment actions is not too high and punishment is used in an efficiency-promoting (not antisocial or feuding) manner (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Carpenter et al., 2012).
  - **If control efficiency is moderate or high,** enabling punishment may instead *decrease* efficiency if punishment costs outweigh marginal cooperation gains (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Abbink et al., 2004).
  - **Centralized and formal punishment** is more likely to increase efficiency, especially in contexts where group composition is endogenous or where informal sanctions break down (Castillo et al., 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
  - **Punishment network architecture** (who can punish whom) is a major moderator: denser and more complete punishment possibilities enable more effective deterrence and thus higher efficiency when punishment is used sparingly (Carpenter et al., 2012).
  - **Punishment cost and effectiveness** matter: *Costless* or highly efficient punishment is much more likely to increase efficiency than costly or low-multiplier punishment (Kanitsar, 2021; Kocher & Matzat, 2016).
  - **Visibility and identity** of punishers, as well as feedback mechanisms, can have ambiguous effects: public information alone does not substitute for formal punishment and can sometimes reduce efficiency in non-punishment settings (Becchetti et al., 2015; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Prediction from Design Dimensions:** The following dimensions are most crucial for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency (see next section for mapping to literature evidence).
- **Non-Payoff Outcomes are Not Sufficient:** High cooperation levels, in isolation, are not sufficient evidence of higher efficiency if achieved via very costly or frequent punishment.
- **Qualifiers/Robustness:** The effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is less robust in real-world field environments compared to standard lab PGGs (Noussair et al., 2015) and can differ by cultural or group composition (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

## Directly/Strongly Informed Dimensions:
- **player_count:** Widely reported; studies typically use 4–5 players (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Carpenter et al., 2012; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
- **num_rounds:** Direct evidence from experiments varying from single to 30+ rounds; decay and long-run effects well captured (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **mpcr:** Marginal per-capita return is explicitly stated and manipulated; its role in baseline cooperation and responsiveness to punishment is covered (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Cost and effectiveness of punishment (fine-to-cost ratio) are directly manipulated and their effect on efficiency analyzed (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Kanitsar, 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **chat:** Some studies include or exclude communication; generally, no communication is the standard (Carpenter et al., 2012; Pancotto et al., 2023).
- **centralized vs. peer punishment:** Several studies compare, showing distinct efficiency outcomes (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021).

## Indirectly Informed/Moderately Covered:
- **all_or_nothing:** Both all-or-nothing and continuous contribution variants are examined; effects on punishment effectiveness and efficiency noted (Kanitsar, 2021; Becchetti et al., 2018).
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries:** Player information and round structure manipulation are sometimes used to affect behavior but less frequently as primary explanatory variables.
- **show_punishment_id:** Visibility of punishment identity is explored in a few studies, typically showing complex effects via retaliation, deterrence, or norm signaling (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

## Contextually Discussed/Lightly Informed:
- **default_contrib:** Framing of default action (opt-in/opt-out) is rarely a focus but sometimes mentioned in motivation or design justification (Pancotto et al., 2023).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** While some studies directly compare reward and punishment, most focus on one or the other; the comparative efficiency of reward is well established in a few key studies (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Peng, 2022).
- **punishment network structure:** Only a subset of studies, but those that do (Carpenter et al., 2012; Kanitsar, 2021) provide especially critical guidance regarding how network completeness or directedness moderates efficiency effects.

## Effectively Missing:
- **Specific parametric variations and interactions:** Edge cases (e.g., very large groups, extreme MPCR values, unusual punishment technologies) and the full interaction space across all 14 dimensions are not exhaustively covered.
- **Reward and punishment combination:** Combinations of reward and punishment are less commonly directly explored in PGG contexts; most studies exclude one or the other.
- **Other information and signaling mechanisms:** Some feedback technologies (e.g., public vs. private social feedback) are discussed mainly in adjacent games, not standard PGGs.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **External Validity:** Several papers caution that lab findings may not generalize to field settings, as real-world contexts tend to show weaker or more heterogeneous punishment effects (Noussair et al., 2015).
- **Ambiguity and Context Dependence:** There is not a universal direction for efficiency change when enabling punishment; effects depend on details of punishment cost, structure, execution, and social context, with clear evidence for both efficiency gains and losses.
- **Sparse Coverage of Some Dimensions:** Some design dimensions are lightly covered or unaddressed (reward-punishment combinations, show_punishment_id, default_contrib).
- **Difference Between Behavioral and Payoff Outcomes:** Many studies focus on or report only contribution/cooperation rates, which do not always track efficiency trends due to the possibility of costly or antisocial punishment eroding gains.
- **Heterogeneity Within and Across Societies/Groups:** Heterogeneity in responses to punishment, prevalence of antisocial punishers, or norm-keepers makes broad generalization risky (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Punishment Use and Strategy Mix:** Efficiency effects are often sensitive to the strategic mix within groups (strong reciprocators vs. norm-keepers), which may be unobservable outside experimental settings.
- **Dynamics and Endgame Problems:** Some studies show late-round cooperation and efficiency decay, or reversal of earlier gains/losses in longer experiments.
- **Design x Context Interactions:** Mechanisms effective in one network or institutional context may have opposite effects in a structurally different environment (Carpenter et al., 2012; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Lack of Longitudinal and Cross-Institutional Comparison:** Few studies follow the same populations over multiple institutional changes, limiting the ability to predict the effect of sequential interventions.
- **Adjacent vs. Exact Settings:** The literature includes many adjacent game forms (e.g., coordination, trust, CPR, bargaining), meaning some findings must be extrapolated with caution to standard PGGs.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature provides strong, direct, and nuanced evidence for prediction of efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled in PGG-type environments—*conditional on critical design dimensions such as punishment cost/effectiveness, network structure, centralization, and group context.* Notably, *punishment often increases cooperation but can decrease efficiency if used excessively or antisocially*. Reward, when compared directly, is generally more efficient. Predicting net efficiency effects thus requires careful attention to both the punishment technology and the relevant dimensions as informed above.
