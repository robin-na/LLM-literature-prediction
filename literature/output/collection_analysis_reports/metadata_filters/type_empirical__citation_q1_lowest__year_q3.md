# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is broad but strongly weighted toward empirical, lab-experimental studies, with a substantial subset of observational and field-experimental work. Its core is experimental economics/public goods literature, with a spectrum of studies mapping closely to classic public goods game (PGG) designs (linear, repeated, fixed group, standard payoff structure). Many papers manipulate or observe variants of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms (peer punishment, leader/centralized punishment, third-party punishment, endogenous/exogenous enforcement, punishment cost/impact structure, information conditions, and institutional context). A substantial minority investigate adjacent settings: CPRs, trust/donation/investment games, contests, bargaining/ultimatum games, and team production or market experiments. The proportion of papers that directly analyze efficiency (as group payoff or ratio to the social optimum) as the primary outcome is high in the core PGG literature, though many other studies focus primarily on behavioral measures (contributions, cooperation rates, punishment rates).

The evidence base is strongest for *empirical* effects of punishment on efficiency/payoff in standard lab-based repeated PGGs, with a subset addressing design dimensions of punishment technology and institutional context. There is only limited formal *theory* work reported, and most mechanism arguments are inferential rather than formal. Compared to the scope of possible PGG design permutations, direct dimension-by-dimension evidence is uneven: classic variants (player count 4, 10+ rounds, continuous contributions, full information) are well covered; more complex design features (chat, default framing, reward/punishment combinations, information structure, visibility of punishers, etc.) are more sparsely or indirectly studied.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. PGG or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`)**
- **Exact relevance:** A large subset of papers implement standard linear repeated PGGs (e.g., Harrell, 2019; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Gürdal et al., 2021; Ramalingam et al., 2019; Lim & Zhang, 2020). These directly match the prediction environment.
- **Close relevance:** Several studies use close variants (CPRs, contests, threshold PGGs, trust games), preserving key social dilemma features but modifying some parameters or framing (e.g., Heine & Strobel, 2020; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).
- **Adjacent/Weak relevance:** Many others work in game-theoretically similar environments (ultimatum/gift-exchange, exclusion, sorted matching, coordination games), providing indirect evidence for social dilemma settings but with caution required for transfer.
- **None:** A minority are purely non-PGG (animal studies, hypothetical vignettes, etc.).

**b. Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`)**
- **Exact:** The core PGG studies manipulate the presence/absence and structural features of punishment (peer, centralized, third-party, endogenous, exogenous, cost, magnitude, monitoring) (e.g., Harrell, 2019; Nicklisch et al., 2021).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some focus on reward-only, exclusion (a form of social sanction), or symbolic sanctions (e.g., peer approval/rating, ostracism).
- **Weak:** A portion discuss punishment contextually (as a threat, in survey/field data) but do not experimentally manipulate it.
- **None:** Some studies neither implement nor discuss punishment.

**c. Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`)**
- **Exact:** A substantial group explicitly report efficiency, group payoff/earnings, welfare, or surplus as a main dependent variable (e.g., Harrell, 2019; Gürdal et al., 2021; Lim & Zhang, 2020; Faillo et al., 2020).
- **Close:** Others use closely related proxies (investment success, project completion, final wealth).
- **Adjacent:** Many focus on contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency, or norm compliance, which are correlated but not equivalent to efficiency.
- **Weak/None:** Behavioral, neural, or psychological outcomes with no payoff reporting.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based outcomes:** 
  - Efficiency (group payoff as fraction of full cooperation): Harrell (2019), Benard & Barclay (2020), Lim & Zhang (2020), Gürdal et al. (2021), Glöckner et al. (2018), Faillo et al. (2020), Robbett (2019), Suleiman & Samid (2021), Lippert & Tremewan (2021), Chugunova et al. (2020), Langenbach & Tausch (2019), Nicklisch et al. (2021).
  - Total group earnings/payoff.
  - Welfare or surplus.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:** 
  - Contribution rates, cooperation rates, punishment assigned, punishment received, norm compliance, willingness to punish, response to norm violations, emotional reactions.
  - Many studies focus on these as primary outcomes.
- **Other/adjacent outcomes:**
  - Inequality, distributional equity, resource extraction, group success/failure in threshold settings, variance in contributions/payoffs.

**Distinction noted:** Several papers increase or decrease contributions without corresponding gains in efficiency due to the direct cost of punishment (e.g., Ramalingam et al., 2019; Glöckner et al., 2018). Thus, prediction must not conflate behavior with payoff outcomes—the literature sometimes highlights divergence between cooperation and efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**A. Enabling Punishment Usually Raises Efficiency, But Not Universally**
- In standard repeated PGGs, introducing peer or centralized punishment reliably increases efficiency/group earnings (Harrell, 2019; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Lim & Zhang, 2020; Gürdal et al., 2021, Hou et al., 2019). This effect is robust across cultural settings and under both exogenously and endogenously established institutions.

**B. Punishment Institution Details Crucially Moderate Effects**
- **Centralization & Authority:** Centralized/leader punishment is more efficient than peer punishment, due to lower punishment cost and less counter-punishment (Harrell, 2019; Lim & Zhang, 2020; Castillo & Hamman, 2021).
- **Selection of Punisher/Manager:** Democratic selection of authority sometimes further increases efficiency (Benard & Barclay, 2020), though not always (Castillo & Hamman, 2021).
- **Multiple Punishment Channels:** Efficiency is highest when both observed and delayed/unobserved punishment channels are available; each alone may raise contributions but not efficiency (Glöckner et al., 2018).
- **Monitoring/Information:** The cost and availability of monitoring (information about others' contributions) are strong moderators; cheap monitoring allows punishment to increase efficiency (Nicklisch et al., 2021).

**C. Game Structure and Context Can Reverse or Nullify Efficiency Gains**
- **Punishment Cost and Effectiveness:** High-cost punishment can reduce or eliminate efficiency gains and sometimes reduce efficiency (Robbett, 2019; Glöckner et al., 2018; Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Institutional Competition/Migration:** When reward and punishment options coexist, migrants initially prefer reward communities (higher initial payoffs), but over time, punishment can outperform as rewards decline and migration stabilizes (Chugunova et al., 2020).
- **Framing/Action Structure:** In Give-frame PGGs, punishment may not raise efficiency due to offsetting costs, while in Take-frame games, punishment often increases efficiency (Ramalingam et al., 2019).
- **Endowment Inequality:** Efficiency gains from punishment are greater in groups with unequal endowments because punishment targets coordination on salient norms; with equal endowments, punishment may actually reduce efficiency due to wasted cost (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).

**D. Anticipation and Threat of Punishment Sometimes Suffices**
- Efficiency effects may arise more from the *threat* or anticipation of punishment than from its actual use; migration and institution choice settings find high efficiency with little actual sanctioning (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).

**E. Situations Where Punishment Fails or Reduces Efficiency**
- **High Baseline Efficiency:** Where control efficiency is already high, adding punishment can fail to increase efficiency or even reduce it, if punishment expenditures are not offset by further gains (Robbett, 2019; Heine & Strobel, 2020).
- **Anti-social or Mis-targeted Punishment:** If punishment is used against high contributors or due to ambiguity in norm definition or observable actions, efficiency gains are dampened or reversed (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Robbett, 2019).
- **High Uncertainty or Monitoring Cost:** When outcome uncertainty is high or information costly, effectiveness of punishment declines (Nicklisch et al., 2021; Hajikhameneh & Rubin, 2019).
- **Network/Structure Effects:** In sparse, generalized exchange structures (e.g., cyclical networks), even costless punishment fails to raise efficiency (Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Crowding Out & Signaling:** Pre-announced or personalized punishment policies can reduce intrinsic motivation, crowding out voluntary cooperation and reducing efficiency (Klempt & Pull, 2018).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Baseline Guidance:** In repeated, standard PGGs with moderate or low control efficiency, enabling punishment—particularly when well designed (moderate cost, high impact, information available)—yields a reliable increase in group efficiency consistent across studied settings and cultures (Gürdal et al., 2021; Harrell, 2019; Lim & Zhang, 2020).

- **Dimension Sensitivity:** The *magnitude* of efficiency gains is strongly sensitive to:
  - Structure of punishment (centralized > peer, provided the authority is cooperative).
  - Cost and effectiveness of punishment (lower cost/higher impact more effective).
  - Monitoring/information (cheap monitoring increases efficiency gains).
  - Network structure (dense sanction networks, public good settings outperform sparse or generalized exchange).
  - Framing (punishment more effective in "Take" than "Give" contexts).
  - Endowment inequality (inequality creates coordination focal points, amplifying gains from punishment).

- **Limits:** If control (no-punishment) efficiency is already high (due to high MPCR, prior institution, successful reward/approval mechanisms, or other factors), the added benefit of punishment is often null, and the cost of punishment can even lower efficiency (Robbett, 2019; Glöckner et al., 2018). Also, in institutional or cultural settings where strong reciprocators or norm-keepers dominate, or where ambiguity about desired behavior exists, punishment effectiveness is variable (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).

- **Institutional Details:** Prediction should account for details such as whether punishment is peer vs. centralized, cost and impact per punishment, monitoring/information structure, and the possibility of anti-social or ambiguous punishment. Inclusion of coordination mechanisms (e.g., leader selection, discussion, known number of rounds, reward mechanisms) can interact with or substitute for punishment.

- **Adjacent Designs:** For CPRs, contests, trust games, or other variants, or where punishment is symbolic/social rather than monetary, efficiency effects may be positive, null, or even negative, depending on context, structure, and population behavior. Transfer of findings from these settings to standard PGGs should be made cautiously.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count` (most studies fixed at 3 or 4, some N=8 or greater; studies find higher efficiency gains from punishment in larger groups due to social cost offset, e.g., Lim & Zhang, 2020).
- `num_rounds` (spanning one-shot to 30+ rounds; repeated games needed for punishment to have sustainable effects).
- `mpcr` (manipulated in many studies; efficiency effects are robust across different MPCR, but higher MPCR may reduce marginal benefit of punishment).
- `all_or_nothing` (binary vs. continuous contributions addressed; continuous more common in exact PGGs).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (many studies experimentally vary punishment cost per unit, effectiveness, and structure; lower punishment cost and higher impact favor efficiency gains).
- `chat` (rarely included in baseline PGGs, but some report no chat as default).
- `reward_exists` / `reward_cost` / `reward_tech` (some studies compare or combine with reward mechanisms—findings suggest rewards alone are less effective at raising efficiency, but combined with punishment can potentiate gains).
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries` (manipulated in some studies, especially around transparency and feedback effects).

**Indirectly/Contextually Informed:**
- `default_contrib` (opt-in/out framing rarely altered).
- `show_punishment_id` (identification of punishers addressed in a few studies; mixed evidence about its effect).
- Aspects like endogenous institution/migration, positive vs. negative social information, coordination/leadership mechanisms.

**Effectively Missing or Sparse:**
- Joint manipulation of all 14 dimensions is very rare.
- Only a few studies combine chat, default contribution propensity, punishment and reward, and feedback/visibility simultaneously.
- Deep evidence on `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` is limited, as is systematic evidence on the effect of chat or opt-in/out framing.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Overrepresentation of standard lab PGGs:** Evidence is strongest in classic, repeated, anonymous, small-N public goods games; less so in large groups, field settings, or with real-world heterogeneity.
- **Payoff vs. behavior gap:** Many studies infer payoff or efficiency effects from contribution behavior, but these can diverge when the cost of punishment is high.
- **Sparse evidence on multidimensional interactions:** Most studies manipulate a small subset of design features at a time, limiting certainty around simultaneous or interaction effects among, e.g., punishment, reward, information, and chat.
- **Limited generalizability to field settings:** While some cross-cultural and field/lab-in-field studies are present, institutional and contextual moderation is underexplored compared to lab effects.
- **Caveats on negative and null effects:** Some prominent cases show no efficiency gain, or even losses, from punishment—especially when cost is high, information poor, or population uses punishment for anti-social or ambiguous purposes.
- **Endogenous behavior and anticipation:** Where the threat of punishment suffices, actual observed punishment is low; in environments with ambiguous or mis-coordinated norms, addition of punishment can undermine or fragment cooperation.
- **Missing or unmeasured dimensions:** Default framing, chat effects, simultaneous manipulation of feedback and institution, and reward/punishment identity visibility are under-studied.
- **Ambiguous transfer to adjacent environments:** CPRs, contests, team-production, coordination, and bargaining games offer adjacent evidence, but results are often context-dependent and may not translate directly to standard PGG prediction tasks.

---

**In summary**: The literature set provides *strong* and *direct* evidence that enabling punishment in repeated PGGs almost always increases efficiency over control *if* the institution is well designed—i.e., cost-effective, with reliable monitoring and clear norm targets. Institutional design details (centralization, cost, information, and group heterogeneity) are critical. Efficiency gains are not universal: the context, structure, and norm clarity shape outcomes, and actual punishment use may be low if norms/expectations are well coordinated. For some environments (high baseline efficiency, high cost, ambiguity, or weak network structure), enabling punishment can fail or backfire. Prediction models should use control efficiency as a strong baseline predictor, adjusted for design features and context as summarized above.
