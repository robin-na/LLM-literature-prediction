# 1) Evidence Base

**Breadth and Types:**  
This is a large paper set (140 papers) consisting overwhelmingly of empirical, experimental studies, with the dominant mode being laboratory experiments in economics, psychology, and related fields. The majority focus on public goods games (PGG) or close variants and manipulate or measure punishment/sanctioning mechanisms. A minority of papers include field experiments, agent-based models, or observational data; these are mostly adjacent or weakly relevant. There is a strong emphasis on controlled, parameterized designs, allowing assessment of design dimensions.

**Empirical vs. Theory:**  
The literature is almost entirely empirical, analyzing the impact of experimental manipulations (e.g., enabling/disabling punishment, varying punishment cost/tech) on measurable outcomes. Theory, mechanism, and conceptual arguments are typically confined to discussion sections; direct theoretical modeling is rare in this set.

**Narrowness/Breadth for Prediction Task:**  
For the task—predicting the efficiency impact of enabling peer punishment in PGG-like environments given detailed game design dimensions and control efficiency—the evidence base is broad and rich for standard linear PGGs with monetary punishment, but becomes sparse for highly nonstandard environments (e.g., nonlinear CPR games, games with complex network structures, or non-monetary/reputational punishment). Most parameter combinations (e.g., continuous/all-or-nothing, 3–6 players, 6–30 rounds, with/without chat, standard MPCRs, standard punishment cost/impact) are well covered. However, certain institutional or cultural features (e.g., bribery/corruption, unequal/unstable punishment power, informational noise, and rare game variants) have more limited or context-dependent coverage.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**  
- **‘Exact’ relevance**: A large subset (especially top- and mid-ranked in the digest) are direct implementations of the standard linear PGG (Voluntary Contribution Mechanism), often closely matching the prediction task context.
- **‘Close’ relevance**: Many others use close variants—weakest-link PGG, common-pool resource games, team production games, or cumulative threshold PGGs.
- **‘Adjacent’ or ‘Weak’**: Some studies use dyadic games, trust games, dictator/ultimatum games, or field/observational studies, with tenuous mapping to the prediction task.

**Punishment/Sanctions:**  
- **‘Exact’**: Well represented—most studies that are PGGs offer direct, costly punishment/sanction options, usually peer-based, sometimes centralized or designated.
- **‘Close’/‘Adjacent’**: Reward-only, non-monetary (social ostracism, exclusion, gossip), or exogenously imposed punishment appear regularly; these are not always directly mappable but may offer mechanism insights.
- **‘Weak’ or ‘None’**: A minority focus on no-sanction, only monitoring, or entirely non-punitive interventions.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**  
- **‘Exact’**: Many key papers report group payoff, earnings, or efficiency as primary outcomes.
- **‘Close’**: Some infer efficiency from contribution data or related payoff variables (e.g., in trust or team production games).
- **‘Adjacent’/‘Weak’/‘None’**: A substantial fraction—especially psychology-oriented studies—only report behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, cooperation rates, punishment incidents) and do **not** analyze group payoffs or efficiency.

**Summary:**  
The set offers high direct relevance for PGGs with punishment and efficiency outcomes, especially for lab-based, canonical designs. However, extrapolation to unfamiliar or highly complex environments (nonlinear payoffs, endogenous group formation, field contexts, etc.) is much less certain.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-based Outcomes (‘Efficiency’):**
- **Directly reported**: Many PGG lab experiments report group efficiency, total payoff, average group earnings, or surplus as a fraction of the social optimum ((Kuwabara & Yu, 2017); (Wang & Qin, 2015); (Gürerk, 2013); (Fischer et al., 2016); (Andrighetto et al., 2016); (Fatas & Mateu, 2015); (Harrell & Simpson, 2016); (Gächter et al., 2017); (Grieco et al., 2017); (Qin & Wang, 2013); (Dorrough et al., 2017); (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015); (Feinberg et al., 2014)).
- **Indirectly inferred**: Some studies report only group contributions but connect them to payoffs (because payoffs are a strict function of contributions in standard VCM), while others (especially in trust games or team production) use closely related measures.
- **Absent or not measured**: A large number of studies, especially those from psychology or focused on behavioral mechanisms, do not report efficiency, total earnings, or any payoff-based group aggregate.

**Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**: Most commonly reported when efficiency is absent.
- **Punishment frequency/intensity**, antisocial vs. prosocial punishment, norm compliance.
- **Trust/trustworthiness**, behavioral spillovers, strategy classification (conditional cooperator, free rider, etc.).
- **Social approval, emotional drivers** (anger, legitimacy perceptions, etc.).

**Distinction Maintained:**  
The digest consistently notes whether reported outcomes are payoff-based or behavioral. Several guidance notes explicitly warn not to infer efficiency from cooperation/contribution rates unless the payoff mapping is explicitly described.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## General Patterns

- **Punishment often increases efficiency in standard linear PGGs**: Numerous papers demonstrate that enabling peer or centralized punishment increases group efficiency over no-punishment controls, despite punishment costs ((Kuwabara & Yu, 2017); (Wang & Qin, 2015); (Gürerk, 2013); (Andrighetto et al., 2016); (Harrell & Simpson, 2016); (Gächter et al., 2017); (Feinberg et al., 2014); (Qin & Wang, 2013)).
- **Design details matter acutely**: Efficiency gains are highly sensitive to punishment parameters—cost, effectiveness, centralization, prosociality of punisher, informational structure, production technology ((Kuwabara & Yu, 2017); (Fatas & Mateu, 2015); (Fischer et al., 2016); (Grieco et al., 2017); (Harrell & Simpson, 2016); (Muthukrishna et al., 2017)).
- **Efficiency gains are not universal**:
    - **High antisocial punishment/retaliation** can lead to efficiency losses or null effects, especially if punishment is costless, repeated, or poorly targeted ((Fischer et al., 2016); (Dorrough et al., 2017); (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015)).
    - **Effectiveness varies by country, culture, and social value orientation** ((Balliet & Van Lange, 2013); (Fatas & Mateu, 2015); (Gächter et al., 2017)).
    - **Nonlinear or complex environments (common pool, weak milestones, claim games)** often do **not** see efficiency gains from punishment ((Cason & Gangadharan, 2016); (Fatas & Mateu, 2015); (van Soest et al., 2016)).
- **Centralization and legitimacy**: Centralized or designated punishers (particularly prosocial ones) yield higher efficiency, while decentralized/peer punishment can backfire if poorly coordinated ((Kuwabara & Yu, 2017); (Harrell & Simpson, 2016); (Grieco et al., 2017)).
- **Information structure is critical**: Public versus private information about contributions, about rounds remaining, about identities of punishers—each can alter both punishment deployment and the resulting efficiency ((Dorrough et al., 2017); (Fischer et al., 2016); (Gürerk, 2013)).
- **Contextual moderators**: Communication/chat, social learning/history, cultural trust, presence/absence of rewards, group size and round length, presence of rewards—all modulate the net effect of punishment on efficiency ((Andrighetto et al., 2016); (Gürerk, 2013); (Barclay & Raihani, 2016); (Fatas & Mateu, 2015); (Feinberg et al., 2014)).
- **Efficiency increases from punishment may plateau or reverse over time**—especially if punishment systems allow feuding or cycles of retaliation ((Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015)).

## Key Empirical Findings

- **Positive efficiency effects of punishment** are generally strong and robust **when**:
    - Punishment is costly (not free), sufficiently targeted, and not subject to rampant antisocial use.
    - Punishment rights are stable and evenly distributed, or designated to a consistent, prosocial authority.
    - The baseline efficiency under control is moderate or low (enough scope for improved cooperation).
    - The production technology links contributions directly and monotonically to group payoffs (linear VCMs).

- **Negative or null effects** surface **when**:
    - Punishment is costless or weakly/noisily targeted, leading to excessive/antisocial punishment ((Fischer et al., 2016); (Dorrough et al., 2017); (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015)).
    - The production function is nonlinear or enables exploitation through negative actions (claim games, nonstandard settings).
    - There is significant instability or inequality in the assignment of punishment power ((Dorrough et al., 2017)).
    - Bribery or corruption is possible ((Muthukrishna et al., 2017)).
    - Punishment can be used for non-normative, leveling, or competitive purposes, especially under local competition ((Barker & Barclay, 2016); (Gervais, 2017)).
    - Punishment mechanisms are poorly integrated with information feedback, communication, or are combined with features that crowd out trust ((Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015); (Romaniuc et al., 2016)).

- **Indirect evidence**: Most studies reporting only behavioral outcomes confirm that punishment increases cooperation, but without group payoff data, they urge caution in equating cooperation rises directly with efficiency gains ((Balliet & Van Lange, 2013); (Choi & Ahn, 2013); (Nelissen & Mulder, 2013); (Stagnaro et al., 2017)).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

## General Heuristics from the Literature

- **In standard linear PGG with moderate control efficiency, enabling costly, well-targeted peer punishment is expected to increase efficiency, typically moving group payoffs closer to the social optimum.**
- **The treatment effect is mediated and moderated by key design dimensions:**
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness**: Low-cost or costless punishment tends to reduce efficiency via antisocial or deterrence-ineffective use. Moderate, costly punishment with high impact (e.g., 1:3 fee-to-fine) is most effective ((Kuwabara & Yu, 2017); (Qin & Wang, 2013)).
    - **Institutional structure**: Efficiency gains are maximized with centralized or designated, prosocial punishment; decentralized peer punishment is effective if antisocial use is limited ((Harrell & Simpson, 2016); (Grieco et al., 2017); (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017)).
    - **Information and communication**: Communication and transparency about contributions, punishment, and outcomes improve efficiency gains from punishment ((Andrighetto et al., 2016); (Gürerk, 2013)). Instability, unequal power, or heavy informational noise may undermine these gains ((Dorrough et al., 2017); (Fischer et al., 2016)).
    - **Group size and repetition**: Longer repeated interactions and fixed group composition tend to support stronger and more durable efficiency effects ((Balliet & Van Lange, 2013); (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015)). The efficacy of punishment does not appear to scale linearly with group size within the typical 3–5 player range, but may decay with larger or more unstable groups.
    - **Game framing and contribution technology**: All-or-nothing games, lowest-link games, and nonlinear/cumulative production structures interact with punishment; in some contexts, punishment is highly effective (e.g., weakest-link), while in others, it is destabilizing or inefficient ((Fatas & Mateu, 2015); (Freytag et al., 2014); (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016)).
    - **Presence of rewards or alternative sanctions**: In designs that allow both punishment and rewards, efficiency effects depend on their relative cost/effectiveness and the players' use patterns ((Choi & Ahn, 2013); (Molenmaker et al., 2014)).
    - **Corruption possibilities**: If the punishment institution is corruptible, efficiency effects can reverse ((Muthukrishna et al., 2017)).

## Model Implications

- **If the control efficiency is already close to optimal, enabled punishment typically yields a smaller absolute increase, and in some designs may reduce efficiency if punishment costs overtake the gains in cooperation.**
- **Prediction should be most confident for classic VCMs with full information, moderate-size groups (3–5), moderate to high MPCR (0.3–0.5), continuous contributions, and costly peer punishment with transparent impact.**
- **Predictions should be more cautious (expecting weaker or even negative effects) in:**
    - Nonlinear/common-pool resource games.
    - Designs with high risk of antisocial or competitive punishment.
    - Unstable punishment power or noisy monitoring.
    - Presence of corruption/bribery channels or network complexities.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (Strong empirical support):**
- `player_count`: 3–5 most commonly studied; effects are moderated but not strongly driven by small group-size variation.
- `num_rounds`: Repetition length affects both baseline efficiency and treatment effect of punishment.
- `chat`: Directly and robustly shown to be a strong positive moderator; communication often raises efficiency independently of or in combination with punishment.
- `all_or_nothing`: Varies; punishment works differently in all-or-nothing vs. continuous games; both types covered in the literature.
- `default_contrib`: Framing manipulations (opt-in vs. opt-out) are infrequent; some evidence included.
- `mpcr`: Highly informative; efficiency and punishment effects are sensitive to MPCR.
- `punishment_cost`/`punishment_tech`: **Key**; cost/impact ratio is critical for prediction; both high and low cost designs included.
- `show_other_summaries`: Outcome transparency is directly manipulated in several studies, influencing punishment targeting and effects.

**Indirectly informed/contextually discussed:**
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Less emphasis, though several studies provide joint punishment/reward designs.
- `show_punishment_id`: Mixed evidence on anonymity vs. identifiability of punishers.

**Only contextually discussed/effectively missing:**
- **Design features with sparse empirical mapping:**
    - Highly dynamic or endogenously evolving network structures.
    - Detailed implementation/framing of reward/penalty (especially in field contexts).
    - Large group sizes (>7).
    - Structural features like scheduled removal/activation of punishment or credible threats of removal.
    - Complex correlation between multiple informational signals (e.g., summaries, round numbers, punishment sources).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavioral versus payoff outcomes:** Many papers report only cooperation/contribution rates, *not* group efficiency or payoffs. Prediction based on these measures requires inference and carries risk when contribution-payoff mapping is nonlinear or complicated by punishment costs.
- **Lab–field generalizability:** While lab evidence is strong for classic VCMs, field and more complex systemic environments are underrepresented; real-world implementation may yield weaker or reversed effects ((Romaniuc et al., 2016); (Molenmaker et al., 2014)).
- **Nonlinear and complex environments:** The efficiency-punishment relationship is much less well understood in games with nonlinear payoff structures, endogenous group formation, or high environmental noise ((Cason & Gangadharan, 2016); (Fatas & Mateu, 2015)).
- **Antisocial/competitive punishment:** Contexts prone to retaliation, leveling, or locally competitive punishment may see reduced or negative efficiency effects—a critical caveat not always predictable from design dimensions alone ((Dorrough et al., 2017); (Barker & Barclay, 2016); (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015)).
- **Institutional detail sensitivity:** Small differences in punishment implementation—centralized vs. peer, identity exposure, cost magnitude, turnover of punishers—can produce fundamentally different efficiency impacts. Generalizing between 'punishment present' and absent without qualifying institutional details risks error.
- **Cultural and trust moderators:** Cross-cultural differences in trust, acceptance of punishment, and use of antisocial punishment can strongly modulate outcomes, complicating out-of-sample prediction ((Balliet & Van Lange, 2013); (Fatas & Mateu, 2015)).
- **Missing dimensions:** Some design parameters relevant to modern online or field settings (e.g., rapid changing group membership, AI monitoring, complex reward/penalty mix) are not substantively empirically addressed.
- **Ambiguity in mapping contribution to efficiency in All-or-Nothing/Threshold/Claim games:** Not all high-cooperation/prevention mechanisms correlate with high efficiency in these designs—contextual mapping is essential.

---

**In sum:**  
This literature base is *highly relevant* and contains strong empirical support for predicting the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in canonical lab-based PGGs across key design dimensions. However, the effect of punishment on efficiency is not universal, and careful attention must be paid to the detailed institutional, informational, and cultural context when making predictions outside of the well-established experimental core. Explicit reporting of payoff-based group outcomes is critical for grounding quantitative prediction—contribution or cooperation data alone is an insufficient substitute unless the contribution-payoff mapping is strict and documented. Where design dimensions are only contextually discussed or not empirically mapped, predictions should be made with caution, and ambiguity or lack of evidence should be preserved.
