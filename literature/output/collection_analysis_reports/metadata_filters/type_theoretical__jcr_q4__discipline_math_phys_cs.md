# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists exclusively of theoretical contributions (no empirical or laboratory experimental papers), focused largely on formal modeling, simulation, and theory-driven mechanism analysis. The literature set is broad in its coverage of social dilemmas, encompassing not only public goods games (PGG) but also related structures such as Prisoner's Dilemma variants, volunteer's dilemma, donation games, and games on networks. A minority of papers focus specifically and exactly on standard public goods games, while many provide adjacent or closely related models that require careful translation to the prediction task.

Most papers incorporate explicit or implicit mechanisms for punishment or sanctioning, considering a wide range of punishment designs (peer, institutional, collective, metanorms, action- vs. outcome-based, etc.), and some explore the interaction of punishment with reward mechanisms, communication, network structure, group size, and psychological factors.

Outcomes analyzed are almost entirely theoretical: payoffs, efficiency, equilibrium welfare, and formal existence conditions for cooperative/efficient equilibria. Only a few papers directly report group efficiency or “treatment vs. control” efficiency deltas, and none analyze experimental effect sizes or variance. This base therefore offers high mechanistic depth and qualitative insight but limited empirical calibration for absolute efficiency prediction.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. PGG or Variant:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Several papers model settings that are formally equivalent to canonical public goods games (Deng et al., 2012; Botta et al., 2021; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006).  
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Many others use closely related repeated social dilemmas, e.g., repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, volunteer's dilemma, or network games, which share structural properties but are not strictly PGGs (Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Castro & Toro, 2008; Kurokawa et al., 2010; Uchida et al., 2024).

**b. Punishment or Sanctions:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Punishment is a focal mechanism in most papers, with studied variants including peer punishment, institutional punishment, collective punishment, metanorms, and community enforcement (Deng et al., 2012; Botta et al., 2021; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006).  
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Some models consider related mechanisms like “walk-away” (partner switching), network exclusion, or endogenous adjustment after defection (Kurokawa, 2022; Kurokawa et al., 2010). Others focus on emotional, reputational, or signaling variants of sanctioning.  
- **Weak or none:** A minority discuss only the control case or endogenous/voluntary cooperation without explicit punishment (Beraldo & Sugden, 2016; Bellucci, 2022).

**c. Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**  
- **Exact relevance:** About half of the papers provide explicit efficiency or group payoff analysis (Deng et al., 2012; Botta et al., 2021; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006; Voelkl, 2015).  
- **Close or adjacent:** Others center on existence conditions for efficient equilibria or analyze efficiency as one of several possible outcomes (Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).  
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:** Several focus on cooperation rates, norm following, or punishment frequency without direct payoff outcomes (Thöni, 2014; Zhang & Pei, 2022), or treat them only indirectly.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related Outcomes (directly relevant to prediction):**
- **Efficiency** (group payoff relative to maximum possible): Explicit in Deng et al. (2012), Botta et al. (2021), Ishikawa & Fontanari (2025), Bowles & Gintis (2004), Voelkl (2015), Kendal et al. (2006), Jaffe (2004), Gioffré & Tampieri (2025), among others.
- **Total group payoff/earnings:** Often used interchangeably with efficiency.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (not efficiency):**
- **Contribution rate / cooperation rate:** Often measured, but only indirectly related to payoff and subject to over-interpretation if punishment is costly (Thöni, 2014; Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- **Punishment frequency and type:** Tracked in behavioral models, but these do not always translate to efficiency improvements (Thöni, 2014; Jaffe, 2004).
- **Norm compliance, learning dynamics, strategic adaptation:** Analyzed in models focusing on mechanism or system dynamics.

**Others:**
- **Stability and basin of attraction of cooperative equilibria:** Informs likelihood of high-efficiency state, but does not guarantee payoff improvement if costly punishment is frequent.

**Distinction:**  
Models and findings make clear that higher cooperation/contribution rates under punishment do not always mean higher efficiency, due to punishment costs sometimes exceeding benefits.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**a. Enabling punishment generally increases efficiency in PGG or PGG-like settings—contingent on parameter values.**
- **Strong, well-calibrated punishment** (high severity, shared cost, effective targeting) reliably increases group efficiency by deterring free-riding and stabilizing cooperation (Deng et al., 2012; Botta et al., 2021; Voelkl, 2015; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).
- **Thresholds matter:** The increase in efficiency is not unconditional; punishment must be above a minimum cost-to-impact threshold to destabilize defection but not so costly that net payoff declines (Deng et al., 2012; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).
- **Group size effect:** Larger groups may require more severe, concerted, or institutional punishment to maintain efficiency, but such mechanisms can deliver higher net efficiency if implemented (Deng et al., 2012; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025). However, basin-of-attraction issues mean large groups may also be less likely to reach cooperation from random starts.
- **Peer punishment can backfire:** When punishment is costly, frequent, or antisocial (used against cooperators), group efficiency can decrease or, at best, not improve (Jaffe, 2004; Zhang & Pei, 2022; Thöni, 2014).
- **Institutional and concerted punishment:** These are generally more efficient due to shared costs and reduced frequency (Deng et al., 2012; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
- **Metanorms and rewarding punishers:** Reinforcing punishment through social or institutional reward can greatly expand the parameter space for high efficiency (Kendal et al., 2006).

**b. Contrasting mechanisms and moderators:**
- **Punishment intensity vs. frequency:** Rare but severe (or credibly threatened) punishment can be more efficient than continual, mild punishment (Deng et al., 2012).
- **Psychological and informational moderators:** Prospect-theoretic weighting (overweighting punishment risk) can make infrequent punishment highly effective (Uchida et al., 2024). Truthful information sharing or communication amplifies punishment effects (Annen, 2011).
- **Action-based vs. outcome-based punishment:** Action-based (punishing the act rather than the outcome) is more efficient in volunteer's dilemma-type contexts (Friehe & Tabbach, 2018).
- **Antisocial punishment reduces efficiency:** Environments fostering antisocial or misdirected punishment may see a reduction, not an increase, in group efficiency (Thöni, 2014; Zhang & Pei, 2022).

**c. Limitations of generality**
- **Risk of efficiency decrease:** When punishment is too costly, misapplied, or when baseline control efficiency is already high, enabling punishment may not improve and can harm efficiency (Jaffe, 2004; Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- **Initial conditions and path-dependence:** Some models show bi-stability—enabling punishment can, in theory, support both high- and low-efficiency equilibria depending on starting conditions and design (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Uchida et al., 2024).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Strongest prediction:** Enabling peer or institutional punishment in PGG-like settings with low to moderate baseline (control) efficiency is expected to substantially increase efficiency if punishment is sufficiently severe/effective, not overly costly, and antisocial punishment is minimal. The magnitude of improvement depends on the cost-to-impact ratio for the punishment mechanism and group size (Deng et al., 2012; Botta et al., 2021; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006; Voelkl, 2015).
- **Control efficiency baseline matters:** If control efficiency is already high, marginal improvement may be small and punishment costs may outweigh gains (Jaffe, 2004).
- **Parameter sensitivity:** Prediction should account for:
    - *Player count:* Larger groups benefit more from concerted or institutional punishment. Simple peer punishment may not scale (Deng et al., 2012; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
    - *MPCR (marginal returns):* Lower MPCR (more difficult cooperation) requires more efficient/targeted punishment to reach high efficiency.
    - *Punishment cost and severity:* There exists a threshold cost-to-impact ratio; efficiency gains occur only above threshold and with proper calibration.
    - *Punishment implementation ("tech"):* Action-based, focused, or institutionally shared punishment mechanisms are more likely to yield efficiency; mere availability is insufficient if implementation is inefficient (Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
    - *All-or-nothing vs. continuous contributions:* Extreme all-or-nothing environments may magnify punishment impact—either positively or negatively—depending on cost calibration (Deng et al., 2012; Botta et al., 2021).
    - *Reward existence and coupling with punishment:* Rewards for punishers, or combined reward/punishment schemes, dramatically expand the conditions for efficiency improvement (Kendal et al., 2006).
- **Empirical calibration is missing:** Absolute effect sizes are not provided by theory, and empirical variance is unknown. Predictions using only this literature should be treated as qualitative, directionally reliable, but quantitatively ambiguous.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- **player_count / group size:** Central in almost all theory papers modeling collective action; explicit in formulas and findings (Deng et al., 2012; Botta et al., 2021; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Voelkl, 2015; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
- **mpcr:** Explicitly discussed in nearly every study modeling payoff structure and equilibrium conditions (Deng et al., 2012; Botta et al., 2021; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech, punishment magnitude:** Extensively analyzed as key moderators of punishment effect (Deng et al., 2012; Botta et al., 2021; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Kendal et al., 2006).
- **all_or_nothing:** Most models are binary-action, and this is tied to their structural assumptions.
- **reward_exists / reward_cost / reward_tech:** Explicitly analyzed in a subset dealing with metanorms or combined incentive schemes (Kendal et al., 2006; Voelkl, 2015).

**Indirectly Informed/Contextual Dimensions:**  
- **num_rounds, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries:** Discussed in repeated game and information models but not systematically analyzed for their marginal effects (Kurokawa et al., 2010; Annen, 2011; Andersson, 2020).
- **default_contrib:** Only discussed obliquely via framing/context/salience but not directly modeled.

**Sparse or Missing Dimensions:**  
- **chat:** Rarely, if ever, modeled formally; some indirect discussion of communication or information sharing (Annen, 2011; Golman, 2016).
- **show_punishment_id:** Not systematically addressed, though some models touch on anonymity in punishment targeting (not parameterized).
- **show_other_summaries:** Mentioned in models of information structure but not a focal mechanism.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of empirical effect sizes:** No direct experimental or field data quantifying efficiency improvements from punishment; all findings are theoretical or simulation-based.
- **Ambiguity in quantitative translation:** While qualitative (directional) guidance is strong, these models do not predict absolute efficiency levels or the magnitude of gain from control to punishment-enabled treatment.
- **Potential for negative or null effects:** Peer punishment, especially when costly or prone to antisocial application, can reduce efficiency (Jaffe, 2004; Zhang & Pei, 2022); models highlight boundary conditions but rarely estimate break-even points.
- **Generalizability risk:** Adjacent models (repeated PD, volunteer's dilemma, network games) require caution when mapping results back to canonical PGGs.
- **Underexplored design features:** Critical dimensions like communication (chat), punishment/reward anonymity, or interface framing are only contextually or hypothetically discussed.
- **Initial condition/path-dependence:** Many models indicate multiple equilibria; the realized efficiency may depend on unmodeled features or historical path, especially in large groups (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Uchida et al., 2024).
- **Contextual moderators absent from dimensions:** Factors like psychological weighting (prospect theory), antisocial norms, reputation systems, and culture are highlighted as moderators but are not parameterized in the main design dimensions.
- **No control-treatment efficiency mapping:** There is limited direct guidance for how a measured control (no punishment) efficiency translates, stepwise, to punishment-enabled efficiency—most theory assumes either defection baseline (very low) or full defection/cooperation equilibria.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature provides strong theoretical (but not empirical) support that enabling effective, well-calibrated peer or institutional punishment in public goods games generally raises efficiency, particularly when the control game’s efficiency is low and design parameters (cost, severity, group size) are favorable. However, the effect is sensitive to punishment cost, implementation, antisocial punishment, and initial conditions. Many design dimensions are structurally included in models, but key empirical and behavioral factors—communication, framing, psychological risk weighting—are less systematically addressed, limiting precise quantitative prediction. Predictions based on this literature should be interpreted as directionally robust but requiring further empirical calibration for magnitude.
