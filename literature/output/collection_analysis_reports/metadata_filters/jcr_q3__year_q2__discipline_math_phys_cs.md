# 1) Evidence Base

The supplied paper set (39 theory papers, none with empirical or lab experimental data) is broad in theoretical scope within evolutionary game theory but is largely focused on formal modeling rather than empirical estimation of outcomes. The vast majority are population models or agent-based simulations, with a primary focus on mechanistic argumentation and parameter analysis. Coverage of public-goods-game (PGG) or PGG-like structures is strong, and many papers focus explicitly on punishment/sanctioning mechanisms (peer, pool, graduated, exclusion, institutional, etc.), including comparative analyses with reward or alternative interventions. A minority of papers are only adjacent or contextually relevant, focusing on related social dilemmas (e.g., Prisoner’s Dilemma, common-pool resources, or coordination games), strategy evolution, or mechanisms like institutional tagging or linkage.

For the targeted prediction task—estimating the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in PGGs, conditional on game design and control efficiency—the evidence base is theoretically rich but lacks direct empirical outcome measurement for the specific prediction context.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Relevance assessment by dimension:**

- **pgg_or_variant:**
  - *exact:* Many papers model standard PGGs or their close variants (e.g., Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Hauser et al., 2014; Dercole et al., 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).
  - *close/adjacent:* Several papers model games that are not exact PGGs (e.g., repeated PD, resource dilemmas, mutualism), but share critical dynamics (e.g., Lee et al., 2015, 2017; Camera & Gioffré, 2014; Jordan & Rand, 2017).

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**
  - *exact or close:* Over half the papers directly analyze peer or pool punishment, sanctioning, or related mechanisms.
  - *adjacent:* Some study alternative or broader mechanisms (e.g., exclusionary sanctions, institutional tagging, commitment signaling, linkage, reward).

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
  - *exact:* A solid set of papers report efficiency, group welfare, or average payoff as primary outcomes (e.g., Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Dercole et al., 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Archetti & Scheuring, 2013).
  - *close/adjacent:* Many more focus on cooperation rates or prevalence of strategies; some provide explicit argumentation about linkage to efficiency but do not formally report payoff outcomes (e.g., Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Kaiping et al., 2016).
  - *weak:* A few only discuss efficiency contextually or as implications without formal modeling or quantitative results.

**Summary:**  
Task relevance is generally high for theory/mechanism; moderate for efficiency outcomes; and strongest where models map directly to PGGs with explicit peer punishment and group payoff.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (group payoff, efficiency, average earnings):**
  - Directly measured in a substantial proportion of PGG-focused theory papers.
  - Supported by explicit formulas, equilibrium analysis, or simulation averages.
  - Central for prediction: e.g., Oya & Ohtsuki (2017), Dercole et al. (2013), Archetti & Scheuring (2013), Lee et al. (2015, 2017).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution/cooperation rate, strategy prevalence, punishment frequency):**
  - Often the primary reported outcomes, especially in spatial or evolutionary models (e.g., Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Kaiping et al., 2016; C. Y. Zhang et al., 2017).
  - These are not equivalent to group efficiency, though often interpreted as proxies in the absence of direct payoff calculation.
  - Some papers infer likely efficiency implications from cooperation levels but do not formally report them.

- **Intermediate/indirect outcomes:**
  - Frequency of strategy types, transitions between regimes, or dynamic properties (e.g., persistence of cooperation, phase transitions, hysteresis).

- **Efficiency contextually discussed:**
  - A few papers only mention efficiency as a desired group outcome or as a qualitative implication of behavioral stability (e.g., Syi, 2014; Centola, 2013).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesized cross-paper findings for efficiency outcomes when enabling punishment in PGGs:**

- **Positive effects conditional on context:**  
  Most theory papers (e.g., Dercole et al., 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2014) predict that enabling punishment in standard PGGs—especially when antisocial punishment is absent and punishment is not too costly—substantially increases group efficiency, with equilibrium average payoffs approaching full cooperation values. This is robust for well-designed peer or institutional punishment, and for environments with sufficient transparency or institutional support.

- **Critical moderating factors:**
  - *Population structure*: Spatial or group structure can expand the conditions under which punishment increases efficiency (Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Kaiping et al., 2016; Van Cleve, 2016).
  - *Type of punishment*: Pool (institutional) or shared-cost punishment are more robust to second-order free riding and antisocial punishment; decentralized/peer punishment is vulnerable when antisocial punishment is possible (Gao et al., 2015; Hauser et al., 2014).
  - *Antisocial punishment*: The presence of antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) nullifies or reverses the efficiency gains of enabling punishment (Hauser et al., 2014).
  - *Honesty/corruption in enforcement*: When punishment is delegated and subject to corruption, the effect of punishment on efficiency can be highly variable, ranging from high to very low efficiency depending on enforcer honesty and transparency (Lee et al., 2015, 2017).
  - *Benefit function linearity*: If the PGG has a linear benefit function, punishment drives maximal efficiency; if nonlinear (e.g., threshold, sigmoid), efficiency can be high even without punishment, and the marginal effect of adding punishment is reduced (Archetti & Scheuring, 2013).
  - *Cost–benefit ratios*: Punishment can be counterproductive or ineffective when it is too costly relative to cooperation, or when the fine is insufficient (Dercole et al., 2013).

- **Negative or null effects:**  
  In well-mixed populations with high mutation rates or when anti-social punishment is possible, enabling punishment often does not improve or can even reduce efficiency relative to the control (Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Hauser et al., 2014).

- **Bistability and initial conditions:**  
  Several models exhibit bistable outcomes: enabling punishment can bring high or low efficiency depending on starting conditions, frequency of honest enforcers, or the informational environment (Lee et al., 2015, 2017).

- **Efficiency gains mainly when control efficiency is low:**  
  The largest marginal gains from punishment are predicted for inefficient baseline (control) games; in already high-efficiency controls (e.g., due to voluntary participation, reciprocal strategies, or nonlinear benefits), punishment adds less.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Theoretical results across the literature base suggest the following operational guidance for predicting treatment efficiency after enabling punishment, given game design and control efficiency:

- **Baseline:**  
  If control efficiency is near the maximum (e.g., due to favorable MPCR, small player count, voluntary participation, or nonlinear benefits), enabling punishment is predicted to have little or no effect on efficiency.

- **Effect size:**  
  When the control condition is inefficient (low cooperation, low group payoff), enabling punishment—assuming it is pro-social, not excessively costly, and robust to antisocial abuse—should increase efficiency, potentially approaching the full-cooperation optimum (Dercole et al., 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).

- **Dimension-specific prediction:**
  - *Player count*: Larger groups can dilute both punishment effectiveness and cooperation, but shared or institutional punishment helps mitigate this; effect of punishment may weaken at high player counts absent institutional mechanisms (Dercole et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2017).
  - *Punishment cost/tech*: Lower punishment costs and higher effectiveness favor higher efficiency gains, especially when costs are shared (Schoenmakers et al., 2014).
  - *Antisocial punishment*: If the game design allows antisocial punishment, expect little efficiency gain; if only pro-social punishment is possible, expect a strong positive effect on efficiency (Hauser et al., 2014; Gao et al., 2015).
  - *Population/interaction structure*: Spatial structure, community or group-level selection, and clustering are associated with larger efficiency gains from punishment (Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Kaiping et al., 2016).
  - *Institutions and observability*: Visible, honest, and well-identified institutional punishment leads to higher and more stable efficiency increases (Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2015).

- **Limiting cases:**  
  Punishment may fail or reduce efficiency if it is too harsh, misapplied (e.g., with high error rates), subject to corruption, or fails to be publicly visible as a deterrent.

**Prediction should explicitly account for whether effects are supported by payoff outcomes or inferred from cooperation rates. When payoff/effect mapping is only indirect, or when results depend on subtle design details, prediction uncertainty should be acknowledged.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**  
- `player_count`: Frequently modeled; explicit in effects on punishment and cooperation dynamics.
- `num_rounds`: Explicit in repeated games/outcomes.
- `mpcr`: Central parameter in almost all models.
- `punishment_cost` & `punishment_tech`: Directly parameterized and analyzed in nearly all relevant theory papers.
- `all_or_nothing`: Modeled as binary or continuous in different papers.
- `show_punishment_id` (transparency/observability): Directly analyzed in papers on institutional punishment, corruption, and observability.
- `reward_exists`: Included in a minority as a comparative factor (e.g., reward vs punishment), but not the primary focus.

**Indirectly or contextually informed:**  
- `chat`, `default_contrib`: Discussed mainly as part of the information structure or player behavior framing, not closely modeled.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `reward_magnitude`: Covered in some reward-comparison models but less central.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Information feedback to players is explicitly modeled in some evolutionary simulations; less attention outside those.
- `punishment_magnitude`: Sometimes grouped with `punishment_cost` as a ratio (cost-to-fine).

**Effectively missing or sparse across the set:**
- Precise mappings for `chat`, `default_contrib`, and interface/framing dimensions are largely absent.
- Empirical variation in feedback, framing, communication, or user experience is not modeled.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical evidence absence:**  
  No lab or field experimental data are present, so all findings are theoretical or simulation-based.

- **Efficiency often inferred, not measured:**  
  Many papers report cooperation rates, strategy frequencies, or dynamics, not direct efficiency or group payoff. Efficiency effects are often inferred rather than calculated, especially in spatial or evolutionary models.

- **Parameter mapping to real experiments:**  
  While theory covers a wide parameter range, mapping abstract model parameters to concrete game design dimensions can be ambiguous, especially for multi-dimensional design features not always represented in the models.

- **Limited coverage of full dimension set:**  
  Some design dimensions relevant to real-world or experimental PGGs (chat, communication protocols, default options, multi-level feedback) are rarely modeled, limiting their predictive value for games using such features.

- **Generalizability to real populations:**  
  Evolutionary models may not capture bounded rationality, learning, or other behavioral complexities of human play in lab or field experiments.

- **Sensitivity to antisocial punishment, corruption, and errors:**  
  Results on punishment’s effects are highly sensitive to whether antisocial punishment is permitted, whether monitoring/punishment is accurate, and whether enforcement can be corrupted. Real-world or experimental implementations may differ in these respects from theoretical assumptions.

- **Ambiguity in contexts with bistability:**  
  Some models indicate that the same punishment regime can produce both high- and low-efficiency equilibria depending on initial conditions or small changes in population composition; predictions in these regions are necessarily uncertain.

- **Limited comparative studies:**  
  Few papers compare multiple punishment mechanisms (e.g., peer vs. pool vs. graduated) within a unified framework for efficiency outcomes.

**Summary:**  
While the literature is mechanistically rich and generally supportive of the importance of design dimensions and baseline efficiency for predicting the treatment effect of punishment, direct quantitative guidance for empirical prediction is limited. The strongest, most direct evidence supports the prediction that enabling punishment (under pro-social, well-designed, and transparent conditions) increases efficiency only when baseline is low—but that this effect is nullified or reversed by antisocial punishment, high costs, or corruption risk. Uncertainty is high where models disagree or key design/implementation details are missing or only weakly addressed.
