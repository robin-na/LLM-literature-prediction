# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set of 13 items mixes empirical (primarily lab-experimental) and theoretical work, with more theoretical papers and conceptual models than large-scale field experiments. The scope covers both public goods games (PGG) and a variety of adjacent collective-action and enforcement contexts, including repeated social dilemmas, agent-based simulations, sequential bargaining games, and evolutionary models. Several papers are highly focused on efficiency or payoff outcomes in PGGs with punishment (e.g., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012), while others focus on cooperation rates, antisocial punishment, communication, or social norms (e.g., Thöni, 2014; Brosig et al., 2004; Gordon & Lea, 2016). Only a subset directly addresses how group efficiency changes when enabling peer punishment in a standard or near-standard PGG setup. Overall, the evidence base is moderately broad for mechanism discovery but narrower in terms of exact task relevance for predicting efficiency changes from design features in public-goods-game-like environments with punishment enabled.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant:**  
  - *exact*: Several papers analyze linear PGGs or close variants (e.g., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012; Thöni, 2014; Martinsson et al., 2015). Some theoretical models are directly applied to PGG parameters (e.g., Kurokawa et al., 2010; Kendal et al., 2006).
  - *close/adjacent*: About half the set addresses related social dilemmas, bargaining games, or group cooperation environments that are structurally similar to PGGs but not identical in all dimensions (e.g., Brosig et al., 2004; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Jaffe, 2004; Huang, 2007; Lopez, 2017).
  - *none*: A few items have only thematic overlap, focusing on expectations or attitudes rather than game structure (e.g., Gordon & Lea, 2016).

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**  
  - *exact*: Multiple papers examine costly peer punishment directly applied to defectors in PGGs (e.g., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012; Thöni, 2014).
  - *exact/adjacent*: Some address punishment in broader or more stylized forms (e.g., reward-punishment metanorms, concerted/antisocial punishment, or structured institutional detection—Kendal et al., 2006; Jaffe, 2004; Huang, 2007).
  - *none*: Some have no punishment/sanction dimension (e.g., Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015).

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
  - *exact*: A subset (notably Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012; Kurokawa et al., 2010; Kendal et al., 2006; Jaffe, 2004) directly report group efficiency or analogous payoff-based outcomes.
  - *adjacent/none*: Several focus primarily on contribution rates, norm compliance, or reputational/psychological responses, with at best partial or indirect implications for efficiency (e.g., Thöni, 2014; Brosig et al., 2004; Huang, 2007; Gordon & Lea, 2016).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**  
  - *Efficiency, total group payoff, welfare, surplus, aggregate wealth:*  
    Specifically measured in Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015), Deng et al. (2012), Jaffe (2004), Kurokawa et al. (2010), Kendal et al. (2006). Some theory models report mean fitness or analogous measures.
  - *Indirect markers (adjacent):*  
    Some studies only infer efficiency from contribution rates or presumed group benefit (e.g., communication studies), failing to directly quantify payoff outcomes.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
  - *Contribution/cooperation rates, norm compliance, punishment frequency, motivations, reputational outcomes, etc.:*  
    These are the focus in Thöni (2014), Drouvelis et al. (2015), Brosig et al. (2004), Lopez (2017), Gordon & Lea (2016). Antisocial punishment, conditional cooperation, and social class differences are often described in contribution or motivational terms without direct reference to group efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment can increase efficiency, but is highly contingent on implementation:**  
  - *Design of punishment mechanism matters substantially:*
    - Standard, anonymous, single-stage peer punishment in repeated PGGs (e.g., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015) leads to higher efficiency compared to no punishment, as increased contributions more than offset punishment costs.  
    - However, when the punishment environment is "rich" (multiple punishment stages, fixed identities, and full information), opportunities for retaliation and feuding neutralize efficiency gains, as increased contributions are offset by high punishment costs and destructive cycles (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - The effectiveness of punishment in theory also depends on the severity and rarity of its application; rare but severe, concerted punishment is most efficiency-enhancing, especially in larger groups (Deng et al., 2012).

- **Cost structure and metanorms shape efficiency outcomes:**  
  - The presence and costliness of punishment and the existence of additional metanorms (e.g., rewarding punishers or punishing non-punishers) can shift the system to a high-efficiency equilibrium if costs are not excessive (Kendal et al., 2006).

- **Antisocial punishment and behavioral heterogeneity may undermine efficiency:**  
  - Frequent antisocial punishment (defectors punishing cooperators) can decrease or even reverse the efficiency gains hypothesized from enabling punishment (Thöni, 2014; Jaffe, 2004). This is likely where design features (lack of anonymity, fixed identities) or subject pools encourage irrational or vengeful punishment.

- **Communication and context matter:**  
  - While communication (e.g., chat or face-to-face) is shown in adjacent games (sequential bargaining) to increase the likelihood of efficient outcomes, its effect in standard PGGs is less directly tested in this set (Brosig et al., 2004).

- **Non-punishment levers (priming, class, social status):**  
  - No direct evidence that these factors affect efficiency with respect to punishment-enabled games, though they may shape baseline cooperation rates (Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015; Gordon & Lea, 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct prediction of treatment efficiency from game dimensions plus control efficiency is best informed when:**
  - The punishment mechanism is standard (one-stage, anonymous, imperfect information): Expect efficiency to rise above control, as punishment deters free-riding enough to offset its own costs (Empirical: Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Theory: Deng et al., 2012; Kendal et al., 2006).
  - The mechanism allows for retaliation (multiple stages, identity revealed): Do **not** expect an efficiency boost; increased contributions are offset by punishment costs and cycles (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
  - The punishment is rare but severe (concerted, high impact for defectors, low frequency/cost): Predict the largest efficiency gains, particularly in large groups (Theory: Deng et al., 2012).
  - The environment is prone to antisocial punishment (based on design or participant characteristics): Efficiency gains may be neutralized or reversed (Thöni, 2014; Jaffe, 2004).
  - If reward exists, especially for punishers, expect higher probability of attaining efficient outcome (Kendal et al., 2006).

- **Design features absent from or only weakly addressed in this literature (e.g., opt-in/opt-out framing, explicit reward magnitude/cost, certain UI visibility features) limit specificity of prediction.**
- **Only a few papers provide both control and treatment efficiency outcomes under comparable conditions, but theoretical models allow for parameterized adjustment when punishment cost/severity/group size is well specified.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**  
- `player_count`: Frequently manipulated and/or explicitly parameterized (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012; Kurokawa et al., 2010).
- `num_rounds`: Explicit in repeated game setups (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Thöni, 2014; Kurokawa et al., 2010).
- `mpcr`: Key efficiency parameter, explicitly modeled (multiple theory/empirical).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (structure and delivery): Central and directly analyzed (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012; Jaffe, 2004).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`: In theory models analyzing metanorms and institutional sanctions (Kendal et al., 2006; Lopez, 2017).

**Indirect or contextually discussed:**  
- `all_or_nothing`: Sometimes manipulated, some theory uses binary contribution (Deng et al., 2012).
- `chat` (communication): Addressed in sequential bargaining contexts, not directly in PGGs (Brosig et al., 2004).
- `show_n_rounds`: Sometimes modeled as part of repeated game structure (Kurokawa et al., 2010).
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Touched upon in experiments manipulating information exposure and identity revelation (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Gordon & Lea, 2016).

**Effectively missing or very sparse:**  
- `default_contrib` (framing of default decisions): Not addressed.
- `reward_magnitude`: Discussed only in models, not empirically.
- `punishment_magnitude`: Most papers state cost structure, but not in full detail for prediction purposes.
- UI-specific visibility controls (e.g., exactly what is shown each round): Rarely specified in prediction-relevant detail.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited direct empirical comparisons**: There are few studies providing head-to-head efficiency contrasts between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions within otherwise identical game designs.
- **Heterogeneity in punishment mechanisms**: Findings about efficiency are strongly conditional; changes to the punishment structure (number of stages, anonymity, ID visibility) can reverse the direction of the effect, and these dimensions are not standardized across studies.
- **Non-payoff proxies dominate**: Much of the literature uses behavioral proxies (contribution rate, compliance, intentions) instead of direct efficiency outcomes, which may mislead predictions if cost structures are not modeled.
- **Sparse coverage of several design dimensions**: Aspects like default contribution framing, chat, reward magnitude/cost, and fine-grained UI details are infrequently investigated.
- **Theory predominates in key areas**: Several of the most relevant findings for efficiency come from stylized theoretical models, which may not fully capture motivational or institutional details present in lived or lab environments.
- **Generalizability across contexts is uncertain**: Some empirical work is tied to specific subject pools or environments (e.g., rich punishment environments, artificial societies, unreliable anonymity), limiting transferability.
- **Potential for antisocial or vengeful punishment is acknowledged but not well quantified**: While theory and empirical reports highlight its harm to efficiency, the prediction of when/how much antisocial punishment will occur remains underspecified for new game designs.
- **Reward and punishment together are not consistently examined**: The combined/interactive effects of reward and punishment mechanisms are better addressed in theory than in empirical measurement.

---

**Summary:**  
This literature base provides moderately strong theoretical and some empirical grounds for predicting that enabling peer punishment often increases efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments—*but only* when the punishment design limits destructive cycles, cost is reasonable, and antisocial punishment is rare. When prediction of efficiency must take into account detailed game design features, only some dimensions are well covered, and quantification becomes more speculative where the design departs from those directly tested or modeled. Interpret statistical predictions with caution where empirical evidence is patchy and theory assumptions may not hold.
