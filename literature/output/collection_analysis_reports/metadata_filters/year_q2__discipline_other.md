# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 29 papers, with a mix of empirical (primarily laboratory experiments, a few field and observational studies) and theory (analytical and simulation-based) works. A moderate number directly focus on standard or variant public goods games (PGGs), while a significant portion addresses adjacent game types (coordination games, common-pool resource dilemmas, repeated network sharing, etc.) or provides conceptual and evolutionary context. Empirical papers most often examine contribution and punishment mechanisms, with relatively fewer reporting actual payoff- or efficiency-based outcomes. Theory papers generally propose mechanisms whereby punishment should promote cooperation and, by implication, higher group efficiency. Overall, the evidence base is broad in theory and behavioral mechanisms but is much narrower for empirical, directly relevant payoff outcomes required for the specific prediction task of treatment efficiency given design and control efficiency.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**

- **Exact**: Several papers are anchored in linear or threshold public goods games, with game designs closely matching the target scenario (e.g., Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014; Bell et al., 2016).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Many theory papers consider PGG-analogous settings (coordination games, gift-giving, sharing in networks, etc.). These often share structural features but may differ in implementation, e.g., binary/all-or-nothing decisions or explicit network topology (e.g., Zhang & van der Schaar, 2013; O’Connor, 2016; Xu et al., 2014; Vasconcelos et al., 2013).
- **Weak/None**: Some sources address cooperation in real-world or evolutionary contexts with only indirect links to PGGs.

**punishment_or_sanctions**

- **Exact**: A substantial subset centers on the effect of punishment or sanctioning institutions—often via experimental manipulation.
- **Close/Adjacent**: Others explore reputation-based, centralized, reward-based, or exclusionary mechanisms, or use models with punishment-like constructs (e.g., meta-punishment, conditional access).
- **Weak/None**: A few papers make only passing reference to punishment as a background for interpreting cooperative outcomes.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**

- **Exact**: Few papers directly report efficiency (group payoff relative to the optimum) as a primary outcome (e.g., Vlerick, 2016; Zhang & van der Schaar, 2013; Xu et al., 2014; Vanderschraaf, 2016).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Some report group payoff, profit, or earnings—suitable proxies for efficiency but not always normalized relative to the optimum (e.g., Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014).
- **Weak or None**: The majority focus on cooperation/contribution rates or punishment frequencies, not reporting efficiency or only discussing the mechanisms.

**Summary:** Overall, the evidence base has **moderate task relevance** for PGGs and punishment, but direct empirical evidence on efficiency or group payoff as defined for the prediction task is sparse. Many findings are indirect or mechanism-based, rather than direct quantitative estimates for efficiency with punishment enabled.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes:**
- **Efficiency** (group payoff/optimal payoff): Rarely the primary metric; usually must be inferred.
- **Group payoff/welfare/surplus/total coins**: Sometimes reported, often as mean earnings per participant or per group (Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014).
- **Proxies**: In threshold games, 'group achievement' (e.g., success in avoiding catastrophe) is sometimes used as a payoff proxy.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**: The most commonly reported, both at group and individual level.
- **Punishment acts and assignments**: Frequency/intensity of punishment administered or received.
- **Norm compliance, honesty, or exclusion rates**: Behavioral measures tied to underlying model mechanisms rather than outcomes.
- **Market entry or normative violations**: Used in adjacent paradigms (e.g., trading experiments).

**Explicitly Not Efficiency:**
- Papers often clarify that increases in contribution or cooperation rates do not always map directly to increased efficiency, especially if punishment is costly or cycles of retaliation occur.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Synthesis:**
- **Enabling punishment in repeated public goods games reliably increases cooperation rates and, in most settings, results in higher group payoff or efficiency relative to no-punishment baselines, particularly when punishment is credible, sufficiently severe, and experienced directly (Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014).**
- **The positive effect on efficiency is often mediated by stabilization of contributions over time and reduction in decay, not merely by short-term spikes in cooperation.**
- **Punishment cost, severity, and structure are critical—punishment that is too mild/slight has limited or no effect on efficiency, while severe or class-action style sanctions produce stronger, more persistent improvements (Eisenberg & Engel, 2014).**
- **Centralized or 'third-party' punishment is suggested (theoretically) to yield more stable efficiency gains than decentralized peer punishment, though this is context-dependent (Janssen, 2015).**
- **Actual group payoff increases can be blunted if costly punishment leads to retaliatory cycles or excessive expenditure on punishment rather than contribution (Cushman, 2015; Bruner, 2013).**

**Theoretical Synthesis:**
- **Game-theoretic and evolutionary models nearly universally predict that punishment—when credible and not overly burdensome—transforms the incentive structure, creating new equilibria with higher group welfare (Vlerick, 2016; Vanderschraaf, 2016; Zhang & van der Schaar, 2013; Xu et al., 2014; O’Connor, 2016).**
- **Extensions to reputation-based systems show that efficient protocols (balancing punishment probability and strength) can yield full or nearly full efficiency, but the effect can be reversed if punishment is too weak or too strong (Zhang & van der Schaar, 2013; Xu et al., 2014).**
- **Structure and context—such as group size, connectivity, communication, and monitoring—moderate the benefit of punishment for group efficiency (Janssen, 2015; Vasconcelos et al., 2013).**

**Caveats and Contradictions:**
- **Increases in cooperation due to punishment do not always translate to increased efficiency—costs of punishment can outweigh cooperation gains in some cases.**
- **Harsh/overzealous sanctioning or poorly tuned protocols (too strict access, excessive punishment) may reduce overall welfare (Angourakis et al., 2015).**
- **Some models suggest that punishment must be individually rational for punishers (i.e., incentivized/rewarded) to be sustainable and effective; otherwise, group efficiency does not rise (Bruner, 2013).**
- **Retaliatory or antisocial punishment introduces risk of reduced efficiency, though these dynamics are not quantified in most papers reviewed.**

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the above, the literature most directly supports the following guidance for predicting the average efficiency of a PGG-like game with punishment enabled (given design dimensions and control efficiency):

- **Direction of effect:** Models and most empirical evidence strongly indicate that enabling well-calibrated punishment—especially peer punishment that is not excessively costly—will increase average group efficiency above that of the same game with punishment disabled, *all else equal.*
- **Magnitude of effect:** The size of the efficiency gain depends critically on the specifics of the punishment regime (severity/magnitude, cost, likelihood of imposition), baseline (control) efficiency, group size, and information structure. Severe, likely, and targeted sanctions yield higher increases, with the effect plateauing or possibly declining if punishment is too costly or leads to counterproductive dynamics.
- **Moderators:** Direct evidence flags **punishment cost, punishment magnitude/technology, player count, number of rounds, MPCR, and information visibility** as important. Effects are stronger in smaller groups, higher-MPCR settings, repeated games, and when punishers are incentivized or have non-personal motives.
- **Indirect evidence** supports the positive effect for reputation-based and exclusion-based sanctions and with communication, but cautions that effects may be weaker in large, less transparent, or highly connected groups.
- **Control efficiency** should be expected to moderate the treatment effect: games with higher baseline cooperation/efficiency may see smaller absolute gains; in low-efficiency baselines, punishment enables larger improvements.
- **Note**: If the empirical outcome reports are in terms of behavioral contributions or cooperation rather than efficiency, predicted efficiency gains should be made cautiously, as high contributions can be offset by (costly) punishment expenditures.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Frequently specified and analyzed. Effects on efficiency are often moderated by these.
- **all_or_nothing**: Appears in models and some lab experiments.
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds**: Sometimes specified, with evidence of importance for monitoring and cooperation stability.
- **centralized vs. peer punishment (implied in punishment_tech)**: Centralization discussed as a key moderator.

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**
- **chat/communication**: Not always manipulated, but identified as a strong moderator (Janssen, 2015).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Sometimes discussed (rarely systematically manipulated), mainly in the context of incentive alignment and alternative sanctioning (Bruner, 2013; Montoya et al., 2015).
- **default_contrib**: Rarely manipulated directly; some mention regarding opt-in/opt-out framing (Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017).
- **show_punishment_id (identity disclosure)**: Mentioned in evolutionary or reputation contexts; sparse in direct experimental evidence.

**Effectively Missing:**
- **reward_magnitude**: Not described in sufficient detail in this set.
- **Full parameterizations on chat, reward options, and information structure are generally incomplete, limiting fine-grained prediction by these variables.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse direct evidence on efficiency as defined for this prediction task.** Most empirical studies report behavioral or non-normalized payoff outcomes, requiring inference for efficiency estimates.
- **Few studies provide complete factorial variation across all design dimensions; inferences across dimensions such as reward, chat, and default contributions are often contextual, not empirical.**
- **Magnitude of predicted efficiency boost is not well quantified, especially for large groups, highly asymmetrical MPCRs, or rare design features (e.g., opt-out contributions, hidden identities).**
- **Retaliatory/antisocial punishment, and cycles of punishment, are noted risks, but their quantitative impacts on efficiency are not captured systematically.**
- **Limited real-world or field experimental evidence reduces external validity for diverse application contexts.**
- **Much theoretical evidence is qualitative/mechanism-based, lacking empirical validation for specific parameter regimes.**
- **Ambiguity remains regarding negative side-effects of punishment (e.g., over-punishment, negative affect, exclusion of unlucky contributors), highlighted in model-based and ethnographic theory but not empirically resolved.**

---

**In summary:**  
The literature robustly supports the prediction that, for standard PGGs with repeated interaction and sufficient punishment severity and opportunity, enabling punishment will increase average group efficiency compared to a control game, with the effect moderated by player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost and intensity, and available information. However, quantitative prediction is hampered by limited direct efficiency data, incomplete factorial coverage, and insufficient attention to negative side-effects in most studies. Predictions for efficiency should be made based on these robust directions but with substantial uncertainty about magnitude when moving outside well-studied parameter regions.
