# Evidence Base

The paper set is broad and diverse, comprising both empirical (mostly lab-experimental) and theoretical studies. Crucially, a substantial subset (about 15–20) are **directly empirical lab experimental studies of public goods games (PGGs) with punishment**, reporting efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes. Several high-quality theory papers (e.g., Gintis, Bednar, Prétôt et al., Antoci & Zarri, Corriveau, Camera & Gioffré) offer mechanistic predictions or equilibrium estimations for efficiency impacts of punishment in PGGs and close variants. The remainder includes weaker or adjacent evidence from games with related structure (common-pool resource games, trust/ultimatum games, dictator games, or coordination games), papers reporting only behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates), and studies focusing on broader institutional, motivational, or cultural aspects.

**Empirical coverage** is strong for small-group, repeated, linear PGGs with standard peer punishment. There is also some direct coverage of centralized/democratic punishment, third-party punishment, weakest-link (complementary) production functions, and institutional punishment funded from group resources. Several papers add evidence about communication (chat), player count, number of rounds, information conditions, and the comparative value of reward. Theory papers address heterogeneity, group size, monitoring technology, and the equilibrium selection mechanisms that shape efficiency outcomes under different sanctioning structures. There is moderate coverage of costless versus costly punishment, institutional versus peer enforcement, and network effects.

However, **evidence is sparser or only indirectly available for**: complex or nonlinear payoff structures, large-N settings, endogenous institution formation, subtle game design features (e.g., opt-in contribution defaults, nuanced information display), and interaction effects among multiple design dimensions. Studies focusing directly on real-world environments, large groups, or field settings are rare and generally do not report directly on efficiency. Many adjacent studies only infer effects on efficiency via behavioral proxies.

# Task Relevance

Assessing across three target-relevance dimensions, the paper set features:

- **pgg_or_variant:**  
  - **exact**: A substantial number of papers (roughly 15–20) are experiments or theory on public goods games or immediate, structure-preserving variants (linear, binary, or weakest-link provision, with or without centralized enforcement).  
  - **close**: Dynamic CPR games, trust games with group interaction, and common-pool resource management models provide adjacent but non-identical settings.  
  - **adjacent/weak**: Trust games, dictator games, and some observational studies are included but less relevant.

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**  
  - **exact**: Most directly relevant papers manipulate the presence/parameters of costly peer or centralized punishment. Many explore additional treatments (third-party enforcement, counterpunishment, reward).
  - **close/adjacent**: Reputation feedback, social ostracism, and indirect sanctions often appear, but are less frequently directly compared with costly, explicit punishment.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
  - **exact/close**: About half the directly relevant PGG/punishment studies measure group efficiency, average earnings, surplus, or welfare. Some theory papers offer predictions on equilibrium efficiency.  
  - **adjacent/weak**: Numerous studies report only contribution rates or punishment frequencies, using these as proxies for efficiency, which limits their direct applicability.

In summary, **the literature is strong and directly relevant for the prediction task for standard PGGs with canonical punishment manipulations and when efficiency/group payoff is the outcome of interest**. Relevance is lower for adjacent game types, for studies without direct efficiency data, and for unexplored or subtle design features.

# Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (directly relevant):**
  - **Efficiency (group payoff relative to maximum, total earnings, welfare, surplus):** Many empirical and theoretical studies report this outcome, either directly or via well-specified mappings from contributions to group earnings.
  - **Total coins generated, group profit, resource sustainability:** Some adjacent studies in dynamic environments or CPRs.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (secondary relevance):**
  - **Contribution/cooperation rates, norm compliance, punishment frequency, willingness to punish/reward, trust, satisfaction:** Frequently reported, especially in adjacency or when group payoff is not the focal measure.
  - **Punishment assigned (targeting, proportion, severity), antisocial punishment, fairness perceptions.**
  - **Emotional and psychological measures (anger, satisfaction, norm perceptions).**  

**It is critical to distinguish:**
- **Efficiency (our primary prediction target)** from cooperation rates and punishment behavior. Many studies show that punishment increases cooperation rates, but owing to the costs of punishment and the presence of antisocial punishment or noise, the *net effect on efficiency can be positive, negative, or null* depending on the design.

# Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## General Lessons Synthesized Across Papers

- **Enabling costly peer punishment in repeated standard PGGs typically increases efficiency relative to control, but only under certain game parameter regimes (e.g., punishment is not too cheap, not too expensive, and antisocial punishment is rare)** (Gintis, 2003; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Sparks et al., 2024).
- **The structure and legitimacy of punishment institutions are key moderators:** Centralized/designed, democratic, or third-party punishment often outperforms standard peer punishment in terms of efficiency, mainly by targeting defectors more selectively and reducing wasteful or antisocial punishment (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Hou et al., 2019; Fischer et al., 2016).
- **Punishment can backfire or fail to increase efficiency** when (a) costless or extremely cheap punishment is allowed (leads to excessive sanctioning), (b) antisocial punishment is culturally prevalent or incentivized, or (c) centralized punishment power is unequally or unstably distributed (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Dorrough et al., 2017).
- **Game structure matters:** Linear (VCM) and weakest-link/complementary provision functions yield very different responses to punishment; efficiency gains from punishment are far greater in “all or nothing”/weakest-link settings where the effect of one individual’s low effort is magnified (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
- **Information environment is crucial:** The more informative and transparent the setting (contributions and payoffs visible), the greater the efficacy of punishment for increasing efficiency (Fischer et al., 2016; Bednar, 2006; Camera & Gioffré, 2014).
- **Communication (chat, normative messages) interacts strongly with punishment:** Communication alone can sometimes create greater efficiency gains than punishment alone, and combining punishment with communication may not further increase efficiency (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
- **Punishment cost/impact ratio is a strong moderator:** There is robust evidence that some punishment cost is necessary for efficiency (too-cheap punishment creates inefficiency through overuse, too-costly punishment can make punishment unused or ineffective) (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Nikias & Sy, 2021).
- **Punishment effectiveness (punishment_tech), network structure, and monitoring technology also shape results**, though direct empirical evidence for these dimensions is moderate to sparse and more often addressed in modeling work (Bednar, 2006; Prétôt et al., 2024; Camera & Gioffré, 2014).
- **Reward-only or combined reward/punishment mechanisms**: Reward alone is less effective than punishment alone for increasing or sustaining efficiency; combining reward and punishment can provide marginal additional gains (Hou et al., 2019).

# Prediction Guidance

## How Should the Literature Inform Prediction of Treatment Efficiency?

**Key guidelines and caveats:**

- **If prediction is for a repeated, small-group, linear PGG with standard peer punishment and moderate cost/impact ratio, expect that enabling costly punishment typically increases efficiency over control—so long as antisocial punishment is not pervasive and punishment is not costless.** This is robust across diverse contexts (Sparks et al., 2024; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Gintis, 2003).
- **If control efficiency is low (i.e., strong free riding in the absence of punishment), enabling costly punishment is more likely to produce a large efficiency gain** (Gintis, 2003; Hou et al., 2019; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
- **The marginal efficiency effect of punishment depends strongly on the type of punishment institution:** Centralized, democratic, or third-party punishment mechanisms improve efficiency more than indiscriminate peer punishment, especially when punishment cost is not excessive (Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Fischer et al., 2016).
- **Punishment produces weaker or negative effects on efficiency when:**
  - **Punishment is costless or too easy:** Produces inefficient, excessive punishment cycles (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017).
  - **Antisocial punishment is common or incentivized:** No net gain, possible decrease in efficiency (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
  - **Centralization is combined with unstable or unequal assignment:** Destroys efficiency (Dorrough et al., 2017).
  - **Payoff structure is nonlinear or complex (CPRs):** Peer punishment less effective; communication may outperform (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
  - **Monitoring is too costly or noisy:** Punishment cannot raise efficiency due to errors and high cost (Bednar, 2006; Nakao, 2009).
- **Communication availability is a strong moderator:** If chat or messaging is allowed, efficiency gains from punishment are frequently smaller or redundant; in some cases, chat yields the largest effect (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
- **Duration (number of rounds) matters:** Efficiency gains from punishment often emerge only over time as initial costs are recouped through higher contributions (Sparks et al., 2024; Bednar, 2006). In short games, net efficiency may be lower due to initial punishment costs.
- **Institutional punishment funded from group resources (not individual “second-order free riders”) is predicted to maximize efficiency if investment exceeds a threshold and monitoring/punishment are effective** (Prétôt et al., 2024; Bednar, 2006).
- **Reward-only interventions do not substitute for punishment:** They may support cooperation but are less robust for sustaining efficiency (Hou et al., 2019; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).
- **Where only behavioral data (e.g., increased contribution) are available, translation to efficiency must be cautious if punishment costs are high or antisocial punishment exists.**

**In summary**:  
*Prediction of average efficiency in the punishment-enabled version should be conditioned by control efficiency and should strongly account for:*
  - Punishment institution type (peer, central, democratic, third-party)
  - Punishment cost and technology (cost/impact ratio)
  - Production function shape (linear, weakest-link, nonlinear)
  - Communication/channel availability
  - Player count and number of rounds for capturing long-run effects
  - Information visibility (contributions, payoffs)
  - Evidence of antisocial punishment propensity (culture, context)

# Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

## Directly Informed Dimensions:
- **player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: These are among the most systematically manipulated and analyzed variables in the literature, both in empirical tests and in structural modeling (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Bednar, 2006; Nikias & Sy, 2021).
- **punishment_exists (on/off toggle):** The central manipulation in the bulk of relevant PGG lab studies.
- **chat (communication)**: Directly included in several key studies; shown to strongly moderate punishment’s effect (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
- **all_or_nothing**: Precisely tested in Fatas & Mateu (2015) and some theoretical work; modifies sensitivity of efficiency to punishment.

## Indirectly or Occasionally Informed:
- **default_contrib:** Rarely a primary focus, though conceptually relevant in differentiating opt-in/opt-out schemes.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Addressed in only a few empirical papers; reward generally less effective than punishment for efficiency.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Information structures (who sees what, how transparent is the game, fixed IDs) are sometimes manipulated and shown to matter (Fischer et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016), but less systematically explored across the field.

## Missing or Only Contextually Discussed:
- **Subtle design and interface features (e.g., real-world scale, endogenous institution formation, sophisticated reward/punishment structures, dynamics in large groups):** Generally under-represented.
- **fine gradations of network topology, stochastic matching:** Sometimes theorized, rarely tested directly in lab settings.

# Important Limitations

- **Overrepresentation of small, short, stylized lab games:** Most empirical evidence comes from small group sizes (3–6), short horizon (< 40 rounds), under controlled laboratory conditions. Generalizability to larger or real-world settings is uncertain.
- **Variable mapping from behavioral to efficiency outcomes:** Many papers report only cooperation/contribution rates. The effect of punishment on efficiency is not always positive, and cost of sanctioning is a critical moderator. Translation from behavior to efficiency is sometimes assumed, not measured.
- **Limited attention to complex production functions:** Most studies use linear provision; results may not generalize to nonlinear, threshold, or dynamic resource environments (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
- **Cultural and contextual moderation is real but poorly predictive:** Effects of antisocial punishment, legitimacy, or social norms show strong cross-cultural variation (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Dorrough et al., 2017), but these are hard to parameterize for prediction without direct control group data.
- **Heavily contextual moderators often lack direct parameterization:** E.g., chat quality, legitimacy perceptions, subtle information cues; these may be key in naturalistic settings but are only coarsely or sporadically measured in most studies.
- **Conflicting findings and mechanism ambiguity:** Some studies suggest punishment reliably increases efficiency; others show it can reduce efficiency or even destabilize cooperation (especially with costless/ineffective punishment, or when institutions are unstable).
- **Sparse direct evidence on large N, long horizon, field or online settings:** Laboratory effects may overstate the efficiency gains from punishment under more complex, diffuse, or anonymous conditions.
- **Missing “second-order” effects:** Theory predicts second-order free-riding can undermine efficient enforcement, but empirical evidence is limited (Prétôt et al., 2024).
- **Reward and information display dimensions underexplored:** Far fewer studies directly compare reward/punishment combination regimes or the impact of nuanced feedback features.

# Final Takeaways

- **The literature is highly informative for prediction of efficiency outcomes under standard small-group, linear public goods games, especially when the key design dimensions are specified and control efficiency is known.**
- **Enabling costly, well-designed, and legitimate punishment opportunities generally increases efficiency, but effects can be null or negative in the presence of costless punishment, antisocial punishment, poor information, or perverse incentives.**
- **Prediction should incorporate moderators including institution type, punishment cost/tech, communication, information, and the production function.**
- **Real-world or complex games may not see the same efficiency gains as well-controlled lab PGGs; extrapolation to large, heterogeneous, or less transparent groups should be made with caution.**
