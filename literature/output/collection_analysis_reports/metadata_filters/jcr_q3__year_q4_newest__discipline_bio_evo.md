# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set comprises a mix of empirical and theoretical research, with an emphasis on evolutionary modeling and experimental economic game studies. Of the 18 papers, three are empirical laboratory experiments (including one field-informed study), while the remaining majority are theoretical models and simulations. The literature is generally broad in its theoretical treatment of collective action and social dilemmas but narrows substantially when focusing on experimental, efficiency-outcome public goods game (PGG) treatments with direct manipulation of peer punishment. Only a handful of the papers tightly match the design and outcome requirements of the downstream prediction task.

Most of the theoretical contributions focus on evolutionary mechanisms (e.g., kin structure, multilevel selection, institutional incentives), with varying degrees of abstraction from the canonical PGG. The empirical studies that do target PGGs with peer punishment are vital for understanding realized efficiency effects under specific design conditions. However, many papers offer only indirect evidence for efficiency impacts, reporting behavioral outcomes or model properties instead of group payoff measures.

# 2) Task Relevance

The relevance of this literature set for prediction of efficiency changes due to peer punishment in PGG-like environments can be assessed separately across three target criteria:

- **pgg_or_variant:**  
  - *Exact*: A core subset of papers (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Espín et al., 2022; Duong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Kristensen et al., 2025) study or model standard public goods games or very close variants.  
  - *Close*: Some theory papers (e.g., Kurokawa, 2023; Cooney, 2025; Fontanari & Santos, 2024) model games with payoff structures similar to PGGs but introduce modifications (e.g., opting out, others-only benefits, group selection).  
  - *Adjacent/Weak*: Several others (e.g., Murase, 2025; Kroumi, 2025; Watson et al., 2024) focus on adjacent dilemmas (indirect reciprocity, spatial games, prisoner's dilemma, etc.).

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**  
  - *Exact*: Some papers directly investigate peer punishment as a design feature (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Espín et al., 2022; Cooney, 2025; Li et al., 2024; Murase, 2025).  
  - *Close*: Others address institutional or prosocial/antisocial exclusion or closely related forms of sanctioning.  
  - *Adjacent/None*: Several focus instead on reward, environmental 'punishments', kin structure, or do not manipulate punishment mechanisms directly (e.g., Fontanari & Santos, 2024; Garfield et al., 2023).

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
  - *Exact*: Only a small number of studies (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Duong et al., 2024; Cooney, 2025; Kristensen et al., 2025) use efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as a direct outcome.
  - *Adjacent*: Many report only on behavioral outcomes such as cooperation or contribution rates, punishment frequency, or norm compliance (e.g., Espín et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024; Kido & Takezawa, 2024).
  - *None*: Several do not report payoff outcomes at all (e.g., Garfield et al., 2023).

In summary, the set contains some highly relevant empirical and theoretical work (especially Sparks et al., 2024; Cooney, 2025; Duong et al., 2024), but much of the literature provides only indirect or adjacent support for the specific prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes** (directly relevant for prediction):
- **Group earnings / efficiency**: Directly measured in Sparks et al. (2024), Cooney (2025), Kristensen et al. (2025), Kurokawa (2023), Duong et al. (2024), Murase (2025), Fontanari & Santos (2024), Kroumi (2025).
- **Institutional cost to promote cooperation** (Duong et al., 2024): Closely related to group-level efficiency.
- **Long-run average payoff, welfare, or surplus**: Explicitly modeled in several theory papers, sometimes as steady-state outcomes.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (informativity is indirect for the prediction task):
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**: Common in theoretical modeling papers (e.g., Kido & Takezawa, 2024; Li et al., 2024; Lu & Wang, 2024; Pandula et al., 2024).
- **Punishment assignment rates and patterns**: Focus in Espín et al. (2022), Li et al. (2024), Bruner & Smead (2022).
- **Norm compliance, strategy frequencies, evolutionary stability**: Frequent in evolutionary models.

Several empirical papers do not report efficiency or payoff at all, focusing instead on social or behavioral constructs. The clearest separation is observed in studies where the main outcomes are group earnings or average payoffs (directly relevant), versus those that report only behavioral rates (indirectly relevant).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### Empirical Evidence

- **Enabling costly peer punishment generally increases group efficiency in repeated PGGs, particularly over longer time horizons.**  
  Laboratory experiments with repeated PGGs (Sparks et al., 2024) show that costly punishment, even though it initially reduces group earnings due to the cost of punishing, leads to the stabilization of cooperation and an eventual increase in group efficiency compared to control games without punishment. Non-costly, non-material disapproval fails to sustain efficiency over time.

- **Cultural, gender, and group-compositional moderators affect who punishes whom but do not consistently alter contribution rates or efficiency.**  
  In one-shot PGGs, the presence and target of punishment varies with social composition, though this does not always translate to overall changes in group payoff (Espín et al., 2022). However, efficiency effects are not directly reported.

### Theoretical Modeling

- **Punishment’s effect on efficiency depends on cost structure, group size, and institutional context.**  
  Models show non-monotonic effects: while increasing the strength of punishment can promote cooperation, it may reduce group efficiency if punishment is too costly (Cooney, 2025; Duong et al., 2024). Efficiency gains from punishment are maximized when costs are low to moderate and group sizes are small to moderate.

- **Evolutionary models highlight the importance of population structure and game design.**  
  Factors such as kin homophily, selective extinction (group-level sanctions), and nonlinearity in returns to cooperation shape whether punishment will be efficiency-enhancing (Kristensen et al., 2025; Kroumi, 2025; Fontanari & Santos, 2024).

- **Punishment is efficiency-promoting under conditions of limited detectability of defection.**  
  In models of indirect reciprocity, punishment becomes dramatically more efficient (increasing payoff) when defection is hard to detect, but can be unnecessary or harmful when detection is easy or punishment is too costly (Murase, 2025).

### Negative or Mixed Effects

- **Punishment/spite can decrease efficiency, especially in large groups or when costs are high relative to impact.**  
  When punishment becomes spiteful or is implemented in large groups with standard cost structures, models show either neutral or negative effects on efficiency (Kurokawa, 2023), unless the cost-to-harm ratio is exceptionally favorable.

### Indirect or Adjacent Evidence

- Most theoretical and empirical work on cooperation rates, norm diffusion, exclusion, or ostracism supports the view that sanctions (including peer punishment) enable higher cooperation, but their effect on efficiency is only inferred, not demonstrated with payoff outcomes.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **If the baseline (control) efficiency is low due to defection, enabling peer punishment at reasonable cost is likely to increase group efficiency over time, particularly in repeated PGGs with small to moderate player counts and sufficient rounds (Sparks et al., 2024; Cooney, 2025; Murase, 2025).**  
- **The efficiency gain is less certain or may be negative if:**
    - Punishment costs are high relative to magnitude (Kurokawa, 2023; Cooney, 2025).
    - Group size is large (Kurokawa, 2023).
    - Most defection can be directly and cheaply detected/countered without punishment (Murase, 2025).
- **Cultural, network, and group-structural moderators may affect the distribution of punishment and its targets, but are less clearly related to changes in average efficiency (Espín et al., 2022).**
- **Where direct evidence is missing, use behavioral outcomes (contribution rates) as an upper bound for the expected impact on efficiency, bearing in mind that unless costs for administering punishment are small, the efficiency increase will be offset by those costs.**
- **If the control game already yields near-maximum efficiency, adding punishment likely provides little or even negative efficiency impact due to added costs (Sparks et al., 2024; Kurokawa, 2023).**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Multiple studies and models explore its effects, showing efficiency gains from punishment are greatest in small groups and can decline in larger groups due to diffusion of responsibility and elevated punishment costs (Sparks et al., 2024; Cooney, 2025; Kurokawa, 2023).
- `num_rounds`: Especially well-informed in repeated experiments/theory; long games allow for punishment to have lasting efficiency effects (Sparks et al., 2024).
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`: Central in both empirical and theoretical work; efficiency effects are highly sensitive to these parameters.
- `mpcr`: Explicit in lab experiments and theory for the marginal return of contributions (Sparks et al., 2024; Kristensen et al., 2025).

**Indirectly Informed Dimensions:**
- `all_or_nothing`, `reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech`: Sometimes included in models (Duong et al., 2024), less central for pure punishment predictions but inform potential interactions.
- `chat`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: These are either contextually mentioned (e.g., chat: Sparks et al., 2024, but held constant) or discussed in terms of their absence.

**Effectively Missing or Only Contextually Discussed:**
- `default_contrib`: Framing effects are generally not reported in efficiency outcome studies.
- Behavioral visibility parameters (e.g., `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`) are sometimes part of the game environment but not systematically varied for efficiency outcomes.
- `reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech`: Only minimally treated in theoretical incentive scheme comparisons.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited direct empirical evidence linking peer punishment to efficiency**: Only a few laboratory studies (notably Sparks et al., 2024) report efficiency or group payoff under experimentally manipulated punishment. The rest mainly offer theoretical support or focus on adjacent behavioral outcomes.
- **Behavioral and efficiency effects are not always aligned**: Many studies equate increased cooperation or contribution rates with increased efficiency, but, as group punishment is costly, such increases do not guarantee higher group payoff or welfare (Kurokawa, 2023; Cooney, 2025).
- **Generalizability across design variants is uncertain**: Most findings are drawn from canonical repeated PGGs with specific player counts, MPCRs, and punishment structures. Extrapolation to other parameter regimes (e.g., variable punishment tech/cost, group size, or communication settings) may not hold.
- **Sparse treatment of complex institutional or technological variations**: Most studies hold secondary design features constant (e.g., chat/communication, show_punishment_id, default_contrib) or do not report their influence on efficiency.
- **Cultural, network, and demographic moderators are incompletely mapped**: While some papers (Espín et al., 2022; Kristensen et al., 2025) explore the effects of cultural and group structure, these are typically not linked directly to efficiency impacts.
- **Potential publication bias towards pro-social effects**: Some theoretical models and experimental designs focus primarily on positive or expected punishment effects, potentially limiting insight into parameter ranges with negative or null efficiency effects.
- **Lack of disaggregated data for nuanced prediction**: Most studies do not provide phase diagrams or outcome surfaces across all interaction terms for the 14 design dimensions, which would be necessary for high-precision quantitative prediction.

In summary, while the literature set provides strong empirical and theoretical support that costly peer punishment can increase efficiency in public goods game environments under many—but not all—design conditions, direct evidence on treatment efficiency as a function of all 14 dimensions is sparse. Prediction should be cautious, relying heavily on the few direct efficiency studies and acknowledging the uncertainty and design sensitivity highlighted in theoretical work.
