# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 209 sources, with a strong empirical base in laboratory experiments, supplemented by theoretical modeling, simulation analyses, and observational studies. The core evidence includes a substantial number of standard public goods games (PGGs) and their close variants, alongside a wide array of adjacent paradigms (e.g., trust games, CPRs, coordination games, ultimatum/dictator games). The central focus of the literature is on the impact of punishment (and occasionally reward) mechanisms on cooperation and, crucially, on payoff-related outcomes.

A substantial portion of the empirical studies report direct measures of group efficiency, surplus, or total earnings—though behavioral outcomes such as contribution rates and punishment frequency are even more common. Theoretical works provide mechanistic and equilibrium insights, often highlighting conditions for cooperation or efficiency but sometimes lacking real-world nuance or empirical calibration.

The evidence base is relatively broad and includes differing implementations of punishment (peer, institutional, third-party), various group sizes and horizons, and diversity in cultural and contextual moderators. However, quantitative, dimension-sensitive evidence on efficiency effects of punishment is densest for standard linear PGGs, while more complex or naturalistic designs, variants (e.g., minimum effort games, CPRs, trust games), or highly institutionalized/field contexts are less consistently represented. Some design features (e.g., anonymous vs. transparent monitoring, presence of communication, type of punishment) are much better covered than others (e.g., framing defaults, nuanced feedback features).

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance:** The majority of high-quality empirical and theoretical papers focus on standard PGGs or close canonical variants (linear PGGs, VCMs). There is robust coverage of the exact structure at the heart of the prediction task.
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** A significant subset of studies uses games adjacent to PGGs (e.g., threshold PGGs, common-pool resources, minimum effort games, trust/ultimatum games), which provide important contextual or mechanistic insight but require careful mapping to prediction in standard PGGs.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Peer punishment, costly punishment, and formal/institutional sanctions are extensively studied; effects of punishment-enabling interventions and their technology (cost, target, effectiveness) are directly addressed in many papers.
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Some studies analyze reward or hybrid mechanisms, sanctions embedded in institutional or cultural frames, or less conventional punishment forms (gossip, ostracism, exclusion).
- **Weak/none:** Some reviews or theoretical syntheses discuss punishment mechanisms in abstract or as one part among many, offering less precise evidence.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcomes:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Notably, a dozen or more high-quality experimental and theoretical studies report direct measures of group efficiency, profit, welfare, or surplus—enabling robust mapping to the prediction task.
- **Close/adjacent/weak relevance:** Many more papers focus on cooperation or contribution behavior as a proxy, requiring careful translation to efficiency. Some only discuss predicted efficiency effects or report behavioral results without corresponding payoff data.
- **None:** A portion of the literature is purely attitudinal, psychological, or mechanism-focused, offering no direct efficiency outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (Most relevant for prediction):**
  - **Direct measures:** Efficiency (total group earnings/payoff divided by maximum possible), group surplus, welfare, total coins/money generated.
    - These are given in numerous lab experiments (e.g., Zhang et al., 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2023), meta-analyses, and key theory papers.
  - **Indirect/proxy measures:** Sometimes group efficiency must be inferred from average individual profit or from a combination of contribution and punishment cost data.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (Important but must be distinguished):**
  - **Contribution rates / cooperation levels:** Commonly used, often reported in parallel with payoffs but not always convertible to efficiency (e.g., if the cost of punishment is large).
  - **Frequency/amount of punishment assigned, norm compliance, exclusion rates, exit, or reputational sanctioning:** Useful for mechanism, but only indirectly linked to efficiency.
  - **Attitudes, motives, psychological/emotional responses, social norm perceptions:** Covered extensively in adjacent literature but not directly predictive of payoff outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### What is clear:
- **Enabling (costly) punishment in repeated, standard lab PGGs generally increases group efficiency relative to no-punishment control, provided punishment is not excessively frequent or antisocial.**  
  - This is robustly supported by direct payoff measures in multiple studies (Zhang et al., 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Sparks et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2019; Duell et al., 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025).
- **The size of the efficiency gain is moderated by**:
  - **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** Low-cost, high-impact punishment yields larger efficiency improvements. If punishment is too costly or widely applied, gains can be completely offset or reversed (Botelho et al., 2022; Tanimoto, 2018; Kroupa, 2014; Eldakar et al., 2018).
  - **Type of punishment mechanism:** Centralized/institutional punishment (especially with commitment) is more likely to deliver efficient cooperation than peer punishment, unless peer punishment is well targeted and used sparingly (Duell et al., 2024; Prétôt et al., 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
  - **Transparency, monitoring, and observability:** Effectiveness is highest with individual-level feedback, public endowment information, and strong monitoring (Chen et al., 2025; Eisenkopf & Walter, 2022; Chen et al., 2025).
  - **Game length:** Efficiency gains may be realized only in longer repeated games, as initial losses due to punishment costs are offset over time (Sparks et al., 2024; Kroupa, 2014).
  - **Contextual features:** Cultural setting, group composition (prevalence of strong reciprocators/norm-keepers), presence of antisocial punishment, or setting-specific dynamics can moderate or reverse the effect (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Angelsen & Naime, 2024).

### Where evidence is mixed or context-dependent:
- **Punishment can sometimes reduce efficiency, especially when:**
  - Punishment is overused, poorly targeted, or antisocial (punishment of cooperators): leading to direct welfare loss (Botelho et al., 2022; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024; Botelho et al., 2022).
  - The cost of punishment is high relative to its impact (Botelho et al., 2022; Kroupa, 2014; Tanimoto, 2018; Rumble et al., 2022).
  - Control efficiency is already high (cooperation established without punishment), punishment can introduce unnecessary costs (Botelho et al., 2022).
  - The institutional or psychological context undermines the effectiveness of punishment (e.g., high power asymmetry, high cognitive load, low transparency; Eldakar et al., 2018; Ye et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025).
  - Special cases: In certain resource competition, threshold games, or specialized institutions, punishment has no or even negative effect on efficiency (Milinski & Marotzke, 2022; Goto & Matsui, 2025).

### Where payoff results are indirect:
- **Large part of the literature shows increased cooperation rates with punishment but does not measure or report efficiency.** Such results are only indirectly informative for payoff prediction and must be interpreted in light of potential punishment costs and other game parameters.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General recommendation from the literature:**  
- **When predicting the average efficiency of a public-good game with punishment enabled (vs. control), expect a positive treatment effect on efficiency, conditional on:**  
  - Medium- or low-cost, high-effectiveness punishment technology.
  - Peer punishment used selectively or institutionally organized punishment.
  - Control efficiency not already near the group maximum (ceilings limit the benefit).
  - Low rates of antisocial punishment.
  - Sufficient time horizon for punishment to deter free riding and for benefits to accrue.

- **When should you predict no increase or a decrease in efficiency?**
  - If the punishment cost is high and/or use is frequent.
  - If the environment is structurally prone to antisocial or misapplied punishment (high societal norm-keeper prevalence, closed or culturally specific settings with pre-existing high cooperation, or high cognitive load).
  - If the design induces excessive punishment of cooperators, or if punishment is accessible at too low cost (easy retaliation or escalation).
  - In short, one-shot games or conditions where repeated interaction/feedback effects are weak.

- **Best-informed predictors/dimensions in the literature for predicting treatment efficiency:**  
  - *player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, chat, show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id*: These are well covered in high-quality empirical and theoretical work.
  - *reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech*: Reward options are less common but effects are sometimes compared (Hou et al., 2019; Peng, 2022). Generally, punishment is more effective than reward for sustaining cooperation but not always more efficient.
  - *all_or_nothing, default_contrib*: These are less frequently manipulated but do appear in some key studies.

- **Special guidance:**  
  - *Cultural, group composition, and institution-specific moderators must be considered*, especially for predictions outside standard lab settings.
  - *Details of the punishment institution (centralized/peer, observability, commitment, selection procedures)* can alter the effect.
  - *Feedback and transparency* (who sees what, when) matter substantively.
  - *Control (baseline) efficiency* is necessary for calibrating the marginal gain or reduction with punishment—a high baseline leaves less room for improvement; a low baseline increases the expected marginal effect.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed dimensions (well supported empirically):**
  - `player_count` (group size): Extensively covered; smaller groups often more manageable, efficient punishment more effective.
  - `num_rounds`: Horizon repeatedly manipulated; longer games favor positive effect of punishment.
  - `mpcr`: (Marginal per capita return): Central driver of payoff structure; lower MPCR usually strengthens the relative impact of punishment.
  - `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`: Their role as primary moderators of both usage rates and net efficiency outcomes is firmly established; "efficient" (high-impact/low-cost) punishment is better for efficiency.
  - `chat`: Communication's effects are well documented—directly or indirectly via expectation management, though some variance.
  - `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`: Information structure is often discussed as a direct moderator.
  - `all_or_nothing`: Present in many discrete-choice and threshold games.
  - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Studied, though much less than punishment.

- **Indirectly informed or contextually discussed:**
  - `default_contrib`: Framing (opt-in vs. opt-out) is rarely the direct experimental variable but is sometimes incidentally reported or theorized.
  - `show_punishment_id`: The direct effect of anonymity in punishment is discussed, especially for antisocial punishment, but not always disentangled experimentally.

- **Effectively missing or only weakly covered:**
  - Many specifics of framing, subtle feedback mechanisms, or rarely used design parameters (e.g., default contribution) are underexplored or only contextually mentioned.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Gaps in direct, quantitative efficiency estimates for edge-case designs:**  
  - Some design dimensions and combinations (e.g., rare framing settings, complex group structures, certain hybrid punishment/reward systems, subtle feedback cues) are not well represented.
- **Over-reliance on standard lab PGG designs for strong conclusions:**  
  - Evidence is most confident for small/medium groups, moderate rounds, explicit linear returns, and a few common punishment technologies.
- **Many papers report only cooperation/contribution, not efficiency:**  
  - Behavioral results often need additional payoff data or assumptions for conversion to efficiency predictions; such inferences may be error-prone if punishment is costly.
- **Selection bias towards "successful" punishment:**  
  - Several empirical studies select parameter regimes where punishment is expected to function as designed. Effect heterogeneity may be underestimated.
- **Institutional, cultural, and psychological moderators often unexplored or uncontrolled:**  
  - Some results may not generalize outside of certain cultural, institutional, or laboratory contexts.
- **Payoff effects in adjacent or non-PGG designs require careful mapping:**  
  - Results from CPRs, threshold games, dyadic trust games, or coordination games must not be naively equated to PGG outcomes.
- **Sparse evidence on the interaction of rarely used design dimensions:**  
  - Framing defaults, nuanced feedback structure, or multi-layer information/trust mechanisms are seldom the focal variable.

---

**In summary**:  
- There is strong, directly relevant evidence that enabling efficient, well-targeted punishment in standard repeated PGGs increases group efficiency compared to the control baseline (no punishment), provided punishment costs are not excessive and misuse (especially antisocial punishment) is minimized.  
- The efficiency gain is contingent on key design dimensions: group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment technology, transparency, and communication.  
- A significant body of related literature shows that the efficiency gain can be zero or negative under high punishment costs, frequent or antisocial use, short horizons, or in contexts with strong baseline cooperation.  
- For the 14 game design dimensions in the prediction task, the literature provides solid empirical and theoretical support for most, though some less-common features remain under-explored.  
- Many studies report only non-payoff behavioral outcomes; these must not be subsumed under efficiency unless explicitly measured or carefully mapped.  
- Limitations include incomplete coverage of edge-case designs, institutional/cultural moderation, and occasional difficulty in mapping from behavioral to payoff outcomes.  
- Overall, the literature supports predictive modeling of treatment efficiency—as a function of design parameters and control efficiency—with due attention to cost–benefit calibration, contextual fit, and the precise structure of the punishment institution.
