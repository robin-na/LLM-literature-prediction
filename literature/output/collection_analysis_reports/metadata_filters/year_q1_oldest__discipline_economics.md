# 1) Evidence Base

**Empirical vs. Theory:**  
The literature base is rich, with a strong concentration of high-quality **empirical laboratory experiments** directly testing public goods games (PGGs) with and without punishment. There is also a substantial **theoretical** (game-theoretic and evolutionary) literature, often providing mechanistic or boundary condition arguments about punishment's effectiveness in sustaining cooperation and efficiency. Several field and framed-field experiments extend results to natural resource and real-world contexts.

**Breadth and Depth:**  
For the core prediction task—estimating the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments—this paper set is **broad and deep**. Many studies test canonical n-player linear PGGs, directly manipulating punishment and carefully quantifying efficiency or total payoff. Others test close variants (common-pool resource games, threshold public goods, ostracism, delegated punishment, endogenous institution choice), adding valuable nuance and realism. However, some papers use adjacent or related games (e.g., dyadic PDs, trust games, contests), which must be interpreted with caution for direct PGG prediction tasks.

**Outcome Quality:**  
Numerous studies measure **efficiency or directly related payoff-based group outcomes** (total payoff, welfare, earnings, surplus), not just behavioral proxies such as contributions or cooperation rate. This supports rigorous, quantitative synthesis.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant (Relevance to PGGs):**  
- **exact:** The large majority of key studies (especially the empirically strongest) are **exactly** PGGs or very close operationalizations (VCM, CPR games with public good structure).
- **close:** Many field and common-pool resource experiments, as well as threshold and ostracism designs, are **close** variants—structurally similar with relevant dynamics for the prediction task.
- **adjacent:** Some studies (trust games, PDs, contests) are **adjacent**: informing mechanisms, but with caveats for direct applicability.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact:** Numerous studies directly manipulate **peer punishment or formal sanctioning** in PGGs, including details on **punishment cost, technology, and frequency**.
- **close/adjacent:** Many also examine exclusion/ostracism (functionally punitive), legal sanctions, delegated/centralized punishment, and informal sanctions (e.g., reputation, disapproval), which can substitute or interact with material punishment.
- **weak/none:** A small subset mention or contextualize punishment without direct manipulation.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact:** Many studies report **group efficiency** or directly comparable measures (group earnings, surplus as a percentage of the social optimum).
- **close:** Some report total group payoff or exogenous outcomes assumed to tightly track efficiency.
- **adjacent/weak:** Others focus on contributions or behavioral measures, which are *not* equivalent to efficiency—especially when cost of punishment is nontrivial.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**  
  - **Efficiency** (payoff relative to full cooperation) is frequently measured in exact-match PGGs.
  - **Group earnings/welfare/total payoff/surplus** are used to operationalize efficiency, particularly when true maximum is calculable.
  - Close variants use analogous measures (e.g., net yield in CPR, success rates in threshold games).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
  - **Contribution rates, cooperation, norm compliance, and frequencies of punishment/reward** are widely used but are *not* substitutes for efficiency.
  - Some studies report only contributions or punishment assigned, cautioning that punishment costs can leave efficiency unchanged or reduced—even if cooperation rises.
- **Both types:**  
  - The best-informed studies reliably report both, making clear the sometimes divergent impacts on cooperation and efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### Direction and Magnitude of Punishment Effects

- **Typical Lab PGG (linear, 3–5 players, 10–20 rounds, MPCR 0.4–0.5, no chat):**
  - **Enabling peer punishment usually increases group efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) game.**
  - The magnitude of the gain is often in the **10–20%** range, but can reach much higher or be nearly zero depending on key moderators (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007; Noussair & Tucker, 2005).

- **Efficiency gains are *not guaranteed*.**  
  - In some designs, the direct costs of punishment and side effects (e.g., antisocial punishment, retaliation, feuds, misdirected punishment) fully or more than offset cooperation gains, leading to unchanged or even lower efficiency with punishment (Nikiforakis, 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
  - The effect can be **negative** in settings with high prevalence of antisocial punishment, counter-punishment, or when punishment cost is low and used excessively.

- **Key moderators identified empirically and theoretically:**
  - **Punishment cost-effectiveness (cost/impact ratio):**  
    - **Higher impact per cost** yields greater efficiency gains (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kranz, 2010; Ambrus & Greiner, 2012).
    - **Cheap punishment** can backfire, with excessive use destroying resources (Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Bolle et al., 2011).
  - **Targeting and institution design:**  
    - **Unrestricted punishment** (can punish anyone, for any reason) often delivers little or no net efficiency benefit; targeted, consensus-based or rule-based punishment (e.g., only low contributors) yields higher efficiency (Ertan et al., 2009; Casari & Luini, 2009).
  - **Game horizon (number of rounds):**
    - **Short games:** Punishment can reduce efficiency, as costs outweigh gains before cooperation stabilizes (Fehr et al., 2010; Tyran & Feld, 2006).
    - **Long games:** Efficiency gains from sustained cooperation often come to dominate (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012).
  - **Monitoring/information structure:**  
    - **Perfect monitoring** (everyone knows who did what) enhances punishment efficacy; **imperfect/noisy monitoring** can result in punishment misfires, reducing efficiency (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Abreu et al., 1991).
  - **Institutional choice:**  
    - **Endogenous (voted) punishment regimes** tend to yield better efficiency than exogenous ones, as selection/commitment effects crowd in cooperation (Sutter et al., 2010; Putterman et al., 2011; Tyran & Feld, 2006).
  - **Communication and reputation:**  
    - **Communication alone** can sometimes increase efficiency more than punishment; **punishment plus communication or reputation** is even more effective (Bochet et al., 2006; Cardenas, 2004).
  - **Group size:**  
    - The effect of enabling punishment scales down in larger groups, unless the punishment institution is adjusted for monitoring capacity (Carpenter, 2007).
  - **Social/cultural context:**  
    - Negative or zero efficiency effects more likely in low-trust/high-antisocial-punishment contexts (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Kocher et al., 2012).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct prediction:**  
  - For canonical lab linear PGGs with moderate group size, known rounds, no communication, and realistic punishment cost/impact (typically 1:3 cost-impact), enabling peer punishment should be expected to raise efficiency by **≈10–20% of the cooperative maximum** relative to control, with the largest gains emerging in the latter rounds (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007; Page et al., 2005; Noussair & Tucker, 2005).

- **Design-adjusted prediction:**  
  - The efficiency gain from punishment is **highly sensitive** to:
    - **Punishment cost/impact ratio:** Higher impact per cost (e.g., 1:3 or higher) → more efficiency; low cost/high frequency or low impact → less or negative efficiency (Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
    - **Information/monitoring:** Perfect observation → positive effect; noisy/low observation → possible negative effect due to errors/costs (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012).
    - **Institutional design:** Rule-based/consensus punishment or punishment restricted to free riders → larger efficiency gain. Unrestricted punishment (including anti-social) or available counter-punishment → small or negative efficiency change (Casari & Luini, 2009; Nikiforakis, 2008).
    - **Game horizon:** Longer games increase the likelihood that efficiency gains from stabilized cooperation will dominate (Fehr et al., 2010).
    - **Communication:** Allows punishment to have stronger efficiency effects or even be substituted entirely (Bochet et al., 2006).
    - **Cultural context/group norms:** In high-trust settings, punishment may crowd out intrinsic motivation and reduce efficiency (Vollan, 2008; Kocher et al., 2012).

- **Use of control efficiency:**  
  - **Control (no-punishment) efficiency** identifies baseline:  
    - If control efficiency is already high (near optimal), punishment may have small or even negative effect on efficiency (Kocher et al., 2012; Kube & Traxler, 2011).
    - If control efficiency is low, average improvement with punishment will be higher, provided key moderators above are favorable.

- **When evidence is on contribution rather than efficiency:**  
  - **Do not interpret contribution increases as efficiency increases** if punishment costs are high or untargeted.
  - Use adjusted or net efficiency estimates where available, considering both raised contributions and punishment-induced resource loss (Ertan et al., 2009).

- **Institutional choice matters:**  
  - **Endogenous (voted or group-selected) punishment schemes** often yield higher efficiency—especially when voting signals commitment or aligns preferences, even with mild punishment (Sutter et al., 2010; Putterman et al., 2011; Tyran & Feld, 2006).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Best informed (directly or via nuancing empirical studies):**  
- `player_count` (Group size): Extensively manipulated and analyzed; effect not strictly monotonic.
- `num_rounds` (Game horizon): Multiple studies show importance; long horizons critical for efficiency gains.
- `mpcr` (Marginal per-capita return): Often varied; higher MPCR generally enhances efficiency gain from punishment, but with limits (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kranz, 2010).
- `punishment_cost` & `punishment_tech` (Cost and impact/effectiveness): Heavily manipulated; central to cross-study effect size variations.
- `chat` (Communication): Directly manipulated; shown to sometimes substitute for punishment or magnify its effects.
- `show_other_summaries` (Feedback about other players): Evidence on feedback/monitoring; perfect info boosts punishment effectiveness.
- `all_or_nothing` (Discrete vs continuous contributions): Most studies use continuous, but some test binary forms; main mechanisms persist but magnitude of effects varies.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Studied as moderator/alternative or as comparison to punishment.

**Indirect or contextually discussed:**  
- `default_contrib` (Framing of contribution): Sometimes part of context manipulation (e.g., opt-out framing), but less standard.
- `show_n_rounds` (Visibility of game length): Empirically present in most lab studies, though only sometimes manipulated.
- `show_punishment_id` (Identifiability of punishers/rewarders): Examined in relation to anti-social punishment and retaliation, but not universal.

**Sparse, less directly informed:**  
- Some dimensions such as `default_contrib`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id` are less commonly manipulated or analyzed systematically (though when used, e.g., in anonymity/identification studies, they are shown as important moderators).

**Missing or rarely addressed:**  
- There is relatively little direct evidence on very large player counts (beyond ~20), highly complex structures (nested games, multi-level hierarchies), and certain parameter combinations (e.g., high MPCR and high punishment cost). Integration across such extreme/novel designs requires cautious extrapolation from available evidence.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Heterogeneity and Context Sensitivity:**
  - The impact of punishment on efficiency is **not uniform or additive**—it is heavily moderated by cost structure, targeting, monitoring, institutional design, communication, group composition, and social/cultural context.
  - Some contexts yield negative or absent efficiency effects, especially with antisocial punishment, counter-punishment, high punishment frequency, or poor targeting.

- **Data Limitations on Certain Dimensions:**
  - A few prediction dimensions (e.g., default contribution framing, precise feedback variations, some visibility/anonymity variables) are not equally covered or are only contextually discussed.
  - Some important real-world variables (long/unknown horizon, extreme asymmetry of returns, network/graph structure, cultural context) are addressed only in close/adjacent studies.

- **Ambiguity on Transferability:**
  - Many strong empirical findings come from small-group, lab-based, Western student populations. Direct application to field or highly scaled environments requires careful attention to context and external validity.

- **Behavioral vs Payoff Outcomes:**
  - Some well-cited papers emphasize contribution or norm compliance effects of punishment, but do not directly report efficiency or group payoff. In such cases, inferring efficiency outcomes requires cross-walking from similar contexts where both outcomes are measured.

- **Treatment Definition Complexity:**
  - “Enabling punishment” can cover a wide range of structural choices (peer vs centralized, one-shot vs multi-stage, targeted vs unrestricted, anonymous vs identified). The literature clearly shows that these details strongly moderate the efficiency effect—direct mapping across variants is not always safe.

- **Uncertain Negative Effects:**
  - Potential pathologies include:
    - Antisocial or perverse punishment destroying group value.
    - Counter-punishment/feuding cycles, especially when identification is possible.
    - “Crowding out” of intrinsic motivation in highly normed groups.
    - Misalignment between contribution and efficiency due to high cost punishment use.

---

**Bottom line:**  
**The literature robustly supports the view that enabling peer punishment in PGG-like environments often, but not always, raises efficiency above the baseline, with the size, direction, and reliability of the effect highly sensitive to punishment cost-effectiveness, targeting, game horizon, monitoring structure, communication, and social context.** For downstream prediction tasks, these design dimensions plus observed control efficiency should be carefully combined, using close empirical analogues as the basis for calibration, and noting when outcome measures in studies are behavioral rather than efficiency-based. Ambiguities and limitations should be preserved—punishment is not a universal efficiency booster, and prediction must account for known moderators and possible failure modes.
