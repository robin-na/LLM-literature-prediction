# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises **six papers**, primarily **empirical laboratory experiments**, with a focus on behavioral economics, cooperation, and social punishment. Of these, **one paper (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015)** provides direct, high-relevance empirical evidence relating to the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in public goods games (PGG). A second paper (Brosig et al., 2004) examines sequential bargaining games with punishment and communication, offering adjacent but less directly transferable insights. Three papers (Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015; Lopez, 2017) are somewhat contextually related, invoking public goods contributions, social class, or the psychology of collective action, but lack direct manipulation of punishment or efficiency as primary outcomes. The final paper (Gordon & Lea, 2016) is more removed, focusing on perceptions of punisher status, not economic game efficiency or actual behavior.

**Overall, the evidence base is narrow for the specific efficiency prediction task:** Only one study rigorously investigates the causal effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in repeated PGGs, while others are more peripheral, treating communication, social status, or attitudes as their key focus.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact**: 3 papers (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015) use standard or classical PGG setups.  
- **Adjacent**: 2 papers (Brosig et al., 2004; Lopez, 2017) address other collective or bargaining games with public goods elements.  
- **Weak/None**: 1 paper (Gordon & Lea, 2016) does not use PGG or close variants.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact**: 3 papers include punishment or sanction conditions (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Brosig et al., 2004; Lopez, 2017).  
- **None**: 2 papers lack punishment (Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015).  
- **Contextual**: 1 paper examines punishment perceptions (Gordon & Lea, 2016), not actual sanctions.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact**: Only Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) reports direct measures of efficiency or group payoff.  
- **Adjacent**: Brosig et al. (2004) and Drouvelis et al. (2015) speak to efficiency through the occurrence of efficient splits or by secondary reporting of earnings.  
- **None**: Martinsson et al. (2015), Lopez (2017), and Gordon & Lea (2016) do not address efficiency; they focus on contributions, attitudes, or reputational consequences.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related/Efficiency Outcomes:**  
- **Directly measured, analyzed, and compared:**  
  - Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015): Explicit group earnings and efficiency relative to full cooperation.
- **Reported or suggested but not primary:**  
  - Brosig et al. (2004): Frequency of efficient (equal-split) outcomes in bargaining; actual payoffs not directly analyzed as treatment means but implied.
  - Drouvelis et al. (2015): Average earnings reported, but focus is on contributions.

**Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
- **Contribution rates, cooperation, attitudes, beliefs:**  
  - Drouvelis et al. (2015); Martinsson et al. (2015): Contributions in PGG with/without interventions such as priming or social class.
  - Brosig et al. (2004): Choices, punishment rates, splits in bargaining.
  - Lopez (2017): Willingness to participate, punitive and reward sentiment (self-reported).
  - Gordon & Lea (2016): Perceptions of punishers, status, reputational outcomes.

**Note:** Most papers emphasize **contribution and behavioral measures** rather than group payoff or efficiency, and only Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) systematically analyzes payoff-based efficiency effects of punishment.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of Empirical and Mechanism Evidence:**

- **Punishment can increase efficiency in PGG, but only under restrictive conditions:**  
  - With a single, anonymous punishment stage per round (no retaliation, limited information), enabling peer punishment leads to increased efficiency over time (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Punishment’s efficiency benefits are easily nullified by design choices:**  
  - When punishment is unconstrained—allowing for multiple punishment stages, retaliation, and visibility of identity—punishment still increases *contributions*, but efficiency does **not** improve, as higher punishment costs (from feuding and retaliation) offset cooperation gains (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Communication has a strong moderating effect in adjacent games:**  
  - In sequential bargaining games, enabling communication—especially face-to-face—increases efficient outcomes, overriding the influence of punishment mechanisms (Brosig et al., 2004). This suggests communication is a key moderator in environments where punishment or reward are options.
- **Other behavioral and contextual findings (adjacent to target task):**  
  - Priming, social class, and the structure of the collective action problem affect cooperation rates, baseline contributions, or punitive sentiment, but these do **not** offer direct causal evidence for efficiency effects of punishment (Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015; Lopez, 2017).
  - Observer studies show that the perceived effectiveness of punishment may depend on punisher status, but these are not linked to concrete efficiency outcomes (Gordon & Lea, 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Quantitative prediction of efficiency when enabling punishment in a PGG is informed directly by Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015):**
    - **Expect efficiency to rise above control** only when the punishment protocol is tightly *constrained* (single stage, anonymity, limited information).
    - **Do not expect efficiency improvement**—and possibly even expect no net gain or a decrease—if punishment is *rich* (multiple stages, ID fixed, full information), due to higher punishment costs and social feuding.
- **Baseline (control) efficiency provides a stable reference:**  
  - In Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015), baseline efficiency is consistent, serving as an anchor for predicting the treatment effect due to enabling punishment.
- **Specific design dimensions (see below) are critical moderators:**  
  - The efficiency impact of enabling punishment crucially depends on dimensions such as number of punishment stages, anonymity/fixed identities, and punishment cost.
- **Communication, while not tested in a full PGG with punishment, is identified as a strong moderator in bargaining (Brosig et al., 2004):**
  - If chat or communication is present, efficient outcomes may increase even without punishment, and the marginal value of punishment may diminish or interact in complex ways.
- **Mechanism and context papers (Lopez, 2017; Gordon & Lea, 2016) are useful for identifying conditions or moderators (such as publicness of goods, need for labor, or social status of punishers) but insufficient for direct efficiency estimation.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions (by at least one high-relevance study for efficiency):**
- **player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing (contribution format), mpcr (marginal per-capita return), punishment_cost, punishment_tech (structure, anonymity, stages):**  
  - Substantively analyzed in Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015). Key moderators for the treatment effect of punishment on efficiency.
- **chat:**  
  - Explored in bargaining with punishment and reward (Brosig et al., 2004); not in PGG, but strong adjacent evidence for its importance.
- **reward_exists:**  
  - Addressed in terms of sentiment, not implementation, by Lopez (2017).

**Indirectly/Contextually Mentioned or Absent:**
- **default_contrib:**  
  - Contextually addressed in contributions (Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015) but not in relation to punishment or efficiency.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:**  
  - Not directly manipulated for efficiency outcomes, though identity visibility is critical in Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) as part of the punishment structure.
- **reward_cost, reward_tech:**  
  - No studies provide direct evidence of the effect of peer rewards on efficiency in PGGs.
- **punishment_magnitude:**  
  - Only addressed as part of punishment_tech (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Brosig et al., 2004), but primarily in behavioral terms, not efficiency analysis.

**Design dimensions with no direct efficiency evidence:**
- default_contrib, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech, reward_magnitude

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse direct evidence:**  
  - Only one paper (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015) provides empirical, payoff-focused, within-experiment evidence for the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in canonical PGGs.
- **Limited coverage of prediction dimensions:**  
  - Most of the 14 design dimensions are not systematically manipulated or cross-analyzed for their effect on efficiency with/without punishment.
- **Generalizability constraints:**  
  - The main payoff-based findings are specific to laboratory PGG settings with 4 players, 30 rounds, and particular punishment mechanisms; findings may not generalize to other group sizes, MPCRs, or richer settings.
- **Overrepresentation of behavioral (non-payoff) outcomes:**  
  - Most papers focus on contributions, cooperation rates, or attitudes—not efficiency or group payoff.
- **Adjacent evidence is sometimes contextually but not quantitatively informative:**  
  - Communication, reputation, social class, and priming are important moderators, but their interaction with punishment remains empirically underexplored for efficiency.
- **Potential confounds (e.g., repeated feuding, retaliation) illustrated but not fully mapped:**  
  - While feuding and retaliation are shown to offset efficiency gains from punishment, the boundaries of these effects (across other settings and parameterizations) are not fully determined with the available evidence.

---
**In summary:** Prediction of the efficiency impact from enabling peer punishment in PGGs is strongly dependent on details of the punishment environment (especially anonymity, structure, and information about identity). The presence of these moderators should be encoded carefully in prediction models, referencing the limited but specific evidence from Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015). Most other papers in this set provide only adjacent or contextual guidance. The literature set is too narrow on critical design and payoff dimensions to support fine-tuned or general predictions without caution.
