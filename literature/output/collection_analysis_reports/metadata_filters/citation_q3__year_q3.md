# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is broad and contains a substantial number of both empirical (lab experiment, field experiment, observational) and theoretical (mathematical/computational modeling, conceptual) studies—206 in total. The evidence pool is particularly rich in standard linear public goods game (PGG) designs and covers a variety of punishment and reward mechanisms, institutional features, and behavioral contexts. Many empirical studies operationalize efficiency (or closely related payoff outcomes) and directly compare control and punishment-enabled conditions; others focus on mechanism, behavior, or non-payoff effects. Theory and simulation papers often offer comparative statics and conditions under which enabling punishment increases or decreases efficiency. The base covers varying group sizes, round numbers, punishment and reward tech/costs, and sometimes examines more complex environments (e.g., optional participation, network, resource, or leadership games).

# 2) Task Relevance

Relevance assessment by dimension:

- **pgg_or_variant:**  
  Most papers (especially empirical and theory PGG studies) are of `exact` relevance (e.g., Ozono et al. 2020; Gürerk et al., 2018; Engl et al., 2021; Nockur et al., 2021). Others shift to `close` or `adjacent` when studying, for example, trust games, common-pool resource games, or indirect reciprocity games with similar payoff logics but differing in structure/mechanism.
  
- **punishment_or_sanctions:**  
  A significant core directly manipulates or models costly peer, leader, democratic, or institutional punishment (`exact`). Some, however, use close analogs (exclusion as "punishment", ostracism, or weak "sanction" variants: `close` to `adjacent`).
  
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
  Multiple empirical and theory studies report efficiency, total/group payoff, welfare, surplus, or earnings directly (`exact` or `close`). A large subset, however, focuses on contribution/cooperation rates or punishment/reward behavior without directly reporting efficiency (`adjacent`), limiting their predictive utility for the precise downstream task.

In summary, a substantial subset bears `exact` to `close` relevance across all three target criteria, but there is nontrivial inclusion of `adjacent`/`weak` material, especially in context, mechanism, or behavioral focus papers.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes**  
- **Efficiency** (group payoff relative to full cooperation): directly measured in key experimental and theoretical papers (e.g., Ozono et al. 2020; Gürerk et al., 2018; Engl et al., 2021; Nockur et al., 2021; Marcin et al., 2019; Bruhin et al., 2020).
- **Group/Total Payoff, Welfare, Surplus:** often interchangeable with efficiency for linear PGGs and frequently reported as proxy or direct outcome.
- **Earnings** (sometimes used as group or individual measure).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Not Efficiency)**
- **Contribution rate, cooperation rate:** extremely common as main outcome, but only indirectly linked to efficiency.
- **Punishment frequency, norm enforcement, prosocial punishment:** provide mechanism insight but are not substitutions for payoff.
- **Punishment/reward patterns, norm compliance:** highlight behavioral effects and mechanisms.
- **Inequality, satisfaction, fairness perceptions:** sometimes reported alongside payoffs.

**Distinctions**  
A notable pattern is that many studies equate higher cooperation rates with higher efficiency, but others show that increased contributions via punishment can sometimes reduce efficiency due to high punishment costs (Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Molleman et al., 2019).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical and Theoretical Consensus**
- **Enabling punishment frequently increases contributions/cooperation and can increase efficiency**, but only when punishment costs are not excessive compared to their benefit (Ozono et al., 2020; Gürerk et al., 2018; Engl et al., 2021; Marcin et al., 2019; Marcin et al., 2019).
- **Peer punishment can reduce efficiency** if costs are high, if anti-social (inefficient) punishment is prevalent, or in the presence of uncoordinated over-punishment (Nockur et al., 2021; Bruhin et al., 2020; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Kamei 2020; Ahmad & Loch, 2020).
- **Type/structure of punishment matters:**  
  - Democratic or central punishment tends to sustain or improve efficiency, mainly by reducing anti-social or "wasteful" punishment (Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).  
  - Institutionally imposed or leader-based punishment is often more effective and less subject to anti-social use, but can fail or backfire if subject to corruption or if lacking transparency (Engl et al., 2021; Dong et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2019).
- **Antisocial punishment** (punishing cooperators or random punishment) can dramatically reduce or even reverse efficiency gains (Bruhin et al., 2020; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Kosfeld, 2020).
- **Reward mechanisms** (when available) can enhance cooperation and efficiency, sometimes more robustly than punishment, particularly in environments with decision errors or costly sanctioning (Dong et al., 2019; Gürerk et al., 2018).
- **Information/transparency** (e.g., income visibility, contribution summaries) and the alignment of local/global incentives moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency (Ozono et al., 2020; Hauser et al., 2021).
- **Contextual moderators:**  
  - **Group composition** (prevalence of antisocial punishers, heterogeneity), group size (susceptibility to over-punishment), and coordination mechanisms (democracy, leadership) strongly modulate efficiency effects (Bruhin et al., 2020; Kamei, 2020).
  - **Baseline efficiency:** If the control game is already efficient/cooperative, adding punishment may reduce efficiency (Nair et al., 2018). If baseline efficiency is low, punishment is more likely to yield efficiency gains.
  - **Cost-to-impact ratios:** Higher punishment effectiveness (penalty/impact per cost) supports higher efficiency; weak or expensive punishment is less efficient (Salahshour, 2021; Zhang & Cao, 2020; Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2020).
- **Spillover and persistence:** Institutional punishment can exert persistent positive effects on efficiency, even after removal (Engl et al., 2021), but can also generate negative downstream effects via increased inequality or norm conflict.
- **Corruption and anti-social dynamics** in punishment/reward systems can substantially undermine intended efficiency gains (Lee et al., 2019; Bruhin et al., 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Prediction of treatment efficiency should always take into account**:  
  - **Control efficiency**: If the game is already highly efficient/cooperative, enabling punishment may offer little or even negative marginal return.
  - **Type and cost/tech of punishment**: Peer punishment with high sanctioning cost is much more likely to reduce efficiency than institutional or democratic variants (Nockur et al., 2021; Marcin et al., 2019; Pfattheicher et al., 2018). Specific cost:impact ratios are crucial.
  - **Group size**: Effects are positive for small/moderate groups but may turn negative due to over-punishment in large groups unless there is explicit coordination (Kamei, 2020).
  - **Cultural/group heterogeneity**: Prevalence of antisocial punishment types predicts cross-cultural and within-group variance in efficiency effects (Bruhin et al., 2020). If the population is prone to antisocial punishment, efficiency can decline.
  - **Reward exists/Reward parameters**: Reward-only or mixed systems sometimes outperform pure punishment systems in efficiency, particularly in error-prone or high-cost contexts.
  - **Transparency, visibility, and information**: Making contributions and income visible enhances positive effects of punishment and reduces antisocial use (Hauser et al., 2021).
  - **Institution structure** (central/leader, democratic, probabilistic execution): Institutional and coordinated punishment is generally more efficient.
  - **Alignment of incentives**: Punishment only improves efficiency if cooperation is locally incentivized (Ozono et al., 2020).

- **Non-payoff outcomes (e.g., high cooperation rates) do not guarantee efficiency gains**: These must be adjusted for the net cost of punishment deployed—overuse or misdirected punishment often cancels out or reverses efficiency gains.

- **Sparse or indirect evidence along certain dimensions (see below) calls for defaulting to nearest well-supported analogs or avoiding strong claims, especially when the context or mechanism departs from the standard PGG plus peer/central punishment paradigm.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed, with payoffs/efficiency outcomes**:
- `player_count` (group size): Extensive coverage; efficiency effects of punishment are consistently moderated by group size, with unique dynamics (over-punishment, coordination challenges) emerging for larger groups.
- `num_rounds`: Most standard PGG studies report rounds; efficiency effects evolve (e.g., punishment has stronger positive impact in longer games, especially under democratic or institutional punishment; see Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Gürerk et al., 2018).
- `mpcr`: Systematically varied, with strong evidence that higher MPCR (more efficient public good) increases the efficacy and efficiency effect of punishment (Salahshour, 2021; Zhang & Cao, 2020).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (cost-to-impact): Direct evidence shows efficiency gains hinge on high effectiveness (penalty/cost) and moderate or low cost. Ineffective or overly costly punishment backfires.
- `all_or_nothing`: Both binary and continuous-contribution PGGs are covered with similar findings.
- `reward_exists` / `reward_cost` / `reward_tech`: A substantial subset compare reward and punishment; reward is sometimes more efficient or robust (Dong et al., 2019), but depends on incentive structures.
- `chat`: Communication is frequently manipulated; while it increases cooperation, its effect on efficiency in the presence of punishment is less well-quantified, but can substitute for or amplify the effect of punishment.
  
**Indirect or contextually discussed**:
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Some studies investigate the effect of information/identifiability/transparency, but only a subset tie these directly to efficiency outcomes.
- `default_contrib`: Framing (opt-in/opt-out) is rarely a focus, but individual studies include manipulations.
- `show_other_summaries`: Explored as part of transparency.
- `show_punishment_id`: Effects on norm compliance and punishment deterrence considered in mechanism papers, but less often mapped to efficiency.

**Effectively missing/sparse**:
- `default_contrib`: Weak, with minimal variance among studies.
- Rich manipulation/comparison of all 14 dimensions simultaneously is absent; most studies vary a limited subset.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency reporting is inconsistent:** Many studies report only behavioral/strategic outcomes, or else select efficiency-related outcomes, making comprehensive prediction across all dimensions challenging.
- **Complex interactions are underexplored:** Most studies vary only a small subset of design dimensions, so joint/multivariate interaction effects (e.g., how chat or reward moderates punishment's efficiency impact in different group sizes) are not fully mapped.
- **Context dependence and structural boundaries:** Findings from trust games, CPRs, or indirect reciprocity games are often `adjacent` but not directly transferable; translation to standard PGGs with standard payout structures may be error-prone.
- **Antisocial and misdirected punishment behaviors are variable:** The frequency and effect of antisocial punishment depend on participant pool, culture, and group design, making universality of positive efficiency effects dubious.
- **External validity concerns:** Lab settings may overestimate the use and effect of punishment compared to real-world (field or ethnographic) environments, where third-party or decentralized punishment is less frequent and restorative mechanisms more prevalent.
- **Uncertainty in dynamic/adaptive environments:** Network evolution, partner choice, and feedback effects are insufficiently covered for their impact on punishment's efficiency effects.
- **Transparency and observability of punishment mechanisms:** Not all studies manipulate or report on visibility of punishment/reward, which is critical in theoretical models emphasizing conditional and reputation-based strategies.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature base is strong and comprehensive for standard PGG and punishment designs but reveals important heterogeneity in efficiency outcomes depending on group size, punishment costs and structure, presence of antisocial punishment, institution type (democratic, leader, peer), and information structure. Notably, enabling punishment is not universally efficiency-enhancing; under some design and population contexts (high punishment cost, large group, antisocial punishment), efficiency falls. Prediction of treatment efficiency should be anchored to empirically matched designs and tuned for group composition and punishment technology; default positive effects cannot be assumed, particularly where control efficiency is already high or risk of antisocial punishment is substantial. Most (but not all) design dimensions have at least some directly informative studies; the greatest uncertainties lie in diverse or hybridized/institutional settings, and in generalizing behavioral-outcome findings to payoff-based efficiency.
