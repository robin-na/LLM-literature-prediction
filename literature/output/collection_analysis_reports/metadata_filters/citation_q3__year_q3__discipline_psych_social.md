# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large (55 papers) and covers a broad theoretical and empirical literature on cooperation, social dilemmas, and sanctions in economic and evolutionary games, with a substantial focus on public goods games (PGGs) and their close variants. Methodologically, the set includes laboratory experiments, field experiments, observational studies, and a significant number of theoretical models and conceptual reviews.

Within this set:
- Around a dozen empirical lab studies provide **direct evidence** on efficiency or related payoff outcomes in public goods games with or without punishment.
- Other studies focus on adjacent or close variants (e.g., CPR games, team production, coalition games), typically with insights on behavioral processes or related outcomes.
- Many papers address social, psychological, or institutional mechanisms affecting cooperation and punishment (e.g., norms, leadership, status, group identity), but do not analyze efficiency per se.
- Several theory papers offer models with explicit payoff analyses, though not always mapping directly onto empirical PGG conditions.
- A large subset discusses non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, norm compliance, punishment frequency), mechanisms, or broader context.
- Some papers lack empirical results, or are only tangentially relevant.

The **evidence base is strongest** for simple, multi-round, 3-5-player PGGs with peer or institutional punishment and basic settings for other game dimensions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) Pgg_or_variant**
- **Exact relevance:** Multiple empirical lab experiments focus on standard or minimally modified PGGs (e.g., Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Hauser et al., 2021).
- **Close relevance:** Several studies use CPR games, team/coalition or leader-driven games, or field experiments analogous to PGGs (e.g., Chavez et al., 2018; Nair et al., 2018).
- **Adjacent/weak relevance:** Many papers use one-shot dyadic games, third-party punishment, or focus on norm psychology/theory (e.g., Krupka-type social norms, trust games).
- **None:** A few are outside the target (e.g., pure dictator games, network models).

**b) Punishment_or_sanctions**
- **Exact relevance:** About half of the relevant PGG studies include peer, central, or democratic punishment as an experimental condition.
- **Close relevance:** Some focus on reward, mutual monitoring, feedback withdrawal or third-party punishment (e.g., Bolton et al., 2018; Tan & Xiao, 2018).
- **Adjacent:** Reviews on social sanctioning, reputation or gossip systems, or focus on the determinants of punishment but without actual punishment in the game.
- **None:** Many do not manipulate or include punishment.

**c) Efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**
- **Exact:** Only a subset of studies reports or analyzes group efficiency (total payoff vs. full-cooperation payoff), or very close surrogates (e.g., total group earnings, realized gains from trade).
- **Close/adjacent:** Many papers report on total contributions, allocation, or earnings but do not present efficiency ratios.
- **Weak/None:** Papers that focus only on behavioral variables (contribution rate, punishment decisions), beliefs, fairness, etc., without reporting payoffs or efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (relevant to prediction):**
- **Group efficiency** (ratio of group payoff to optimal cooperation): directly analyzed in some lab PGG studies (e.g., Nockur et al., 2021), CPR experiments (Chavez et al., 2018), and select theory papers.
- **Total group payoff/earnings/welfare/surplus:** reported more broadly, sometimes without explicit normalization to cooperative optimum (often called "average payoffs" or "profits").
- **Inequality and payoff distribution:** some papers measure Gini coefficients or compare advantaged vs. disadvantaged members.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (not direct proxies for efficiency):**
- **Contribution/cooperation rate:** very commonly measured, but not always mapped to payoffs.
- **Punishment frequency/intensity:** how often/how much punishment is used, not its net effect on efficiency.
- **Norm compliance, trust, satisfaction, fairness judgments, psychological/physiological responses:** relevant for mechanisms, not efficiency.
- **Leadership selection, prestige, gossip, and sanction behaviors:** behavioral processes, not payoff effects.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical (payoff/efficiency):**
- **Peer punishment typically increases contributions but often reduces efficiency due to the cost of administering punishment** (Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018). In baseline multi-round PGGs, peer punishment can result in lower group profit than no punishment, especially when punishment is decentralized and punishment costs are substantial.
- **Democratic or centralized punishment mechanisms** (voting, authority-applied) suppress antisocial/counterproductive punishment and tend to have a **neutral or positive effect on efficiency** relative to control (no-punishment) (Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Kosfeld, 2020).
- **Effectiveness of punishment on efficiency is context-dependent:**
  - When **control efficiency is already high**, introducing punishment often reduces efficiency (Nair et al., 2018; Ahmad & Loch, 2020; Bolton et al., 2018).
  - When **control efficiency is low (baseline contribution poor)**, enabling punishment may improve efficiency, but usually does not restore it to the full cooperation level (Chavez et al., 2018; Nair et al., 2018).
- **Transparency/visibility factors** (e.g., showing others' incomes) strongly moderate the effect of punishment: punishment can lower inequality and improve contributions/efficiency when relevant information is visible (Hauser et al., 2021).
- **Punishment cost-to-impact ratio** is crucial: lower costs or higher impact per unit make punishment more effective for efficiency (Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Kosfeld, 2020; Bhui et al., 2019).
- **Leadership/institutional punishment** is more likely to produce efficiency gains than uncoordinated peer punishment, especially when leaders' incentives are well-aligned with group goals (Kosfeld, 2020; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Chavez et al., 2018).

**Theoretical:**
- **Models support the empirical pattern:** punishment can stabilize cooperation and sometimes increase average payoffs, but only if its cost structure, administration, and alignment of incentives are well-designed (Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Bhui et al., 2019; Kosfeld, 2020; Glover & Kim, 2021).
- **Risk of antisocial punishment, retaliation, and maladaptive social dynamics**: Theories and reviews caution that punishment may backfire or have little benefit if it devolves into cycles of retaliation, is not well-targeted, or if norms are misaligned (Smith, 2020; Raihani & Power, 2021; Ahmad & Loch, 2020).
- **Other mechanisms (reward, reputation, communication, leader communication)** may also raise efficiency, sometimes more robustly than punishment (Boulu-Reshef et al., 2020; Kosfeld, 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**a) General Predictive Implications:**
- **Control efficiency is a necessary baseline**: The effect of enabling peer punishment depends critically on how efficient the control game is to begin with. **If control efficiency is already high, punishment is likely to reduce efficiency (costs outweigh the benefit); if control efficiency is low, punishment can improve efficiency, but rarely to the full-cooperation level** (Nair et al., 2018; Chavez et al., 2018).
- **Type of punishment system is a major moderator**:
  - **Peer punishment** often reduces efficiency (Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Ahmad & Loch, 2020).
  - **Democratic or central punishment** (voting or leader-administered) can be efficiency-neutral or even positive, especially over longer games or with sustained cooperation (Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Kosfeld, 2020).
- **Game design dimensions matter**:
  - **Player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost**: These directly modulate the feasibility and sustainability of punishment-driven cooperation.
  - **Information/reward structure**: Visibility of others' outcomes and the ability to reward as well as punish can shift outcomes (Hauser et al., 2021; Smith, 2020).
- **High punishment costs and easy retaliation generally make efficiency losses from punishment more likely** (Bolton et al., 2018); **low-cost, high-impact punishment supports better efficiency** (Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021).
- **Experimental parameter matches:**
  - Most robust findings: 3-5 player, multi-round, continuous-contribution PGGs (Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
  - Less is known for larger groups, one-shot or very short games, or unusual parameterizations.

**b) For prediction with the available literature:**
- **If the environment matches classic lab PGGs with peer punishment**, expect **lower or similar efficiency** in treatment compared to control, unless the punishment system is democratic/centralized (in which case, effect is neutral or slightly positive).
- **If control efficiency is low (much below full-cooperation), peer punishment may provide a modest efficiency boost, rarely restoring full efficiency.**
- **If the punishment mechanism incorporates a democratic, centralized, or otherwise coordinated structure**, expect more benign or positive efficiency outcomes (and for longer games, improvement over time).
- **If punishment is costly, allows for retaliation, or is likely to be used antisocially,** expect **efficiency losses**.
- **If reward exists, or sanctioning is paired with transparency/feedback**, efficiency may improve, especially if targeting is improved (Hauser et al., 2021).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (empirical evidence on efficiency or payoff):**
- **player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Frequently reported and manipulated in both experiments and models (Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Chavez et al., 2018; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021).
- **punishment_exists/punishment_type**: Whether punishment is present and of what kind (peer, central, democratic), with strong evidence this dimension moderates efficiency outcomes (Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Less commonly, but present in some studies (Hauser et al., 2021).

**Indirectly informed (non-payoff or mechanism outcomes):**
- **chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib**: Sometimes described, less often systematically studied for efficiency, but intuition and mechanism evidence suggest their importance (Boulu-Reshef et al., 2020, some others).
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id**: Visibility/information variables discussed, with **some direct evidence** for the importance of transparency in moderating punishment effects (Hauser et al., 2021; Bolton et al., 2018).

**Only contextually discussed or effectively missing:**
- **default_contrib, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Rarely systematically varied or analyzed for efficiency effects (though show_punishment_id is sometimes discussed conceptually, e.g., Redhead et al., 2021).
- **Reward parameters (reward_cost, reward_tech)** get indirect support but are less thoroughly analyzed in the context of efficiency prediction.
- **Large-scale or highly diverse/heterogeneous group contexts**: Absent or only modeled theoretically; most direct evidence from small-group lab studies.

**Game design dimensions that moderate the punishment→efficiency link (supported by evidence):**
- **Punishment cost-to-impact ratio** (direct: Nockur et al., 2021; theoretical: Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021).
- **Group size and rounds** (direct for small n and up to mid-tens; effects may differ for much larger groups).
- **Type and coordination of punishment** (standard peer vs. democratic/central punishment: direct and substantial empirical support).
- **Visibility of contribution contexts and payoff information** (strong direct and theoretical support for effect on sanction targeting and outcomes).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse direct evidence on efficiency for unusual or novel combinations of design dimensions** (e.g., very large groups, one-shot games, high/low MPCR extremes, simultaneous reward/punishment with other unusual info structures).
- **Limited generalizability to field/real-world settings**: Some field and observational studies suggest punishment rates and effects may be much smaller or even counterproductive outside the lab (Pedersen et al., 2020).
- **Ambiguity in the direction of the efficiency effect**: While empirical findings converge on the inefficiency of peer punishment (due to cost), certain democratic or institutionalized mechanisms show neutral or positive efficiency—this is a source of cross-paper disagreement, especially as game length or repeated interaction increases.
- **Many studies report only behavioral, not efficiency/payoff, outcomes**—inferences about efficiency must be made with caution.
- **Design dimension coverage is uneven**: Strong evidence for basic parameters, but detailed effects of subtle dimensions (e.g., default contribution framing, identity visibility, feedback structure) are less well-documented.
- **Theoretical studies provide conditional results**: Positive efficiency effects from punishment depend on precise alignment of parameters—costs, motivation, administration mechanism, risk of retaliation, etc.—and these may not map to specific prediction cases without further empirical data.
- **Outcome measurement inconsistency**: Not all studies report efficiency as the ratio to maximal cooperation; some report only average payoffs or unnormalized contributions, which may not be strictly comparable.
- **Institutional context and cultural differences**: These are often invoked as moderators but rarely systematically varied in controlled efficiency studies.

**Summary:**  
The available literature provides a strong foundation for predicting the effects of punishment on group efficiency in classic, small-group, multi-round lab PGGs, with critical moderation by punishment type, cost, and baseline efficiency. More nuanced or novel design features, as well as generalizability to more complex real-world or large-scale environments, are less well-supported. Where only behavioral outcomes are reported, they should not be conflated with efficiency, and all predictions should be contextualized by the empirical uncertainties and limits described.
