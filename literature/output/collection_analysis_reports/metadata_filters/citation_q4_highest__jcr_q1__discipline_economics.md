# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set for this analysis is substantial (76 papers), broad in its disciplinary and empirical/theoretical scope, but contains a significant concentration of high-relevance, standard laboratory public goods game (PGG) experiments using punishment as a treatment. There is strong empirical representation: a large subset are lab experiments with canonical linear PGG designs, as well as field experiments, theory papers, and studies in closely related social dilemma contexts. Literature ranges from direct tests of punishment in n-player public goods games to studies in adjacent environments (e.g., prisoner's dilemma, trust games, weakest-link games, commons dilemmas) and includes both empirical results and formal models.

A notable strength of the literature is a robust evidence base with multiple independent direct replications and parameter variations within core PGG designs, providing empirical leverage for prediction. There is also a well-developed theoretical segment modeling efficiency as a function of design parameters and social context. However, the literature becomes less precise and more ambiguous where context moves away from standard lab PGGs—field settings, cultures with high antisocial punishment, or highly dynamic/dynamic-wealth games. Some design dimensions are highly evidenced, while others (e.g., chat, default contribution framing, visibility of punishment identity) are less studied.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant**

- **Exact relevance (majority):** Over half of the paper set addresses classic linear PGGs or direct variants, including within-group, repeated, and one-shot forms with or without punishment (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sutter et al., 2010; Fischbacher & Gächter, 2010).
- **Close/Adjacent relevance (large minority):** Several studies use related settings (CPR, trust games, contest games, repeated PDs, weakest-link games), providing indirect or partial transfer for the downstream prediction task—these often model group payoff but their structure or dynamics may differ (e.g., Brown et al., 2004; Riedl et al., 2016; Vollan, 2008).
- **None/Weak:** Few papers are outside the remit (e.g., studies merely on time preferences or social status).

**Punishment or Sanctions**

- **Exact relevance (substantial core):** Empirical studies directly manipulate peer punishment (costly and direct), delegated/centralized punishment, or expulsion, and specify punishment cost, magnitude, and technology (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Nikiforakis, 2008; Sutter et al., 2010).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some studies investigate exclusion power, partner choice/exclusion, or self-commitment as functional equivalents to punishment; others distinguish between reward and punishment (e.g., Markussen et al., 2014; Kosfeld et al., 2009).
- **None/Weak:** Papers focusing solely on reward or communication, or studies of underlying preferences without a punishment treatment, offer weak direct relevance.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcomes**

- **Exact/Close (strong core):** A significant number directly measure group efficiency, total earnings, net payoff, or group welfare as primary outcomes—this is particularly strong in canonical lab PGGs (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Masclet et al., 2003; Ambrus & Greiner, 2012).
- **Adjacent:** Some studies focus primarily on contribution/cooperation rates and only infer about efficiency through behavioral measures or present only partial payoff outcomes.
- **Weak/None:** Many otherwise informative studies report only on norm adherence, punishment rates, or non-payoff behavioral outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (directly matching ‘efficiency’):** Many top-cited lab experiments and key theory papers report group payoff, average earnings, or normalized efficiency as the main variable of interest (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Markussen et al., 2014; Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Sutter et al., 2010).
- **Closely related payoff concepts:** Total group welfare, coins generated, and surplus also appear and are typically normalized by the maximum possible earnings, which matches the prediction construct.
- **Contribution/cooperation rate alone:** Some highly relevant papers report only participation, contribution frequency, or cooperation rate, requiring caution—they are not proxies for efficiency unless mapped explicitly (see e.g., Weng & Carlsson, 2015; Gächter & Thöni, 2005).
- **Punishment frequency/type:** Several studies report frequencies, structure, or motives for punishment and counter-punishment but not direct payoff/efficiency impacts. These are crucial moderators for mapping design conditions to efficiency but are not efficiency outcomes themselves (e.g., Nikiforakis, 2008; Fehr et al., 2008).
- **Norm compliance and social preferences:** Some theoretical and behavioral outcome studies measure mechanisms underlying cooperation but lack efficiency data, providing context or explaining observed heterogeneity.
- **Behavioral and field-adjacent outcomes:** In field and cross-cultural studies, group payoff is sometimes measured as a field outcome (e.g., tree density, resource extraction), though mapping to normalized efficiency depends on available data (Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical regularities (standard lab PGGs):**
- **Punishment often increases efficiency, but effect size is context-dependent:** In canonical lab PGGs (4–5 players, 10–30 rounds, MPCR 0.4–0.5), enabling costly peer punishment reliably *increases group efficiency relative to control* when baseline efficiency is low—magnitude often 10–20% improvement, particularly in later rounds (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Masklet et al., 2003; Sutter et al., 2010; Fudenberg & Pathak, 2010; Page et al., 2005).
- **Costs of punishment can offset gains:** The increase in contributions generally exceeds the raw efficiency gains because the cost expended on punishment by group members partly or wholly offsets the welfare gain from increased cooperation (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Masclet et al., 2003; Nikiforakis, 2008).
- **Effectiveness of punishment technology and cost structure moderates efficiency impact:** Low-cost, high-impact punishment (or centralized/delegated punishment) can produce substantially higher efficiency gains than peer-to-peer punishment with high cost or low leverage, while strong or misdirected punishment may be detrimental (Sutter et al., 2010; Andreoni & Gee, 2012).
- **Reward can outperform punishment when available:** High-leverage reward mechanisms are sometimes more efficient than punishment (Sutter et al., 2010; Andreoni & Harbaugh, 2003).
- **Endogenous institution choice amplifies efficiency:** Allowing groups to *vote* on whether to adopt punishment dramatically increases both contributions and efficiency relative to exogenously imposed punishment (Markussen et al., 2014; Putterman et al., 2011; Ertan et al., 2009).
- **Targeting matters:** Efficiency gains are largest when punishment is restricted to low contributors (free riders). When punishment can be aimed at high contributors (“antisocial punishment”), efficiency can decrease below control (Ertan et al., 2009; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015; Fehr et al., 2008).
- **Normative/cultural context affects direction and size:** In certain cultures or subject pools with high antisocial or spiteful punishment, the introduction of punishment reduces group efficiency (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Fehr et al., 2008).
- **Field and dynamic designs show even greater variability:** In field PGG variants or dynamic public goods, punishment effects on efficiency are smaller, absent, or even negative; context (trust, legitimacy, endogeneity of rule choice) becomes critical (Vollan, 2008; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015; Gächter et al., 2017).
- **Monitoring quality is a strong moderator:** When monitoring is imperfect or noisy, standard punishment can lower efficiency below control; only very strong punishment restores efficiency (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; ABREU et al., 1991). Timing of information can similarly affect outcomes.

**Theoretical predictions:**
- **General prediction of increased efficiency with punishment:** Core models predict enabling punishment supports higher or full efficiency if design parameters (punishment cost, MPCR, group size) and player social preferences (inequity aversion, normativity) are favorable (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Ellison, 1994; Kandori, 1992; Wolitzky, 2013).
- **Mechanism qualification:** Community enforcement is effective where information about defections is public; in large or anonymous groups with only private cues, punishment is less effective and efficiency gains are limited (Kandori, 1992).
- **Punishment/exclusion design matters:** Harsh forms of punishment (e.g., permanent ostracism) may not enhance efficiency, while forgiving/temporary sanctions can (Ali & Miller, 2016).

**Moderators identified empirically and theoretically:**
- **Group size:** Larger groups generally see weaker punishment efficacy and lower efficiency gains (Camera & Casari, 2009).
- **Round horizon:** Repeated games with finite or known rounds often observe end-game effects; punishment raises efficiency more in earlier and mid-rounds than in final rounds.
- **Communication:** Communication alone increases efficiency, and communication *plus* punishment is often more effective than either mechanism alone (Bochet & Putterman, 2009; Ostrom, 2000).
- **Contribution mechanism:** All-or-nothing vs. continuous contribution interacts with the effects of punishment—most lab evidence pertains to continuous contributions.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

The literature supports the following guidance for the downstream prediction task:

**(A) Baseline control efficiency is a strong predictor:** 
- Where control (no punishment) efficiency is low, enabling punishment will *usually* (but not always) increase efficiency, *provided* design and context are favorable (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sutter et al., 2010).

**(B) Effect size is moderated—and in some cases directionally reversed—by key design dimensions:**
- **Punishment cost and leverage:** Efficiency gains are highest when punishment is low-cost and highly effective for deterring free riding. High-cost/low-impact punishment can reduce or negate efficiency gains (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Markussen et al., 2014).
- **Targeting restrictions:** Rules or institutions that restrict punishment to defectors/free riders maximize efficiency gains; allowing antisocial punishment, counter-punishment, or unrestricted targeting can *reduce* efficiency (Nikiforakis, 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Ertan et al., 2009).
- **Institutional choice/endogeneity:** Efficiency gains are greater when punishment is chosen endogenously (e.g., by group vote) rather than imposed externally (Markussen et al., 2014; Sutter et al., 2010).
- **Communication:** The presence of chat, promises, or credible communication can amplify the efficiency effects of punishment by reducing need for use and misdirection (Bochet & Putterman, 2009).
- **Monitoring and information:** Imperfect monitoring or delayed/noisy information can undermine punishment's effectiveness on efficiency (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; ABREU et al., 1991).
- **Cultural and normative context:** Antisocial punishment, spite, or lack of buy-in to rules can flip the effect negative, particularly outside of standard lab populations (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Vollan, 2008; Fehr et al., 2008; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015).
- **Player heterogeneity and group composition:** Normative conflict (e.g., asymmetric returns, heterogeneity in preferences) increases risk of feuding/counter-punishment, reducing net efficiency (Nikiforakis et al., 2012).
- **Reward mechanisms:** When both reward and punishment are possible, or when reward is highly leveraged/cost-effective, efficiency can be higher than with punishment alone (Sutter et al., 2010).

**(C) Mapping to design dimensions for prediction:**
- **Directly informed:** Player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost, punishment technology/magnitude, punishment existence, reward existence, chat, all-or-nothing, and (in some cases) show_other_summaries and show_punishment_id.
- **Indirect/contextual:** Default contribution framing, show_n_rounds, and detailed feedback features are sparsely tested.
- **Essential moderators often missing:** E.g., prevalence of antisocial punishment, buy-in to rules, or degree of normative conflict often not captured simply by standard design parameters.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (strong empirical/theoretical basis):**
- **player_count:** Nearly all experimental studies specify group size; effect on efficiency is explicitly modeled and empirically tested (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Camera & Casari, 2009).
- **num_rounds:** Number and structure of rounds are standard variables; repeated game effects are well documented.
- **mpcr:** Marginal per-capita return is a central explanatory variable for efficiency prediction (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kosfeld et al., 2009).
- **punishment_cost and punishment_tech/magnitude:** Most empirical studies report these parameters and show they crucially moderate efficiency outcomes.
- **all_or_nothing:** Both all-or-nothing and continuous games are well studied but most payoff data come from continuous settings.
- **chat:** Effect of chat/communication is studied, but most directly in relation to contribution rather than efficiency unless combined with punishment.
- **punishment_exists/reward_exists:** Central manipulation in more than half of studies.
- **show_other_summaries:** Feedback visibility is manipulated in some studies and theory papers.

**Indirectly informed or variable:**
- **reward_cost, reward_tech/magnitude:** Some studies present joint reward and punishment treatments, but coverage is notably less comprehensive.
- **show_n_rounds/show_punishment_id:** Occasionally manipulated (e.g., Fudenberg & Pathak, 2010), these variables are much less studied.
- **default_contrib:** Very rarely varied; typically, the default is opt-in or not discussed.

**Missing or largely untested:**
- High-resolution feedback structures, technological details beyond punishment/reward magnitude, and certain framing variables receive very limited explicit empirical testing.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Most evidence is from lab rather than field:** While rigorous and parameterized, laboratory conditions may poorly reflect field diversity, especially regarding antisocial punishment, cultural variation, or institutional legitimacy.
- **Payoff data do not always address all design dimensions:** Several dimensions relevant in natural settings (e.g., identity salience, group selection, buy-in, complex monitoring, leadership structure) are rarely experimentally varied or reported.
- **Efficiency is not always the primary outcome:** Some important work reports only behavioral outcomes or contribution rates; mapping those to efficiency can be speculative and may mislead predictions if punishment is costly or misdirected.
- **Contextual moderators:** Prevalence of antisocial punishment, group heterogeneity, endogenous versus exogenous institutions, and field implementation fidelity can each dramatically flip the sign or size of punishment’s effect, but are often not directly encoded in recorded design parameters.
- **Design dimension coverage is uneven:** Strong evidence for punishment cost, leverage, and existence; weak empirical evidence for default contribution, feedback detail, or social/punisher/reward identity visibility.
- **Dynamic/public goods and field settings produce divergent findings:** In dynamic or growing-endowment games and real-world settings, punishment is often less effective or negative for efficiency, particularly in the presence of initial inequality or weak rule legitimacy (Gächter et al., 2017; Vollan, 2008).
- **Baseline control efficiency is necessary but not sufficient for prediction:** The efficiency jump from punishment depends as much on context and design interaction as on control group performance.

---

**In summary:** This literature strongly supports the prediction that enabling suitable punishment in lab PGGs with low control efficiency generally increases treatment efficiency, often in the range of a 10–20% improvement—*if* the punishment is well-targeted, not too costly, not subject to antisocial misuse, and either chosen by the group or supported by credible institutional buy-in. However, effects can be null or negative in heterogeneous, contentious, or field-like contexts, or when punishment is misdirected, costly, or combined with poor communication or monitoring. Most design parameters crucial to prediction are well-covered, but some aspects of information flow, communication, and institutional context remain under-explored or only qualitatively informed.
