# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is both broad and deep, with numerous empirical (primarily laboratory experiments) and theoretical (simulation/modeling) studies directly addressing standard and variant public goods games (PGGs) with and without punishment. Several studies report on group efficiency or closely related payoff-based outcomes, while others focus on contribution rates or non-payoff behaviors. The set includes foundational canonical PGG experiments, variants (e.g., threshold, snowdrift, negative externality, resource management), theory and simulation papers exploring moderators, and a wide range of studies on adjacent or contextually relevant mechanisms (reputation, network structure, centralization, communication).

The empirical papers are predominantly lab-based, with designs closely matching the typical PGG framework: repeated rounds, varying group sizes, known or hidden end points, continuous or all-or-nothing contributions, and manipulation of punishment institution parameters (cost, effectiveness, centralization, information, etc.). Some field experiments and theory/simulation studies extend the range of applicability. Most empirical studies focus explicitly on efficiency or group payoff; several theory papers also provide analytic results for efficiency under various institutional and parameter conditions. There is substantial coverage of variants in punishment institution (peer vs. central; endogenous vs. exogenous; deterministic vs. probabilistic) and contextual features (information, group heterogeneity, matching protocol).

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance** dominates: Many papers use standard linear PGGs or minor variants (e.g., all-or-nothing, threshold).  
- **Close relevance** includes snowdrift games, volunteer's dilemma variants, and other multiplayer cooperation dilemmas.  
- **Adjacent relevance** covers two-player PDs, resource games, and norm enforcement dilemmas.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact relevance** is high: Many studies systematically compare punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions, manipulating punishment cost, impact, and institutional design.  
- **Close and adjacent:** Some consider reward, ostracism, or informal sanctions (moral judgments, reputation), or examine punishment-like mechanisms in non-PGG settings.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact and close relevance:** Several papers report group efficiency as the primary or a major outcome, enabling direct mapping to the downstream prediction target.  
- **Adjacent or weak:** Many studies report only on contribution rates, cooperation, or behavioral responses, offering indirect evidence for efficiency.  
- **None:** A minority focus solely on behaviors or attitudes without relevant payoff or efficiency outcomes.

Overall, the literature provides substantial, directly relevant evidence for most aspects of the prediction task, but some institutional and contextual variations introduce caveats.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related Outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, welfare, surplus, total earnings/coins):**
- **Direct measurement:** Many core empirical PGG studies report group efficiency (ratio of observed payoff to maximum possible with full cooperation).
- **Comprehensive reporting:** Some also provide breakdowns by treatment (punishment enabled/disabled), punishment cost, round, and subgroup.
- **Robustness:** Several theoretical papers explicitly define and analyze efficiency or welfare as the outcome of interest under intervention conditions.
- **Payoff-adjacent:** A subset of studies provide ‘adjacent’ payoff data (e.g., group gain, profit, or probability of achieving targets) but not standardized efficiency metrics.

**Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates:** Widely reported—across all papers—but alone cannot determine efficiency where punishment is costly.
- **Punishment frequency, retaliatory dynamics, norm compliance:** Commonly measured, sometimes providing mechanistic insight for interpreting payoff changes.
- **Attitudinal/psychological outcomes:** Fairness, anger, legitimacy, revenge/equality motives (especially in adjacent, non-PGG or non-payoff-focused studies).

**Explicit distinction:** Several highly relevant papers emphasize that higher cooperation/contribution rates under punishment do not necessarily mean higher efficiency because the costliness of punishment can offset gains (e.g., Simpson et al., 2017; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Jiang et al., 2015).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Effects in Standard PGGs:**
- **Punishment increases contributions but not always efficiency:** In many canonical settings, enabling costly peer punishment substantially raises contribution rates but does not always improve or can even reduce efficiency due to costly and sometimes misdirected punishment (Simpson et al., 2017; Harrell & Simpson, 2016).
- **Institutional design is crucial:** Efficiency impact depends on how punishment is enabled (peer vs. central vs. leader, presence of support/incentives, quality of information) (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Dai et al., 2015; Ozono et al., 2017; Nicklisch et al., 2016; Markussen et al., 2016; Kube et al., 2015).
- **Cost and targeting:** High punishment cost, error-prone or antisocial punishment, or decentralized punishment under imperfect information often reduce efficiency relative to control (Simpson et al., 2017; Markussen et al., 2016; Ouss & Peysakhovich, 2015; Dekel et al., 2017).
- **Centralized punishment or audit-based deterrence:** Often more effective at increasing efficiency, especially if deterrent and with minimal error (Dai et al., 2015; Kube et al., 2015).
- **Moral/non-material sanctions:** Costless moral judgments or exclusion can increase both cooperation and efficiency more than costly punishment (Simpson et al., 2017; Nakamaru & Yokoyama, 2014).
- **Endogenous sanction institutions:** Voting-based/endogenously selected punishment regimes can lead to higher efficiency than exogenously imposed ones, contingent on buy-in and group expectations (Dickinson et al., 2015; Kube et al., 2015).

**Moderators and Contextual Factors:**
- **Group structure and composition:** Large group size, heterogeneity (especially with minorities harmed by the public good), or conflicting norms can attenuate or reverse efficiency gains from punishment (Gangadharan et al., 2017; Dekel et al., 2017; Vasconcelos et al., 2015).
- **Punishment technology (cost-to-impact, abundance of resources):** Low-cost, high-impact, or abundant punishment tends to increase efficiency; strict punishment is not always superior to graduated, depending on group structure (Jiang et al., 2015; Ozono et al., 2017; Shimao & Nakamaru, 2013).
- **Information structure:** Efficiency gains require that punishment is well targeted (Nicklisch et al., 2016; Markussen et al., 2016). High noise or imperfect monitoring erodes the efficacy of both decentralized and centralized punishment.
- **Time horizon and matching:** Punishment's positive impact on efficiency grows over repeated rounds and may depend on fixed matching (Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017).

**Variants and Counterexamples:**
- **Harmed minority or competing interests:** In games with a harmed minority, punishment may lower efficiency (Dekel et al., 2017).
- **Combination with reward and communication:** Sometimes reward (rather than punishment) or communication can achieve similar or greater efficiency improvements, especially in homogeneous groups or where reward is cost-effective (Gangadharan et al., 2017; Dai et al., 2015).
- **Detrimental punishment design:** Over-punishment, especially when costs are externalized, error-prone, or uncoordinated, can reduce efficiency relative to no punishment (Ouss & Peysakhovich, 2015; Grimalda et al., 2016; Jiang et al., 2015).

**Theory and Simulation:**
- **Robust theoretical support** for efficiency gains from punishment mechanisms, especially under optimal institutional/cost parameters or with norm internalization (Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Vasconcelos et al., 2015; Powers & Lehmann, 2013).
- **Critical importance of design parameters:** The effect of punishment is not monotonic; efficiency increases only if punishment is tuned appropriately and institutions minimize retaliation, error, and overuse (Okada et al., 2015; Stewart et al., 2016; Nicklisch et al., 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Principle:**  
*The majority of directly relevant studies support the following for standard and close-variant PGGs:*
- **Enabling peer punishment increases cooperation/contributions almost universally, but the effect on efficiency (treatment efficiency relative to control) is highly sensitive to punishment cost, effectiveness, targeting, and institutional context.**
- **Costly punishment often leads to net-zero or negative change in efficiency** unless punishment is highly effective, cheap, and/or centralized, group size is small, information is accurate, and group composition is homogeneous.
- **For settings matching canonical lab PGGs (continuous contribution, moderate group size, standard punishment tech):**
  - If control efficiency is low, and punishment is not too costly/ineffective, **treatment efficiency will typically improve**, but may only approach, not exceed, the control's efficiency where the latter is already high.
  - **If control efficiency is already high, severe or broad punishment can reduce efficiency** because punishment costs outweigh the marginal gain in contributions (Jiang et al., 2015).
- **Centralized or leader punishment typically yields greater efficiency gains than peer punishment**, particularly if the leader is prosocial and the institution deters ineffective or antisocial use (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Kube et al., 2015).
- **Punishment that is symbolic, costless, or non-material (e.g., approval/disapproval or ostracism) may raise both cooperation and efficiency** beyond that achieved by costly punishment (Simpson et al., 2017; Nakamaru & Yokoyama, 2014).
- **Contextual factors (heterogeneity, information uncertainty, possibility of corruption, social norms) can limit or reverse efficiency effects**—in these cases, punishment may have no effect or may reduce efficiency (Dekel et al., 2017; Markussen et al., 2016; Abdallah et al., 2014).
- **The best empirical support links design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, information, matching, and control efficiency) to differential efficiency gains.**

**For prediction using the 14 design dimensions:**
- **Direct evidence is strongest for player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, all_or_nothing, chat, and show_n_rounds.**
- **Variables such as default_contrib, show_punishment_id, and show_other_summaries are more contextually addressed and less well quantified.**
- **Reward dimensions are informed in a subset of studies comparing reward, punishment, or both.**
- **Effect modifiers not in standard dimensions (e.g., population structure, information accuracy, institution choice method, cultural context) are also relevant based on repeated findings.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (supported by multiple exact/close outcome studies):**
- **player_count:** Extensively explored (effects of small vs. large groups; efficiency gains from punishment attenuate as group size increases unless strong institutions are present).
- **num_rounds:** Reports cover both short- and long-run effects, showing that longer repeated interactions enable greater punishment learning and efficiency improvements.
- **mpcr:** Many studies manipulate return rates, showing that low mpcrs make deterrent punishment more beneficial, but too low mpcr may still preclude efficiency gains.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Core moderators—efficiency gains only occur when punishment is effective (high impact per unit cost), not too costly, and well targeted.
- **all_or_nothing:** Explored in several studies (threshold/step-level vs. continuous contribution) with evidence that discrete choices may amplify or dilute punishment effects depending on institution.
- **chat, communication:** Direct impact on efficiency is well measured—communication often boosts cooperation and efficiency, and can substitute for or enhance punishment efficacy.
- **Centralization (peer vs. leader/centralized punishment):** Several studies systematically compare these regimes and their efficiency effects.

**Indirectly or contextually discussed:**
- **default_contrib:** Only occasionally manipulated as framing; little evidence on efficiency impact.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries:** Moderately addressed: information about game length, feedback about group behavior moderate learning, expectation formation, and possibly efficiency.
- **show_punishment_id:** Looked at in terms of reputational and deterrence effects, but not systematically reported for efficiency.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Where studied, rewards often outperform or complement punishment in raising efficiency, especially when also cost-effective or endogenous.

**Sparse or effectively missing:**
- **Game/participant interface features (presentation/framing), detail-level show_punishment_id.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Not all studies report group efficiency or directly comparable payoff outcomes:** Many report only cooperation/contribution rates, which can mislead about net efficiency where punishment is costly or misapplied.
- **Moderators beyond standard design dimensions:** Some critical factors (institutional novelty, norms, pre-existing compliance, cultural context, information accuracy, heterogeneity, and framing) are only occasionally represented in design dimensions but frequently moderate the efficiency effect.
- **Variant structures:** Results from threshold games, snowdrift, or anti-social punishment settings may not generalize to canonical linear PGGs.
- **Potential overrepresentation of small-group, Western-lab settings:** Empirical regularities may differ in large, diverse, or real-world groups.
- **Diverse punishment institutions:** Punishment designs (peer, centralized, probabilistic, severity, cost-externalization, ability to target/reward, etc.) are varied, and results are sometimes highly sensitive to design.
- **Unresolved ambiguity and disagreement:** Even among highly relevant studies, there is disagreement in cases of high punishment cost, error-prone punishment, presence of harmed minorities, or strong confounds (e.g., externalized punishment cost, competition structure).
- **Few studies systematically vary all key dimensions at once:** Most manipulate a subset, making high-dimensional generalization challenging.
- **Some design dimensions (notably, reward tech/cost when reward is present, fine-grained identity visibility, specific interface features) are underexplored or not systematically included in efficiency analysis.

---
**In summary:** The literature set provides strong, nuanced evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in PGGs depends critically on institutional design, cost-effectiveness, error targeting, information, and group/contextual features, with most design dimensions directly or indirectly supported. Prediction should consider baseline (control) efficiency, all core design dimensions, and recognize that efficiency gains are not automatic with punishment—costly, poorly targeted, or error-prone punishment can negate or reverse gains despite higher contributions. Non-material sanctions, communication, and reward mechanisms, under the right conditions, can sometimes outperform costly punishment in raising efficiency. Prediction models based on this literature should be attentive to context, parameterizations, and the possibility for both positive and negative efficiency effects depending on institutional detail.
