# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set contains a diverse mix of **empirical (experimental, some observational)** and **theoretical/review** papers. Empirical work, especially lab experiments, is well represented, focusing largely on standard public goods games (PGGs) and key variations (maintenance/provision, partner vs. stranger matching, institutional punishment, ostracism). Several cross-societal or meta-analytic reviews provide broader context. There is a modest presence of adjacent or analog games (ultimatum/trust games), simulations, and ethological/observational studies, which are less directly relevant.
  
For the **downstream prediction task**—forecasting treatment efficiency from control efficiency plus game design dimensions (with/without punishment)—the evidence base is *moderately strong but uneven*:
- **Direct, quantitative payoff/efficiency outcomes** are present in a relatively small subset (notably Gächter et al., 2017; Choi & Ahn, 2013; Feinberg et al., 2014).
- **Most studies** focus on **non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequencies, trust, norm compliance), with only indirect implications for efficiency.
- Theoretical and review papers synthesize mechanisms and moderators but seldom provide specific efficiency estimates.
- Key gaps remain in covering the full set of *14 design dimensions* for prediction, and in reporting consistent *efficiency metrics*.

# 2) Task Relevance

### a) *PGG or Variant*  
**Relevance: "exact" to "close" for core literature; "adjacent/weak" for others.**
- **Exact relevance:** The majority of empirical and theoretical papers directly study standard linear PGGs or highly comparable variants (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; Choi & Ahn, 2013; Balliet & Van Lange, 2013).
- **Close relevance:** Several studies use repeated social dilemmas or institutional punishment PGGs.
- **Adjacent/weak:** Papers on trust/ultimatum/prisoner’s dilemma or non-economic field designs are less directly applicable.

### b) *Punishment or Sanctions*  
**Relevance: "exact" for most experimental/theoretical PGG studies; "close/adjacent" in others; "none" where only social image, rewards, or trust are considered.**
- **Exact:** The bulk of the literature manipulates or models *peer or institutional punishment* as a treatment vs. control.
- **Close/adjacent:** Some studies cover ostracism, gossip, reputation, or sanctions via exclusion or feedback (e.g., Feinberg et al., 2014; Diekmann et al., 2014).
- **None/weak:** Pure reward-only or communication-only studies offer little on punishment.

### c) *Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome*  
**Relevance: "exact" in a minority; "close" or "adjacent" where behavior is the primary outcome and efficiency can only be inferred.**
- **Exact:** Only a handful present *group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff* as a primary or explicitly reported outcome (Gächter et al., 2017; Feinberg et al., 2014; Mak et al., 2015 [without punishment]).
- **Close:** Many report *group contribution rates* or *monetary outcomes* tightly linked to efficiency (Barclay & Raihani, 2016; Choi & Ahn, 2013).
- **Adjacent:** Most focus on non-payoff outcomes (e.g., behavior, psychological processes, social norms), requiring assumptions to relate them to efficiency.

**Summary:** *Directly relevant evidence is present but not dominant; much of the literature provides only indirect or inferential contributions to the prediction task.*

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

### **Payoff/Efficiency-Related Outcomes (Direct)**
- **Efficiency/group payoff/total earnings:** *Explicitly reported* in some key empirical studies (Gächter et al., 2017; Feinberg et al., 2014; Mak et al., 2015).
- **Monetary outcomes/trust game transfers:** Used as close proxies for group efficiency (Barclay & Raihani, 2016).
- **Market efficiency/successful trades:** In reputation/trust contexts (Li & Xiao, 2014; Diekmann et al., 2014).

### **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Most Common)**
- **Contribution or cooperation rate:** How much participants contribute, not always mapped to payoff (Balliet & Van Lange, 2013).
- **Punishment/reward frequency, antisocial punishment, norm compliance, retaliation, trust, and reputational effects:** Frequently reported, but *not* direct efficiency outcomes.

### **Hybrid or Indirect Outcomes**
- Behavioral outcomes often *suggest* likely changes in efficiency, but require caution (e.g., more contribution does not always yield higher efficiency if punishment costs dominate).
- **Absence of direct efficiency reporting** means *most studies infer efficiency changes* from increases in contribution rates or cooperation, sometimes supplemented with monetary outcome data.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### **Punishment’s Effect on Efficiency**
- **Most standard lab PGGs:** *Enabling peer punishment increases cooperation and (often) efficiency*, sometimes to near-optimal levels, **especially in repeated interactions and with moderate-cost, effective punishment** (Gächter et al., 2017; Choi & Ahn, 2013; Parks et al., 2013).
- **Punishment can decrease efficiency** if *punishment costs are high, retribution/antisocial punishment is frequent, or retaliation occurs* (Kurzban et al., 2015; Sylwester et al., 2013; Rand & Nowak, 2013).
- **Contextual moderators:** Efficacy of punishment is affected by:
    - **Punishment cost:** *Lower-cost punishment* more likely to produce net efficiency gains (Kurzban et al., 2015).
    - **Number of rounds:** *Repeated games* yield higher post-punishment efficiency (Barclay & Raihani, 2016; Balliet & Van Lange, 2013).
    - **Group size:** Sometimes dampens violation reduction effect (Parks et al., 2013).
    - **Social/reputational context:** *Identifiability, communication, and trust* increase treatment efficiency further.
    - **Game framing (provision/maintenance):** Punishment effectiveness is robust across both (Gächter et al., 2017).
    - **Institutional vs. peer punishment:** Institutional punishment is more robust but vulnerable to corruption/bribery, which *can invert the usual positive effect* (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).

### **Rewards and Alternative Sanctions**
- **Reward mechanisms** can equal or sometimes exceed punishment for increasing cooperation and efficiency, and involve fewer negative side effects (Parks et al., 2013; Rand & Nowak, 2013).
- **Ostracism/exclusion or gossip + reputation** have similarly *positive effects on efficiency*, especially in the long run (Feinberg et al., 2014).
  
### **Complicating Mechanisms**
- **Antisocial punishment:** Often reduces or eliminates efficiency gains, especially when the environment incentivizes punishment of contributors or retaliation (Kurzban et al., 2015; Sylwester et al., 2013).
- **Corruption and enforcement failures:** Efficiency gains from punishment depend on the absence of corruption and strong governance (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).

### **Empirical vs. Theoretical Synthesis**
- *Empirical lab studies* document both *large increases in efficiency (sometimes reaching ~optimal)* and *cases where punishment is costly enough to lower total payoffs* despite increased cooperation.
- *Theoretical papers* highlight mechanisms and necessary conditions for positive efficiency effects, and warn of context-dependent reversals.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

## **Empirically-Grounded Guidance**
- **When the control game is a standard repeated linear PGG and the only change is enabling moderate-cost, effective peer punishment:** *Expect a substantial increase* in efficiency, often approaching or achieving optimal levels (Gächter et al., 2017; Choi & Ahn, 2013; Parks et al., 2013).
- **The following design dimensions are especially predictive (informed by literature):**
    - **Player count:** Small groups (3-4) show more robust positive efficiency effects; effect weakens or becomes less clear as group size increases (Parks et al., 2013).
    - **Num rounds:** Longer or repeated games facilitate learning and reputation, hence larger positive effects (Barclay & Raihani, 2016; Balliet & Van Lange, 2013).
    - **Punishment cost/tech:** Higher costs and weak technologies reduce (and can invert) the efficiency gain from punishment (Kurzban et al., 2015).
    - **Chat/communication:** Can boost efficiency—with or without punishment (Mak et al., 2015).
    - **All_or_nothing contributions, MPCR, punishment/reward options, and information structure** all interact with the above.
- **When institutional punishment is corrupted (e.g., bribery allowed):** *Punishment can reduce efficiency*—corruption can nullify or reverse efficiency gains (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).

## **Inferred and Conditional Guidance**
- **If control efficiency is already high (with no punishment):** The *marginal improvement* from adding punishment may be limited, depending on the mechanisms (fewer free riders to target).
- **If control efficiency is low:** The *relative gain* tends to be large; efficiency can often be restored to near maximum under favorable conditions (Gächter et al., 2017).
- **Behavioral outcomes alone** (cooperation/contribution rates) usually *track* efficiency, but beware situations where punishment cost overshadows increased contributions—these can lead to no net gain or even efficiency loss (Kurzban et al., 2015; Rand & Nowak, 2013).

## **Application to Prediction Task**
- **Quantitative prediction is best for well-specified, small-to-moderate group PGGs with moderate-cost peer punishment and no corruption.** In these, *if control efficiency is, e.g., 0.3, post-punishment efficiency can plausibly rise to 0.7–1.0* (supported by Gächter et al., 2017; Choi & Ahn, 2013).
- **Prediction accuracy decreases** as design deviates from standard repeated lab PGGs, or as additional mechanisms (institutional punishment, peer reward, possibility of corruption/bribery, communication, ostracism) are enabled.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

### **Directly Informed by Evidence**
- **player_count**: Frequently reported and manipulated; effect of group size on punishment efficacy is discussed.
- **num_rounds**: Regularly varied; strong evidence of interaction with punishment impact.
- **mpcr**: Explicitly specified in many experiments; findings link higher mpcr to better treatment efficiency.
- **punishment_cost**: Vital dimension, directly manipulated and associated with efficiency outcomes; higher costs reduce net benefit.
- **punishment_tech**: (Effectiveness per coin spent): Specified in several studies, especially for peer punishment.
- **all_or_nothing**: Some studies use continuous, others binary contribution models—efficiency effects interpreted across both.
- **reward_exists**: Comparisons with reward alone or combined with punishment are informative.
  
### **Indirectly or Contextually Discussed**
- **chat**: Impact of communication is shown in a few studies (e.g., Mak et al., 2015).
- **show_other_summaries; show_n_rounds; show_punishment_id**: Information/reputation structures are commonly noted but rarely isolated in empirical tests.
- **default_contrib**: Contribution framing is occasionally mentioned but not a central variable in most studies.

### **Effectively Missing or Sparse**
- **reward_cost; reward_tech**: Seldom manipulated independently; most findings focus on the punishment side.
- **show_punishment_id**: Rarely varied as an independent factor.
- **Combined/multi-factor interactions** (e.g., how chat and punishment interact) are not tested systematically.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Measured outcomes are often behavioral, not payoff/efficiency:** *Many studies do not report efficiency or total payoff*, requiring inference from contribution rates or other behaviors. When punishment is costly or antisocial, higher contribution rates may not mean higher efficiency.
- **Limited coverage of all 14 prediction dimensions:** Core dimensions (player count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, mpcr) are fairly well covered, but others (e.g., reward_cost, show_punishment_id) are sparse.
- **Heterogeneity and context sensitivity:** Punishment effects vary by cultural context, trust levels, matching protocol (partner/stranger), and susceptibility to corruption or antisocial motives; *effects are not universal*.
- **Few studies match the exact prediction scenario:** e.g., reporting pre/post efficiency in both control and punishment treatments, for the full range of design parameters.
- **Non-payoff designs and adjacent games:** Large portions of the literature are only indirectly relevant—using analogs such as the ultimatum or trust game, or focusing on psychological mechanisms rather than efficiency.
- **No comprehensive meta-analytic quantitative synthesis of efficiency outcomes for PGGs with/without punishment.**
- **Evidence about counterproductive punishment (e.g., when punishment cost dominates, or retaliation/antisocial punishment is frequent) is mostly theoretical or piecemeal.**
- **External validity:** Most empirical findings come from university lab settings; generalizability to field or real-world environments is questionable.

---

**Summary:**  
- The literature provides *moderately strong, but incomplete* empirical support for predicting the efficiency benefits of enabling punishment in repeated lab-based public goods games, especially with small groups, moderate punishment costs, and effective punishment technologies.  
- **Prediction is most reliable when control efficiency is low and punishment is well-structured, but care must be taken**: in some cases, especially with high costs, antisocial punishment, or corruption, adding punishment may not improve efficiency and could even reduce it.  
- Most design dimensions influencing predictions are covered, but gaps exist in explicit reporting of efficiency outcomes, manipulation of all prediction-relevant dimensions, and coverage of more complex or real-world social environments.  

**Careful modeling should treat the predicted efficiency gain from punishment as context-dependent—large in many lab scenarios, but not universal.**
