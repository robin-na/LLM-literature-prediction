# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set consists of a substantial number (98) of papers, all theoretical in nature—primarily grounded in mathematical modeling, agent-based simulations, and review/theoretical arguments. There are no empirical or experimental studies directly reporting observed laboratory or field data. The papers range from exact analyses of public goods games (PGGs) with explicit peer punishment features, through close variants (e.g., common pool resource games, iterated prisoner’s dilemmas), to more adjacent domains and conceptual discussions. The breadth of the set is wide, spanning not only public goods games but also various related social dilemma frameworks and institutional theory. 

Most findings are derived from formal models rather than direct measurement, with a strong emphasis on mechanistic explanations, parameter sweeps, and comparative statics. While this allows for careful dissection of causal mechanisms, it also limits the ability to report empirical effect sizes or direct cross-study aggregation of quantitative results. 

In sum, the evidence base is theoretically rich and diverse in scope, directly addressing PGGs with punishment in many cases, but notably lacking empirical effect size measures for the exact prediction target.

# 2) Task Relevance

**`pgg_or_variant`**  
- **Exact:** Many papers are directly about public goods games (PGGs) with structures that match the prediction target, often including design dimensions such as player count, rounds, mpcr, and peer punishment (e.g., Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Eldakar et al., 2007; Sigmund et al., 2011).
- **Close:** A significant subset analyzes close counterparts (e.g., common pool resource games, iterated or spatial PDs, CPR games, partner-choosing mutualisms), which share relevant features and insights around punishment and group payoff, but may not map directly onto every design dimension.
- **Adjacent/Weak:** There are numerous adjacent contributions—discussing behavioral mechanisms, alternative dilemmas, or offering conceptual frameworks—that reference but do not strictly model or measure PGGs.

**`punishment_or_sanctions`**  
- **Exact:** The bulk of relevant reports explicitly model or discuss punishment/sanctioning as peer punishment, pool punishment, or institutional forms in social dilemmas.
- **Close:** Discussions of reputation, reward mechanisms, partner choice, or indirect reciprocity sometimes serve as analogs or complements to punishment, but are not the focus.
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Some analyses center on norm compliance, monitoring, institutional design, or social/cultural sanctions not instantiated as direct payoff-reducing punishment.

**`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`**  
- **Exact:** Several papers provide explicit analyses of group efficiency, total payoff, surplus, or welfare (e.g., Wang et al., 2010; Dejong et al., 2008; Sigmund et al., 2011).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Many discuss contribution rates, norm compliance, or cooperation rates only, which do not directly answer to efficiency but are mechanistically important.
- **Weak/None:** A subset is exclusively behavioral/mechanistic in outcome, offering little or no direct evidence for efficiency or payoff.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes:**  
   - Direct measures and analyses of group efficiency (ratio of actual total payoff to cooperative optimum), total group payoff, or welfare are presented in a meaningful minority of the theoretical papers (e.g., Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Eldakar et al., 2007; Bednar, 2006; Sigmund et al., 2011; Okada & Bingham, 2008).
   - Several analyses provide parameterized predictions of equilibrium payoffs—either as mathematical fixed points or comparative statics across game designs.
   - Some others discuss average payoffs as a secondary outcome while focusing on the prevalence of strategies or behavioral dynamics.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
   - Many studies focus primarily on cooperation/contribution rates, frequency of norm compliance, prevalence of punishers/cooperators/defectors, network or strategy equilibrium types, or other behavioral metrics.
   - These outcomes are important mechanistically but do not equate to group efficiency; in numerous models, increases in cooperation may not translate to net efficiency gains if punishment costs are high (e.g., Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
   - A few analyses stress that punishment can increase compliance or contributions but lower total welfare due to the costs involved (e.g., Jaffe, 2004).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Findings:**
- **Punishment often increases cooperation and average payoffs/efficiency in public goods games compared to no-punishment baselines,** especially when the control efficiency is low (Eldakar et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2010; Dejong et al., 2008; Sigmund et al., 2011).
- **The magnitude and even the sign of the effect are highly contingent on game design parameters:**  
   - **Punishment Cost and Effectiveness:** Lower punishment costs and greater punishment effectiveness improve the efficiency gain from punishment (Okada & Bingham, 2008; Deng et al., 2012; Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
   - **Group Size and Rounds:** Smaller groups, and games with multiple rounds or voluntary participation, more reliably see efficiency gains. In large groups, effects can be diluted or even reversed absent institutional mechanisms (Dubreuil, 2008; Sigmund et al., 2011).
   - **Baseline Efficiency:** If control (no punishment) efficiency is already high due to social preferences or trust, adding punishment may have little effect or even reduce efficiency by crowding out intrinsic motivation or incurring unnecessary costs (van der Weele, 2012; Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
   - **Mutational/Strategy Structure:** The architecture of how new strategies arise (mutation kernels, learning rules) strongly moderates what long-term efficiency levels are achievable (García & Traulsen, 2012).
   - **Type of Punishment:** Pool (institutional) punishment can lead to higher efficiency than peer punishment, but only when second-order free riding is addressed (Sigmund et al., 2011).

**Nuances and Moderators:**  
- **Crowding Out Effects:** Punishment can reduce voluntary contributions in environments with strong social or moral preferences (Orr, 2001; Hwang & Bowles, 2012).
- **Institutional and Social Context:** The positive effect of punishment on efficiency can be lost or reversed when corruption, anti-social punishment, or institutional costs are high (Powers et al., 2012; Jaffe, 2004; Isakov & Rand, 2012).
- **Reward and Compensation:** The presence of reward mechanisms, or compensation for punishers, alters the predicted efficiency impact—often increasing efficiency when both mechanisms are present (Ye et al., 2011; Milinski & Rockenbach, 2012).
- **Monitoring and Information Feedback:** Imperfect or noisy monitoring limits the efficacy of punishment to boost efficiency (Bednar, 2006; Haag & Lagunoff, 2006).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

By synthesizing this literature, the following should guide prediction of treatment efficiency (punishment-enabled) from design dimensions and control efficiency (no-punishment baseline):

- **Expect punishment to raise efficiency when:**
    - Control efficiency is low or moderate, i.e., free riding is prevalent in the absence of punishment.
    - Punishment is not excessively costly (punishment_cost) and is effective (punishment_tech).
    - The group is small or medium-sized; the number of rounds is sufficient for reputational or deterrent effects to manifest.
    - There are no strong crowding out effects, anti-social punishment, or negative psychological/institutional spillovers.
    - No second-order free rider problems undermine the sustainability of punishment.
    - The population structure supports peer monitoring and decentralization (or, in the case of pool punishment, institutional support is strong and costs spread widely).

- **Expect ambiguous or even negative effects on efficiency when:**
    - Control efficiency is already high due to trust, social preferences, or reputation mechanisms.
    - Punishment is costly, weak, or subject to anti-social misuse.
    - Crowding out of intrinsic motivation for cooperation is possible.
    - Group size is so large that monitoring and effective punishment are not feasible.
    - Second-order free riding is prevalent and no countervailing mechanisms (metanorms, rewards for punishers) exist.

- **Dimension-Specific Implications:**
    - **Punishment Cost:** Increasing cost reduces efficiency gain and can even reverse it.
    - **Punishment Effectiveness/Tech:** High effectiveness per unit cost (fine-to-fee ratio) increases efficiency potential.
    - **Group Size (player_count):** Larger groups require more robust/institutional punishment to realize efficiency gains.
    - **Number of Rounds (num_rounds):** More rounds sustain reputation/punishment effects, but with diminishing returns.
    - **Reward Exists (reward_exists):** Presence of rewards can synergize with punishment for higher efficiency.
    - **Information Feedback (show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries):** Full transparency improves the effect of punishment; imperfect or noisy monitoring dampens it.
    - **Participation Voluntariness (not an explicit dimension, often inferred):** Voluntary participation plus punishment is superior for efficiency compared to compulsory settings.

- **Model-Informed Quantitative Prediction:**  
    - Theoretical models (e.g., Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Eldakar et al., 2007; Sigmund et al., 2011) provide formulas relating equilibrium efficiency to key dimensions. If control game efficiency and all relevant design dimensions are provided, these can support quantitative or at least ordinal predictions.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed Dimensions:**
    - `player_count` (Group size): Consistently parameterized and theorized.
    - `num_rounds` (Rounds/Repetition): Commonly modeled as a critical moderator.
    - `mpcr` (Marginal Per-Capita Return): Frequently discussed as payoff structure.
    - `all_or_nothing` (Contribution Structure): Many models assume binary or continuous contributions.
    - `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (Cost and Effectiveness): Explicit in most mechanistic models.
    - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Often discussed as part of alternative or complementary incentive structures.
    - `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds` (Feedback/Information): Treated in several analyses dealing with monitoring or transparency. 
    - `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes discussed in relation to reputation or informativeness.
- **Indirectly or Contextually Discussed:**
    - `chat` (Communication): Rarely, but occasionally considered as facilitating cooperation outside of formal punishment.
    - `default_contrib` (Framing): Only tangentially or in behavioral outcome models.
- **Effectively Missing or Sparse:**
    - `chat`, `default_contrib`: Very rarely modeled or included as explicit design dimensions in formal efficiency analyses.

Some dimensions—such as forms of monitoring or feedback—are implicit in the models’ structure (e.g., perfect vs. noisy monitoring) but not always mapped one-to-one to the supplied 14 dimensions. Contextual moderators (e.g., social preferences, population structure) are often more thoroughly theorized than interface-level design features.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of Empirical Data:** The evidence is exclusively theoretical; no laboratory or field estimates of realized efficiency shifts with/without punishment are provided.
- **Predominance of Mechanism over Measurement:** Outcomes are primarily model equilibria or strategy prevalence, not observed payoffs or direct efficiency measurements.
- **Parameter Sensitivity:** Many qualitative and quantitative findings are sensitive to assumptions about player types (social preferences), network structure, mutation/learning dynamics, and initial conditions.
- **Generalizability:** Some close or adjacent domain evidence (e.g., iterated PDs, asymmetric systems, mutualisms) may not map perfectly onto real-world PGGs with peer punishment.
- **Outcome Distinctions:** Increases in cooperation or contribution may not always translate into net efficiency gains if the cost of punishment outweighs benefits or if negative indirect effects (e.g., reduced trust, anti-social punishment) occur.
- **Incomplete Dimension Coverage:** Several prediction dimensions are under-theorized or absent (e.g., chat, default framing, nuanced aspects of interface-level feedback).
- **Ambiguity and Contradiction:** While many papers predict efficiency gains from punishment, several note mixed, negative, or context-dependent effects—a signal that predictions cannot be categorical and must account for design- and population-specific moderators.
- **Absence of Deep Structural Variables:** Social norms, institutional histories, cultural context, and intrinsic motivations are often referenced as important, yet are not parameterized or mapped to the prediction input dimensions.

---

**In conclusion:**  
This theoretical literature robustly supports the expectation that, in standard PGG-like environments with low baseline efficiency, enabling peer punishment usually raises group efficiency—contingent upon punishment cost, effectiveness, group size, baseline efficiency, and other design features. However, the effect is never universal: key moderators and contextual features can diminish, zero out, or reverse the efficiency gain. Without empirical effect sizes or mapping for several design dimensions, predictions should explicitly accommodate these conditionalities, and untested parameter combinations or edge cases should be treated cautiously.
