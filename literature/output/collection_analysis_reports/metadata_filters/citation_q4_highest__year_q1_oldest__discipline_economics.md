# 1) Evidence Base

The literature base is large (122 papers) and overwhelmingly empirical, with numerous laboratory experiments and several field experiments directly manipulating punishment dimensions within public-goods-game (PGG) or closely related environments. There is also a substantial theoretical literature focused on mechanisms for sustaining cooperation through punishment in repeated social dilemmas. The corpus is broad and includes multiple variants of PGGs (e.g., voluntary contribution mechanisms, common-pool resource games, trust/investment/contest games), a wide range of punishment regimes (peer, central, ostracism, exclusion, legal, delegated, material/non-material), and a diversity of contextual moderations (e.g., communication, monitoring, information structure, group composition, institutional choice). 

Theory papers in the set provide formal models of punishment and cooperation, with constraints and comparative statics that tie design dimensions to efficiency outcomes. However, empirical findings—especially lab experiments—form the backbone of actionable evidence for prediction. There is strong representation of studies reporting direct efficiency or group payoff measures, but a significant minority of studies focus only on contribution/cooperation rates or on the determinants/motives for punishment, requiring careful separation between payoff and non-payoff outcomes for relevance to prediction.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**
- **Exact relevance:** Many papers use the standard linear PGG/VMPG or a close variant (common in lab experiments: 3–5 players, discrete/continuous contributions, fixed/known rounds, MPCR 0.3–0.5).
- **Close relevance:** Some studies use common-pool resource (CPR) settings, trust or principal-agent games, or exclusion-based punishment/flexible institutional design. These often map closely to PGG outcomes for efficiency predictions but may introduce contextual caveats.
- **Adjacent/weak/none:** A meaningful subset uses dyadic games (Prisoner’s Dilemma, trust/investment), contest games, or sender-receiver games. These offer mechanism insights but only adjacent evidence for group payoff effects in multi-person PGGs.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**
- **Exact relevance:** The majority of empirical PGG studies directly manipulate the presence, cost, and effectiveness of peer punishment or institutionally designed punishment (peer/centralized/legal/exclusion/ostracism), including various technologies (cost/impact ratio, possibility for counter-punishment, feedback, identity revelation).
- **Close relevance:** Some studies explore endogenous voting on punishment mechanisms, non-material or social disapproval, normative enforcement, or trust-based informal punishment.
- **Adjacent/weak/none:** Observational studies and games without explicit punishment only touch on related behavioral or reputational mechanisms (often not suitable for direct prediction about efficiency effects).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
- **Exact/close relevance:** A strong component of the literature reports on efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of maximal theoretical payoff), sometimes explicitly normalizing for group size/endowment/social optimum. Several others report closely related metrics (total earnings, welfare, surplus, coins generated).
- **Adjacent relevance:** Some studies only report indirect payoffs (final round payoffs, frequency of efficient outcomes, or comparative payoffs under differing institutions). 
- **Weak/none:** A significant fraction report only behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, frequency/type of punishment, norm compliance) without connecting these to efficiency or group payoff.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (directly informing efficiency prediction):**
- **Efficiency** (net group earnings / social optimum): Frequently reported in PGG/VCM literature, especially in main punishment studies (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007; Noussair & Tucker, 2005; Ambrus & Greiner, 2012).
- **Group payoff/total earnings/welfare**: Reported as primary outcomes in many empirical studies. Examples include studies on centralized punishment, rewards, and exclusion.
- **Surplus/total coins generated**: Some, particularly in CPR variants, report this as the efficiency metric.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (should not be conflated with efficiency):**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**: Commonly measured, often misinterpreted as proxies for efficiency, but their effect on total payoff is contingent on the cost and pattern of punishment.
- **Frequency/intensity/targeting of punishment**: Important for understanding mechanisms but not direct efficiency outcomes.
- **Norm compliance, trust, reciprocity, group sorting**: Frequently discussed as drivers or correlates of behavior.
- **Antisocial/perverse punishment rates**: Reported in studies highlighting contexts where punishment reduces efficiency.

Studies that do not measure efficiency must not be taken as providing evidence on group payoff effects, no matter the direction or magnitude of cooperation changes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis across papers with direct, close, and adjacent evidence:**

- **Enabling peer punishment in standard linear PGGs with moderate player counts (3–5), moderate MPCR (0.3–0.6), fixed/known rounds (10–20), and no communication, generally:**
    - *Increases contributions substantially,* often maintaining high cooperation where baseline (no punishment) results in decay (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007).
    - *Increases efficiency on average, but the efficiency gain is smaller than the increase in contributions* due to the direct cost of punishment (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007; Page et al., 2005; Masclet et al., 2003). Typical efficiency gains in well-parameterized lab experiments are 4–20 percentage points above the control without punishment.
    - *Punishment costs are a major limiting factor:* If punishment is cheap and/or highly effective (high impact/cost ratio), efficiency gains can be large. If punishment is of low impact or cost is high, net efficiency gains can be negligible or even negative (Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Casari & Luini, 2009; Nikiforakis, 2008).
    - *Efficiency gains are typically realized after an initial adjustment period,* as the threat of punishment deters free riding and punishment rates subside (Sefton et al., 2007).
    - *Communication or endogenous institutional choice further amplify efficiency,* sometimes yielding larger improvements than punishment alone (Sutter et al., 2010; Bochet et al., 2006; Ostrom, 2006).

- **Contextual moderators that can render punishment ineffective or even reduce efficiency:**
    - *Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) and widespread counter-punishment* can cause efficiency to fall below the control (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Nikiforakis, 2008).
    - *Imperfect/noisy monitoring* can mean punishment raises cooperation but not net efficiency, or even reduces overall payoffs (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; ABREU et al., 1991; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007).
    - *Variations in feedback/information (e.g., showing contributions vs. earnings, punishment identity) moderate the net effect of punishment* (Nikiforakis, 2010; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007).
    - *Culture/social context/trust* can reverse expected effects: in certain field contexts or cultures with high antisocial punishment, introducing punishment reduces efficiency (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Vollan, 2008; Fehr et al., 2008; Hoff et al., 2011).
    - *Institutional details—scope, targeting, capacity for counter-punishment*—are critical; only well-targeted (e.g., low contributor only), consensus-based, or exclusionary punishment mechanisms robustly generate efficiency gains (Ertan et al., 2009; Casari & Luini, 2009; Andreoni & Gee, 2012).

- **Theory and mechanism arguments converge with empirical findings, consistently predicting:**
    - *Punishment enables and supports high efficiency if a minimum number of (inequity-averse/reciprocating) punishers exist* (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Sethi & Somanathan, 2003; Kandori, 1992).
    - *High punishment cost, poor targeting, or inability to observe/identify free riders sharply curtails the payoff benefit of punishment.* 

- **Effects across design dimensions are contingent:**
    - *Player count:* Small to moderate groups support effective punishment. Efficiency gains decline in very large groups unless information and identity mechanisms are strong (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Camera & Casari, 2009).
    - *MPCR and cost/tech ratio:* Higher MPCR and more effective punishment (high impact per cost incurred) make high efficiency more feasible and extend the range of settings where punishment improves payoff (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Rigdon, 2009).
    - *Punishment institution: Peer punishment often loses more resources to the act of punishing than centralized/delegated punishment or ostracism, which can yield larger efficiency gains (Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Kosfeld et al., 2009; Cinyabuguma et al., 2005; Maier-Rigaud et al., 2010).*
    - *Repeated vs. one-shot: Repeated games allow punishment to have stronger effects; one-shot contexts, especially with antisocial punishment, may see no gain or even negative effects (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Tyran & Feld, 2006).*
    - *Information feedback:* Showing clear contribution histories and punishment actions increases the effectiveness/efficiency of punishment (Nikiforakis, 2010; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007).

- **Reward and punishment interaction**: Well-leveraged rewards, or a combination of reward and punishment, can outperform punishment alone for boosting efficiency (Sefton et al., 2007; Sutter et al., 2010).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Principle:**  
Given the average efficiency of the control game (punishment disabled) and detailed design dimensions, the expected average efficiency when peer punishment is enabled will tend to be higher, **if** (a) punishment is well-designed (targeted at free riders; high impact/cost), (b) antisocial punishment is minimized, (c) monitoring is effective, and (d) social context does not elevate perverse norms or crowding-out effects. 

**Quantitative Adjustment:**  
Where design dimensions closely match standard lab PGGs, a **positive adjustment of 4–20 percentage points** in efficiency (group payoff/social optimum) over the control is supported (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007; Page et al., 2005; Masclet et al., 2003). High-impact punishment (impact/cost ≥2 or 3) yields gains at the upper end; 1:1 cost/impact or less, or open antisocial punishment, yield lower or possibly negative net effects.

**Moderators for Larger/Smaller Predicted Gain:**
- Smaller/larger gains as **punishment cost increases/decreases** and as **punishment effectiveness decreases/increases** (Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Masclet et al., 2003).
- Gains are attenuated or reversed if **antisocial punishment or counter-punishment** is common (Nikiforakis, 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).
- Efficiency gains are **larger with communication** or **institutional choice** enabled (Sutter et al., 2010; Ostrom, 2006; Kosfeld et al., 2009).
- In **heterogeneous or field environments**, gains are smaller or conditional (Vollan, 2008; Lopez et al., 2012; Castillo et al., 2011).

**Design features that must be specifically considered or will modulate the effect:**
- **Player count:** Small groups (3–5): robust effect. Large groups (>8): positive effects only with effective monitoring, institution, or exclusion.
- **num_rounds:** Longer games allow adjustment to punishment; end-game effects matter—efficiency often rises over time post-introduction of punishment (Sefton et al., 2007; Fehr & Gächter, 2000).
- **all_or_nothing:** Most findings are for continuous contribution; all-or-nothing contributions may moderate effect and must be interpreted carefully (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012).
- **chat:** Communication strongly amplifies efficiency gains and can often substitute for punishment (Bochet et al., 2006; Ostrom, 2006; Cardenas, 2004).
- **mpcr:** Low MPCR (<0.4), efficiency harder to achieve—punishment must be more effective to yield net welfare gains (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999).

**Control-Condition Anchoring:**  
Where only control efficiency is available and design dimensions are known, the literature supports adjusting control efficiency upwards by a *contextually and parameter-moderated increment* when peer punishment is enabled, unless:
- The context or game design is known to promote antisocial punishment, retaliation, or perverse targeting;
- Monitoring is imperfect/noisy (in which case partial/negative or null adjustment is warranted);
- Group is large and/or anonymous with limited monitoring (require caution or limiting the predicted gain).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions (empirical estimates and strong theory):**
- **player_count:** Small groups are the dominant laboratory context; effect is well-characterized. Large groups are addressed with caution and linked to decreasing returns to punishment.
- **num_rounds:** Evidence supports both short and long games; end-of-game unraveling is reported and must be factored.
- **mpcr:** Both theory and experiment point to MPCR as a key moderator.
- **punishment_cost & punishment_tech (impact):** Heavily studied; cost-effectiveness ratio central to effect on efficiency.
- **chat:** Well covered; communication amplifies or sometimes substitutes for punishment; rarely combined in the same treatment.
- **all_or_nothing:** Both types present; effect on punishment's value inferred from direct and indirect findings.
- **show_other_summaries & show_n_rounds:** Some evidence that detailed feedback moderates effectiveness; contribution/earnings feedback type matters (Nikiforakis, 2010).
- **punishment_tech (network, targeting, exclusion, consensus mechanisms):** Studies detail how these affect antisocial punishment rates and net gains.
- **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech:** Papers directly contrast reward and punishment (Sefton et al., 2007; Sutter et al., 2010).

**Indirectly Informed Dimensions:**
- **show_punishment_id:** Some theory/experiments address identification/reputation effects, showing their importance (Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007).
- **default_contrib:** Rarely studied directly; may shape initial behaviors but is not a major moderator post-adjustment.
- **delegated/centralized vs. peer punishment:** Strong evidence that delegation and consensus rules can increase net efficiency (Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Casari & Luini, 2009).

**Contextually Discussed or Sparse Evidence:**
- **Group voting/institutional choice:** Discussed in theory/empirical work but not always parameterized for specific prediction (Sutter et al., 2010).
- **Cultural moderators:** Often discussed but quantification is challenging and transferability to other contexts is limited.

**Effectively Missing/Sparse:**
- **Some aspects of technological implementation (automatic vs. manual punishment, user interface elements) and framing effects are infrequently specified.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Scope of Generalizability:** Most quantitative findings are from small-group, lab-based PGGs with Western subject pools, fixed group size/rounds, well-defined punishment stages, and relatively high monitoring and well-specified punishment cost/impact. Field environments, large groups, or cultures with high antisocial punishment may deviate sharply from the lab norms.
- **Measurement Gaps:** Some studies report only contributions or frequency/type of punishment, not group efficiency or total payoff, and must not be over-interpreted for payoff outcomes. Studies that only provide behavioral outcomes are not directly informative for efficiency prediction.
- **Parameter Sensitivity:** The net effect of enabling punishment is **not robust to all game parameters**—slight changes in monitoring, institution, cost, or information can reverse direction or sharply attenuate welfare gains.
- **Antisocial Punishment and Context:** High rates of perverse punishment, counter-punishment, or cultural aversion to sanctions can easily make punishment net-welfare-reducing, a risk especially in one-shot, contextually diverse, or field settings.
- **Dynamic/Endgame Effects:** Short time horizons, end-game unraveling, or one-shot design can limit the sustainability of punishment-induced efficiency gains.
- **Interaction Effects:** Communication and punishment can interact; effects may not be additive. Many studies do not fully cross these moderators in experimental designs.

**In summary:**  
The literature is strong and directly informative about the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in canonical lab PGGs, when the specific design matches studied parameters and institutions. Predictive accuracy decreases in large groups, field settings, or when monitoring, targeting, or social context differ from lab standards. Net efficiency gains from enabling punishment are common, but strictly parameter- and context-dependent, and can be offset or reversed under specific unfavorable conditions. For robust prediction, adjustment from control efficiency should use contextually matched, parameter-sensitive findings, and not simply rely on the direction of contribution rate changes.
