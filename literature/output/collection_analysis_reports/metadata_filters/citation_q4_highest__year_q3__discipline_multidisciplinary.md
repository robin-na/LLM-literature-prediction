# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set consists of 22 papers spanning empirical (experimental and field), theoretical, and observational studies. Of these, a minority deliver empirical laboratory or field experimental data directly relevant to public goods games (PGGs) with punishment; several others provide theoretical models of PGGs with institutional incentives; but the majority address adjacent or weakly related paradigms (e.g., networked Prisoner's Dilemma, norm enforcement in field settings, or observational studies on sanctioning attitudes). The evidence base is thus moderate in coverage for the core prediction task: there are strong theoretical and some empirical studies with exact or close PGG-punishment-payoff coverage, but much of the set supplies only indirect, behavioral, or contextually adjacent findings. Most empirical results directly quantifying the efficiency or group payoff effect of punishment come from a small subset of lab or theory PGG studies, while many others focus on cooperation rates, sanctioning patterns, or cultural context.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**
- *exact*: Several papers address PGGs directly, both theoretically (Duong & Han, 2021) and empirically (Lohse & Waichman, 2020). Others address variants (e.g., threshold public goods, collective-risk dilemmas) that retain key PGG features (Fang et al., 2019; Góis et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2021).
- *close/adjacent*: Many studies are adjacent (spatial PD, common-pool dilemmas, real-world norm violation) or use different operationalizations of group cooperation.
- *weak/none*: Several papers are only tangentially related or use non-economic field/observational data.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**
- *exact*: Roughly half the set studies punishment or sanctions directly as an experimental treatment or model parameter.
- *close/adjacent*: Others discuss informal, institutional, or reputational sanctioning in broader behavioral or sociocultural contexts.
- *weak/none*: Some do not include any punishment or sanctioning mechanism.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
- *exact*: Only a handful measure or model group efficiency or equivalent payoff-based outcomes directly (Duong & Han, 2021; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018).
- *close*: Some use proxies like group achievement or success in reaching payoff thresholds.
- *adjacent/weak*: Many focus on behavioral metrics (contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency) rather than final payoffs or efficiency.
- *none*: A large portion does not report payoff/efficiency outcomes at all.

**Summary**: The set contains some highly relevant sources but overall coverage of the precise intersection—empirical or theoretical effects of punishment on efficiency in (variant) PGGs—is narrow, with much content only indirectly relevant.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes**:
- *Efficiency/group payoff* metrics (the focus of the prediction task) are reported or modeled directly in a minority of works (Duong & Han, 2021; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018; Góis et al., 2019).
- Some theoretical works calculate institutional cost or group achievement as a proxy for efficiency (Duong & Han, 2021; Góis et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2021).
- Most PGG lab studies without direct payoff data focus on cooperation/contribution dynamics, with some benchmarking final group earnings.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
- The majority of papers report cooperation/contribution rates, norm compliance, punishment actions/frequency, social sanctioning behavior, or perceptions of appropriate sanctions, without calculating group payoff, efficiency, or welfare.
- Many field and cross-cultural analyses emphasize behavioral or attitudinal responses to norm violations (e.g., sanction use, types, frequency, context).

**Distinguishing outcomes**:
- When payoff-based outcomes are absent, some works (e.g., Fang et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2021) use "group achievement" (fraction reaching behavioral target) as a success measure; these should not be equated with efficiency unless validated.
- Most studies do not follow through to quantify net efficiency after accounting for the cost of punishment itself—a critical distinction for prediction.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical/Experimental PGG/Variant Studies (with efficiency or payoff outcomes):

- **Punishment often raises cooperation but does *not* guarantee higher efficiency**:
  - Peer punishment increases the rate at which groups achieve cooperation targets (e.g., advancing to future rounds or meeting thresholds), but the costs of punishment can fully offset the efficiency gains (Lohse & Waichman, 2020).
  - In games allowing individual (private) solutions, punishment can increase collective action but *reduce* group efficiency due to mutually wasteful punishment exchanges (Gross & De Dreu, 2019).
  - Networked social dilemmas: Punishment in static network Prisoner's Dilemma erases or reverses efficiency gains from reciprocity, with group payoff actually decreasing (Li et al., 2018).

- **Conditions for efficiency gains depend on design and context**:
  - The structure and normative context of punishment—whether there are institutional/normative constraints to prevent antisocial, reputation-based, or indiscriminate punishment—critically shape whether efficiency increases or decreases (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
  - Game mechanics such as the presence of both rewards and punishments, their cost-effectiveness, and their dynamic use (switching from reward to punishment at high cooperation) can optimize efficiency (Góis et al., 2019; Duong & Han, 2021).
  - Institutional punishment (set/controlled by a central authority) can be optimized for required cooperation thresholds, providing theoretical formulas for minimal efficiency cost, but may not generalize to standard peer-punishment PGGs (Duong & Han, 2021).

- **Empirical evidence generally finds efficiency effects are small, zero, or negative when punishment is costly**:
  - Most observed efficiency gains from punishment are wiped out when the cost of enacting punishment is considered, especially in peer-punishment designs (Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018).
  - Endogenous, budget-balanced *reward* mechanisms outperform punishment in raising both cooperation and efficiency metrics (Yang et al., 2018).

- **Design parameters matter**: Effects of punishment on efficiency vary with group size, MPCR, cost/magnitude of punishment, game structure (threshold vs linear PGG), possibility of communication, and player choice sets.

## Theoretical Models (with efficiency/cost outcomes):

- Explicit mathematical frameworks allow calculation of institutional cost-efficiency for various parameter regimes in finite PGGs (Duong & Han, 2021).
- Dynamic strategies (combining reward/punishment) yield highest theoretical group achievement (Góis et al., 2019).

## Additional Observations:

- Punishment's impact is *rarely* positive in pure payoff terms unless design or context specifically favor low-cost, high-impact punishment, normative controls against antisocial punishment, or swift suppression of free-riding prior to group breakdown.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Baseline Prediction**: When predicting average efficiency in a PGG-like game with peer punishment enabled, one should *not* assume that increased cooperation automatically yields higher efficiency. The average efficiency with punishment may be equal to or even lower than in the control (no-punishment), especially when punishment is costly and peer-administered.

- **Adjustment Factors**:
  - *Efficiency will only increase* if the gains from increased public good provision outweigh the cost of administering punishment (including mutual punishment, antisocial punishment, and retaliation effects).
  - *Design dimensions* most critical to predicting effect direction and magnitude include: punishment cost and magnitude (cost/impact ratio), presence of antisocial/retaliatory punishment, possibility of reward as an alternative or complement, group size, marginal per-capita return, communication, and the presence of normative/institutional constraints.

- **Predicted Patterns**:
  - In standard PGGs with peer (costly) punishment, expect neutrality or even negative effects on efficiency: the average treatment efficiency will likely be similar to or lower than control (Duong & Han, 2021; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018). Increased rates of group success may not translate to higher group earnings due to punishment expenditures.
  - In threshold or collective-risk games, punishment may improve the *fraction* of successful groups, but this does not reliably equate to higher *mean* efficiency. The positive efficiency effect is only robust when punishment is low-cost/efficient or is institutionally/normatively controlled to prevent misuse.
  - Institutional or hybrid schemes can be tuned theoretically for cost-effective cooperation, but peer punishment as implemented in standard lab PGGs rarely shows strong net positive efficiency effects unless costs are minimized or norm-support prevents retaliatory punishment (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Duong & Han, 2021).
  - If reward is enabled as an alternative or complement, efficiency gains are more robust (Yang et al., 2018; Góis et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2021).

- **Uncertainties**:
  - Where only behavioral (non-payoff) data are available, do not assume efficiency gains; conversion from cooperation to payoff depends on the cost structure and prevalence of over/punishment.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed**:
- `player_count`: Most exact and close-relevance studies specify and analyze group size.
- `num_rounds`: Important in repeated games; commonly reported.
- `all_or_nothing`: Linear and threshold PGGs both represented.
- `mpcr`: Consistently reported and analyzed in theoretical and some empirical works as a critical moderator.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Core parameters in both theory (cost, magnitude, implementation) and lab studies.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Examined, especially in studies comparing or hybridizing punishment and reward.
- `show_n_rounds`: Less central, but sometimes reported/varied (e.g., as info transparency).

**Indirectly Informed/contextually discussed**:
- `chat`: Occasionally manipulated (with/without communication); rarely as primary focus for efficiency effects.
- `default_contrib`: Implied in framing but rarely the focus of variation or analysis.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes discussed in context of feedback or transparency effects on norm enforcement (Winter & Zhang, 2018; Pisor et al., 2020), but evidence on impact on efficiency is sparse.
- `reward_magnitude`, `punishment_magnitude`: Implied or set as a function of cost in some modeling studies.

**Effectively Missing or Sparse**:
- Many studies do not systematically vary or analyze `default_contrib`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`, `reward_magnitude`, focusing instead on more foundational economic or strategic design dimensions.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Few studies report both exact PGG settings, explicit punishment manipulation, and efficiency/payoff outcomes**; much of the apparent literature is behavioral, attitudinal, or adjacent in focus.
- **Peer punishment as typically implemented often fails to increase efficiency**; while cooperation rises, costs of punishment (including antisocial/retaliatory actions) consume gains.
- **Most efficiency findings are context-sensitive**: Design details like institutionally set punishments, the presence of antisocial punishment, and availability of alternative mechanisms (reward, communication) strongly moderate effects.
- **Limited granularity on some prediction dimensions**: Settings such as feedback/summary display, default contribution framing, and some technical parameters (reward/punishment magnitude) are rarely analyzed for their direct impact on efficiency outcomes.
- **Cultural, real-world, and network context effects** are addressed primarily in non-paradigmatic or observational studies without payoff outcomes, reducing their immediate predictive utility.
- **Generalizability**: Many theoretical models require parameters that are not always controlled or observable in experimental/real-world analogues; empirical findings may not cleanly translate to all game variants.
- **Quantitative prediction is challenging**: Most papers report effect *direction* rather than *magnitude*; theoretical work provides formulas but requires careful mapping to actual design and context.

---

**Bottom line**: The literature set provides strong theoretical and some direct empirical evidence that enabling peer punishment in PGG or close variants does *not*, in typical lab or modeled settings, reliably increase efficiency as measured by group payoff relative to control. Instead, efficiency effects are highly sensitive to the cost/targeting of punishment, availability of counterbalancing reward, and the presence of effective normative or institutional controls. For downstream prediction, modelers should pay close attention to these contextual moderating dimensions and not assume that observed increases in cooperation under punishment automatically translate into efficiency gains. Quantitative prediction is well supported for baseline parameters but less so for nuanced design features or diverse environments.
