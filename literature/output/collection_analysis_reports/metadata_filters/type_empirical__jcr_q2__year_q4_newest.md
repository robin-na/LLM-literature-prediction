# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is empirically rich and overwhelmingly consists of laboratory experiments (a few field or framed field experiments), mainly using public goods games (PGGs) or close variants as paradigms. Most papers provide behavioral and (in a substantial minority) payoff-related outcomes. The evidence base is broad in its attention to diverse game design dimensions—e.g., punishment structure, player count, MPCR, round count, communication, etc.—but findings cluster most densely around standard small-group (3–5 players), repeated linear PGGs with optional peer punishment and no chat. Several papers present direct efficiency (or closely related payoff) results for enabling punishment. There is also a notable subset of adjacent or less directly relevant studies (variants of punishment, alternative dilemmas or outcome measures, studies centered on behavior rather than payoffs). Meta-analyses and reviews supplement the primary studies but are often focused on behavior rather than group payoffs.

**Summary**: The evidence base is strong and directly relevant for standard PGGs with peer punishment, but sparser or more equivocal for broader design features or more complex institutional treatments. Empirical findings dominate, with some mechanism/theory arguments emerging from discussion or modeling within empirical studies.

**Details**:
- *pgg_or_variant*: Large core of exact PGGs (linear, continuous or binary, repeated or one-shot); some papers are CPRs, collective-risk, or market analogs (close).
- *punishment_or_sanctions*: Strong coverage of exact (costly monetary punishment, ostracism, formal/informal mechanisms, counter-punishment); some treatments adjacent (e.g., reporting, deterrence-only, weak sanctions, group-level/collective punishment, or non-monetary appeals).
- *efficiency_or_related_payoff*: Many key studies report efficiency (payoff as a fraction of social optimum, average group earnings, welfare). However, a notable fraction (especially reviews, mechanism studies, or those focusing on cooperation rates) do not directly report efficiency.

**Conclusion**: The literature is highly relevant on all three dimensions, though more so in standard, small-group repeated PGGs with peer punishment than in adjacent paradigms or complex institutional or field settings.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based outcomes**: 
    - *Efficiency*: Average group payoff as a proportion of full-cooperation benchmark (canonical).
    - *Total/group earnings, welfare, surplus, coins generated*: Often directly or indirectly mapped to efficiency.
    - *Drop-off*: Some studies report only contribution levels; only a subset converts to payoff/efficiency ratios.
    - *Variants*: Earnings in related (market/externality) games and CPRs count as payoff only if clear social optimum is specified.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
    - *Contribution rate/cooperation rate*: The most common outcome in behavioral studies (but not synonymous with efficiency).
    - *Punishment frequency and targeting, ostracism/voting/monitoring behavior, norm compliance, compliance rates*: Proxies or mechanism outcomes, not direct welfare/efficiency measures.
    - *Social/emotional measures*: Motivation, fairness judgments, norm adherence, group/individual differences.

- **Important distinctions**: 
    - Several papers show increased cooperation/contribution rates without corresponding increases in efficiency due to high punishment costs (e.g., (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024)).
    - Some studies focus only on compliance, cooperation, or punishment occurrence/targeting, not group payoffs.

**Summary**: About half of the core empirical PGG plus punishment literature provides direct efficiency or group payoff measures; others (especially in adjacent paradigms) only report behavioral or compliance outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General finding:** Enabling punishment in standard PGGs (small group, repeated, voluntary costly peer punishment enabled) most often increases contribution rates; the effect on efficiency (group payoff) is *conditional* and sometimes ambiguous.

- **Punishment often increases efficiency, but with caveats:**
    - Large, direct efficiency gains from punishment are reported when
        - Punishment is not overly costly relative to its impact/setup is close to canonical lab PGG (Kamei, 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Jarungrattanapong, 2022; Zhang et al., 2024; Ntuli et al., 2023).
        - Network/institutional design favors deterrence and/or coordinated use (Kamei, 2024; Peng & Fan, 2023; Pi et al., 2022; Bühren et al., 2025).
        - Formal or monitored institutions, leader punishment, or sanctioning quotas are used (Kamei, 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024).
    - Incomplete, diffuse, or individualistic punishment (especially if costly) may fail to deliver efficiency gains and can reduce group payoffs if punishment is overused or antisocial (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Peng, 2022; Kim et al., 2025).

- **Punishment structure and magnitude matter:**
    - Large/strong punishment mechanisms tend to be more reliably efficiency-enhancing than weak/small ones (Ye et al., 2023; Windrich et al., 2024; Billinger & Rosenbaum, 2023).
    - The network structure (who can punish whom) shapes both efficiency gains and bystander/diffusion effects—more punishment power, if diffused, can *reduce* efficiency (Pi et al., 2022; Peng & Fan, 2023).

- **Antisocial punishment, costliness, and targeting can offset or reverse gains:**
    - When punishment is used antisocially, or out of proportion to defection, costs can dominate and efficiency falls (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Peng, 2022; Kim et al., 2025).
    - Efficiency losses can occur even with higher cooperation if punishment is overused or mis-targeted, especially in larger, less coordinated, or more anonymous settings.
    - Presence of counter-punishment or retaliation mechanisms tends to reduce efficiency (Casari & Tavoni, 2024).

- **Contextual moderators:**
    - Cultural differences: Some societies (UK, US) see stronger efficiency effects from punishment than others (India, Morocco, Turkey) (Kamei et al., 2025; Weber et al., 2023).
    - Decision structure (e.g., self-vs. representative punishment): Efficiency gains are realized only in some decision architectures (Kim et al., 2025).
    - Cognitive ability and comprehension: High cognitive load and poor understanding can reduce the (positive) efficiency effect of punishment; in some repeated high-cognition settings, small punishment can actually reduce efficiency (Ye et al., 2023; Ramalingam & Stoddard, 2024).

- **Adjacency and weak effects:** Adjacent literature (CPR games, market games) generally supports the capacity for targeted, well-designed punishment to increase efficiency, but collective or non-targeted sanctions do not guarantee efficiency gains (De Geest et al., 2022; Kamei et al., 2025; Schaefer, 2023; Jiang & Villeval, 2024).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Core prediction rules from the evidence:**

- **All else equal, enabling appropriately calibrated, targeted, and not excessively costly punishment in a standard lab PGG predicts an increase in average efficiency over control, sometimes approaching the cooperative optimum.**
    - *Supported contexts*: 3–5 player groups, repeated rounds (6–32), continuous or binary contributions, MPCR 0.4–0.6, peer or formal punishment, no chat.
    - *Typical magnitude*: Efficiency increases of 15–30 percentage points are common when punishment costs are moderate and coordination is possible (Jarungrattanapong, 2022; Kamei, 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Peng & Fan, 2023).

- **When punishment is costly, diffuse, or likely to be used antisocially, the net effect can be a *decrease* in efficiency despite higher cooperation rates.**
    - *Indicators*: Voluntary/individual punishment, high punishment frequency, incomplete institutions, strong antisocial punishment observed.
    - *Prediction*: If control efficiency is already high, or if punishment is likely to be overused, net effect may be neutral or negative (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Peng, 2022).

- **Institutional features and game design dimensions are critical:**
    - Punishment *network/tech*: Complete, formal, costless, or deterrent punishment mechanisms are more likely to deliver efficiency gains; incomplete, decentralized, or group-level punishment is riskier (Bühren et al., 2025; Peng & Fan, 2023; Pi et al., 2022).
    - *MPCR*: High MPCR amplifies the effect of punishment (Bühren et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025).
    - *Player count*: Effects are well characterized for 3–5 players; scaling up may see weaker or more variable effects (Jiang & Villeval, 2024; Kamei et al., 2025).

- **Control (no-punishment) efficiency is a strong prior for predicting treatment efficiency, but the effect size varies with the above dimensions**.
    - Where control efficiency is low, adding punishment is more likely to generate large positive gains if punishment is not overused or antisocial (Kamei, 2024; Joseph et al., 2025).
    - Where control efficiency is already high (e.g., due to other mechanisms—commitment, thresholds, endogenous allocation, communication), the addition of punishment may yield small or even negative net effects.

- **Evidence supports interpreting the following dimensions as important moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency**: 
    - punishment cost/punishment magnitude/punishment tech
    - player count
    - MPCR
    - network/structure of punishment (who can punish whom)
    - round number (especially with end-game effects)
    - communication (chat)
    - decision structure/representation
    - presence/absence of rewards
    - baseline efficiency (control game)

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Most punishment effect studies are for 3–5 players; effect is robust in this range (Kamei, 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Jarungrattanapong, 2022).
- `num_rounds`: Multi-round games (6–32) provide the best data; some attention to one-shot cases (Botelho et al., 2022; Joseph et al., 2025).
- `mpcr`: Heavily covered; efficiency gains strongest at moderate-high MPCR; low MPCR can attenuate effect (Bühren et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (including magnitude): Most studies clarify the cost/impact structure; punishment effectiveness is strongly cost/magnitude-dependent (Ye et al., 2023; Pi et al., 2022; Kamei, 2024).
- `all_or_nothing`: Both binary and continuous contribution treatments are studied (Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Joseph et al., 2025).
- `chat`: Generally absent; some studies test communication as moderator (Ntuli et al., 2023; Jarungrattanapong, 2022).
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Sometimes specified; can affect strategic timing of punishment/use (especially in end-game).
- `reward_exists`: A few papers consider joint punishment and reward options (Zhang et al., 2024).

**Indirectly or contextually informed:**
- `default_contrib`: Some adjacency (Liu et al., 2022).
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Reward is less frequently studied as a direct alternate; sometimes only as a comparison arm or theoretical parallel, not as a mechanism combined with punishment.
- `show_punishment_id`: Rarely manipulated; transparency occasionally discussed in relation to punishment abuse or deterrence (Hoeft & Mill, 2024; Hopp & Süss, 2024).

**Missing or thinly informed:**
- Detailed exploration of large group size, endogenous group formation, complex reputation regimes, or multi-level/multi-group games is limited.
- Sequential, dynamic, or institutional choice games are present but less systematically connected to efficiency outcomes.
- Reward-related dimensions (`reward_cost`, `reward_tech`) are seldom the focus and usually discussed only as comparators, not as design levers.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Payoff/efficiency outcomes not always primary or reported**: Many behavioral papers report only contribution rates, punishment frequency, or compliance—requiring caution when inferring efficiency impacts.
- **Generality outside standard lab settings**: Most direct efficiency data come from standard, small group, repeated lab PGGs; effects may not generalize to larger groups, field settings, or highly heterogeneous participant pools.
- **Anti-social punishment and unintended costs**: The literature documents frequent anti-social punishment, mis-targeted punishment, and retaliation, all of which can offset efficiency gains and are difficult to predict from design features alone.
- **Overemphasis on certain mechanisms**: Peer, voluntary costly punishment is highly studied; other institutional approaches (third-party, formal, endogenous, collective) are less mapped in terms of efficiency.
- **Lack of data for some design dimensions**: Certain variables—such as show_punishment_id, nuanced feedback structures, or interaction of multiple design features (e.g., simultaneous chat + punishment)—are rarely tested with efficiency as outcome.
- **Limited longitudinal or dynamic evidence**: Most studies use limited round numbers; long-term efficiency effects or sustainability with/without punishment are underexplored.
- **Cultural/field contexts**: Cross-societal moderation is documented but not fully understood or predictable; findings from Western lab settings may not map to other populations (Kamei et al., 2025; Ntuli et al., 2023).
- **Dependence on control efficiency**: Many papers take control (no-punishment) efficiency as a given, but some show that very high or very low control efficiency can interact with punishment effects in nonlinear ways.
- **Ambiguity in mixed designs**: In studies with both punishment and threshold/reward/other mechanisms, multicollinearity or treatment interaction often clouds isolation of punishment-specific effects.

**In summary:**  
- The literature provides strong, direct, empirically based guidance for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in standard small-group, repeated PGGs, conditioned on game design dimensions and baseline efficiency.
- Key determinants of the efficiency effect are punishment cost, network/tech, MPCR, group size, and whether punishment is likely to be targeted and used efficiently.
- Evidence is weaker for more complex, heterogeneous, or poorly coordinated environments, as well as for dimensions rarely varied in isolation.
- Predictive uncertainty grows with increasing design complexity (especially if the presence of antisocial or counter-punishment is possible), larger groups, or in field/cultural contexts outside the standard lab PGG paradigm.
