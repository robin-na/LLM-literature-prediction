# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base is **broad, robust, and heavily experiment-driven**, with a large number of high-quality laboratory and field experiments focused on public goods games (PGG) and direct variants. In addition, theoretical and computational (simulation) studies supplement the empirical literature by exploring boundary conditions and providing mechanistic insights. The majority of primary studies explicitly manipulate punishment presence, cost, and/or effectiveness, and report group efficiency, total earnings, or comparable payoff-based outcomes. The set includes both studies where efficiency is the primary outcome and studies that focus on behavioral metrics (contribution/cooperation rates, punishment assigned/frequency), with some reporting both. A significant number of papers directly map findings to standard game design dimensions such as group size, rounds, cost/impact of punishment, and information conditions, enabling a nuanced cross-study synthesis.

**Most studies are tightly focused on standard or slightly modified PGGs**, with a smaller proportion devoted to common-pool resource (CPR) games, step-level/threshold PGGs, dyadic or hierarchical variants, and agent-based models. There is substantial representation of both anonymous and face-to-face, communication-present and -absent, and (to a lesser extent) spatial/networked game environments. Rewards and ostracism are tested as alternative interventions in several papers, and the interplay of punishment and reward is examined in some.

The set includes **multiple direct comparisons between control (no punishment) and treatment (punishment enabled) conditions** within identical or near-identical game structures, often across a range of parameterizations. The paper set is thus well positioned to provide empirical generalizations, highlight key moderators, and identify situations where the effect of punishment on efficiency is positive, negative, or mixed.

# 2) Task Relevance

### a) **pgg_or_variant:** **exact**

The literature is **highly relevant on the PGG dimension**. The majority of studies use linear public goods games, voluntary contribution mechanisms (VCM), or very close CPR variants with clearly analogous incentive structures. Some studies use dyadic or multilevel extensions; a set of theoretical and agent-based studies use PD or Chicken games as analogs, which are generally less directly relevant.

- **Relevance label:** `exact` for the bulk of the literature; a smaller adjacent subset for CPR, trust/investment, or other social dilemma variants.

### b) **punishment_or_sanctions:** **exact**

**Punishment and sanctioning mechanisms are the core manipulation in much of the literature.** Most studies explicitly compare baseline (no punishment) to cases with peer punishment, pool punishment, centralized (leader/administered) punishment, or non-monetary/social sanctions (ostracism, disapproval, etc.). Detailed institutional and parameter variations (cost, impact, anonymity, anti-social/consensual filtering) are prevalent. Reward mechanisms are also studied as alternatives in a subset.

- **Relevance label:** `exact` for most studies; a few are `close` (reward only, or informal/ostracism sanctions).

### c) **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** **exact** to `adjacent`

A **large fraction of studies report efficiency or direct payoff-based outcomes as the primary or secondary dependent variable,** with careful accounting for negative payoff effects of imposed punishment. Others focus on total group welfare, average earnings, or surplus as proxies. Some studies only report cooperation/contribution rates (behavioral, not payoff-based), or focus solely on punishment frequency, emotions, or norm compliance—these are less relevant.

- **Relevance label:** `exact` where efficiency/payoff is primary (majority); `close` or `adjacent` for payoff proxies or behavioral outcomes; `none/weak` where only non-payoff outcomes are reported.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**  
- **Group efficiency** (actual group payoff / potential maximum under full cooperation) is commonly and directly measured.
- **Total group earnings, welfare, total coins generated, or surplus** are accepted as proxies for efficiency.
- Some studies quantify **payoff net of punishment costs** ("net efficiency"), which is essential for assessing true social benefit of punishment-enabled conditions.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
- **Contribution rate, cooperation rate**: Frequency with which players contribute or cooperate, often found to increase when punishment is enabled.
- **Punishment frequency/intensity, anti-social vs. pro-social punishment, norm compliance**: Tracked both as outcomes of interest and as mediating mechanisms influencing payoff-based outcomes.

**Distinctiveness maintained:**  
- Many studies explicitly distinguish **increases in contributions/cooperation** from **increases in efficiency**, emphasizing that the latter may not follow if punishment costs are high or misdirected.
- Several papers warn that high contribution rates can **coexist with flat or even reduced efficiency** due to the direct cost of punishing and anti-social/punitive dynamics.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical consensus:**
- **Punishment generally increases contributions**, often dramatically, compared to control groups with no punishment (e.g., Fehr et al., 2002; Sefton et al., 2007; Casari & Plott, 2003).
- **Efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) sometimes, but not always, increases when punishment is enabled**. The direction and magnitude of the treatment effect depend critically on the design of the punishment mechanism, its cost/impact ratio, and the specific game context.

**Key conditions for positive efficiency effects:**
- **Punishment is effective (high impact per unit cost)**: When the "pain" inflicted by punishment is large relative to the cost for the punisher, net efficiency gains are more likely (Nikiforakis et al., 2010; Sefton et al., 2007; Rigdon, 2009).
- **Punishment is used judiciously (less "anti-social" punishment, and threat rather than frequent use):** Efficiency gains are larger when punishment targets free riders, not cooperators, and when retaliation and feuds are structurally discouraged (Casari & Luini, 2009; Nikiforakis & Engelmann, 2011).
- **Consensual or filtered punishment institutions** (consensual punishment, ostracism, business-like rules) outperform individual, unrestricted punishment in efficiency (Casari & Luini, 2009; Maier-Rigaud et al., 2010).
- **Over time, punishment use typically declines in successful groups**, so efficiency losses from punishment costs are front-loaded, with later-round net gains as cooperation is stabilized (Sefton et al., 2007; Fehr et al., 2002).

**Key moderators and caveats:**
- **Communication enables higher efficiency than punishment alone;** in some designs, punishment adds little to the gains already realized by allowing chat/communication (Bochet et al., 2006).
- **Reward mechanisms typically do NOT outperform punishment in sustaining high efficiency**, but can do so in some structures, especially when rewards are net positive (Gürerk et al., 2009; Szolnoki & Perc, 2012).
- **Group size, marginal per-capita return (MPCR), rounds, and heterogeneity** significantly moderate punishment's effectiveness and effect on efficiency (Fehr et al., 2002; Casari & Plott, 2003; Tan, 2008).
- **High levels of anti-social punishment, unequal power, or opportunity for retaliatory feuds can nullify or reverse efficiency gains** (Nikiforakis & Engelmann, 2011).
- **When baseline/control efficiency is already high (without punishment), enabling punishment may provide little or no additional efficiency, and can even reduce it depending on use and costs** (Kocher et al., 2012; Kocher, Martinsson & Visser, 2008).
- **Efficiency gains from punishment can be higher in later rounds, after initial efficiency losses are incurred due to high early punishment use** (Sefton et al., 2007).
- **Some evidence for negative effects:** When punishment is cheap but used excessively, or when anti-social motives dominate, group efficiency drops (Bolle et al., 2011; Webb & Foddy, 2004).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General inference:**
- **If the control (no-punishment) game is inefficient (below social optimum), enabling effective, well-designed peer punishment will almost always increase efficiency,** especially in repeated linear PGGs with standard design parameters, provided that punishment is sufficiently costly to restrain anti-social use and that group size and MPCR are not extreme.
- **However, the efficiency gain is non-monotonic with respect to punishment cost and effectiveness**: If punishment is too cheap or not filtered (allows for anti-social punishment), net efficiency may stagnate or even decline; if too costly, the deterrent effect is lost (Casari & Luini, 2009; Decker et al., 2003).
- **Institutional design is critical**: consensus requirements, anti-social punishment filters, communication, and information availability all moderate the effect.
- **When mapping from control to treatment efficiency:**
  - **Consider initial efficiency loss in early rounds as players learn and deploy punishment**; later-round efficiency can provide a better estimate of the steady-state effect.
  - **In control games with relatively high efficiency, punishment-enabled efficiency is less likely to increase and may decline if used indiscriminately.**
  - **A 10–40% increase in efficiency is common in standard lab PGGs where control efficiency is low and punishment is well specified (Fehr et al., 2002; Sefton et al., 2007).**

**Dimension-modulated expectations:**
- For **low MPCR** (i.e., high incentive to free ride), punishment has the most to gain but also risks larger net efficiency losses if not well targeted (Zhuang et al., 2012; Gürerk et al., 2009).
- **Longer games and fixed partner structures** facilitate cooperation-stabilizing effects of punishment (Fehr et al., 2002).
- **Absence of chat/communication:** punishment is still effective, but less so than with communication (Bochet et al., 2006; Sefton et al., 2007).
- **Higher group size:** punishment effectiveness and net efficiency gains may decline (Carpenter et al., 2009).
- **Privileged group members or productivity heterogeneity can dampen or reverse the efficiency benefit of punishment** (Reuben & Riedl, 2009; Tan, 2008).
- **Anti-social punishment (punishing high contributors), asymmetric punishment power, or ability for retaliatory feuds** are critical negative moderators (Nikiforakis & Engelmann, 2011; Casari & Luini, 2009).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed game design dimensions:**
- **player_count:** Most experiments (3–6 players), and several studies examine group size effects explicitly; evidence indicates decreasing marginal benefit of punishment with increasing group size, but positive effect remains in moderate-sized groups (Fehr et al., 2002; Casari & Plott, 2003).
- **num_rounds:** Strongly represented; repeated games with 10–20 rounds are standard, with clear evidence that longer interaction enables punishment to be more effective.
- **mpcr:** Explicitly analyzed in many studies, with robust evidence that higher MPCR (greater marginal return) increases both the baseline and treatment efficiency, and the impact of punishment (Fehr et al., 2002; Sefton et al., 2007).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech (impact):** Almost universally detailed; the cost-to-impact ratio is a core moderator of prediction, both empirically and in theory (Nikiforakis et al., 2010; Rigdon, 2009; Casari & Luini, 2009).
- **all_or_nothing:** Both binary and continuous contribution environments are tested, with core findings in both settings.
- **chat:** Communication is a strong moderator; absence or presence of chat is explicitly manipulated in several papers.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Reward is directly compared to punishment in several studies, particularly for baseline comparisons (Gürerk et al., 2009).
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Less commonly varied, but some studies manipulate information conditions, feedback, and anonymity (Nikiforakis & Engelmann, 2011; Sefton et al., 2007; Maier-Rigaud et al., 2010).

**Indirect/contextual coverage:**
- **default_contrib (contribution framing):** Occasionally varied or discussed, but typically the explicit choice is the focus.
- **punishment institution details** (consensus vs. individual, ostracism, anonymity): Emphasized in a subset and shown to be key moderators.
- **Heterogeneity in endowment/productivity:** Specifically tested in a few studies.

**Missing/sparsely informed:**
- **Inter-group competition, multimodal communication, extremely large groups (N > 10), dynamic or endogenous MPCR, nuanced information-visibility options, rare game dimensions (e.g., explicit exit options, explicit pre-commitments).**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency gains from punishment are neither universal nor monotonic.** There is persistent ambiguity: in some game structures, punishment increases contribution but not efficiency; in others, it increases both, and in some, it can reduce efficiency, especially if anti-social, retaliatory/feud, or indiscriminate punishment is prevalent.
- **Institutional variation is under-explored:** Many laboratory studies use standard or unrestricted peer punishment; real-world environments may differ significantly in punishment legitimacy, consensus requirements, and informal filters.
- **Behavioral diversity and strong context effects:** Efficiency outcomes depend on group composition, prior experience, cultural context (e.g., field studies in South Africa, Colombia, Japan), and baseline trust/social capital (Kocher et al., 2012; Lopez et al., 2012).
- **Upper bound on generalizability:** Most evidence is from small, fixed, laboratory groups; predictions should be made cautiously for very large N, dynamic games, non-anonymous real-world settings, or highly heterogeneous groups.
- **Short- vs. long-run effects:** Some findings suggest initial efficiency losses and later gains (Sefton et al., 2007); studies with only a few rounds may miss longer-run benefits.
- **Interactions with other institutional dimensions (communication, reward):** The effect of punishment is not independent from the availability of other mechanisms (Bochet et al., 2006; Gürerk et al., 2009).
- **Behavioral outcome focus in some studies:** Some papers report only contribution or punishment rates without efficiency, limiting their direct use for payoff-based prediction.

**In summary:**  
The literature provides strong, nuanced, and often directly parameterized guidance for predicting the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in public goods games. The effect is generally, but not always, positive; careful consideration of punishment cost, impact, group structure, baseline efficiency, institutional design, and round structure is essential. The mapping from control to treatment efficiency should always account for these moderators and acknowledge non-monotonic, context-dependent effects, and the role of initial efficiency and institutional design.
