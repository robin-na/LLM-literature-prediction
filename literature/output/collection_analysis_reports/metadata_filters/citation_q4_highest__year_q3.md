# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is comprehensive in size (63 papers) and contains a mix of empirical (laboratory and field experiments, observational studies) and theoretical works, with a balance between directly relevant PGG treatments and broader behavioral literature. Across papers, there is a substantial core of directly relevant, empirical evidence focused on the effect of enabling punishment in public goods games (PGGs), including standardized lab PGGs and theoretical models closely matching canonical PGG structures (e.g., Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Liu et al., 2018, 2019; Duong & Han, 2021). A secondary tier consists of adjacent or variant game paradigms (e.g., threshold games, common-pool resource games, spatial/networked PGGs, and prisoner's dilemma games) and mechanism-focused reviews. Many other papers address norm enforcement, reputation, or cooperation in contexts that are conceptually relevant but do not report payoff-related outcomes or manipulate punishment.

Overall, the evidence base is strong and moderately wide for the direct prediction task, with richer coverage of design dimensions and outcome data concentrated in standard repeated PGGs and close theoretical analogues, but with important limitations on generalizability to all possible game structures.

# 2) Task Relevance

## pgg_or_variant
- **Exact relevance**: A core subset of papers employs standard repeated PGGs with and without punishment interventions (Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Liu et al., 2018, 2019; Duong & Han, 2021).
- **Close relevance**: Several works focus on direct variants—spatial/networked PGGs, common-pool resource games, collective-risk games, and games with endogenous/exogenous institutions (Zhu et al., 2020; Góis et al., 2019; Chen & Szolnoki, 2018).
- **Adjacent/weak relevance**: Many others analyze related paradigms (prisoner's dilemmas, trust games, third-party punishment), or use observational vignettes conceptualizing social dilemmas or norm violations, but not actual PGGs.

## punishment_or_sanctions
- **Exact relevance**: Numerous papers experimentally manipulate or theoretically model peer or institutional punishment mechanisms directly within PGGs.
- **Close/adjacent**: Others consider conditional exclusion, reward mechanisms, or broader norm enforcement, sometimes in different game forms.
- **None/weak**: Some papers focus exclusively on reward, observability, gossip, or general norm psychology, lacking explicit punishment implementations.

## efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome
- **Exact/close relevance**: Several key papers report group efficiency, average group payoff, or aggregate welfare as the main or central outcome variable (Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Duong & Han, 2021; Liu et al., 2018, 2019; Lohse & Waichman, 2020).
- **Adjacent/weak relevance**: Many papers report only non-payoff behavioral outcomes, such as contribution or cooperation rates, frequency of punishment, or norm compliance. Some papers reference efficiency in passing or as a conceptual point, but do not measure or report it.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**: A subset of the literature directly measures group efficiency (average group payoff relative to the maximum possible), total earnings, group welfare, or surplus (Arechar et al., 2018; Duong & Han, 2021; Liu et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Liu et al., 2019; Li et al., 2018). Some theoretical models provide explicit formulas for aggregate efficiency based on game parameters.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**: Most remaining studies report contribution/cooperation rates, strategic behavior frequencies, norm enforcement behavior, or psychological/moral judgments. Antisocial punishment, punishment assignments, and qualitative increases in cooperation are commonly covered, but without efficiency or total payoff data (e.g., Fang et al., 2019; Pleasant & Barclay, 2018; Lindström et al., 2018).
- **Indirect proxies**: In some theoretical works, outcomes such as 'group achievement' (fraction of groups reaching a threshold) are reported, which may map to efficiency in threshold games, but are not equivalent.
- **Missing efficiency data**: Many review and mechanism-centered papers do not report efficiency or any payoff-based measure.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical and Theoretical Synthesis

- **General tendency**: Enabling peer or institutional punishment in repeated PGGs usually increases group efficiency compared to controls without punishment, provided punishment is not prohibitively costly and is targeted at norm violations (Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Liu et al., 2018). The effect is both robust and sizable in controlled, canonical designs.
- **Contingency on costs and design**: The effect size is strongly moderated by punishment cost, punishment effectiveness (‘fine’/cost ratio), and prosocial versus antisocial targeting (Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Duong & Han, 2021; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Liu et al., 2019). When punishment is non-normative, antisocial, or triggers mutual punishment (feuding), efficiency gains can be offset or reversed (Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Pleasant & Barclay, 2018).
- **Limits and offsetting costs**: In threshold games and environments where the cost of punishment does not translate into higher, sustained cooperation or where additional costs (e.g., resource depletion, mutual punishment) accumulate, net efficiency gains may be null or negative (Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Chen & Szolnoki, 2018; Gross & De Dreu, 2019).
- **Comparison to rewards/exclusion**: Evidence suggests reward schemes may sometimes outperform punishment for efficiency, especially at low baseline cooperation rates or when the cost of rewarding is lower than costly punishment (Yang et al., 2018; Duong & Han, 2021; Wang et al., 2021). Exclusion mechanisms and hybrid strategies can also boost efficiency, depending on thresholds and implementation (Liu et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2018).
- **Robustness**: The positive effect of punishment on efficiency is generally found in lab experiments and theoretical models, though not uniformly across all environments—spatial, multi-generational, or private-solution contexts may yield different outcomes.

## Mechanistic Arguments

- **Normative context**: The presence of group/institutional norms or constraints decreases antisocial punishment and increases efficiency (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- **Cultural/institutional moderators**: Cultural context and adoption of punishment institutions moderate both the occurrence of punishment and its effectiveness (Henrich & Muthukrishna, 2021; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020).
- **Ecological/resource context**: When the productivity or growth rate of the collective resource is low, punishment may not increase efficiency (Chen & Szolnoki, 2018; Yan et al., 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Directly informed contexts**: In standard repeated PGGs (4–5 players, 10–20 rounds, no communication, continuous contributions, 1:3 or similar cost-to-impact punishment ratio, moderate/typical MPCRs), enabling peer punishment reliably increases group efficiency above baseline control efficiency (Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Liu et al., 2018). This implies that, for prediction, a nonzero increment to efficiency relative to the control is warranted when punishment is enabled under these parameters.
- **Moderating design dimensions**: The prediction should be adjusted downward if:
    - Punishment is costly (high cost-to-impact ratio)
    - Antisocial or retaliatory punishment is prevalent due to contextual factors (e.g., partner competition, low normative consensus), as efficiency gains may be eliminated (Pleasant & Barclay, 2018; Gross & De Dreu, 2019)
    - Punishment targets are ambiguous or identity concealment is possible (to the extent allowed by design)
    - Baseline (control) efficiency is already near optimum.
- **Mechanism caveats**: If the design context encourages mutual punishment feuds, partner competition, or allows alternate solutions (individual/private), punishment can reduce efficiency even if cooperation or collective outcomes rise (Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Lohse & Waichman, 2020).
- **Diminishing returns and threshold effects**: Efficiency improvements from punishment have upper bounds and may plateau at high baseline cooperation, and when punishment is institutionally or normatively constrained (Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Duong & Han, 2021).
- **Transfer from adjacent games**: Use greater caution drawing quantitative predictions from spatial, threshold, or networked games unless the design closely matches canonical PGG parameters and efficiency is explicitly measured.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

For the 14 prediction dimensions:

- **Directly informed**:
    - `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Liu et al., 2018, 2019; Duong & Han, 2021; many theory papers)
    - `all_or_nothing` (many PGG models test both continuous and binary contributions)
- **Indirectly informed or contextually discussed**:
    - `chat` (occasionally manipulated; effect is typically positive for cooperation but rarely interacts systematically with punishment in efficiency outcomes)
    - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (several theory and empirical papers examine alternative or hybrid incentive mechanisms)
    - `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` (feedback and observability are sometimes included; effects more often assessed for cooperation or norm compliance)
    - `default_contrib` (rarely, but sometimes addressed in framing effects)
- **Effectively missing**:
    - Some papers do not specify or manipulate several design dimensions (e.g., feedback presentation, default option), limiting their parameter mapping.
    - Contexts such as `show_punishment_id` (anonymity vs. identity revelation), though referenced occasionally, are not systematically varied in relation to efficiency in most studies.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Gaps in design coverage**: Not all 14 game design dimensions are systematically manipulated or jointly analyzed in relation to efficiency outcomes. Some are present only as context or framing.
- **Non-payoff/efficiency outcomes prevalent**: A large portion of the literature discusses only non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contributions, cooperation rates). Only a subset provides the group efficiency data required for literal prediction tasks.
- **Ambiguity in boundary cases**: There is ongoing ambiguity regarding the impact of punishment costs, targeting (antisocial punishment), private solutions, and ecological/resource constraints, with some evidence showing null or even negative effects on efficiency.
- **External validity**: Many results are drawn from idealized laboratory contexts or infinite-population theory; their calibration to field or more complex real-world settings is uncertain.
- **Cultural/institutional moderators**: Cross-cultural and institutional variation in punishment norms and uptake is highlighted in theory and reviews, but empirical efficiency data across diverse contexts is limited.
- **Mechanism vs. outcome**: Several review and theory papers identify mechanistic moderators without providing quantitative estimates, making their guidance valuable but not parameter-specific for prediction.
- **Limited evidence on novel hybrids**: For hybrid institutional/peer punishment, variable observability, and rare design features (multiple treatment arms, complex feedback), direct efficiency comparisons are sparse.
- **Potential for over-generalization**: Several cautionary notes stress that increased cooperation does not always map onto increased efficiency, especially with costly or norm-violating punishment.

---

**In summary:** While the literature provides strong, directly relevant evidence that enabling punishment can, under canonical PGG settings, increase group efficiency relative to control, there are significant boundaries and moderators, especially concerning punishment cost, antisocial punishment, alternative solution availability, and normative context. Most, but not all, prediction dimensions are informed either directly or by mechanism argument, but only a subset is systematically tested for their impact on efficiency. Prediction should be modulated to account for these limitations and for contexts where efficiency improvements are not guaranteed.
