# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set provides a **broad and rich empirical foundation** with substantial theoretical supplementation for analyzing punishment effects in public-goods-game (PGG) environments. The bulk of the evidence comes from **lab experiments** with canonical, repeated linear PGGs that directly report on group payoff or efficiency outcomes under various punishment regimes. There is also a subset of **field experiments** (especially in resource dilemmas, trust games, or contest games), as well as **conceptual and formal theoretical models** investigating the mechanisms and moderators of punishment effects.

Empirical studies most often focus on small to medium group sizes (typically 3–5 players, up to 16 in a few cases), with well-specified information structures and punishment technologies. Theoretical work provides conditions and comparative statics for when punishment leads to higher efficiency, often highlighting moderators missed in the lab. A number of adjacent studies (not strict PGGs) test related settings (e.g., common-pool resources, contest games, trust/dictator/sequential allocation games).

**Most relevant evidence is empirical and experimental**, with clear attention to both behavioral outcomes (contribution, cooperation) and **payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group profit, welfare)**. There is good coverage on standard design dimensions, though evidence is thinner for features such as chat, rewards, visibility of rounds, and nuances of feedback or identity display. Regional/cultural variation, endogenous versus exogenous institution choice, and punishment targeting (to free-riders versus indiscriminate) are captured in several studies.

Theoretical and conceptual papers provide supporting mechanism arguments and clarify boundary conditions, but are largely consistent with the main empirical trends.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant: exact/close**

- The literature is *highly relevant* to canonical PGGs, with most empirical evidence based on repeated linear VCMs or tightly structured close variants (common-pool resources, exclusion/expulsion mechanisms, etc.).
- Adjacent environments (e.g., contest games, trust games) are less directly relevant but support context interpretation and boundary testing.

**punishment_or_sanctions: exact/close**

- Most relevant papers manipulate explicit, costly *peer punishment*, and several extend to *institutional, centralized, or expulsion* forms.
- Some papers analyze *reward* or mixed regimes for comparative purposes.
- Coverage of punishment variants (counter-punishment, third-party punishment, targeted vs. unrestricted) is robust for canonical PGGs.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome: exact/close**

- Several studies directly report *group efficiency* (payoff relative to social optimum), *average group payoff*, or *welfare*.
- Many directly compare efficiency across control (no punishment) and treatment (punishment-enabled) games.
- A few studies report only on behavioral measures or adjacent outcomes (contributions, cooperation frequency), which are clearly marked as such.

**Summary**: The core literature is of **exact or close relevance** for the prediction task: forecasting group efficiency under peer punishment in PGGs, conditional on design dimensions and control efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, welfare):**
- Many empirical studies measure and report *group efficiency* (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Masclet et al., 2003; Sutter et al., 2010; Putterman et al., 2011; Ambrus & Greiner, 2012).
- Reporting is often explicit, with efficiency calculated relative to the fully cooperative benchmark.
- Studies also use *total group earnings*, *net income after punishment costs*, *surplus*, or *welfare* as proxies (e.g., Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Kosfeld et al., 2009).
- In some cases, only *average group payoff* or change relative to baseline is reported.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- *Contribution rate*, *cooperation rate*, and *use of punishment* are reported widely and often increase more than efficiency (emphasized by Masclet et al., 2003; Ertan et al., 2009).
- Studies frequently note that increased contributions do not always translate into efficiency gains, due to the cost of punishment or misdirected punishment (Nikiforakis, 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).

**Interim Outcomes:**
- Several papers report on the *net effect*: increased contributions partly offset (or, in rare cases, outweighed) by the cost/burden of punishment (e.g., Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Nikiforakis, 2008).

**Theory outcomes:**
- Models often emphasize sustainable efficiency in equilibrium, conditional on the presence of certain player types, punishment cost/effectiveness, or information structure (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kandori, 1992).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Central pattern:**
- *Enabling peer punishment* usually increases contributions and often efficiency, but the size and direction of the efficiency gain *strongly depend* on punishment effectiveness, cost, accuracy, and targeting.

**Positive effects:**
- In standard repeated linear PGGs, peer punishment increases efficiency by **10–20%** (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Masclet et al., 2003; Sutter et al., 2010; Ertan et al., 2009), sometimes more in low baseline settings (Kosfeld et al., 2009; Page et al., 2005).
- Institutional forms such as **delegated/centralized punishment** or *endogenous institution selection* typically yield the *strongest and most reliable* efficiency gains (Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Kosfeld et al., 2009; Putterman et al., 2011).
- Efficiency gains are *greater* if punishment is **targeted exclusively at free riders** and not allowed against high contributors (Ertan et al., 2009).
- *Endogenous choice* (voting) over institutions further increases efficiency relative to exogenous imposition (Sutter et al., 2010; Vollan, 2008, but context dependent).

**Mixed and negative effects:**
- **Efficiency does not always rise:** When punishment is *misdirected* (antisocial punishment; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Fehr et al., 2008), when **counter-punishment** is possible (Nikiforakis, 2008), or where *normative conflict* or cultural context favors punishment of cooperators (Fehr et al., 2008; Vollan, 2008), punishment can reduce group earnings below the no-punishment baseline.
- With **imperfect or noisy monitoring**, intermediate-strength punishment can reduce efficiency even as it increases cooperation; only very strong punishment can (sometimes) restore net efficiency (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Abreu et al., 1991).
- The **cost of punishment** can offset the gains from higher contributions, especially if punishment is overused or not tightly targeted (Page et al., 2005; Nikiforakis et al., 2012).

**Moderators/Boundary conditions:**
- Punishment’s positive effect on efficiency is **larger** when:
    - Control efficiency is low (i.e., the baseline is a poorly cooperating group).
    - Punishment is highly *effective* (high punishment magnitude relative to cost, strong deterrent).
    - Punishment is **targeted at free riders only**.
    - The group can vote on or endogenize sanctioning institutions.
    - Monitoring is perfect or nearly so.
    - The game is relatively small-group (4–5), repeated, and with clear identification of defectors.

- The effect is **smaller or negative** when:
    - **Misuse/antisocial punishment**, norm conflict, or cultural environment promotes punishment of cooperators.
    - Punishment is untargeted/unrestricted.
    - Counter-punishment opportunities exist.
    - Monitoring is imperfect.
    - Group size is very large (diluting punishment credibility).

**General principle:**
- *Punishment increases contributions in almost all settings, but only increases efficiency when the cost of punishment does not outweigh its deterrent effect and when punishment is well-targeted.* Poor targeting, overt counter-punishment, or high-cost, low-effect punishment risks net efficiency losses.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Directly Supported Guidance:**
- When predicting **treatment efficiency** (with peer punishment enabled) given control efficiency and game design, the **most robust expectation** in canonical repeated PGGs is a **moderate to strong efficiency gain** (10–20%, sometimes more) except:
    - In contexts of high antisocial punishment or counter-punishment,
    - Where monitoring is weak or punishment is weakly targeted,
    - Or where control efficiency is already high and crowding-out/trust effects matter.

- If control efficiency is high, **marginal efficiency gain from punishment may be small**; in some theoretical models, adding punishment can even reduce efficiency through crowding-out of trust (van der Weele, 2012; Vollan, 2008).

- **Game design moderators matter:**
    - **Player Count:** Efficiency gains are strongest in small groups (typically n=3-5). The effect diminishes in larger groups or when accountability is diluted.
    - **Num Rounds:** More rounds allow punishment threats to be effective. One-shot games see little efficiency gain; repeated games see more.
    - **MPCR:** Higher MPCR (return to public good) increases the likelihood that punishment supports efficient cooperation.
    - **Punishment Cost/Effectiveness:** High-impact, low-cost punishment is efficient; otherwise, costliness can outweigh gains.
    - **All-or-Nothing vs. Continuous:** Both see efficiency gains from punishment but all-or-nothing (threshold goods) may respond more strongly to targeted punishment.
    - **Monitoring/Information:** Imperfect or delayed monitoring reduces the effectiveness of punishment and may decrease net efficiency.
    - **Chat/Communication:** Evidence suggests that combining punishment with communication, especially credible promises, enhances efficiency; punishment alone does not always suffice (Bochet & Putterman, 2009).
    - **Endogenous Choice:** Allowing groups to choose their institution or sanction scheme amplifies efficiency gains (Sutter et al., 2010; Putterman et al., 2011).
    - **Cultural/Social Context:** Some settings with high trust or strong norms may achieve high efficiency without punishment; adding punishment can crowd out intrinsic motivation and decrease efficiency (Vollan, 2008; Fehr et al., 2008).

**Cautions:**
- Where evidence is based on** non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (contributions, cooperation), state that efficiency inference is indirect.
- **Do not assume monotonic relationships:** Some increment in punishment magnitude, frequency, or scope can reduce efficiency (e.g., via retaliation, feuding, or crowding-out).
- **Specific design features** like counter-punishment, expulsion power, restriction of punishment targets, and centralization can make a decisive difference.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count` (well-covered): Small groups (3–5) best studied; larger and variable group effects discussed.
- `num_rounds` (well-covered): Treatments with 10–50 rounds; one-shot games analyzed for boundary comparison.
- `mpcr` (well-covered): Most PGGs use 0.3–0.65; varying MPCR is directly shown to moderate punishment’s efficacy.
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech` (well-covered): Manipulated across studies; high leverage/low cost is more efficient.
- `punishment_exists` (ubiquitous): Central study feature.
- `all_or_nothing` (covered): Some studies manipulate continuous vs. binary contribution (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012).
- `chat` (partially covered): Communication, especially non-binding promises, can interact with punishment to boost efficiency.

**Indirectly or Contextually Informed:**
- `default_contrib` (framing): Covered primarily in studies on contextual framing and social learning, less so in PGG with punishment.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Compared to punishment in a few key studies (Sutter et al., 2010; Masclet et al., 2003); reward can sometimes yield higher efficiency gains.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Feedback and information conditions tested in a few studies, but not always systematically.
- `show_punishment_id`: Anonymity vs. identification is discussed, especially effects of observed vs. unobserved punishment (Fudenberg & Pathak, 2010).

**Sparse or Effectively Missing:**
- Precise treatment of **feedback dimensions** (`show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`) is rare; most studies assume anonymous punishment.
- Detailed manipulation of `default_contrib` and its interaction with incentives is not common for PGGs with punishment.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Antisocial and counter-punishment effects** are **context-dependent** and in some populations or regions (e.g., Russia, India, rural field settings), enabling punishment can **reduce** efficiency or even be welfare-destroying.
- **Monitoring quality and information structure** are critical: imperfect or delayed monitoring can negate or reverse efficiency gains (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Abreu et al., 1991).
- **Behavioral outcomes ≠ efficiency**: Many papers focus on contributions/cooperation, and increases in these do not guarantee net efficiency gains, especially if punishment is overused or misdirected.
- **External validity concerns**: Most evidence is from laboratory settings with student subjects in Western countries; field experiment results are more ambiguous, suggesting social and ecological context matters greatly.
- **Sparse coverage of some design dimensions**: Direct manipulations of chat, reward, identity revelation, feedback structure, and nuanced framing are underexplored in PGGs with punishment.
- **Edge-case ambiguity**: For games with high initial efficiency (due to norms, communication, or context), adding punishment may crowd out cooperation or even lower efficiency; the marginal benefit of punishment is much smaller when control games are already highly efficient.
- **Usually focuses on short- to medium-term rounds (≤50)**: Long-run, emergent dynamics or adaptation are less commonly studied.
- **Potential publication bias**: There may be underreported null or negative effects for punishment, especially in non-lab or high-trust environments.

---

**In summary**: The literature offers strong, direct, empirically grounded prediction support for the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in standard PGG settings, conditioned on baseline efficiency and design dimensions—especially group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/effect, targeting, and information structure. Exceptions and context-dependencies (antisocial punishment, imperfect monitoring, high initial efficiency, strong norms) must be carefully noted. Less can be concluded for rarely manipulated features (feedback structure, framing, chat) in PGGs with punishment. The prediction task should explicitly consider these moderators and clarify when efficiency inferences are supported by payoff, not just behavioral, outcomes.
