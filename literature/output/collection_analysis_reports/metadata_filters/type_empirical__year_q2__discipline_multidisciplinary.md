# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set is both **rich** and **diverse** regarding laboratory experimental studies on (a) public goods games (PGGs), (b) the availability of punishment or sanctions, and (c) outcomes related to both efficiency/payoff and behavioral responses. The majority of papers are **empirical laboratory experiments** directly manipulating punishment in PGGs and close variants, with a smaller number on adjacent designs (e.g., dyadic prisoner's dilemmas, networked cooperation games). There is **ample empirical data** on group payoff/efficiency and design moderators in standard lab PGGs. Field experiments and observational studies are rare, and adjacent or non-payoff-focused studies are a substantial minority.

**Theoretical discussions** or pure mechanism modeling are not primary here; most papers provide quantitative, experimental results. The evidence base is thus particularly strong for classic, repeated, multi-person lab PGGs with and without punishment, and moderate to sparse for field settings, unusual design variants, or contexts with high complexity, noise, or corruption.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Many core studies use standard, repeated PGGs (Simpson et al., 2017; Andrighetto et al., 2013; Gächter et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017).
- **Close relevance:** Several investigate step-level, snowdrift, maintenance/provision, or threshold games, and modified PGGs (Gross et al., 2016; Jiang et al., 2013).
- **Adjacent/weak/none:** Dyadic PDs, dictator/ultimatum/TPP games, and field/intergroup/other non-PGG social dilemmas make up a notable minority.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Numerous studies experimentally manipulate peer punishment, institutional punishment, or both, including peer-to-peer and centralized (Gross et al., 2016), with detailed design information.
- **Close relevance:** Several focus on indirect, reputational, or ostracism-based sanctions; others only allow for social disapproval, not material punishment.
- **Adjacent/weak:** Various investigate only antisocial punishment, compensation, or reward mechanisms, sometimes with nonstandard or costless sanctioning.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Multiple studies report **direct efficiency effects** (ratio of observed to potential group earnings)—the target outcome.
- **Close relevance:** Others provide total/group earnings, welfare, or surplus, which serve as high-fidelity proxies.
- **Adjacent/weak:** Studies focused solely on cooperation rates, contribution behavior, punishment frequency, or psychological measures without direct payoff/efficiency outcomes are common, but generally less useful for the prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (directly relevant):**  
    - *Efficiency* (group payoff relative to optimum): Explicitly measured in core lab PGG punishment studies (e.g., Simpson et al., 2017; Andrighetto et al., 2013; Gächter et al., 2017; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013).
    - *Group earnings*, *total payoff*, *welfare*, *surplus*: Often directly reported or easily derivable.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (less directly relevant):**  
    - *Contribution rate*, *cooperation rate*, *punishment frequency/intensity*, *antisocial punishment*, *norm compliance*, *retaliation*.
    - Many papers detail the *dynamics* of punishment assignment, underlying motives, and social/psychological moderators, but these are distinct from efficiency.

- **Spillovers and indirect proxies:**  
    - Some studies use trust/trustworthiness in subsequent games as spillover measures, providing adjacent information.
    - In close-variant games or where payoff is highly correlated with cooperation (but not identical), careful distinction is made (e.g., increased contribution does *not* always correspond to increased efficiency if punishment is costly).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## General Patterns:
- **Classic repeated PGGs with costly peer punishment:**  
    - **Punishment robustly increases cooperation/contributions**, but does *not always* increase group efficiency. In many standard designs with costly peer punishment, the cost of punishment often largely cancels out the payoff gains from increased cooperation, so **efficiency does not improve** (Simpson et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016).
    - If punishment is *costless*, or if the cost/fine ratio is highly favorable (e.g., 1:3 or greater), **efficiency gains can be observed** if excessive punishment is avoided (Andrighetto et al., 2013, with norm-signaling; Gächter et al., 2017, with long repeated games; Gross et al., 2016, with centralized voluntary punishment).

- **Moderating factors:**
    - **Communication/norm signaling**: Norm-signaling or chat alongside punishment can **reduce punishment frequency** and increase efficiency by lowering the need for costly sanctions (Andrighetto et al., 2013).
    - **Centralization/structure**: Allowing groups to *centralize* punishment power (Gross et al., 2016) or enforcing second-order punishment (Hilbe et al., 2014) leads to **higher efficiency** relative to decentralized peer punishment.
    - **Design parameters**: The effect of punishment is **sensitive to punishment cost/technology**, round number and group size, information structure (e.g., anonymity, noise), and the underlying incentive properties (MPCR, all-or-nothing, etc.) (Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017; Jiang et al., 2013; Ozono et al., 2017).

- **Contextual variables and boundary conditions:**
    - **Noisy monitoring**: With substantial noise in contribution monitoring, all punishment institutions can **reduce earnings** below control (van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
    - **Antisocial punishment/competition**: In settings with antisocial punishment or strong between-subject competition, enabling punishment may **decrease or not affect efficiency** (Paál & Bereczkei, 2015; Kubena et al., 2014).
    - **Negative social returns (MPCR < 1/n)**: Punishment can enforce destructive norms, *reducing* group efficiency (Abbink et al., 2017).
    - **Institutional defects (e.g., corruption, bribery opportunities)**: Institutional punishment can decrease efficiency if corrupted (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
    - **Game length**: Punishment's **efficiency benefits increase with more rounds**—the cost is amortized as cooperation stabilizes (Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017).

- **Reward and alternative sanctioning mechanisms:**
    - **Reward alone** may increase contributions, but is often less effective than punishment or needs to be combined for maximum efficiency (Dong et al., 2016).
    - **Costless/non-material sanctions (moral judgments, gossip, norm signaling)** are particularly effective at increasing both cooperation and efficiency, as they avoid the efficiency-draining cost of material punishment (Simpson et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016).

## Heterogeneity and Contradictions:
- While most PGG lab studies indicate positive or neutral effects of punishment on efficiency (if cost/impact ratio is favorable and design is standard), several show **negative efficiency effects** under specific conditions: high punishment costs, antisocial punishment, negative social returns, corruption, noisy monitoring, or short time horizons (Wu et al., 2016; Abbink et al., 2017; Muthukrishna et al., 2017; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design is well-supported in standard lab PGGs with the main design dimensions accounted for**.
    - **If the control game has high baseline efficiency**, and punishment is costly, adding peer punishment typically does *not* improve efficiency unless additional norm-signaling or communication is enabled (Simpson et al., 2017; Andrighetto et al., 2013).
    - **If the control game has low efficiency (bad decay or low MPCR)**, introducing punishment will likely *raise* efficiency, but the **net gain is moderated by the cost structure**: only when the cost-to-impact ratio is favorable (e.g., 1:3 or better), or punishment is rare (mainly a deterrent), will efficiency rise substantially.
    - **Institutional innovations (centralization, leader support, voluntary power transfer, second-order punishment)** markedly **increase efficiency** compared to simple peer punishment (Gross et al., 2016; Ozono et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014).
    - **Other factors**: Short games (few rounds), all-or-nothing games, games with competitive/rank-based payoff, or noisy environments **reduce the expected efficiency gain (or induce loss) from punishment** (Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017; van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Paál & Bereczkei, 2015).

- **Caveats:**  
    - **Punishment is not always efficiency-enhancing**. If punishment costs are high or antisocial punishment operates, efficiency can actually fall versus control. Look for MPCR, punishment cost, group structure, and social norm features as strong moderators.
    - *Efficient prediction from design requires key dimensions* (see next section). Any model or forecast omitting moderator interactions (e.g., the presence of chat, visibility of punishment, or structure of institution) risks large error.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**The following prediction dimensions are directly or strongly indirectly informed:**
- **player_count**: Explicitly manipulated or reported in nearly all relevant PGG studies (range 2–12; mostly 4–6).
- **num_rounds**: Most lab PGGs span 4–50 rounds; time horizon is a key moderator.
- **mpcr**: Systematically varied; effects on punishment impact and baseline efficiency are robust and well-documented.
- **all_or_nothing / continuous**: Both formats represented, with nuanced differences in effect size and stability.
- **chat / communication / norm-signaling**: Several high-relevance studies directly test chat or norm-signaling as critical or interacting with punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2013).
- **punishment_cost / punishment_tech(impact)**: Most efficiency studies directly report cost-to-impact ratio (commonly 1:3 or 1:2); considered a fundamental moderator.
- **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech**: Fewer, but several studies include reward as an alternative or combined mechanism, often comparing directly to punishment.

**Some dimensions are moderately or contextually addressed:**
- **default_contrib**: Rarely a manipulated dimension in punishment/efficiency studies, but present in studies of framing and dynamics.
- **show_n_rounds / show_other_summaries**: Sometimes manipulated or reported; effects on strategic cooperation and punishment are discussed, but less systematically.
- **show_punishment_id**: Well-specified in a subset of studies; anonymity often maintained, but a few examine onymity or identifiability as moderators.

**Dimensions that are under-addressed or missing:**
- **Punishment/reward assignment technical subtleties** (e.g., who can be punished, consensus vs. individual assignment): Occasionally manipulated, but less systematically.
- **Real-time field experiments, large group n, or high complexity “ecological” designs**: Scarce relative to classic, small-n, lab, continuous PGGs.

**Dimension-level evidence in context:**
- **When punishment is anonymous and costly, efficiency gains are often absent; when punishment is visible, cost-effective, and associated with explicit norm signaling or centralization, substantial efficiency gains appear. The presence of even minor chat or norm communication can modulate the effect size. High noise or antisocial punishment risk often reverses effects.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Generality**: The evidence base is strongest for classic repeated lab PGGs with small groups, clear incentives, and standard cost/impact settings. Extrapolation to field or complex environments is not directly supported.
- **Design Gaps**: Not all 14 prediction-relevant design dimensions are fully, independently explored. Some interactions (e.g., between default settings, summary feedback, punishment identifiability) may be missing or thinly treated.
- **Payoff–efficiency distinction**: While compensation and behavioral outcomes abound, strictly efficiency-based results (payoff ratios) are less common outside highly controlled experiments.
- **Context-leakage**: Antisocial punishment, corruption, and group competition can **invert** expected effects—evidence in these settings is relatively sparse but critical for reliable prediction.
- **Boundary contexts**: Studies with high monitoring noise, very large groups, strongly competitive/rank-based payoffs, or institutional detail (foot voting, bribery, exit) are relatively rare or show divergent results, creating uncertainty at the tails of the design space.
- **Control efficiency as moderator:** Some studies do not report baseline/control efficiency directly, and variation in control performance can itself interact with treatment effects, limiting cross-study generalization.
- **Field/Population Variation**: Most studies are with university subjects in lab settings; external validity—especially cultural, demographic, or ecological heterogeneity—is not well covered.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature directly and robustly illuminates how key PGG design features and control efficiency interact with punishment effects on efficiency, particularly in laboratory settings with standard incentives and group sizes. Efficiency increases with punishment mainly when cost structures are favorable and additional institutional or communication supports are in place; in many standard peer punishment setups, efficiency improvement is not observed despite higher cooperation. Contextual and design-level moderators, as well as the risk of efficiency losses in poorly chosen or noisy environments, are repeatedly emphasized. Some game dimensions are well-studied while others remain to be mapped. Limitations include gaps in large-scale or field environments and lack of attention to certain design-interaction effects.
