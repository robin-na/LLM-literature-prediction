# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is *large* (70 papers) but overwhelmingly *theoretical* and simulation-based, with virtually no *empirical* or *experimental* studies involving human subjects or real-world groups. Most papers feature mathematical modeling, evolutionary dynamics, and agent-based simulations across public goods games (PGGs) and closely related social dilemmas (prisoner's dilemma, collective-risk dilemmas, trust games).  
While the set is broad in terms of coverage of possible mechanisms, variations, and contextual factors (institutional design, network structure, spatiality, heterogeneity, reward, and punishment schemes), it is *narrow* with respect to direct empirical tests of peer punishment’s effect on efficiency in laboratory or field PGGs.

Among these papers, a modest subset reports *efficiency* (group payoff relative to maximum possible), while many papers focus on related but non-payoff behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates, norm compliance, etc.). Direct mapping to the prediction task—estimating treatment efficiency from game design parameters plus control efficiency—rests mainly on theoretical and simulation results rather than empirical measurement.

---

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - *exact*: Many papers model the standard PGG or spatial/networked variants closely resembling PGGs (e.g., Sun et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2024).  
  - *close/adjacent*: A large minority use adjacent games (collective-risk dilemmas, trust games, repeated PD, multi-agent regulatory games) (e.g., Yan et al., 2024; Camera & Gioffré, 2025; Melkonyan et al., 2022).  
  - *none/weak*: A few only reference PGGs for analogy and focus on different structures entirely.
- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - *exact*: Heavy coverage of punishment, with diverse mechanisms: peer/central/institutional, costly punishment, reward, exclusion, reputation, dynamic intensity.  
  - *close/adjacent*: Some papers study punishment analogues (ostracism, resource exclusion, partner switching, penalty mechanisms, adaptive exit), or reward/punishment mixtures.  
  - *none/weak*: Papers on alternative mechanisms (information acquisition, minimum contribution, aspiration updates, imitation) omit punishment.
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - *exact*: Only a handful of studies report group efficiency or payoff as the *primary* outcome (e.g., Sun et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024; Yang & Yang, 2024; Cui et al., 2022; Kang et al., 2024).  
  - *close/adjacent*: Others report “group achievement”, “welfare”, or “average payoff” but not as standardized efficiency ratios, or focus on qualitative increases in group benefit.  
  - *weak/none*: The majority focus on cooperation or behavioral strategy rates and infer efficiency outcomes only indirectly.

**Summary:**  
Literature relevance is *highest* for theoretical and simulation models of punishment in PGGs, moderate for institutional/central punishment or adjacent games, and *weak* for empirical payoff-based measurement or exact calibration of efficiency effects in peer punishment treatment conditions.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (*directly relevant to efficiency*):  
  - *Group efficiency* (payoff relative to maximum possible, e.g., Sun et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024)  
  - *Total earnings/welfare/surplus* (aggregate payoff, e.g., Shen et al., 2022; Cui et al., 2022)  
  - *Average group payoff* (“system payoff”)  
- **Closely related but not standardized** (*some mapping to efficiency possible*):  
  - *Group achievement* (group stays above threshold, e.g., Hua & Liu, 2023)  
  - *System stability/social welfare*  
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (*not efficiency*):  
  - *Contribution/cooperation rates* (e.g., prevalence of cooperators, fraction using a strategy)  
  - *Punishment/reward frequency*  
  - *Norm enforcement/compliance rate*  
  - *Strategy profile stability/distribution*  
  - *Oscillation/stability of cooperation*  
  - *Resource/confrontation rates, exit rates, etc.*

**Note:**  
Non-payoff behavioral outcomes are often interpreted (by authors) as *proxies* for efficiency, but these are *not* equivalent and cannot be directly substituted for group payoff or efficiency in the prediction task.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Synthesis of Cross-Paper Insights (Payoff-Related):

1. **Enabling punishment in PGGs increases group efficiency/payoff in most models**, especially when:
   - Punishment is *not too costly* and/or is sufficiently *effective* (Sun et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2022).
   - The control (no-punishment) condition features *low cooperation* (Sun et al., 2025).
   - The *targeting* of punishment is focused (punishing just the lowest contributor is most efficient: Huang et al., 2024).
   - *Network structure* supports local clustering or small-world properties (Cui et al., 2022; Lim & Capraro, 2022).
   - *Reward* is also present, especially if rewards are used as much as possible and punishment is only minimally required (Huang et al., 2024; Yang & Yang, 2024).
   - *Fine redistribution* (punishment proceeds allocated to cooperators/punishers) can offset the cost of punishment and support efficiency at high punishment costs (Sun et al., 2024).

2. **Efficiency gains from punishment are highly parameter-dependent** and not always monotonic:
   - Excessively costly or inefficient punishment can *reduce* efficiency or cause collapse of cooperation/payoff (Sun et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2022).
   - *Threshold effects*—there is typically a minimum required punishment level to tip the system from low to high efficiency (Huang et al., 2024).
   - *Non-monotonicity*: At too high punishment costs or at intermediate population structural parameters, punishment loses effectiveness (Sun et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2024).

3. **Network and institutional structure matter**:
   - *Spatial and networked* populations (small-world or regular lattices) often support higher efficiency from punishment than well-mixed groups (Cui et al., 2022; Lim & Capraro, 2022).
   - *Institutional punishment* (centralized/tax-based) can yield even higher efficiency in some models (Yang & Yang, 2024), but with different (and sometimes less optimistic) dynamics than peer punishment.

4. **Combined and alternative sanctioning mechanisms**:
   - *Reward and punishment together* are more effective than either alone (Sun et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024).
   - *Reputation-based*, *dynamic*, or *adaptive* punishment mechanisms can achieve higher efficiency by reducing punishment costs or targeting non-cooperators more effectively (Kang et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025).

5. **Exceptions / Negative or Mixed Findings**:
   - In dyadic and small-group scenarios, or when punishment is very costly, *punishment can lower efficiency* by introducing direct losses, retaliation, or escalation (Rumble et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2024; Camera & Gioffré, 2025).
   - *Inefficiency* can result if punishment is overused, misdirected, or not offset by efficiency gains from increased cooperation (Sun et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2022).
   - Heterogeneity in population structure (relatedness, reputation, group size) can produce *non-monotonic or U-shaped effects* on efficiency (Dong et al., 2024).

## Based on *non-payoff proxies*:
   - Most models consistently show that punishment increases cooperation rates/strategy prevalence, but these effects only partially and unevenly map onto actual efficiency or group payoff.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **General Direction**:  
  The literature supports the *typical* outcome that enabling (peer) punishment in a PGG (relative to control) *increases efficiency*, especially when baseline (control) efficiency is low and game parameters (punishment cost, effectiveness, targeting, MPCR) are in cooperative-favoring ranges (Sun et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2022).

- **Quantitative Mapping**:  
  Theoretical and simulation studies provide *explicit formulae* for efficiency as a function of punishment cost/effectiveness, MPCR, and group size (Huang et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025), which can be used to *calibrate predictions* if the relevant parameters are known and consistent with those in the models.

- **Important Moderators**:
  - *Punishment cost*: Lower cost increases gain from punishment; increasing too much can nullify benefit or lower efficiency.
  - *MPCR*: Low baseline returns increase value of enabling punishment, but there is a threshold below which even punishment cannot restore cooperation.
  - *Reward existence*: When both reward and punishment are available, use as much reward as possible and minimum necessary punishment for maximum efficiency.
  - *Fine redistribution*: When punishment is expensive, redistributing fines to cooperators/punishers sustains efficiency.
  - *Network structure*: Small-world or spatial structures enhance the efficiency benefit from punishment (Cui et al., 2022; Lim & Capraro, 2022).
  - *Player count and group size*: Effects may be non-monotonic; larger groups are sometimes harder to coordinate, and optimal punishment/reward thresholds shift with size (Huang et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2022).

- **Limits and Caveats**:
  - In small groups or dyads, or if punishment is not targeted or is too costly, punishment can *reduce* efficiency (Rumble et al., 2022; Camera & Gioffré, 2025).
  - There is *little to no direct empirical validation* for these predictions in this paper set; predictions rely on theoretical models.

- **If control efficiency is high**:  
  The marginal benefit from enabling punishment is generally much lower—the main effect occurs where control (no-punishment) cooperation and efficiency are poor.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

### **Directly Informed Dimensions** (modeled in efficiency-relevant studies):

- `player_count`: Heavily covered; effects of group size are modeled and often interact with punishment effects (Sun et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2024).
- `num_rounds`: Modeled in repeated/finite-horizon games, especially for reputational and dynamic commitment effects (Dong et al., 2024; Melkonyan et al., 2022).
- `all_or_nothing`: Modeled commonly, especially for discrete-contribution frameworks (Huang et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2022).
- `mpcr`: A primary manipulated parameter in almost all theoretical papers.
- `punishment_cost`: Modeled as a main effect moderator in all payoff-relevant punishment studies.
- `punishment_tech` (punishment effectiveness): Explicitly varied and found critical for determining gain from punishment.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`: Included and often interacted/compared with punishment (Huang et al., 2024; Yang & Yang, 2024).
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Included in some models as structural knowledge or information parameters (Dong et al., 2024).

### **Indirectly Informed/Contextual Dimensions**:

- `chat`: Rarely modeled; cited as a potential moderator of cooperation in reviews but lacking formal analysis (Lazarus, 2023).
- `default_contrib`: Not directly manipulated but adjacent through minimum contribution policies (Chessa & Loiseau, 2024).
- `show_punishment_id`: Discussed conceptually as relevant to deterrence and trust, but not formally analyzed for efficiency outcomes.
- `reward_tech`: Included in models that feature reward but less frequently as a focal dimension.

### **Dimensions Effectively Missing or Weakly Addressed**:

- `chat`, `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id`: Very limited treatment; their effects on efficiency in the context of punishment are not directly modeled.
- Multi-dimensional interactions (e.g., chat × punishment_cost) are not systematically explored in efficiency-outcome studies.

---

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical Evidence Gap**: The overwhelming reliance on theoretical and simulation models means there is little direct experimental or real-world evidence for the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in PGGs as a function of design dimensions and baseline control efficiency.

- **Generalizability**: Model assumptions (homogeneity, rationality, spatial structure, infinite populations, evolutionary strategies) may not hold or transfer to real-world groups or typical lab experiments.

- **Efficiency Measurement**: Few studies report *efficiency in the precise predictive sense* (treatment group payoff as a ratio to full cooperation). Many report related but non-identical outcomes, risking overgeneralization.

- **Behavioral Outcomes ≠ Efficiency**: Many findings—especially those about cooperation rate, strategy prevalence, or compliance—cannot be automatically equated with increased efficiency, particularly in the presence of costly punishment and complex feedbacks.

- **Parameter Sensitivity/Boundary Conditions**: The payoff benefit of punishment is *highly parameter-dependent*. Excessive punishment cost, improper targeting, non-optimal network structure, or certain population heterogeneities can *reduce* or even *reverse* the efficiency gain from enabling punishment.

- **Dimension Coverage**: Some key design dimensions in the prediction task (e.g., chat, default contribution, visibility of punishers, framing, summary information) are only sparsely or indirectly addressed, limiting parameter-specific prediction precision.

- **Indirect Inference for Peer Punishment**: Many results derive from institutional or central punishment settings; the mapping to voluntary, peer-enabled punishment—the focus of the prediction task—may not be exact.

- **Ambiguity in Mixed or Negative Findings**: Some models document negative or non-monotonic effects of punishment (e.g., in small groups, or at intermediate relatedness; see Dong et al., 2024).

**Summary**: This paper set provides strong *theoretical* justification and parameter mapping for predicting the positive impact of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments under a wide range of game design settings—*but* it should be applied cautiously because direct empirical confirmation and direct, comprehensive coverage of all prediction task dimensions (especially with real group behavior) is missing. Model-based predictions should be validated against empirical data when available, and boundary cases (high punishment cost, small groups, inefficient targeting) require particularly conservative extrapolation.
