# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is broad and robust, encompassing 229 sources with a strong empirical core, especially for standard and closely related public goods and common-pool resource games. Both experimental lab and field/real-world (framed or lab-in-the-field) studies are included, with substantial representation from theory and simulation work complementing empirical findings. Many papers directly manipulate or observe the presence/absence of punishment or institutional sanctions, varying key design parameters and reporting on efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes. There is strong coverage of canonical linear PGGs, as well as variants (threshold PGGs, spatial/networked, CPRs, asymmetric/heterogeneous endowments, and some adjacent games like the Prisoner's Dilemma and Trust Game). However, coverage is less dense (and more inferential) for settings with highly complex group structure, field diversity, or alternative sanctioning/reward schemes. The majority of studies reporting on efficiency explicitly distinguish between contribution rates/cooperation and payoff-based efficiency metrics.

# 2) Task Relevance

Task relevance is assessed on three dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - *exact*: The majority of high-relevance empirical and theoretical papers are based on the canonical repeated linear public goods game (PGG) or well-justified close variants (threshold PG, CPR, spatial/networked).
  - *close*: Some studies focus on CPR games, asymmetric roles, or threshold/club good variants. Most maintain the payoff structure necessary for mapping efficiency effects, even if behavior or group interaction departs from canonical PGG.
  - *adjacent/weak*: A number of papers use two-player games (e.g., PD, Trust Game, Dictator, etc.) or focus mainly on behavioral mechanisms, not directly on group provision or efficiency.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:
  - *exact*: The literature is rich in lab and theory studies directly manipulating the availability, cost, effectiveness, or institutional form of peer punishment, centralized punishment, or ostracism/exclusion.
  - *close/adjacent*: Reward, feedback, or exclusion mechanisms are covered where overlapping with punishment logics; some work looks at crowding effects from combining punishments with other interventions.
  - *weak/none*: Papers focusing exclusively on reputational, communication, or framing/no-punishment interventions.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
  - *exact*: Many papers report efficiency (group payoff relative to full cooperation) or equivalent metrics (group profit, social welfare, surplus), sometimes normalized to the social optimum.
  - *close/adjacent*: Some use group earnings, compliance, or resource stock as proxies, or infer efficiency direction from contribution rates, provided costs of punishment are specified.
  - *weak/none*: Others only report contributions, conditional cooperation, punishing behavior, or attitudinal outcomes without clear mappings to group payoff or without cost accounting.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related (used for efficiency predictions):**
  - Total group payoff/earnings.
  - Group welfare, surplus, or average earnings.
  - Ratio of realized to maximum (full cooperation) payoff (efficiency).
  - Resource stock (in renewable or CPR settings).
  - Frequency of provision (threshold success, only if mapped to payoff).

- **Non-payoff/behavioral (adjacent or indirect):**
  - Contribution rates, cooperation rates.
  - Norm enforcement rates.
  - Frequency and severity of punishment.
  - Antisocial/prosocial punishment/deviance.
  - Punishment targets, group composition responses.
  - Behavior under social/emotional or informational manipulations.
  - Compliance, trust, fairness, and related psychological measures.

- **Explicit in the digest:** Many studies carefully distinguish between behavioral shifts (higher cooperation) and net efficiency (group payoff after accounting for punishment costs or enforcer losses).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis—Empirical and Theoretical Convergence:**

- **Punishment increases contributions reliably, but the effect on efficiency is variable**: Many studies confirm that enabling peer or institutional punishment in repeated PGGs almost always raises average contributions, but these do not universally translate to higher efficiency because of costly/enforcement overhead and potential mis-targeting (Page et al., 2013; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Angelovski et al., 2018).

- **Efficiency increases with punishment are most robust when:**
  - Punishment is low- or moderate-cost, high-impact (Fatas et al., 2020; Grieco et al., 2017; Waichman, 2020).
  - The punishment system is well-calibrated and norm violation is clearly observable (De Geest & Kingsley, 2019; Koch et al., 2021).
  - The institutional structure supports or coordinates enforcement (Redistribution, endogenous voting, central institution: Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Sasaki, 2014).
  - Relational, ethnic, or informational fragmentation is low, or punishment can be credibly targeted (Mantilla et al., 2021; Robbett, 2016).
  - Strategic design makes antisocial punishment, retaliation, or destructive cycles rare (Khadjavi et al., 2017; Fatas et al., 2020).

- **Efficiency gains are lower or disappear when:**
  - Punishment is high-cost or poorly targeted, especially due to incomplete information about endowments/contributions (De Geest & Kingsley, 2019; Robbett, 2016; Peng, 2022).
  - There is motive or scope for antisocial punishment, ethnic favoritism, or retaliatory cycles (Mantilla et al., 2021; Vollan et al., 2019).
  - Institutional design weakens deterrence (e.g., cost to acquire right to punish, low monitoring or feedback, procedural hurdles) (Ramalingam et al., 2016; Kingsley & Brown, 2016; Tan & Xiao, 2012).
  - Baseline (control) efficiency is already high, often due to strong social information/feedback (Javaid & Falk, 2015), in which case punishment can reduce efficiency.
  - Network or group structure fosters misaligned incentives (e.g., incomplete or congested punishment networks, group asymmetry) (Fatas et al., 2020; Pi et al., 2022).

- **Theoretical findings reinforce these moderators:** Evolutionary and mechanism/replicator models echo empirical findings: punishment raises mean payoff/efficiency only when its use is not too costly, it is credibly or accurately targetable, and group structure (size, network, persistence) permits the needed enforcement (Sui et al., 2017; Powers, 2018; Eldakar et al., 2013).

- **Reward mechanisms** may achieve similar or greater efficiency at similar cost levels (Zhuang et al., 2012; Cong et al., 2016; Yao & Chen, 2014), but the presence of both is especially effective in some endogenous-institution models.

- **Heterogeneity and information matter:** The efficiency effect of punishment is especially contingent on the observability of individual capacities and actions—without which, mis-targeted punishment can destroy efficiency (Waichman, 2020; De Geest & Kingsley, 2019).

- **Emotional and social context can moderate efficiency effects:** Contexts inducing anger can strengthen the efficiency benefits of punishment, while happiness or certain social cues may reduce or reverse them (Lee & Min, 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Baseline & Moderator Principle:**  
  Use the average efficiency of the control (no-punishment) game as an anchor, with adjustments based on game design dimensions that moderate punishment's cost-effectiveness and targeting.

- **Game parameters where efficiency reliably increases with punishment:**
  - Small/moderate groups (3–5), repeated rounds (≥5–10), clear payoffs (linear or threshold, known MPCR), and complete information about others' contributions and endowments.
  - Punishment with a favorable cost-impact ratio (≥1:3 impact:cost and below thresholds producing excessive punishment cycles or antisocial punishment).
  - Well-calibrated or transparent punishment/feedback, low procedural burden, and accurate identification of defectors.
  - Homogeneous groups or groups with strong social ties, with minority/majority group composition not incentivizing in-group favoritism.
  - No high baseline efficiency from other mechanisms (e.g., social feedback or leadership).

- **Game designs where efficiency gains are limited or unlikely:**
  - High punishment costs or requirements to pay for the right to punish, insufficient or ambiguous feedback, or incomplete information (especially about endowments/contribution capacity).
  - High prevalence of antisocial punishment, in-group favoritism, or network/structural features facilitating revenge cycles.
  - Baseline efficiency already near the social optimum due to other mechanisms.

- **Institutional and technical dimensions strongly moderate effect:**
  - Nature of punishment (peer vs. central), exogenous vs. endogenous institution, presence of voting or covenants, and opportunities for strategic mis-targeting.
  - Structure/topology of punishment network—punishment technology (punishment_tech), centralized or pooled, the number and identification of punishers, and enforcement mechanisms.
  - Feedback/punishment identification (show_punishment_id, show_other_summaries): increased targeting and transparency magnify positive effects.

- **Presence of rewards or substitutes for punishment:**
  - If both reward and punishment are available, or if a redistributive fine exists, group efficiency is frequently even higher (Page et al., 2013; Yao & Chen, 2014).

- **Control efficiency ceiling/floor:**
  - When control efficiency is already high, less room for efficiency improvement—and punishment may backfire; when control efficiency is low and design moderates are favorable, enabling punishment typically boosts efficiency.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions (strong empirical/theoretical evidence):**
- **player_count**, **num_rounds**: Effects of group size and interaction duration are widely manipulated and linked to punishment effectiveness and efficiency.
- **mpcr**: Central in nearly all payoff-sensitive studies; higher MPCR supports cooperation, but interacts with punishment cost-effectiveness.
- **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech**, **punishment_exists**: Extensive coverage of cost, scale/impact, technological/institutional means.
- **all_or_nothing**: Present in most canonical and threshold PGG/CPR studies; some explore mixed or continuous contributions.
- **show_other_summaries**, **show_punishment_id**, **show_n_rounds**: Feedback/information treatments directly tested as moderators of efficiency effects.

**Indirectly informed dimensions (contextually or inferentially):**
- **chat**: Some studies manipulate communication/chat, showing it can substitute for or interact with punishment (Koch et al., 2021; Brick et al., 2016).
- **reward_exists**, **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**: Reward mechanisms studied as alternatives or complements to punishment, often finding equal or greater efficiency gains, particularly when both are available (Zhuang et al., 2012; Cong et al., 2016).
- **default_contrib**: Framing and opt-in/opt-out not often isolated, but framing/context affects baseline efficiency and interaction with punishment in some settings (Messer et al., 2013).

**Sparse/missing dimensions:**
- **Mechanistic or field diversity (complex real-world settings, very large groups, dynamic population):** Present but less systematic; extreme values or institutional complexity are less studied or theorized.
- **Some edge/adjacent design features:** Real-organization behavior, open-access field settings, and highly complex network/learning variants are more often the subject of behavioral or indirect study, with fewer group efficiency reports.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavior vs. payoff distinction:**  
  Many studies report only behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation/contribution rates, norm compliance, punishment frequency) without mapping to efficiency or explicitly accounting for punishment costs. These cannot be used for direct quantitative prediction of efficiency and may overstate likely group payoff gains.

- **Boundary conditions and context-dependence:**  
  Effects of punishment on efficiency are highly moderated by social context, group diversity, network structure, and information environment. Prediction models must account for the possibility of negative, null, or even backfiring effects (e.g., when antisocial punishment is high, information is incomplete, or baseline efficiency is already high).

- **Limited coverage of some marginal or complex design spaces:**  
  Large groups, asymmetric/player-role games, dynamic and field settings, and real-world diversity are less systematically studied for efficiency outcomes.

- **Incomplete mapping of all 14 design dimensions to outcome:**  
  Some variables (like default contribution framing, nuanced reward mechanisms, or specific feedback features) are less often isolated or systematically varied.

- **Less direct evidence for adjacent/weakly related game structures:**  
  Adjacent literature supports some generalizations but may not provide strong quantitative effect size priors for non-canonical PGGs.

- **Potential for publication bias or over-representation of positive results:**  
  Although the literature contains many mixed and negative results, most direct lab evidence of efficiency loss is found in diverse social/field or highly heterogeneous environments, so published estimates may overstate punishment's average effect in natural settings.

- **Theory/model limitations:**  
  Simulation and evolutionary models clarify mechanism and boundary conditions but may not fully translate quantitatively to finite, human-group experiments.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature provides strong, nuanced evidence that the efficiency effect of punishment in PGG-like environments is positive in many canonical designs—particularly with transparent, well-targeted, and low-cost punishment, full information, and moderate group size—but frequently null or negative in heterogeneous, incomplete information, high-cost, or highly fragmented settings. Predictive models for treatment efficiency given design dimensions and control efficiency should weight these moderators heavily and not assume an unconditional efficiency gain from enabling punishment. The most reliable dimension-level predictors are group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, information/feedback structure, and baseline (control) efficiency. 

**Caution is warranted when translating behavioral outcome findings to efficiency predictions or when applying results in marginal or field-realistic environments where context and implementation details may severely moderate the effect.**
