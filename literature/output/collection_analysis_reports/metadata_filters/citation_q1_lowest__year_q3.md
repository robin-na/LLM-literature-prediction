# 1) Evidence Base

**Scope and Nature of the Literature**:  
The paper set is robust and comprehensive for laboratory and some field evidence on public-goods-game (PGG) environments with and without punishment. The core evidence base is dominated by _empirical, experimental lab studies_ directly manipulating punishment (usually peer or centralized) in repeated linear PGGs. This is complemented by a substantial number of _theory/modelling papers_ that extend and formalize mechanism predictions. There are also _adjacent_ studies from CPR (common-pool resource), trust, and related social dilemma games that use similar design elements but differ in crucial ways (e.g., threshold games, principal-agent settings, exclusion, etc.).

**Balance of Empirical and Theoretical Evidence**:  
- **Empirical laboratory PGG studies**: Cover most mainstream design variations—varying group size, rounds, punishment and monitoring cost, centralized vs. peer punishment, cultural context, framing, and information structure.
- **Field and lab-in-the-field experiments**: Less common, but provide external validity checks and insight into consequences of real-world heterogeneity and context.
- **Theory/simulation studies**: Offer formal mechanism-based arguments about when and why punishment may (or may not) increase efficiency, and help generalize findings to more environments, including large or networked populations.
- **Adjacent or indirect evidence**: Many studies address similar mechanisms (e.g., exclusion, reward, partner choice, reputation), or report on behavioral outcomes (like cooperation rate) with only secondary attention to efficiency.

**Breadth for the Prediction Task**:  
The set gives a broad and nuanced empirical and theoretical foundation specifically for predicting **average efficiency outcomes** of introducing peer punishment in typical PGGs and close structural relatives. There is also some representation of more complex and contextualized environments (e.g., different institutions, networked games, endowment heterogeneity). Indirect or ambiguous support is provided for uncommon or edge-case design features.

# 2) Task Relevance

**(a) `pgg_or_variant`**  
- **exact**: The majority of the cited papers are directly about repeated linear (and sometimes binary/all-or-nothing) public goods games, with standard PGG mechanics (e.g., Harrell, 2019; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Ramalingam et al., 2019; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Lim & Zhang, 2020).
- **close**: A significant number of adjacent designs are present (e.g., resource extraction games, trust games, contest games, threshold games, coordination (stag-hunt), and CPRs) which often have similar free-riding incentives, punishment phases, and efficiency measurement, but are not strictly PGGs.

**(b) `punishment_or_sanctions`**  
- **exact**: The key experimental and theoretical papers systematically manipulate peer punishment (costly or costless, peer or centralized).
- **close/adjacent**: Some studies report on adjacent sanctioning mechanisms (third-party punishment, reward only, exclusion/ostracism, symbolic sanctions) or broader institutional enforcement.
- **missing/weak**: A minority are matched environments without any form of punishment or for solely reward-based or reputation-based interventions.

**(c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`**  
- **exact**: A substantive subset directly reports on group earnings, welfare, or efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation). These are highly relevant.
- **close**: Others use close proxies (e.g., group income, rent dissipation, net profit, or success rates in threshold games)—not always mapped precisely to linear PGG 'efficiency' but conceptually aligned.
- **adjacent/weak**: Many studies focus on cooperation rates or norm compliance without explicit efficiency outcomes and must be used with caution for predictive purposes.
- **none**: Some papers are entirely about behavioral or psychological outcomes with no direct connection to pays.

**Summary**: The literature directly addresses all three dimensions with high reliability for PGGs with and without punishment and efficiency as the outcome, but the evidence becomes sparser and more interpretative as design moves away from standard PGGs, punishment takes less familiar forms, or the efficiency outcome is not reported or is only implied.

# 3) Outcomes Measured in the Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (`efficiency_or_payoff`)**:
- Most high-relevance studies (empirical and theory) provide direct measures of **group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare**—these directly inform the prediction target.
- Some studies instead report **group earnings as a share of the social optimum, surplus, or the frequency of optimal collective outcomes** (especially in threshold games or surplus allocation games).
- Close proxies are sometimes reported (e.g., average group profit, avoidance of rent dissipation, net income after costs).

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
- **Contribution or cooperation rate**: Many report average or time-series contribution as the primary or only outcome, without calculating associated efficiency or welfare. (Not equivalent but often positively correlated.)
- **Punishment frequency/intensity**: Some focus on the use/distribution of punishment, not its effect on group earnings.
- **Norm compliance, solidarity, exclusion, or reputation**: Others examine behavioral, psychological, or social dynamics that affect cooperation, but do not map directly to efficiency.
- **Attitudinal or neural outcomes**: A minority of adjacent studies report on beliefs, emotions, or neural correlates; these are not informative for the main prediction task.

**Key distinction**: Direct efficiency or group payoff data are essential for calibrating predictions. Where only behavioral outcomes are available, caution must be used—especially when punishment increases cooperation but may still **reduce net efficiency** due to its costliness.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Central empirical/theoretical synthesis:**
- **Enabling punishment _often_ increases efficiency, but effects are highly contingent on design dimensions and context**.
  - _Standard linear PGG, peer punishment_: Repeated finding that punishment raises contributions and usually increases efficiency _when control (no-punishment) efficiency is low_ (e.g., Harrell, 2019; Gürdal et al., 2021; Lim & Zhang, 2020; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Ramalingam et al., 2019).
  - _But_: If the control game already has moderate/high efficiency or if punishment costs are high relative to impact, **punishment's added cost can offset or even overtake the gains from increased contributions** (see Glöckner et al., 2018; Kanitsar, 2021; Robbett, 2019; Tanimoto, 2018).

- **Institutional structure matters:**  
  - **Centralized punishment (by an authority/leader)** tends to be more efficient than peer punishment, as it minimizes redundant punishment and counter-punishment (Harrell, 2019).
  - **Peer punishment** is vulnerable to anti-social punishment, coordination failures, and higher cumulative punishment costs, which can undercut efficiency gains.
  - **Monitoring/information structure**: Cheap, accurate monitoring enables high efficiency with punishment; costly or limited information dampens the positive effect (Nicklisch et al., 2021).

- **Cost-effectiveness is key:**  
  - Efficiency gains from punishment are highest when the **cost-to-effectiveness (punishment tech) ratio is favorable**—many theory papers formalize the thresholds for this (Tanimoto, 2018; Gao et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2019).
  - If punishment is costless or near-costless, efficiency rises substantially (Kanitsar, 2021). If costly but not impactful, efficiency can decline or fail to improve.

- **Framing and contextual moderators:**  
  - The effect of punishment on efficiency _depends on framing_—in Take-frame social dilemmas (where non-contribution is active), punishment improves efficiency more than in Give-frame (Ramalingam et al., 2019).
  - Societal and cultural variation: The magnitude of efficiency improvement due to punishment varies by population mix of 'strong reciprocators' versus 'norm-keepers' (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
  - Initial endowment inequality may moderate effect: In some CPR-like games, punishment is more efficient in heterogeneous groups where it helps coordinate on a focal norm (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).

- **Information visibility:**  
  - Feedback about contributions and punishment affects deterrence. High-precision transparency can backfire and actually _reduce_ cooperation or efficiency under certain conditions (Engel, 2019; Berger & De Silva, 2021).
  - Anonymous versus identified punishment has variable effects—sometimes anonymity reduces counter-punishment but also lowers norm enforcement.

- **Punishment and rewards:**  
  - Enabling reward in addition to punishment sometimes increases cooperation and efficiency, but the effect size is often smaller than for punishment alone unless combined (Hou et al., 2019).

- **Other design features:**  
  - **Player count (group size)**: Larger groups sometimes see _smaller per-person efficiency gains_ from peer punishment unless centralized punishment or reputation is possible (Lim & Zhang, 2020; theory: Shimura & Nakamaru, 2018).
  - **Number of rounds**: The effect of punishment on efficiency is more pronounced over multiple rounds where learning and deterrence compound.
  - **Communication/chat**: Not systematically varied in most core studies; when present, can substitute for or reinforce incentive mechanisms.

- **Negative/ambiguous effects:**  
  - Some settings report _no improvement or even reductions in efficiency_ upon enabling punishment, mostly due to the high cost of punishment, misapplication, or anti-social punishment (Heine & Strobel, 2020; Robbett, 2019; Safarzynska, 2020).
  - The design and implementation of punishment—timing, certainty, and punishment targets—are essential moderators.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct Implications for Prediction:**  
1. **Baseline control efficiency is a strong predictor**: If efficiency is already high without punishment, little room for gain and cost of punishment may dominate; if baseline efficiency is low, punishment is more likely to increase efficiency.
2. **Game design variables most crucial for prediction**:
   - `player_count` (group size): Smaller groups may realize higher efficiency gains.
   - `mpcr`: Higher MPCRs make cooperation more attractive and may reduce need for punishment; lower MPCRs require more sanction to enforce cooperation.
   - `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`: Lower cost and higher effectiveness yield larger efficiency improvements.
   - `punishment_tech` (structure: peer vs. centralized): Centralized punishment is generally more efficient.
   - `punishment_exists`: Switch from 0 (disabled) to 1 (enabled) creates a structural 'jump' in efficiency, moderated by other design features.
   - `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Information/feedback structure may alter how punishment is used and perceived, thus affecting efficiency.

3. **Conditional predictions**:
   - When **peer punishment is enabled** (as per the prediction task), _expect a non-negative effect on efficiency on average_, but magnitude depends on punishment cost, targeting, monitoring, and punishment institution.
   - Be cautious in predicting positive jumps if:
     - Punishment is costly/ineffective (low cost-to-impact ratio),
     - Coordination on punishment targets is challenging,
     - Anti-social punishment is prevalent,
     - Information about others’ contributions or punishers is limited or noisy.
   - Where evidence is only from contribution rates, not payoff, note potential for only modest or zero efficiency gain due to punishment costs.

**Variables with strong, direct, and consistent evidence**: `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `punishment_exists`, at times `show_other_summaries`, and `show_punishment_id` (as information moderators).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly and consistently informed dimensions**:
- `player_count` (group size): Varied extensively; central to nearly all studies.
- `num_rounds`: Repeated (usually 10–30 rounds) PGGs are the norm; some variation explored.
- `mpcr`: Marginal per-capita return; nearly always specified and manipulated.
- `punishment_cost`: Manipulated in both empirical and theoretical work; strong moderator of efficiency outcomes.
- `punishment_tech`: Peer versus centralized punishment, punishment effectiveness; directly manipulated in several key studies (Harrell, 2019; Lim & Zhang, 2020).
- `all_or_nothing`: Often specified but less directly manipulated.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Frequently reported; studies sometimes manipulate information about others' contributions and/or punishment allocations (Nicklisch et al., 2021; Engel, 2019).

**Indirect or contextually discussed dimensions**:
- `chat`: Sometimes present or absent, but rarely the focus of direct manipulation.
- `default_contrib`: Occasionally referenced as framing ('give' vs 'take'; Ramalingam et al., 2019).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Addressed insofar as studies compare punishment to reward or use mixed incentive regimes.
- `show_n_rounds`: Whether players are told how many rounds remain, sometimes specified, not often experimentally manipulated.

**Effectively missing or minimally addressed in direct prediction**:
- `default_contrib` (contribution framing): Scant direct evidence for interaction with punishment effect.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Sparse empirical evidence for net efficiency effects in reward-only treatments.
- Rarely do studies precisely manipulate all 14 prediction dimensions in factorial designs; thus, multidimensional interactions (e.g., chat × punishment × information visibility) are only partially addressed.

# 7) Important Limitations

**1. Incomplete design coverage**:  
No single paper or set experiments all 14 design dimensions; almost all evidence uses typical laboratory parameter values (e.g., 3–6 players, 10–30 rounds, standard mpcrs, and peer punishment with linear costs).

**2. Outcome reporting gaps**:  
- Many studies report on **contributions or cooperation rates exclusively**, not on group efficiency or total payoff. Where non-payoff outcomes are used, **translation into efficiency is not always straightforward** (as higher cooperation does not guarantee higher net returns if punishment is costly).
- Some reported efficiency gains are based on simulation/theory rather than observed behavior.

**3. Ambiguity in mechanism and heterogeneity**:  
- There is **disagreement and ambiguity** in findings for certain contexts: punishment sometimes fails to increase, or even reduces, efficiency (Robbett, 2019; Glöckner et al., 2018; Heine & Strobel, 2020).
- The magnitude (and even direction) of effects can be **highly sensitive to punishment cost, effectiveness, anti-social punishment, information structure, and population composition**.
- Cultural/contextual moderators (e.g., reciprocal norms, trust, group heterogeneity) are not always measured or controlled.

**4. Generalizability and external validity**:  
- Most evidence is from standard lab experiments with undergraduate subjects; **generalization to field or high-stakes environments is limited**.
- Peer punishment institutions are less widespread and less effective outside the laboratory or in large populations.

**5. Inference from adjacent designs**:  
- Adjacent games (CPRs, trust games, contest games, coordination problems) only approximate PGGs; prediction based on these must be made cautiously.
- Some key findings on negative effects (crowding out, failed targeting, inefficiency in punishment) arise in adjacent or contextually different environments.

**6. Missing or poorly specified interactions**:  
- Multidimensional moderating effects (e.g., group size × information × punishment structure) are only partially explored; extrapolation is often necessary but less reliable.
- **Information about how multiple dimensions interact** (e.g., the impact of chat in peer-punishment games) is sparse and not available for all combinations.

**Conclusion**:  
While the literature gives a strong and nuanced empirical and theoretical basis for predicting the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in most lab-standard PGG designs—conditional on control efficiency and a handful of core design variables—caution is warranted for less-studied dimensions, settings with complex institutions, or environments far from the standard repeated linear PGG. When only behavioral outcomes are reported, inferences about efficiency must be explicitly qualified.
