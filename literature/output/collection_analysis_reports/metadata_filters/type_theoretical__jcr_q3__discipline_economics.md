# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists entirely of **theoretical literature**: all 47 papers are theory works, with none reporting new empirical or experimental results, though several are explicitly calibrated to, or discuss, relevant laboratory settings (e.g., Dutta et al., 2021). The papers are conceptually and formally rich, spanning a range of models: canonical public goods games (PGGs), repeated and dynamic games, variants with networked or endogenous structure, games with voluntary participation, and games with both punishment and reward mechanisms. This literature base is **broad in theoretical scope**—offering diverse mechanisms and parameter explorations—but **narrow in direct empirical evidence for quantitative prediction**.

While many papers aim to explain, generalize, or reconcile experimental findings, **almost all findings and guidance are theoretical in nature**. Several papers (e.g., Dutta et al., 2021; Kranz, 2010; Hwang, 2017) are tightly calibrated to specific public goods game designs and provide directly relevant models for predicting efficiency outcomes. Others explore adjacent environments, focusing on mechanism design, dynamic learning, reputation, community enforcement, or endogenous partner choice. There are also papers primarily covering reward or network exclusion (rather than punishment), and some focused on behavioral or motivational mechanisms without direct efficiency implications.

**Empirical findings** appear only as illustrative references or calibration targets for the theory; no paper reports new experimental efficiency data.

**Summary:**  
- No new experimental/empirical data; entirely theoretical and modeling evidence.
- Direct applicability for treatment efficiency prediction is strongest for a core set of PGG-focused, punishment-enabled theoretical models; other papers provide adjacent or indirect evidence.
- Theoretical coverage is broad, covering many relevant dimensions and mechanisms.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) Public Goods Game or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`):**
- **Exact relevance:** Multiple papers model classic or canonical public goods games (Dutta et al., 2021; Hwang, 2017; Kranz, 2010; Darcet & Sornette, 2008; Carpenter et al., 2004; Wang et al., 2023; Barrett, 2020; Greiff, 2013; others specify standard linear PGG features).
- **Close relevance:** Several focus on environments structurally close to PGGs (joint effort, voluntary participation, team trust, or donor-recipient games with similar cooperation dilemmas).
- **Adjacent/Weak/None:** Some papers center on adjacent repeated dilemma games (Prisoner’s Dilemma, trust games, coordination games). A minority provide only contextual or methodological reference.

**b) Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`):**
- **Exact relevance:** Many papers’s central topic is (peer or institutional) punishment in a PGG or close variant.
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some focus on reward/exclusion or link punishment to reputation, partner choice, or endogenous contracting.
- **Weak/None:** Papers focused only on voluntary contributions or reward mechanisms provide weak relevance for punishment effects.

**c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`):**
- **Exact/Close:** A substantial subset model or explicitly report group efficiency, welfare, surplus, or total payoff (Dutta et al., 2021; Kranz, 2010; Dughera, 2022; Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023; Chassang & Zehnder, 2016; Buchholz et al., 2014; others).
- **Adjacent:** Several papers derive results about cooperation rates or equilibrium selection that imply, but do not directly report, efficiency outcomes; some analyze intermediate behavioral indicators (contribution rate, norm compliance) with only adjacent payoff discussion.
- **Weak/None:** A handful do not address efficiency or payoff outcomes, focusing instead on psychological, motivational, or procedural dynamics.

**Conclusion:**  
- The theoretical literature provides **strong `exact` and `close` relevance** for all three target dimensions in a substantial subset of papers, especially those calibrated to or abstracting canonical PGGs with peer punishment.
- The majority of papers are `exact` or `close` in pgg and punishment, and at least `close` in efficiency or group payoff, with the remainder being more conceptual or adjacent.
- **However, the literature is systematically theoretical, so empirical relevance for prediction may be more limited than the labeling suggests.**

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**
- *Efficiency (group payoff as a proportion of full cooperation):* A primary and explicit focus in Dutta et al. (2021), Hwang (2017), Kranz (2010), Dughera (2022), Sugaya & Wolitzky (2023), Buchholz et al. (2014), Chassang & Zehnder (2016), Mihm & Toth (2020), others.
- *Group welfare, surplus, total coins/payoff generated:* Frequently derived or upper/lower bounds provided.
- *Quantitative and comparative statics:* A number of models provide formulas or simulation results linking design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost) to efficiency outcomes.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- *Contribution/cooperation rates:* Used as primary measures in some models (e.g., Carpenter et al., 2004; Darcet & Sornette, 2008; López-Pérez, 2008), often with the expectation that higher contribution rates typically—but not invariably—imply higher efficiency.
- *Punishment/reward rates, frequency of sanctioning, norm compliance, evolution of prosocial types:* Analyzed in several models.
- *Dynamics/equilibrium selection:* Many works focus on the existence, stability, or speed of convergence to high-cooperation equilibria rather than quantifying resultant group payoff.

**Empirical versus theoretical status:**  
- All efficiency and behavioral outcomes discussed are theoretically modeled or simulated, not empirically observed in new experiments.
- Some models are closely calibrated against known lab results, but most use parameterized theory or agent-based simulations.

**Summary:**  
- Payoff-based outcomes are prominent and, in a subset, primary. Many models connect design dimensions to predicted efficiency.
- Nonetheless, much of the surrounding literature focuses on behavioral measures, psychological rationales, or the adaptive stability of cooperation, often only implying (not directly measuring) efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Enabling punishment in canonical PGGs with peer punishment** almost universally increases group efficiency, often substantially, compared to no-punishment baselines (Dutta et al., 2021; Kranz, 2010; Hwang, 2017; Buchholz et al., 2014).
    - The magnitude depends on punishment cost, effectiveness, and the composition of the group (fraction of compliers or norm-internalizers).
    - The effect is robust across a range of standard parameters—most direct evidence is for 4-player, 10-round, continuous PGGs with fixed MPCRs.

- **Punishment effectiveness is strongly moderated by:**
    - **Player count (group size):** Unlike standard free-rider intuitions, theory shows that with sufficiently coordinated punishment, efficiency may actually increase with group size (Hwang, 2017; Buchholz et al., 2014), provided the punishment mechanism scales (nonrival enforcement).
    - **Punishment cost and technology:** Lower punishment costs and higher punishment effectiveness (damage per unit) yield larger efficiency gains (Kranz, 2010; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015); if costs are high or punishment is ineffective, efficiency gains may not materialize.
    - **Monitoring/information/observability:** Rich information (e.g., ability to see others' contributions or punishment behavior) is crucial for punishment to be effectively targeted and thus generate efficiency (Mihm & Toth, 2020; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007; Lippert & Spagnolo, 2011).
    - **Ability to coordinate/target punishment:** Efficiency is maximized with targeted and coordinated punishment; random or contagious strategies are less robust/impose costs even on cooperators (Lippert & Spagnolo, 2011; Jindani, 2020).
    - **Nature and structure of the repeated game:** High continuation probability (patience), repeated interaction, and the possibility of community enforcement or reputation greatly enhance the effect of punishment on efficiency (Stahl, 2011; Takahashi, 2010; Deb & González-Díaz, 2019).

- **Design interactions and edge cases:**
    - If participation is compulsory and a significant share of players are non-compliers, punitive mechanisms may not increase efficiency (De Silva et al., 2010).
    - Highly costly punishment can reduce efficiency despite increasing cooperation (Yu et al., 2016).
    - In networked or endogenous partner games, selective exclusion or endogenous threat of punishment can substitute for explicit costly punishment (Greiff, 2013; Rob & Yang, 2010).
    - Cultural or social disagreement, or high propensity for counter-punishment, can undermine the efficiency benefits of punishment (Barrett, 2020).

- **Non-payoff behavioral measures** (e.g., norm compliance, cooperation rates) typically—but not always—track efficiency, but can diverge under certain evolutionary, motivational, or network conditions.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct guidance:** Where the game structure matches canonical PGGs with standard peer punishment (e.g., Fehr & Gächter–type settings), enabling punishment should be predicted to increase efficiency over the control, often substantially. Theoretically, this effect is robust across plausible variations in most core design features (player count, num_rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) **provided coordination, observability, and compliance are sufficient** (Dutta et al., 2021; Kranz, 2010; Hwang, 2017).

- **Parameter-based moderation:** Prediction of treatment efficiency should explicitly account for:
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** Lower cost and higher effectiveness predict larger gains.
    - **Player count:** Efficiency gains can increase with group size if coordination is feasible/nonrival, but may diminish if enforcement does not scale.
    - **Information/monitoring design:** Strong observability and feedback are necessary for punishment to be effective; insufficient information will attenuate the effect.
    - **Baseline (control) efficiency:** Efficiency gains are larger when control efficiency is low; if baseline cooperation (without punishment) is already high, marginal efficiency gains may be small or negative due to the direct cost of punishment.
    - **Reward mechanisms:** If rewards or motivational leadership are available, predicted efficiency may be even higher than with punishment alone or the combination may optimize efficiency (Dughera, 2022; Greiff, 2013).

- **Boundary conditions:**  
    - In environments with high punishment cost, poor observability, or cultural/moral disagreement, the positive effect on efficiency may be reduced or lost; in extreme cases, punishment may reduce long-term welfare (Povey, 2014).
    - In some evolutionary, repeated, or population-heterogeneous settings, the effect of punishment on efficiency can be non-monotonic or even negative in the long run.

- **Indirect/adjacent evidence:** In repeated games with endogenous relationship formation, reputation, or network exclusion replacing explicit punishment, efficiency can also be high under certain conditions, but mapping these models to explicit punishment interventions may be challenging.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed design dimensions:**
- **player_count:** Multiple models examine group size effects and quantify scaling of efficiency with coordination capacity and enforcement technology (Dutta et al., 2021; Hwang, 2017; Buchholz et al., 2014).
- **num_rounds:** The literature frequently models repeated settings and considers effects of game length or patience.
- **all_or_nothing:** Many theoretical frameworks contrast binary vs. continuous decision environments.
- **mpcr:** A core parameter—explicit comparative statics and efficiency formulas are supplied (Kranz, 2010; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015).
- **punishment_cost:** Key parameter in virtually all punishment models; lower cost predicts higher efficiency effect.
- **punishment_tech (punishment effectiveness, targeting, possible forms):** Models distinguish technologies (peer, coordinated, institutional, tax-based).
- **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech:** Addressed in several models, often as a direct comparison with punishment (Dughera, 2022; Greiff, 2013).

**Indirectly informed or contextually discussed:**
- **chat:** Often discussed as relevant to coordination/communication but usually not parameterized for payoff outcomes.
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id:** Key in models exploring information structure, monitoring, and observability (Mihm & Toth, 2020; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007; Lippert & Spagnolo, 2011); direct mappings to these design features are present but not always quantitative.
- **default_contrib:** Rarely addressed directly; some mention framing effects but not as a primary theoretical parameter.

**Effectively missing:**
- **Several fine-grained interface/feedback dimensions** (e.g., default_contrib, UI features) are largely un-modeled in payoff terms.
- **Empirical calibration of new design features** is absent.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Absence of new empirical data:** All findings are theoretical or simulation-based; quantitative calibration to current or diverse lab environments is lacking. The direct empirical effect size of punishment on efficiency—especially out-of-sample or across exotic designs—remains uncertain.

- **Parameter dependence:** Model-based predictions are sensitive to key parameters (punishment cost, effectiveness, group heterogeneity, observability). Many papers stress that efficiency gains are **not unconditional**—results can reverse if key conditions fail.

- **Generalizability and design specificity:** Most robust findings pertain to canonical PGGs with standard punishment mechanisms, fixed group composition, and commonly-studied parameter values. **For novel designs, edge cases, or parameter settings outside those studied, prediction is speculative.**

- **Non-monotonicity and mechanisms:** Several papers emphasize that the effect of punishment on efficiency is non-monotonic or ambiguous under certain dynamic, group-selection, or evolutionary conditions. Some suggest high punishment may reduce intrinsic motivation in the long term.

- **Limited coverage of fine-grained design features:** The 14-dimension set is only partially mapped in the theory. Information structure, punishment/reward features, and observability are addressed, but interface and feedback design elements are under-modeled.

- **Exclusion of field settings and heterogeneity:** Findings are lab- and theory-oriented; institutional, cultural, or field generalizations are unsupported.

---

**Summary Statement:**  
The literature provides a *strong theoretical foundation* for predicting that enabling (peer or coordinated) punishment in standard PGGs will increase treatment efficiency, especially when punishment is inexpensive, effective, information is available, and baseline efficiency is low. Theories allow mapping several critical game dimensions to expected efficiency effects, but empirical calibration and direct effect sizes are limited or missing, and many design/interface dimensions remain under-explored. Ambiguity and sensitivity to game details persist, requiring caution in extrapolating predictions to novel or out-of-sample environments.
