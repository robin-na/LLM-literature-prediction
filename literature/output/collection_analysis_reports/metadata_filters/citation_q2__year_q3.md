# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is large (n=219) and unusually comprehensive, encompassing both empirical (lab, field, and observational) and theoretical/modeling papers. The majority of high-relevance papers are experimental studies using standard and variant public goods game (PGG) designs. There are also a substantial number of theory/modeling papers implementing or simulating institutional designs, evolutionary dynamics, and mechanism arguments. The paper set contains both narrow (parametrically close to standard PGG with punishment) and broad (CPRs, PDGs, extensions, and adjacent mechanisms) studies. There is relatively strong representation of studies reporting payoff-based efficiency outcomes, though many studies focus on behavioral outcomes instead (e.g., cooperation rates, punishment frequency).

# 2) Task Relevance

## a) `pgg_or_variant`
- Most directly relevant studies use exact PGG or very close variants (linear PGGs with manipulation of punishment, centralized/peer/third-party punishment, etc.).
- Several important studies use close CPR, trust, or donation game variants. While these differ in institutional detail, their findings are often transferable but should be applied with caution.

**Relevance label:** Many papers are `exact` or `close`, with a smaller proportion being `adjacent` or `weak`.

## b) `punishment_or_sanctions`
- A substantial number of studies use or specifically manipulate peer punishment, centralized punishment, or related sanctioning institutions (e.g., exclusion, fine, forfeiture).
- Papers span individual, democratic, dictator, endogenous (voted), and exogenous punishment mechanisms; many specify punishment cost and technology. Some compare punishment to reward or communication.

**Relevance label:** Majority of studies are `exact` (peer or central punishment), with some `close` (exclusion, fines in CPR), and a significant number of `adjacent` papers focusing on reward, feedback, or informal sanctioning.

## c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
- Roughly half of the high-relevance papers report or allow calculation of efficiency (normalized group payoff), group profit, surplus, welfare, or average earnings. Some provide only contribution, cooperation, or behavioral outcomes.
- Several key studies distinguish between changes in cooperation and actual efficiency improvements (i.e., cases where increased cooperation is offset by costly punishment).

**Relevance label:** For efficiency or related outcomes, many core studies are `exact` or `close`, but a considerable fraction are only `adjacent` (contribution rates), with some key findings explicitly noting divergence between cooperation rates and efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:** These include group efficiency (payoff/maximum), group earnings, welfare/surplus, normalized profit, and total coins generated. Many empirical studies report these as primary outcomes, especially in standard PGG experimental designs; theory papers often model group average payoff or analogous welfare measures.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:** These include contribution/cooperation rates, frequency and severity of punishment, norm compliance, conditional cooperation, and antisocial punishment rates. Many studies (especially those focusing on mechanism/behavior) report these as primary outcomes without direct mapping to efficiency.
- **Other outcomes:** Some studies focus on the distribution of payoffs (inequality effects), stability or sustainability of cooperation, or rates of escalation and antisocial behavior. A minority report only qualitative mechanisms or social/psychological variables.

**Importantly:** Several studies note that increased cooperation does not always map to increased efficiency when punishment costs are high or when punishment is misapplied (e.g., antisocial punishment, targeting errors).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesis across studies with direct efficiency or payoff outcomes shows the following:

## a) **Positive, Robust Effects Under Standard Designs**
- In canonical linear PGGs with peer or centralized punishment, enabling punishment typically increases efficiency compared to no-punishment baselines, as long as (i) punishment is not excessively costly or (ii) antisocial punishment is rare (e.g. [Gordon & Puurtinen 2021]; [Schlösser et al., 2018]; [Castillo et al., 2021]; [Dutta et al., 2021]).
- Efficiency gains are strongest where punishment is well-targeted, information about behavior is complete, and sanctioning is symmetric and not open to exploitation or immune individuals.

## b) **Moderators/Factors that Limit or Reverse Gains**
- **Costliness and Targeting of Punishment:** High punishment costs, presence of antisocial punishment, or frequent mis-targeted punishment (e.g., peer punishment with incomplete information) can offset or eliminate efficiency gains ([Burton-Chellew & Guérin 2021]; [De Geest & Kingsley 2019]; [De Geest & Stranlund 2019])
- **Group Composition and Social Context:** Heterogeneity in endowments, ethnicity, or social value orientation can reduce or reverse efficiency gains, especially if in-group favoritism or inability/unwillingness to punish occurs ([Mantilla et al., 2021]; [Vollan et al., 2019]; [Honjo & Kubo 2020])
- **Antisocial and Retaliatory Punishment:** High rates can make punishment 'destructive', reducing overall group payoff even if cooperation rises ([Vollan et al., 2019]; [Burton-Chellew & Guérin 2021])
- **Network and Institutional Structure:** The type of network (star, complete vs. line/circle), type of punishment regime (democratic, individual, dictator), and possibility for central or collective sanctioning all moderate the size and even the sign of efficiency effects ([Fatas et al., 2020]; [Ambrus & Greiner 2019]; [Shreedhar et al., 2020])
- **Information Feedback:** Efficiency gains require that norm-violators can be unambiguously identified and feedback is salient ([Waichman & Stenzel 2019]; [De Geest & Kingsley 2021]; [Kamei 2019])
- **Baseline Control Efficiency:** The treatment effect of punishment depends on starting control efficiency: when control groups are already highly efficient (e.g., due to high baseline cooperation or communication), adding punishment may reduce efficiency due to cost ([Bühren & Dannenberg 2021])
- **Potential for Misuse (Extortion/Corruption):** Punishment systems vulnerable to extortion or self-interested misuse can collapse efficiency ([Abbink et al., 2020]; [Barron & Guo 2021])

## c) **Special/Boundary Cases**
- When public goods game is inefficient (MPCR<1), enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and can even be welfare-reducing, unless complemented by reward ([Kamijo et al., 2020])
- Reward and communication often have larger or more sustainable positive effects on efficiency than punishment alone ([Ertör-Akyazi, 2019]; [Koch et al., 2021])

## d) **Mechanism (Theoretical) Arguments**
- Many theory papers converge that the efficiency benefit of punishment (or its dynamic emergence via institutional design) is contingent: depends on cost/benefit structure, possibility for social/collective enforcement, and system parameters (e.g., drift, mutation, rationality).
- Some models show threshold or non-monotonic effects: Mild, well-calibrated punishment increases efficiency; too strong or misapplied punishment can destroy it.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Rule:**  
- For most repeated, linear PGGs with standard parameters, enabling peer or centralized punishment increases average efficiency versus control without punishment, but the effect size and direction are strongly moderated by design features and group context.

**Guidance by Design Dimension:**
- **When information about contributions is complete, sanctioning is symmetric, punishment cost is not excessive, and control efficiency is low:** Expect a substantial positive effect on efficiency from enabling punishment ([Gordon & Puurtinen 2021]; [Dutta et al., 2021]; [Ambrus & Greiner 2019]; [Kamei 2019]).
- **When control efficiency is already high (from high baseline cooperation/communication):** Adding punishment may reduce efficiency due to superfluous cost ([Bühren & Dannenberg 2021]).
- **If antisocial punishment, targeted errors, or ethnic/ingroup favoritism is likely:** The efficiency gain is attenuated or may be negative ([Mantilla et al., 2021]; [Vollan et al., 2019]).
- **Incomplete information about endowments or actions:** Punishment may backfire by being misapplied, reducing efficiency ([De Geest & Kingsley 2019]).
- **Pool (centralized) punishment versus peer punishment:** Both can increase efficiency, but centralized mechanisms (with appropriate cost) are often more robust to sabotage and anti-social behaviors ([Castillo et al., 2021]; [Kol'veková et al., 2021]).
- **Group size, number of rounds, network structure:** Larger groups and certain network structures can make punishment less effective or costlier; efficiency gains from punishment can decline or reverse in such settings ([Fatas et al., 2020]; [Wang & Lv 2019]).
- **Punishment cost and magnitude:** High-impact, low-cost punishment yields larger efficiency gains; as the cost-of-punishing rises or punishment becomes weak, the efficiency gain can vanish or reverse ([Hintze et al., 2020]; [Podobnik et al., 2019]).

**Adjustment for Control Efficiency in Prediction:**  
- Use the measured efficiency in the control (no-punishment) condition as the baseline.
- The incremental impact of enabling punishment depends on (a) observed control efficiency (lower = larger potential gain), and (b) design/cultural moderators above.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- `player_count` (group size): Many studies manipulate or report systematic effects; large, medium, and small groups are studied.
- `num_rounds`: Almost universally specified and tested; longer repeated games allow for enforcement/punishment effects.
- `mpcr` (marginal per capita return): Explicitly manipulated in many studies, with findings that higher MPCR often reduces the *need* for punishment and alters its effectiveness.
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech` (fine): These two, and their ratio ("cost-effectiveness"), are directly manipulated in many studies and identified as key moderators of the efficiency effect.
- `punishment_exists` (presence/absence): Central to all high-relevance studies.

**Indirectly Informed/Contextual Dimensions:**  
- `chat`: Communication/communication protocol is a major moderator in some studies ([Koch et al., 2021]; [Ertör-Akyazi, 2019]), sometimes indirectly (if only communication is allowed).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Compared in some studies (esp. Kamijo et al., 2020; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019), but less often jointly manipulated with punishment (a gap).
- `all_or_nothing`, `default_contrib`: Some studies vary the contribution frame, but evidence about their direct impact on treatment efficiency or on the punishment effect is much sparser.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Feedback and information about peers/punishers are referenced as moderators, but only a subset of papers systematically manipulate these or map them to efficiency effects.
- `punishment_tech`: Sometimes refers to targeting or architecture, not just fine size; well reported in some, but ambiguous in others.

**Effectively Missing/Sparse:**  
- Few studies provide fine-grained quantification of the effect of `default_contrib` (contribution framing), all-or-nothing versus continuous, or systematically combine reward and punishment technologies.
- `show_n_rounds` (number of rounds shown to players) is referenced, but effects on efficiency are not robustly mapped.
- Some game design dimensions are frequently held constant or not reported, making cross-study extraction for these variables difficult.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Incomplete or Indirect Evidence for Some Dimensions:** Several prediction-relevant dimensions (reward-related, framing, detailed information feedback) are not consistently or systematically tested across studies.
- **Ambiguity from Non-Payoff Outcome Studies:** Many studies report only on cooperation/contribution and do not separate efficiency effects from behavioral changes, especially where punishment costs are high.
- **Design-Context Dependence/Boundary Conditions:** Some studies highlight circumstances where punishment reduces or has no effect on efficiency (e.g., incomplete information, high cost, antisocial or misapplied punishment, high baseline efficiency, institutional corruption/extortion).
- **Mechanism and Theory Studies Are Conditional:** Theoretical work often identifies necessary conditions or thresholds for punishment to improve efficiency, rather than universal effects.
- **External Validity and Realism:** Generalization beyond the (often artificial) laboratory settings may be limited—some mechanisms work differently in real-world, field, or larger-scale settings.
- **Heterogeneity in Measurement and Reporting:** Not all studies report efficiency as fraction of the fully cooperative outcome; mapping behavioral outcomes to efficiency sometimes requires inference.
- **Disagreement and Ambiguity in Evidence:** Some domains (heterogeneous groups, CRP vs. PGG, network structure) produce conflicting results on whether and when punishment increases efficiency.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature base is broad and rich for the prediction task, with a concentration of directly relevant empirical and theory studies mapping key PGG design dimensions (group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/impact, information feedback, institutional structure) to efficiency changes under punishment. The weight of evidence supports an expected efficiency gain from enabling punishment, but only when implementation, context, and baseline conditions align favorably (well-targeted, not overly costly, low antisocial punishment, complete information, moderate control efficiency). In many contexts, efficiency gains are limited, absent, or negative when these conditions are not met. The literature directly informs prediction for several design dimensions, but leaves some gaps for reward-related and feedback/visibility variables, and carries limitations on generalizing beyond lab or stylized experimental settings.
