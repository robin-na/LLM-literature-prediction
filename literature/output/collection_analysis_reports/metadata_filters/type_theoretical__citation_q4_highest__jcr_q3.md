# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is composed entirely of theoretical works (no new empirical or laboratory experimental results). It is broad in its conceptual coverage of mechanisms relevant to cooperation, punishment, sanctioning, and payoff determination in public-goods-game (PGG) and PGG-like settings. The evidence base offers high granularity with respect to theory, addressed through analytical models, simulations, and reviews of previous experimental findings. Direct empirical effect sizes and real-world laboratory data relevant for quantitative prediction are lacking; most results are of the form “enabling punishment/sanctioning can/is predicted to increase efficiency under specific model conditions.”

Within the theoretical literature, there is some diversity: many papers model canonical n-player PGGs with explicit peer or institutional punishment, while others analyze indirect reciprocity, reputation mechanisms, commitment, retaliation, partner choice, spatial structure, and population migration. A substantial subset of the papers treat payoff-based outcomes (group payoff, efficiency, welfare) as primary, but many others focus on cooperation rates or strategic composition—providing only indirect evidence for efficiency predictions. The evidence is generally strongest for canonical, stylized public goods games under Nash or evolutionary dynamics; less so for settings that introduce richer communication, chat, reward, and information structures.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- Many papers are of `exact` relevance, directly modeling canonical or close variants of the public goods game (e.g., Cressman et al., 2012; Gintis, 2000, 2003; Henrich & Boyd, 2001; Rand et al., 2010; Hauser et al., 2014).
- Others are `close`, using games that differ slightly (e.g., threshold PGGs, variants with loners, or structured populations).
- A sizeable fraction are only `adjacent`, focusing on related games like Prisoner’s Dilemma, indirect reciprocity, or multi-level norm evolution.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- Substantial coverage at the `exact` and `close` level (e.g., peer and institutional punishment, antisocial punishment, conditional punishment, punishment-based norm enforcement).
- Some papers discuss only punishment-like features (e.g., partner control, exit, reputation loss), which are `adjacent` rather than direct implementations of costly peer punishment.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- Several papers report efficiency or total payoff outcomes (`exact`), especially in the context of equilibrium or evolutionary stability (e.g., Gintis, 2003; Cressman et al., 2012; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).
- Others report group payoff/total earnings as secondary or imply them from increased cooperation rates (`close` or `adjacent`).
- Many papers report only on behavioral outcomes (cooperation rate, punishment frequency); these are only `adjacent` or `weak` for efficiency-based prediction.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (directly relevant):**  
  - Efficiency (defined as the realized group payoff divided by the maximum possible from full cooperation) is explicitly modeled and often derived in canonical PGG models.
  - Closely related variables include group total payoff, average earnings, “welfare,” or “surplus.”

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (indirect):**  
  - Most commonly: cooperation rate or contribution rate, frequency of punishment, stability or abundance of strategies (e.g., proportion of punishers, cooperators, defectors, loners), norm compliance, or reputation score.
  - These outcomes are often strongly (but not always monotonically) related to efficiency in models with linear payoff structures, but mapping is less clear in models with retaliation, antisocial punishment, network heterogeneity, or complex cost structures.

Notably, many theoretical papers infer implications for efficiency from increased cooperation rates or the stability of cooperative equilibria. However, several explicitly show that increased punishment or cooperation does not always translate to higher group payoff due to the costs of sanctioning and factors like anti-social punishment or retaliation.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **General result:** Most theoretical models with exact PGGs and peer/institutional punishment predict that enabling punishment increases efficiency, provided punishment is not excessively costly, is pro-social (not anti-social), and retaliation or other counterstrategies are limited (Cressman et al., 2012; Gintis, 2000, 2003; Henrich & Boyd, 2001).

- **Moderators that constrain or reverse the effect:**
  - **Antisocial punishment:** Models universally find that if anti-social punishment (punishing cooperators) is permitted, enabling punishment does not increase and can even decrease efficiency (Rand et al., 2010; Hauser et al., 2014; García & Traulsen, 2012).
  - **Retaliation:** Where retaliation against punishers is possible (especially if punishers are identifiable), the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is much reduced or eliminated, especially in larger groups (Janssen & Bushman, 2008; Giardini & Conte, 2012).
  - **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** The efficiency gain is highest when punishment cost to the punisher is low and the fine inflicted on defectors is high (Gintis, 2000; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
  - **Punishment implementation:** Conditional (proportional) punishment mechanisms are generally more efficient per unit cost than unconditional ones, especially when punishment is not cheap (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
  - **Information/visibility structure:** When identification of defectors is noisy or the identity of the punisher is revealed, efficiency gains are reduced; when information about punishment is public and accurate, the positive effect is stronger (Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).
  - **Group size:** The positive effect of punishment is more robust in smaller groups; as group size increases, sustaining efficient cooperation becomes harder unless supporting mechanisms (institutional, informational) are strong (Suzuki & Akiyama, 2007; Hilbe et al., 2015).
  - **Reputation and indirect reciprocity:** In repeated and reputation-based games, efficiency is maximized when punishment is justified (no loss of reputation for punishing defectors), and social norms are “serious” (Ohtsuki & Iwasa, 2004, 2006; Chalub et al., 2006).
  - **Game baseline characteristics:** If control efficiency is already high (e.g., via diminishing returns or strong direct reciprocity), the marginal benefit of adding punishment may be small (Foster, 2004; Hilbe et al., 2015).

- **Important boundary conditions:**
  - **Institutional vs. peer punishment:** Institutional punishment is generally more stable and leads to higher efficiency compared to decentralized peer punishment, especially when established at sufficient strength (Cressman et al., 2012; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).
  - **Reward mechanisms:** Reward on its own cannot sustain full efficiency (Sasaki & Unemi, 2011; Hauert, 2010), but can complement punishment if combined.

- **Potential for negative or null effect:**  
  - If punishment is costly and used inefficiently (e.g., punishing both defectors and cooperators, or when retaliation/anti-social punishment is common), group efficiency may decrease relative to the control (Rand et al., 2010; Hauser et al., 2014; Janssen & Bushman, 2008).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Quantitative predictions from this literature are model-dependent and generally take the form:**  
  - _If punishment is enabled and is sufficiently cheap and/or effective, and antisocial punishment or retaliation are excluded, group efficiency (compared to control efficiency without punishment) will increase, often approaching the full cooperation benchmark._

- **Design dimensions that should be weighted heavily based on these findings:**
  - **Punishment cost and technology (effectiveness):** Predict larger efficiency gains from punishment when the cost per fine to the punisher is low and the deduction from defectors is high.
  - **Presence of anti-social punishment or retaliation:** If the design allows retaliation or anti-social punishment, predict little or no effect—or even negative effect—of enabling punishment, regardless of other parameters.
  - **Player count (group size):** Increases in group size generally make it harder for punishment to sustain efficiency unless institutional mechanisms are strong.
  - **Information/visibility (punisher identification, outcome feedback):** When punishers are anonymous and outcomes are visible to all, expect larger positive effects.
  - **Baseline efficiency:** If control games are already highly efficient (due, for instance, to high MPCR, coordinated play, or repeated interaction with reputation/feedback), the incremental value added by punishment is likely small.

- **If only non-payoff behavioral outcomes are available:**  
  - Use findings about cooperation rates as proxies for potential efficiency, but apply caution: high cooperation rates do not always imply high efficiency if punishment costs are large or if excessive punishment is used.

- **Ambiguity and limits:**  
  - In cases where design dimensions fall into regimes with strong antisocial punishment or easy retaliation by punished individuals—and especially if these are not directly measured—predictions should assume no effect or negative effect of enabling punishment.
  - Theoretical models generally predict greater than zero efficiency gain under “standard” settings but stress context dependency.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`/`group size`: Modeled in most theoretical PGG papers; group size is a central parameter affecting cooperation/efficiency stability.
- `num_rounds`: Heavily considered in repeated game models; affects time horizon for cooperation and impact of punishment.
- `mpcr`: Explicit in nearly all exact PGG models—key for cost/benefit analysis.
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`: Central to almost all exact and close-relevance papers; cost-to-impact ratio is a dominant determinant of efficiency gain from punishment.
- `punishment_exists` (treatment presence): Modeled via inclusion or exclusion of punishment mechanism.
- `show_punishment_id` (punisher identification): Directly analyzed in context of retaliation/anti-social punishment and efficiency.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds` (information availability): Sometimes directly discussed (Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007).

**Indirectly or contextually informed:**
- `all_or_nothing`: Some works use threshold or binary decisions (Bach et al., 2006); many use continuous contributions.
- `chat`: Rarely directly modeled; some results on communication and information flow (Lippert & Spagnolo, 2011).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Modeled in a subset of papers (Sasaki & Unemi, 2011; Hauert, 2010; Cressman et al., 2012) but not as focus of punishment-enabled efficiency prediction.
- `default_contrib`: Occasional mention in framing; not a focus.

**Effectively missing or only contextually mentioned:**
- `show_n_rounds` (explicit in a few models but rarely central).
- `chat`, `default_contrib`: Modeled/mentioned only as contextual features or in adjacent game families.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of direct empirical evidence:**  
  - All findings are theoretical/model-based; effect sizes, practical implementation details, and empirical anomalies may not be captured.
  - Calibration to real experimental or field data is not possible with this set.

- **Behavioral vs. payoff outcomes:**  
  - Many papers measure non-payoff outcomes (contribution rates, strategy stability, etc.), inferring efficiency only indirectly.
  - Cost of punishment can cancel out or even overwhelm group benefits from increased cooperation if not tracked in payoff calculations.

- **Assumptions about implementation:**  
  - Models typically assume either peer or institutional punishment but may differ from experimental peer punishment implementations encountered in laboratory work.
  - Some mechanisms (e.g., perfect detection, no mistakes, homogeneous groups) may not match real-world or experimental details.

- **Context dependency:**  
  - Many model results are highly sensitive to design dimensions not always parameterized in practice (e.g., network structure, retaliation, information flow).
  - Presence and form of anti-social punishment, retaliation, or identification are critical but can be nuanced and difficult to classify in actual experiments.

- **Missing dimensions:**  
  - Reward-only and combined reward-punishment mechanisms are not the focus and should not be generalized from these results.
  - Chat, opt-in contribution frames, and other dynamic or social dimensions are generally absent.

- **Ambiguity in ambiguous cases:**  
  - When models differ in mechanism implementation, predictions may disagree (e.g., positive effect with pro-social only punishment vs. negative/none with anti-social punishment allowed).

# Summary

The theoretical literature provides strong, consistent model-based support for the prediction that enabling **effective, pro-social punishment** in public goods games **increases group efficiency** compared to control games without punishment, with the effect moderated by punishment cost/effectiveness, group size, information structure, and the presence/absence of anti-social punishment or retaliation. However, if punishment is expensive, retaliatory, or anti-social, or if baseline efficiency is already high, these effects may be reduced or reversed. Many of the 14 game design dimensions critical for prediction are addressed in detail (especially punishment cost, group size, rounds, and punishment technology), but chat, default contribution framing, and some information features are largely missing from the models. The lack of direct empirical findings and the focus on behavioral proxies in many cases limit the granularity and precision of quantitative predictions for actual experimental or applied settings.
