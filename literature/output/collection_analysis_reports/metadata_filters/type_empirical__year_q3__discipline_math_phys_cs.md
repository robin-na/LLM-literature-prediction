# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is primarily composed of **experimental lab studies** (and a few field experiments) examining cooperation, punishment, and efficiency in public goods games (PGGs) or closely related social dilemmas. The empirical focus is pronounced, with nearly all papers reporting results from controlled experiments involving variation in game design elements. A **minority of papers are strictly theoretical or simulation-based**, though many studies incorporate theoretical discussion or modeling alongside their main empirical results.

The **breadth** of the evidence is notable for covering a variety of:
- Game structures (linear PGGs, threshold PGGs, generalized exchange, PD, trust games, surplus allocation, CPR games, networked games)
- Punishment regimes (peer, centralized, automatic, exclusion, social feedback, reward systems)
- Game parameters and design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost, communication, information sharing, endogenous/exogenous group formation)

For the downstream prediction of **punishment's effect on efficiency in PGG-like environments**, the evidence is **broad for lab PGGs with canonical punishment mechanisms** (especially peer and centralized punishment). However, **direct, systematic evidence mapping precise design dimensions to efficiency outcomes is limited**, and some studies are only indirectly informative or cover adjacent designs.

---

# 2) Task Relevance

The literature is assessed on three key relevance axes:

**a) PGG or Variant**
- `exact` relevance: Many papers (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, Castillo et al., Yang et al., Grund et al., Nax et al., Liao et al.) use canonical linear PGGs, threshold PGGs, or closely-matched paradigms.
- `close` or `adjacent`: Several use generalized exchange, PDs, CPR, or surplus allocation games with similar strategic structure, but not all game features align directly with standard PGGs (e.g., Kanitsar, Di Guida et al., Bolton et al., Fabbri et al.).

**b) Punishment or Sanctions**
- `exact` relevance: Several PGG experiments directly manipulate the presence or absence of peer or centralized (third-party) punishment (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, Castillo et al., Kanitsar).
- `close`/`adjacent`: Some focus on exclusion, feedback, refusal to cooperate (social or indirect punishment), or reward as alternatives or complements to punishment (e.g., Grund et al., Przepiorka & Diekmann, Becchetti et al.).
- `none`: A subset feature no punishment/sanction mechanism (e.g., Ren & Zheng, Weng et al., Xu et al.).

**c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**
- `exact`: A subset of papers report direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, or welfare (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, Castillo et al., Kanitsar, Di Guida et al., Przepiorka & Diekmann, Bolton et al.).
- `close`/`adjacent`: Others use behavioral proxies (contribution rates, success rates, compliance), which are positively correlated with efficiency but are not direct efficiency measures (e.g., Yang et al., Liao et al., Becchetti et al., Fabbri et al.).
- `none`: Some report only behavioral, attitudinal, or structural outcomes, not payoff-based measures.

**Overall**, the set is **most relevant for PGGs with punishment and group efficiency as outcome**, but includes considerable adjacent-material that needs careful filtering regarding its direct applicability to punishment-enabled efficiency prediction.

---

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**
- **Efficiency (group earnings/payoff relative to full cooperation):** Frequently measured in canonical PGG and directly reported in several high-relevance papers (Suleiman & Samid, Castillo et al., Kanitsar, Di Guida et al., Przepiorka & Diekmann, Bolton et al.).
- **Total group payoff/welfare/surplus:** Closely related measures used interchangeably with efficiency.
- **Revenue/compliance:** Used in field experiments (Fabbri et al.) as a proxy for public good provision and efficiency.
- **Final wealth:** Used in PD/PGG hybrids or games with wealth visibility (Zhang et al., 2020).

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates,** public good provision, and investment (Yang et al., Grund et al., Sun et al., Sun & Luo, Pan et al.).
- **Punishment/reward frequency and intensity.**
- **Strategy updating, switching, or norm compliance (Xu et al.).**
- **Social feedback/public ratings (Przepiorka & Diekmann).**

**Distinctions:**
- **Direct efficiency evidence** is present in some but not all papers; where absent, increases in contributions/cooperation are often interpreted as likely to raise efficiency, but this is an indirect inference and exceptions exist (e.g., efficiency can fall if punishment is too costly).
- **Some studies report only on cooperation rates, not efficiency,** so their results must be interpreted cautiously for prediction about group payoffs.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### General Effects of Punishment on Efficiency

- **Enabling punishment (especially peer or centralized) usually increases average efficiency (group payoff),** relative to a no-punishment PGG baseline, but gains are:
  - **Moderate and variable:** The increase is not always large, and depends on group composition and institutional design (Suleiman & Samid, Castillo et al., Kanitsar).
  - **Context-dependent:** Stronger in societies with more strong reciprocators and weaker or absent in groups with 'norm-keepers' or where antisocial punishment is prevalent (Suleiman & Samid).
  - **Robust in centralized punishment:** Centralized institutions (manager-enforced) consistently raise efficiency across tested punishment cost regimes; the selection method for the manager (democratic or random) is less important (Castillo et al.).
  - **Contingent on punishment cost:** Peer punishment increases efficiency only if punishment is costless or sufficiently cheap. Costly punishment may not improve, and can even reduce, efficiency (Kanitsar).

- **Special cases:**
  - **Automatic, weak, or misaligned punishment can reduce or fail to increase efficiency** (Yang et al.); **punishment that is non-strategic or strictly efficiency-destroying fails to deter and only adds cost.**
  - **Exclusion or refusal-to-cooperate mechanisms (social punishment) can increase efficiency** when enabling voluntary cooperation (Di Guida et al.), but additional features (e.g., repeated interaction, endogenous grouping) may only increase variance, not average efficiency.
  - **Mutual punishment/retaliation systems (e.g., feedback withdrawal in two-sided market games) can reduce efficiency** due to escalation and breakdown of incentive structures (Bolton et al.).

- **Information conditions and group structure moderate punishment's effect:**
  - **Network structure:** Peer punishment is effective in dense (public good) but not sparse (generalized exchange) networks (Kanitsar).
  - **Wealth visibility and inequality:** Wealth information can interact with punishment to reduce efficiency in unequal groups, even if punishment is rarely used (Zhang et al., 2020).
  - **Role of communication and information feedback:** Communication and public information about contributions/feedback can raise efficiency, sometimes substituting for or amplifying the effect of punishment (Przepiorka & Diekmann, Weng et al.).

### Mechanisms

- **Anticipation of punishment (not its actual application) is a major driver of increased cooperation** and efficiency (Suleiman & Samid, Zhao et al.).
- The **effectiveness of punishment depends on its strategic value** (i.e., it must deter free-riding where it matters for payoffs; Zhao et al.).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

### Pattern for Prediction:

- **If a baseline (punishment-disabled) game is inefficient but conforms to standard PGG structure, enabling punishment usually increases efficiency (payoff),** but:
  - The **magnitude of the increase is moderated by punishment cost, network structure, society/composition of players (strong reciprocators vs. norm-keepers), and whether punishment is peer or centralized**.
  - **Centralized punishment** is robustly effective in improving efficiency relative to control.
  - **Peer punishment effectiveness is mostly positive when punishment is low-cost; can be null or negative if punishment is costly or network is sparse**.

- **Automatic, weak, or inefficient punishment** (especially fixed and unresponsive sanctions, or those "punishing the lowest" irrespective of defect status) may at best have no effect or at worst reduce efficiency due to added costs (Yang et al.).

- **Punishment via refusal to cooperate/exclusion** increases efficiency when baseline cooperation is low, but increasing the opportunity for repeated interaction/matching elevates variance (heterogeneity) rather than average efficiency (Di Guida et al.).

- **When reward mechanisms or social feedback/public ratings are present (possibly with punishment),** expect further efficiency gains compared to punishment alone; the effect size depends on the cost-effectiveness and salience of reward (Fabbri et al., Becchetti et al., Przepiorka & Diekmann).

- **Information conditions (e.g., visibility of contributions, wealth, or identities) significantly moderate efficiency outcomes,** particularly in the presence of inequality.

### **Operational Prediction Logic:**
- Ceteris paribus, **treatment efficiency = control efficiency + punishment effect**, where:
    - *Punishment effect* is **positive** if:
      - Punishment is peer or centralized and costless/low-cost,
      - Network is dense or complete,
      - Group/society composition favors strong reciprocators over norm-keepers,
      - No substantial antisocial punishment or excessive retaliation.
    - *Punishment effect* is **null or negative** if:
      - Punishment is costly/inefficient,
      - Enforced weakly/automatically or applied non-strategically,
      - Network structure is sparse,
      - Game allows mutual retaliation or contains two-sided moral hazard,
      - Wealth visibility in unequal environments,
      - Societal composition high in norm-keepers.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Many experiments use 3–10 players; group size effects are directly considered.
- `num_rounds`: Treatment/control comparisons often control for or report number of rounds.
- `mpcr`: Frequently specified and analyzed as a moderator (e.g., lower MPCR often increases baseline inefficiency, making punishment potentially more effective).
- `punishment_cost`: Directly manipulated in peer and centralized punishment studies (Suleiman & Samid, Kanitsar).
- `punishment_tech` (punishment effectiveness): Studied through cost–impact ratio and differences (Castillo et al., Suleiman & Samid).
- `all_or_nothing`: Studied directly in all-or-nothing PGGs (Becchetti et al.).
- `reward_exists` and `reward_cost`: Covered in studies with mixed punishment/reward (Becchetti et al., Fabbri et al.).
- `show_n_rounds`: Considered in design, specification; implicit in studies controlling round knowledge.

**Indirectly Informed/Contextual Dimensions:**
- `chat`: Present in some studies (not always analyzed).
- `show_other_summaries` and `show_punishment_id`: Implicitly discussed in studies examining effect of information feedback or public ratings (Przepiorka & Diekmann).
- `default_contrib`: Framing sometimes manipulated but not systematically studied.
- `punishment_exists`: Central to main comparison.
- `reward_tech`/`reward_magnitude`: Not present in all studies, but covered where relevant.

**Sparse or Missing Dimensions:**
- **Rarely addressed directly:** Details of contribution framing (`default_contrib`), `reward_tech`, specific interface or real-time technical implementation details.
- **Limited attention:** The effect of identity revelation (`show_punishment_id`) is only considered where feedback/rating/publicity are mechanisms; not systematically studied.
- **Group composition and societal context:** Identified as key moderators (Suleiman & Samid), but rarely operationalized for prediction.

---

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency vs. Behavioral Outcomes:** Not all studies report efficiency/payoff; reliance on contribution rates or compliance as proxies can be misleading if punishment is costly or misaligned.
- **Generalizability:** Findings from specific lab-game structures (e.g., symmetric, small groups, linear PGGs) may not generalize to large, bounded, or more complex real-world environments. Many adjacent studies use variants that alter strategic incentives.
- **Punishment Heterogeneity:** The **type, cost, and targeting accuracy of punishment** critically affect effects on efficiency—many studies do not systematically vary all these features, potentially limiting transferability.
- **Participant Composition/Context:** **Cultural, societal, and individual difference effects are highlighted as strong moderators** (Suleiman & Samid), but are not controlled or parameterized for prediction in most lab studies.
- **Dimension Coverage:** Not all 14 prediction dimensions are evenly addressed or systematically manipulated in experimental designs; some (e.g., default contribution frame, identity visibility) are missing or only contextually discussed.
- **Ambiguity and Mixed Effects:** Some evidence contradicts the typical pattern (e.g., mutual feedback withdrawal can decrease efficiency; strong punishment can backfire). There is ambiguity and need for caution when generalizing.
- **Adjacent Designs:** Results from PDs, CPRs, trust games, and field compliance studies are informative but not always directly portable to PGG efficiency prediction.
- **Reward Mechanisms and Combined Institutional Effects:** Reward effects are only examined in a small number of studies and effects often depend on both cost structure and interaction with punishment.

---

**In conclusion:**  
The literature offers **solid empirical guidance for predicting the effects of enabling punishment on efficiency in canonical PGGs**, especially in standard, dense, low-cost punishment settings. However, **for precision prediction across a full set of design dimensions and all PGG-like environments, evidence is incomplete, and key moderators (such as punishment cost, group/network structure, information conditions, and group composition) must be explicitly considered for accurate translation to treatment efficiency prediction**.
