# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises a substantial, moderately diverse mix of **empirical laboratory experiments** and **theory/modeling papers**, with some contextual observational/field studies. The **core empirical base** is comprised of well-established, lab-based public goods game (PGG) experiments directly testing the effect of punishment on both contribution rates and group efficiency/payoff (e.g., Fehr et al., 2002; Reuben & Riedl, 2009; Decker et al., 2003). These are complemented by several theory/modeling papers that explore mechanisms and moderators of punishment in PGGs and closely related cooperative dilemmas (e.g., Baker & Rachlin, 2002; POLLOCK, 1988; Whitmeyer, 2004), as well as adjacent domains (ultimatum games, trust games, common-pool resource games).

The evidence base is **broad in behavioral mechanisms and theoretical scope**, but **narrower in directly comparable quantitative data** on efficiency across the full range of design dimensions; only a subset of studies report efficiency or closely related group payoff as the main outcome. Nonetheless, the literature robustly covers repeated, linear PGGs with varying group size, rounds, MPCR, and punishment cost/effectiveness, and includes some coverage of centralized versus decentralized punishment, heterogeneous groups, and variations such as step-level and random punishment.

# 2) Task Relevance

**(a) pgg_or_variant**  
- **Relevance:** Many studies are *exactly* PGGs or linear/step-level variants. Some adjacent evidence comes from prisoner's dilemmas, common-pool resource games, trust/ultimatum games, and observational field contexts.
  - **Exact:** Fehr et al. (2002), Decker et al. (2003), Reuben & Riedl (2009), Gürerk et al. (2009), Fatas et al. (2010)
  - **Close:** POLLOCK (1988), Noailly et al. (2009), Guillen et al. (2007), Webb & Foddy (2004)
  - **Adjacent/None:** Many studies in bargaining/trust or field settings—informative for underlying mechanisms, but less for quantitative efficiency outcomes.

**(b) punishment_or_sanctions**  
- **Relevance:** The majority of relevant studies *directly* implement punishment or sanction options, either peer-to-peer, centralized, or collective; some discuss only the threat or theoretical possibility, while others offer entirely contextual relevance.
  - **Exact/Close:** Fehr et al. (2002), Decker et al. (2003), Reuben & Riedl (2009), Fatas et al. (2010), Guillen et al. (2007)
  - **Adj/None:** Several only discuss theoretical mechanisms or measure related constructs (rejection, disapproval, message sending) without explicit cost/punishment mechanisms.

**(c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**  
- **Relevance:** There is a mix—some studies directly measure and report *efficiency or group payoff*; many others focus on behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment frequency) without calculating efficiency, or use indirect proxies (provision rate, welfare).
  - **Exact/Close:** Fehr et al. (2002), Decker et al. (2003), Fatas et al. (2010), Webb & Foddy (2004), Reuben & Riedl (2009)
  - **Adj/Weak/None:** Many studies only infer efficiency from behavioral change, or focus on non-payoff outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Based Outcomes (Direct):**
  - **Group efficiency:** Explicitly, the ratio of total group payoff to the theoretical maximum (Fehr et al., 2002; Reuben & Riedl, 2009; Decker et al., 2003; Fatas et al., 2010; Gürerk et al., 2009).
  - **Total/group payoff, welfare, surplus:** Sometimes stated as "group profit," "team surplus," "group earnings," etc.

- **Behavioral (Non-Payoff) Outcomes:**
  - **Contribution or cooperation rate/frequency:** Much more commonly measured (almost all empirical PGGs).
  - **Punishment frequency/severity assigned; norm compliance rates.**
  - **Punishment targeting (defectors/cooperators).**
  - **Social or emotional outcomes:** Judgments of moral wrongness, social approval/disapproval, status, or emotional reactions.
  - **Provision rate in step-level games (proxy when efficiency/payoff not given directly).**

- **Adjunct measures and contextual outcomes:** Provision of public good (yes/no), status, norm perception, etc.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of Cross-Paper, Payoff-Focused Findings:**

- **Punishment Often Increases Contributions, But Efficiency Gains Vary:**
  - In *many linear PGGs*, enabling punishment reliably increases contributions; however, the effect on efficiency (i.e., group payoff after accounting for punishment costs) is less consistent (Fehr et al., 2002; Decker et al., 2003; Gürerk et al., 2009).
    - *Fehr et al. (2002):* Punishment yields a dramatic, robust increase in efficiency (payoff up by ~40%), especially when punishment is targeted and not prohibitively costly.
    - *Decker et al. (2003):* More severe/collective punishment rules increase contributions but do not always improve efficiency—costly punishment can offset contribution gains.
    - *Gürerk et al. (2009):* Reward-based incentives achieve higher efficiency than punishment, even though punishment can lift contributions.

- **Efficiency Effects Are Highly Moderated:**
  - **Cost and effectiveness of punishment:** Central; if punishment is cheap and effective, efficiency gains are likely (Fehr et al., 2002; Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Heller & Sieberg, 2008).
  - **Group composition/heterogeneity:** In groups with heterogeneous MPCRs or privileged members, punishment often increases inequality and delivers less efficiency gain (Reuben & Riedl, 2009).
  - **Punishment structure—centralized vs. decentralized, severity, targeting:** Centralized or well-targeted punishment is often more efficient; random or collective punishment can be costly and inefficient unless punishment costs are reimbursed (Fatas et al., 2010; Decker et al., 2003; Guillen et al., 2007).

- **Negative or Nonlinear/Path-Dependent Effects:**
  - Excessive or mis-targeted punishment can reduce efficiency, especially when emotionally motivated or insufficiently targeted at defectors (Decker et al., 2003; Whitmeyer, 2004).
  - Non-monotonicities and multiple equilibria are possible—introducing punishment does not always yield higher efficiency, particularly at intermediate levels of monitoring or when the group lacks structure (Whitmeyer, 2004; POLLOCK, 1988).

- **Other Important Moderators:**
  - **Population/group structure and size:** More structured populations (multiple independent groups, spatially local punishment) see stronger positive effects (POLLOCK, 1988; Noailly et al., 2009).
  - **Reward vs punishment:** Rewarding prosociality often results in higher efficiency than punishment for anti-sociality (Gürerk et al., 2009).

- **Control Efficiency Matters:**
  - In privileged/heterogeneous games, control efficiency may be higher, but the incremental efficiency gain from punishment is much smaller (Reuben & Riedl, 2009).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the above findings, **to predict the average efficiency of a public goods game when peer punishment is enabled, conditional on game design and control efficiency:**

- **Expect contributions to increase under punishment, but do not uniformly expect efficiency gains.** The net efficiency outcome is a **delicate balance** between increased group payoff from higher cooperation and net losses from punishment costs. The *cost-effectiveness* of the punishment mechanism is paramount.

- **Key design dimensions to consider:**
  - **Marginal per-capita return (mpcr):** High MPCR generally strengthens positive punishment effects; low MPCR can make punishment less effective and more costly.
  - **Punishment cost and technology:** Lower punishment cost and appropriate severity increase the likelihood of net efficiency gain (Fehr et al., 2002; Heller & Sieberg, 2008).
  - **Group composition/heterogeneity:** Efficiency gains from punishment are *much weaker* in heterogeneous (privileged) groups even if control efficiency is high (Reuben & Riedl, 2009).
  - **Type and targeting of punishment:** Decentralized, individually-targeted punishment (especially if cost-effective) is more likely to increase efficiency. Collective or random punishment can erode efficiency unless costs are recycled (Decker et al., 2003; Fatas et al., 2010).
  - **Centralized vs. decentralized punishment:** Some empirical evidence suggests centralized mechanisms (e.g. leader-administered) can be efficient if the incentive structure is well-designed and not prone to misuse (Gürerk et al., 2009).
  - **Reward options:** When available, rewards typically boost efficiency more than punishment.

- **Historical path and initial conditions** may matter—punishment can generate persistent behavioral changes, but can also induce negative dynamics if introduced at unfavorable times or with intermediate monitoring efficacy (Whitmeyer, 2004).

**Quantitative expectations must remain cautious:** predicting strict magnitudes is difficult given the context-dependent, sometimes non-monotonic literature. It is reasonable to predict that, under standard linear PGGs with moderate cost-effective punishment, efficiency will increase in treatment compared to control *unless* costs are high, groups are privileged/heterogeneous, or punishment is poorly targeted or excessive.

Other less commonly covered dimensions include **default_contrib**, **show_punishment_id**, and aspects of summary feedback, which are infrequently explored as direct moderators of efficiency outcomes in punishment-enabled PGGs.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Partial coverage of design space:** Only a subset of the 14 predictor dimensions are systematically varied and empirically tested. Effects of chat, framing (`default_contrib`), visibility (`show_punishment_id`), and feedback (`show_other_summaries`) on efficiency are under-explored.

- **Efficiency vs. contribution:** Many studies *do not* measure efficiency/payoff directly, and prediction must sometimes rely on indirect evidence (contribution rates or behavioral proxies). This introduces error, as increased contributions do not always translate to greater efficiency due to punishment costs.

- **Context and generalizability:** The bulk of strong evidence comes from artificial, incentivized lab settings with small groups, known MPCR, and full information structures. Transferability to field conditions or highly heterogeneous/large groups may be limited.

- **Theoretical non-monotonicities and path-dependency:** Mechanism and modeling papers establish that punishment may only improve efficiency under certain parameter regimes (punishment must be cost-effective, targeted, and either group structure or historical conditions must be favorable). Some findings warn of possible efficiency loss with excessive or misaligned punishment, multiple equilibria, and path-dependency. 

- **Limited direct evidence for some dimensions:** For example, **reward-based mechanisms**, and the combined/interactive effects of chat, summary feedback, and identity disclosure, are less frequently tested for their impact on efficiency.

- **Limited outcome diversity in some studies:** Many behavioral studies focus on non-payoff outcomes, limiting the pool of effect sizes for efficiency prediction.

- **Potential publication bias:** Most evidence reflects positive or nuanced effects; null or negative results may be underrepresented.

---

### In summary:

- The literature provides **reasonably robust guidance** that enabling peer punishment in repeated, standard PGGs *often* increases efficiency, but the magnitude (and even direction) of effect is *highly sensitive* to group homogeneity, punishment cost-effectiveness, targeting, and design details. 
- Several *key design parameters*—MPCR, punishment cost, group structure, and targeting mechanism—are well covered and must be considered primary moderators.
- Limitations in coverage, the prevalence of non-payoff outcome reporting, context dependence, and potential non-monotonicity all **necessitate caution** in making fine-grained, quantitative predictions, especially outside standard linear PGG lab scenarios.
