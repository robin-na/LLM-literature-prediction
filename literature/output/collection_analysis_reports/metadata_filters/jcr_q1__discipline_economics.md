# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is large (n=190), varied, and rich in empirical studies, with a strong focus on laboratory experiments using standard Public Goods Games (PGG) and close CPR (common-pool resource) variants. The evidence base is heavily empirical with relatively few theory-only papers, though the theoretical contributions included are highly relevant and often provide explicit comparative statics or equilibrium characterizations. Most studies focus squarely on efficiency (group payoff/welfare/surplus) as the outcome of interest, especially in punishment-enabled versus punishment-disabled conditions. A broad range of punishment institutions (peer, centralized, third-party, voting-based, expulsion, etc.) and design moderators (cost, severity, monitoring, group composition, etc.) are considered. The paper set is broad for the prediction task but is denser for canonical lab PGGs, less so for field settings or highly complex institutional environments.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) `pgg_or_variant`:**  
- **Relevance:** *exact* for the core of the literature; nearly all key empirical and theoretical papers directly study linear public goods games or minimal variations (adjacent: CPR or trust games with similar structure; close: dynamic, contest, or coalition games).  
- **Coverage:** Extensive evidence for canonical PGGs (typically 3-5 players, continuous or all-or-nothing contribution, multiple rounds); moderate but meaningful coverage for close variants (CPR, trust games with sanctioning, threshold/cooperation games, contest games).

**b) `punishment_or_sanctions`:**  
- **Relevance:** *exact* in most empirically relevant papers, which systematically manipulate peer punishment, centralized punishment, expulsion, democratic rule selection, or sanction technology parameters. Some variants (adjacent, close) focus on social exclusion, monitoring, coalition exclusion, or institutional enforcement rather than direct monetary punishments.
- **Coverage:** Deep experimental coverage for peer and centralized costly punishment; moderate for reward, expulsion, ostracism, reputation, and combined institutions; detailed contextualization for punishment type, cost, and technology.

**c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`:**  
- **Relevance:** *exact* in core studies, with efficiency, group payoff, or welfare explicitly measured and compared between punishment-enabled and control. Some studies report closely related outcomes (net earnings, surplus, welfare) when efficiency is not expressly normalized to the social optimum.
- **Coverage:** Extensive for standard lab PGGs; spotty in adjacent domains where main outcomes are contributions or cooperation. Explicit efficiency ratios are frequent in the best-aligned work.

**Summary:**  
Overall, coverage is *exact* or *close* for canonical lab PGGs with costly, explicit punishment and efficiency/welfare as main outcomes. Coverage is *adjacent* or *weak* for cultural field studies, one-shot scenarios, or studies reporting only behavioral outcomes (contributions, punishment assigned) without mapping to efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

## Payoff-Related Outcomes
- **Efficiency:** Ratio of group payoff to social optimum; frequently, explicitly reported in lab PGGs with/without punishment.
- **Group Payoff/Earnings/Surplus/Welfare:** Aggregate participant earnings; net of punishment cost, sometimes as a share of the theoretical maximum.
- **Net Welfare Change Due to Sanctions:** Studies often measure the net effect of increased cooperation offset by cost of sanctions.

## Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Related but Distinct)
- Contribution or cooperation rate.
- Frequency, intensity, and targeting of punishment.
- Frequency of expulsion/exclusion.
- Measures of norm compliance, retaliation, and anti-/pro-social punishment.
- Voting behavior in institution choice.
- Changes in perceived norm salience, conditional cooperation, or reputation.

## Distinction Maintained
Several papers emphasize that gains in contribution do not always translate to gains in efficiency—the cost of punishment can partially or fully offset the gains from deterring free riding. Conversely, in cases of well-targeted or institutionally robust punishment, both contributions and efficiency can rise (see Sutter et al., 2010; Fehr & Gächter, 2000).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## General Regularities
- **Punishment often increases efficiency over control, but not always:** Most canonical lab PGGs show sizeable net efficiency gains when punishment is moderate in cost, highly leveraged, and targeted (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Dickinson et al., 2015; Masclet et al., 2003; Ertan et al., 2009).
  - **Magnitude:** Efficiency gains typically in the range of 5-30 percentage points over control, contingent on design dimensions. Gains are smaller than gains in contribution rates due to cost of punishment.

- **Targeting and method of punishment are crucial:**  
  - *Targeted* (anti-free rider) punishment increases efficiency (Ertan et al., 2009; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Kube et al., 2015).
  - *Unrestricted* or *antisocial* punishment (punishing cooperators, or high spite) can reduce or eliminate efficiency gains, or even lower efficiency below control (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Fehr et al., 2008; Nikiforakis, 2008).

- **Institutional design is powerful:**
  - Endogenous/voted or democratic punishment/reward regimes generate higher efficiency than exogenous or imposed regimes, primarily by reducing misdirected punishment and enhancing legitimacy (Sutter et al., 2010; Putterman et al., 2011; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).

- **Monitoring and information structure matter:**
  - High-quality monitoring (accurate, cheap, immediate) increases the efficiency gain from punishment; imperfect monitoring (noise, cost) can eliminate or reverse gains (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012, 2019; Nicklisch et al., 2016).
  - Revealing punishment identity or contributions makes punishment more credible, but also increases risk of retaliation in some contexts.

- **Punishment effectiveness and cost trade-off:**
  - High-leverage (large impact per unit cost) punishment is more efficient. High-cost, low-impact punishment often fails to generate net efficiency gains (Sutter et al., 2010; Masclet et al., 2003).

## Contextual Moderators and Exceptions
- **Group composition and context:**
  - Efficiency gains from punishment are strongly moderated by group diversity, baseline cooperativeness, and the risk of antisocial punishment. If baseline efficiency is already high (e.g., highly cooperative groups), adding punishment may reduce efficiency (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Gächter & Thöni, 2005).
  - Heterogeneous or conflict-prone groups are more likely to experience destructive or retaliatory punishment cycles, undermining net gains (Nikiforakis et al., 2012; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).

- **Cultural and field settings:**
  - In field or cross-cultural settings, institutional legitimacy, prevalence of antisocial punishment, norms of fairness, and local trust can cause punishment to backfire or to be less effective than in canonical lab studies (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011).

- **Alternative mechanisms:**
  - Centralized or delegated enforcement, exclusion/expulsion, and well-implemented rewards are sometimes more efficient than peer punishment (Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Cinyabuguma et al., 2005; Sutter et al., 2010; Masclet et al., 2003).
  - Communication and voluntary group sorting can substitute for punishment, potentially achieving comparable gains in efficiency (Bochet & Putterman, 2009; Kosfeld et al., 2009).

- **Treatment-control efficiency mapping:**
  - The size of the efficiency gain from punishment is strongly predicted by control efficiency: if control efficiency is high, further gains are small or negative; if control efficiency is low, punishment can produce substantial gains—contingent on targeting and cost structures (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; van der Weele, 2012).

## Cases Where Punishment Reduces Efficiency
- High prevalence of antisocial or misdirected punishment (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Fehr et al., 2008; Nikiforakis, 2008).
- High cost-to-impact ratio or excessive escalation/retaliation (Masclet et al., 2003; Hopfensitz & Reuben, 2009).
- Voluntary or opt-in punishment regimes where participation and ambition are not enforced (Del Ponte et al., 2025).
- Institutional mismatch with group norms or lack of legitimacy (Vollan et al., 2013; Castillo et al., 2011).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Principle:**  
Enabling punishment in a public-goods-game-like environment most often increases treatment efficiency relative to a punishment-disabled control, but the magnitude and even the direction of the effect depend crucially on key design dimensions and contextual factors.  
 
## Predictive Insights from Literature
- **Control efficiency is highly predictive:** Punishment rarely generates large gains if the control already achieves high efficiency; may reduce efficiency if control is at or near optimum (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Masclet et al., 2003).
- **Game design dimensions critically moderate the effect:**
    - **Player count:** Smaller groups often see larger gains; as group size increases, effectiveness depends on monitoring and norm enforcement capacity (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999).
    - **Punishment cost/tech:** Lower-cost, higher-impact punishment is more likely to generate net efficiency gains (Masclet et al., 2003; Heller & Sieberg, 2010).
    - **Monitoring and feedback:** Reliable, cheap, and clear monitoring supports positive efficiency effects of punishment (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Nicklisch et al., 2016).
    - **Targeting:** Restricting punishment to free riders (and prohibiting antisocial punishment) substantially improves net gains (Ertan et al., 2009; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).
    - **Institutional choice:** Allowing groups to endogenously choose and design sanctioning regimes leads to better targeting, higher efficiency, and greater compliance (Sutter et al., 2010; Putterman et al., 2011).
    - **Chat/communication:** Communication alone can boost efficiency; when combined with punishment, effects are often additive or synergistic (Bochet & Putterman, 2009; Kosfeld et al., 2009), but chat alone can sometimes approximate the positive effects of punishment in highly cooperative groups.

- **Negative or null effects:**  
    - If punishment is possible but costly, unrestricted, or frequently misdirected (especially in competitive or antisocial contexts, or with high noise), efficiency gains are eliminated or reversed (Nikiforakis, 2008; Markussen et al., 2016; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).
    - Enabling counter-punishment (opportunity for targets to punish back) can reduce efficiency relative to no-punishment controls (Nikiforakis, 2008).

- **Reward vs. punishment:**  
    - Efficiently implemented reward institutions often outperform punishment for boosting efficiency; the combination can be most powerful if both are appropriately targeted and affordable (Sutter et al., 2010; Andreoni et al., 2003).

- **Mapping control to treatment:**  
    - When the control (no-punishment) efficiency is moderate or low, expected treatment efficiency with punishment can approach 90-100% of social optimum in well-designed, small-group, canonical lab PGGs with low-cost, high-impact punishment.
    - Treatment efficiency may only marginally exceed control efficiency (or not at all) in high-cooperation controls, in cases of high punishment cost/noise, or when punishment is likely to be misapplied.

**Summary Table: Expectation of Treatment Efficiency from Key Dimensions**
| Control Efficiency | Punishment Effectiveness/Cost | Targeting & Institution | Likely Treatment Efficiency Change |
|--------------------|------------------------------|------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Low                | High effectiveness, low cost | Endogenous, targeted   | Large positive gain (10-30pp)     |
| Moderate           | High effectiveness, low cost | Endogenous, targeted   | Moderate gain (5-15pp)            |
| High               | Any                         | Any                    | Marginal/Zero or negative change   |
| Any                | High cost, low effect, noisy/mistargeted | Unrestricted/antisocial | Negative or zero change           |
| Any                | Reasonable cost/effect       | Reward enabled alone or with punishment | Reward > punishment, combo best   |

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

The following game design dimensions are **directly** or **indirectly** informed by the literature for purposes of predicting treatment efficiency:

## Directly and Repeatedly Informed
- **player_count:** Moderately large evidence base supports effect of group size.
- **num_rounds:** Efficiency dynamics over repeated rounds are well studied.
- **mpcr:** Extensive empirical and theoretical evidence on how higher marginal per-capita return moderates effect of punishment.
- **punishment_cost & punishment_tech:** Deep evidence for punishment cost, effectiveness, and structure. Effects on efficiency are well described both empirically and theoretically.
- **chat:** Several studies directly manipulate communication; chat plus punishment is synergistic but can substitute.
- **all_or_nothing:** Both binary and continuous contribution formats are covered; qualitative effects on efficiency observed.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Effects of enabling or combining rewards with punishment are empirically tested in multiple studies.
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds:** Some studies manipulate information feedback, with monitoring and transparency shown to be key moderators.
- **show_punishment_id:** Anonymity or identification of punishers informs retaliation/antisocial punishment risk.
- **punishment_exists:** All core prediction studies explicitly use this as the key treatment variable.

## Indirectly or Contextually Informed
- **default_contrib:** Some evidence for opt-in/opt-out framing effects but limited direct mapping to efficiency shifts with punishment.
- **show_punishment_id:** Occasionally studied in connection with reputation, retaliation, or social norm enforcement.
- **information structure (monitoring, error, timing):** Extensively studied in more nuanced ways than "show..." variables alone, but not always mapped directly into these fields.

## Missing or Weakly Covered
- **Very large groups:** Most lab work is with 2-5 players; scalability to larger groups is theorized but less often tested.
- **Highly complex/field-institutionalized environments:** Some field studies and complex lab institutions are covered, but findings are more variable; direct mapping to the 14-dimensional design space is sparser.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Boundary Conditions:** Many findings are established for canonical, small-group, repeated lab PGGs with simple linear payoffs and symmetric players. Generalization to large groups, field settings, or environments with strong heterogeneity requires additional caution and is less directly supported.
- **Punishment Targeting:** Numerous studies stress that the downstream effect on efficiency depends critically on how punishment is targeted and the context-specific prevalence of antisocial punishment. If information or institutional design permits misdirected punishment, overall efficiency can decrease.
- **Antisocial/Counter-Punishment:** Opportunity for punishment of cooperators or for counter-punishment can entirely reverse predicted efficiency gains (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Nikiforakis, 2008).
- **Cultural and Contextual Moderators:** Empirical findings from field, cross-cultural, or highly asymmetric contexts show both positive and negative effects of punishment on efficiency, sometimes in contradiction to lab regularities.
- **Measurement of Outcomes:** In some studies, efficiency is reported as group payoff or welfare; in others, only contributions are measured, requiring caution in inferring efficiency, especially when punishment is costly and cooperation rates alone overstate gains.
- **Incomplete Mapping of Secondary Dimensions:** Several nuanced design dimensions (e.g., default contributions, information spillovers, complex feedback structures, non-linearities in payoffs) are less systematically mapped to efficiency differences in this literature, though isolated findings exist.
- **Extrapolation Risk:** Predictions for cases with high initial efficiency, noisy monitoring, voluntary sanction regimes, or complex field environments should account for much greater variance and possible null or negative effects.

**Conclusion:**  
The literature provides a strong empirical and theoretical foundation for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in canonical PGGs as a function of design parameters and control efficiency, but underscores the context-dependence and the need to carefully consider targeting, cost-effectiveness, monitoring, and group composition. Where design choices or context invite misdirected or excessive punishment, or when baseline efficiency is high, enabling punishment can fail to improve, or even reduce, efficiency. Where punishment is cheap, targeted, legitimate, and group conditions warrant it, large efficiency gains are reliably observed.
