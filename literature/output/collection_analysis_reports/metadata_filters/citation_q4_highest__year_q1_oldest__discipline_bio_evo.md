# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set (N=110) offers a broad but uneven evidence base for predicting **treatment efficiency** (group payoff/efficiency when peer punishment is enabled) in public-goods-game (PGG)-like environments, given detailed game design dimensions and a no-punishment control efficiency. The set integrates both empirical (primarily laboratory, some field) and theoretical/game-theoretic approaches.

- **Empirical papers**: Numerous, especially for canonical linear PGGs with/without peer punishment. Many report on contribution/cooperation rates and, less consistently, on group payoff/efficiency. Field experiments and contextually rich settings are less frequent but present.
- **Theory papers**: Extensive, providing evolutionary and dynamic models of cooperation under punishment and other mechanisms (reward, reputation, spatial structure, etc.). Many include explicit payoff/efficiency analyses, but often as equilibrium properties or evolutionary averages rather than experimental measurements.
- **Breadth**: The set strongly overrepresents standard, small-group, linear or all-or-nothing PGGs, with moderate coverage of institutional features, spatial/network structure, and cross-cultural variation. Adjacent literatures (indirect reciprocity, mutualism, CPR, trust/ultimatum games) supplement but have indirect relevance.

Overall, the base is sufficiently large to allow high-confidence qualitative synthesis for standard group PGGs with peer punishment. However, evidence becomes sparser and more ambiguous as design complexity increases (e.g., reputation systems, group competition, variable punishment structure, real-world context).

# 2) Task Relevance

We assess relevance along three axes: **pgg_or_variant**, **punishment_or_sanctions**, and **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**.

- **PGG or variant (pgg_or_variant)**:  
  - *Exact relevance* is prevalent in the core empirical and theoretical papers focusing on standard PGG setups.  
  - Papers addressing close analogs (CPR, threshold games, indirect reciprocity) are numerous and often labeled 'adjacent', providing valuable mechanistic but not directly parameterized insights.
- **Punishment or sanctions (punishment_or_sanctions)**:  
  - *Exact relevance* dominates in both experimental and theory work on peer or institutional punishment.  
  - Some papers focus on rewards or partner control, providing only adjacent/conceptual relevance for punishment-specific predictions.  
  - A strong subset discuss features adjacent to peer punishment (third-party, pool punishment, anti-social punishment, retaliation).
- **Efficiency or related payoff outcome (efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome)**:  
  - While many studies use efficiency, payoff, earnings, or group welfare as outcomes (*exact* or *close*), a substantive fraction (especially those focused on behavioral mechanics or neural/psychological underpinnings) do not measure or report direct payoff-based outcomes.
  - Some central empirical PGG experiments clarify the relationship between contribution rates and efficiency, but others report only contribution, not payoff.

**In sum**: The evidence strongly and directly targets PGGs, peer punishment, and efficiency/payoff outcomes for standard designs, but coverage diminishes for more complex, real-world, or institutionally varied settings.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes** (`efficiency`, group payoff, welfare, total earnings):  
- *Directly measured*: In major laboratory PGGs (e.g., Egas & Riedl, O'Gorman et al., Sääksvuori et al.), efficiency is the primary outcome variable, compared between conditions with and without punishment.
- *Theoretically derived*: Evolutionary game theory and replicator dynamics papers frequently analyze average payoffs or efficiency at equilibrium.
- *Reported as related measures*: Some field experiments and institutional analyses report percentage of the social optimum or mean earnings.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes** (contribution/cooperation rates, norm compliance, punishment frequency):  
- These are the most commonly reported outcomes in much of both the empirical and theory literature. Many studies use contribution rate as a surrogate for efficiency but do not always report the translation to actual group payoff (e.g., many papers in the Fehr & Gächter tradition, numerous cross-cultural studies).
- Numerous papers focus only on behavioral motives, neural correlates, or emergent strategy types, not on payoff outcomes.

**Distinction clarity**:  
- Several reviews note that increased punishment can raise cooperation without always raising efficiency, especially when punishment is costly (e.g., Egas & Riedl, Guala 2012).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## a) **Canonical Finding:**  
**Enabling peer punishment in standard linear-reward PGGs often increases group cooperation but its effect on efficiency (total payoff) depends critically on punishment cost, effectiveness, and game structure.**
- **When costs are low and punishment is effective:** Efficiency increases with punishment enabled. Full or near-full cooperation is sometimes reached with efficiency near group optimum if punishment use declines as cooperation stabilizes (O'Gorman et al., 2009; Gintis, 2000; Henrich & Boyd, 2001).
- **When punishment is costly or overused:** Higher cooperation does not translate to higher efficiency. The cost of administering punishment outweighs the gain in public goods provision, so efficiency stagnates or declines compared to control (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Guala, 2012; Traulsen et al., 2012).
- **Punishment effectiveness (cost-to-impact ratio):** If punishment is expensive or ineffective, it fails to change the equilibrium or may even reduce group payoff (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Gintis, 2000; Gardner & West, 2004).

## b) **Moderators and Contextual Factors:**
- **Intergroup competition:** In environments with group competition, the effect of punishment on efficiency is much more positive and robust (Sääksvuori et al., 2011; Vollan, 2008; Puurtinen & Mappes, 2009).
- **Culture and anti-social punishment:** Cross-cultural experiments show that punishment can have positive, negative, or null effects on efficiency, heavily moderated by the prevalence of anti-social punishment (punishing cooperators), local norms, and trust (Gächter et al., 2010; Gächter & Herrmann, 2009; Vollan, 2008).
- **Retaliation:** If punished individuals can identify and retaliate against punishers, cooperative equilibrium and efficiency gains can collapse (Janssen & Bushman, 2008).
- **Type of punishment:** Pool punishment (especially with second-order punishment) can reduce efficiency despite increasing cooperation, because of the resource loss from punishment itself (Traulsen et al., 2012).
- **Reputation, coordination, and communication:** Reputation mechanisms, group communication, and coordinated or institutional punishment/reward may amplify efficiency gains or reduce the costliness of punishment (Brandt et al., 2003; Hilbe & Sigmund, 2010; Rand et al., 2010).
- **Game structure:** Design features like group size, number of rounds, and MPCR interact with the above factors (BOYD & RICHERSON, 1992; Archetti et al., 2011).

## c) **Other Mechanisms:**
- **Rewards and reputation transfer:** These can substitute for or complement punishment, sometimes achieving even higher efficiency (Sasaki & Unemi, 2011; Semmann et al., 2004; dos Santos et al., 2011).
- **Optional participation and thresholds:** The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on the payoff structure and equilibria of the baseline game (Mathew & Boyd, 2009; Bach et al., 2006).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

## **Synthesized Guidance for Predicting Treatment Efficiency**
- **Use control (no-punishment) efficiency as a strong baseline.**
  - If control efficiency is already high (near full cooperation), enabling punishment is unlikely to further increase efficiency, may even reduce it due to new punishment costs.
  - If control efficiency is low (due to free-riding), enabling punishment *can* increase efficiency, *if* punishment is not too costly/effective.

- **Key moderators affecting the incremental effect of punishment:**  
  a) **Punishment cost and effectiveness** (`punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `punishment_magnitude`):  
     - Low-cost, high-impact punishment is most likely to increase efficiency (Gintis, 2000; Egas & Riedl, 2008).
     - High-cost or low-impact punishment frequently reduces net efficiency (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Traulsen et al., 2012).
  b) **Group composition and context** (`player_count`, `mpcr`, `chat`, `reward_exists`, `show_punishment_id`, cultural/field context):  
     - Efficiency gains are more consistent in small, well-mixed groups.
     - Intergroup competition or field contexts with low trust can make punishment more effective; in high trust or imposed contexts, punishment may backfire (Sääksvuori et al., 2011; Gächter et al., 2010; Vollan, 2008).
  c) **Game structure and information** (`num_rounds`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`):  
     - Sufficiently many rounds and information about others' behavior/identity can make punishment more effective as a threat, reducing actual usage/cost.
  d) **Type of punishment** (`punishment_tech`, e.g., peer vs. pool punishment, anti-social punishment possibility):  
     - Peer punishment works best when anti-social punishment is minimal or prosocial punishment is normatively prescribed (Rand et al., 2010; Gächter et al., 2010).
     - Designated/diffuse punishment regimes can affect efficiency (O'Gorman et al., 2009).

- **Do NOT assume a universal efficiency gain from enabling punishment.**
   - *Empirical regularity*: Cooperation is reliably enhanced, but efficiency is not always improved—cost structure and context are crucial.
   - *In absence of details on punishment cost/effectiveness*: Predict that the treatment efficiency will be no higher (and often lower) than in the control.

- **When predicting efficiency for new designs:**  
   - Favor *theoretical models* for out-of-sample parameter combinations (e.g., large group size, unusual cost/benefit ratios), but note their limitations—most lack real-world context and individual heterogeneity.
   - *Empirical estimates* are more transferable for standard laboratory PGGs; exercise caution extrapolating to field or complex institutional settings (Castillo et al., 2011; Vollan, 2008).

- **If reward/communication/reputation are also enabled, interaction effects can be substantial.** The base evidence is incomplete for high-complexity designs.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Well- or Directly-Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`: The effect of group size on the efficiency impact of punishment is theorized and empirically supported (smaller groups more likely to support efficient punishment effects).
- `num_rounds`, `mpcr`: Well-parameterized in both empirical and theoretical studies.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_magnitude`, `punishment_tech`: Near-universal in theories and in most experimental studies.
- `all_or_nothing`: Explicit in some models and experiments (binary vs. continuous contributions).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Highlighted in adjacent/mechanism-focused literature.

**Indirectly or Contextually Informed:**
- `chat`: Effects of pre-play communication are explored in some CPR and field experiments; generally, chat enables higher efficiency independently of punishment, and may interact (Anderies et al., 2011).
- `show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Occasionally manipulated or theorized (in studies on retaliation, reputation, or dynamic expectations), but not systematically varied.
- `default_contrib`: Framing effects (opt-in vs. opt-out/anchor) are rarely directly manipulated for efficiency analyses—discussed more in review or field papers.
- `show_other_summaries`: Theoretical models sometimes include information flow, but empirical manipulation is sparse.

**Missing or Sparsely Addressed:**
- Fine distinctions of *institutional* vs. *peer* punishment are present mainly in theory; empirical implementation is limited.
- Cultural moderators, voting/self-determination, and real-world trust/social context are highlighted as important but insufficiently parameterized.
- Joint manipulation of multiple mechanisms (punishment plus reward/communication/reputation) is rare; effects of complex treatment interactions are not systematically studied.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency outcome under-reported**: Many studies report contribution or cooperation, but not group payoff/efficiency, sometimes conflating behavioral and payoff outcomes (Guala, 2012).
- **Contextual moderators (culture/trust/field context) are critical but poorly parameterized**: Empirical studies (especially field or cross-cultural) show that context can reverse the sign of punishment effects on efficiency, but predictive models often lack context variables (Gächter et al., 2010; Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011).
- **Identification of dimension-level moderators is often incomplete:** For rare or complex design features (e.g., chat, show_punishment_id, reward + punishment, reputation systems), evidence is sparse.
- **Separation of mechanism from outcome**: Many theoretical models (or empirical studies focused on third-party punishment, indirect reciprocity) illuminate mechanisms but are not calibrated to experimental efficiency data.
- **Interactions and non-linearities rarely systematically studied:** Most studies hold all but one design dimension constant; joint/treatment interaction effects are underexplored.
- **Ambiguity in effect direction and magnitude:** Particularly for settings with anti-social punishment, retaliation, high punishment cost, or low effect, findings in the literature disagree or remain context-dependent.
- **Extrapolation to real-world settings**: Evidence from laboratory PGGs may not extrapolate straightforwardly to natural groups, policy, or institutional settings (Guala, 2012; Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011).
- **Scarcity of high-fidelity field experiments with efficiency measures:** Real-world and field data are rare, with existing studies showing mixed and context-sensitive effects.

---

### In summary, for the specified downstream prediction task, the literature offers strong theory and a substantial empirical basis for predicting the sign and moderators of punishment's effect on group efficiency in standard PGG designs, conditional on core design dimensions. Predicting quantitative effect sizes, or extrapolating to complex, real-world, or cross-culturally diverse settings, is less well-supported due to the limitations above.
