# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is dominated by empirical research, overwhelmingly experimental laboratory studies, with a smaller component of field, observational, and theoretical or qualitative work. Most papers involve structured, incentivized multi-player economic games, with a significant subset using standard or extended public goods game (PGG) designs, though many employ variants or adjacent paradigms (bargaining, coordination, trust, common-pool resource, delegation games, etc.). The evidence is broad in coverage of mechanisms—punishment, reward, monitoring, reputational cues, endogenous group formation—but is narrower regarding direct, quantitative comparisons of no-punishment versus punishment on *efficiency* (total payoff relative to group social optimum) within true PGGs. Several high-relevance lab papers (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015) address the core prediction task, while many others explore adjacent concepts, behavioral mechanisms, or non-payoff outcomes. A small number of field or observational papers broaden scope but are sparse in direct relevance.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) `pgg_or_variant`:**
- **Exact relevance:** Core papers explicitly employ standard repeated PGGs with clear manipulation of peer or centralized punishment (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Close relevance:** Several papers use adjacent games (team investment, minimum effort, delegation, coordination, trust, bargaining, CPRG), which share multi-player social dilemma structure but diverge in game form, player roles, or incentive structure.
- **Adjacent, Weak, or None:** Some evidence comes from non-game settings or hypothetical scenarios; these are contextually interesting but not directly informative for PGG efficiency prediction.

**b) `punishment_or_sanctions`:**
- **Exact relevance:** Many studies manipulate the presence/absence of punishment (peer or centralized), or compare punishment to other sanctions (e.g., reward, monitoring).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Others use exclusion, reputation cues, or informal sanctions; some only discuss punishment as a possible mechanism.
- **Weak/None:** Several studies do not implement any form of punishment, or analyze only reward/monitoring.

**c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`:**
- **Exact relevance:** A subset directly reports group efficiency or analogous net payoff (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Close:** Several studies only report group/cumulative payoffs, surplus, or earnings—often in adjacent games.
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Many papers focus primarily on contributions, cooperation rates, behavioral/psychological outcomes, or norm compliance, with efficiency or payoff only alluded to.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Relevant to Prediction):**
- Group efficiency (payoff relative to optimum; e.g., Suleiman & Samid, Cobo-Reyes et al., Castillo et al.)
- Total/cumulative group earnings, welfare, or surplus
- Aggregate payoffs net of punishment/reward costs

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Important but Distinct):**
- Contribution/cooperation rates
- Frequency and targets of punishment or reward
- Norm compliance, altruistic or anti-social punishment frequency
- Partner choice, exclusion, or group formation behavior
- Attitudinal outcomes, trait or personality predictors
- Perceptions of fairness, willingness to punish

**Clarification:** Many studies report increased cooperation or contributions with punishment, but only some calculate or discuss *net efficiency*, the relevant outcome for downstream prediction. In several cases, higher contribution does not lead to higher efficiency due to punishment costs or anti-social punishment.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Evidence in (Standard) PGGs:**
- *Peer or Centralized Punishment Usually Increases Efficiency, But Effects Are Variable:* Enabling punishment commonly raises group efficiency (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021), though the magnitude of the effect is highly dependent on group composition (e.g., prevalence of strong reciprocators vs. norm-keepers), punishment technology, and societal context.
- *Centralized/Formal Sanctions Outperform Informal in Open Societies:* Centralized (formal) sanctions yield higher efficiency than informal/peer punishment, especially with group openness or migration (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
- *Design Details Modulate Effects Profoundly:*
    - Structure and information in the punishment environment (retaliation possibilities, number of punishment stages, fixed vs. anonymous IDs, feedback) majorly alter the effect. Where complex, visible retaliation is possible, efficiency gains dissipate (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - Punishment cost and effectiveness ratios (fine-to-fee), and sometimes the density of the punishment network, can modulate whether efficiency increases or not (Kanitsar, 2021).

**Key Moderating Dimensions:**
- *Group Composition and Social Context:* More strong reciprocators = greater efficiency gains from punishment; more norm-keepers = lower or absent gains (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- *Openness/Group Mobility:* Formal punishment more effective where members can exit or enter groups (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
- *Punishment Technology Parameters:* Multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, and full identification can facilitate feuding/retaliation, negating net efficiency gains (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- *Network Structure:* Punishment is beneficial in dense (public good) networks but may not be in sparse (generalized exchange) networks (Kanitsar, 2021).
- *Centralized vs. Peer Punishment:* Centralized punishment can robustly raise efficiency, with selection mechanism for managers playing only a minor role (Castillo et al., 2021).

**Adjacent and Contradictory Evidence:**
- *Adjacent Designs Sometimes Show No (or Negative) Efficiency Effects:* In games adjacent to PGGs (team investment, trust, bargaining), introducing punishment may not increase—and may even reduce—efficiency if direct costs of punishment are significant or if punishment is miscoordinated or retaliatory (Calabuig et al., 2024; Herne et al., 2022; Abbink et al., 2004).
- *Field and Ethnographic Results Often Diverge:* Field experiments and observational studies often find weaker or null effects of punishment on cooperation or efficiency (Noussair et al., 2015), and real-world cultural context can make punishment rare or ineffective at sustaining cooperation (Sequeira, 2023).

**Mechanism Excerpt:**
- Efficiency gains often result not from punishing free riders per se, but from increased anticipatory contributions by cooperators and reciprocators; anti-social punishment is rare but can undermine net benefits (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Directly Supported Predictions:**
- **If control (no-punishment) efficiency is moderate/low:** Enabling peer or centralized punishment is likely to increase efficiency relative to control, *provided* the punishment technology:
    - Restricts retaliation (anonymous, single stage per round)
    - Maintains moderate punishment cost and high effectiveness (e.g., fine-to-fee >1)
    - Avoids high levels of anti-social punishment (norm-keepers)
    - Operates in a group or society with a reasonable prevalence of strong reciprocators
- **Magnitude of the efficiency gain** varies depending on the above moderators. In standard 4–5 player, 6–30 round lab PGGs, expect moderate gains (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021).
- **Centralized (formal) punishment** often produces larger and more stable efficiency improvements than informal/peer punishment, especially in dynamic group contexts (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
- **Certain design choices can nullify or reverse efficiency gains**:
    - If punishment is costly and allows for retaliation or feuding (full ID, multi-stage), efficiency may remain unchanged or even decline, despite higher cooperation (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - If punishment is highly coordinated or frequent, direct costs may swamp gains from higher cooperation (Calabuig et al., 2024), in some adjacent games.
    - In field or naturalistic settings, enabling punishment may have little or no effect (Noussair et al., 2015).

**Dimension-by-Dimension Inference (see section 6 below):**
- Some dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, player count, number of rounds, MPCR) can be mapped directly to predicted effects, others (chat, show_punishment_id) are only sparsely or indirectly treated.
- Use empirical findings as a *conditional* modifier on the control efficiency: predicted treatment efficiency = control efficiency + (increment predicted from punishment effect as per game design and context), with design dimension details (especially punishment tech, ID and retaliation structure, network topology, group openness, and culture) crucial for calibration.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count**: Varies from 2 to 16 in studies; most standard PGGs use 4–5.
- **num_rounds**: Common values are 6, 10, 30, with repeated game structure a key moderator.
- **chat**: Most PGGs in evidence base do not allow chat; explicit tests for communication effects are rare.
- **all_or_nothing**: Both continuous and discrete/all-or-nothing contributions are studied.
- **default_contrib**: Contribution framing (opt-in, opt-out) directly tested in a few papers (Pancotto et al., 2023).
- **mpcr**: A major design variable; efficiency and treatment effects are studied at MPCRs 0.3–0.75.
- **punishment_cost** and **punishment_tech**: Fee-to-fine ratios, number of punishment stages, and punishment type (peer vs. centralized) are empirically varied and shown as primary moderators.
- **reward_exists**, **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**: Directly manipulated in several experiments, typically showing reward is less robust than punishment for efficiency gains and interacts complexly with punishment.
- **show_n_rounds**: Nearly all PGGs use known horizon; few studies test horizon salience directly.
- **show_other_summaries**: Some experiments show peer contributions/earnings, others do not; effect is rarely isolated, but feedback can undermine efficiency in some contexts (Becchetti et al., 2015).
- **show_punishment_id**: Identity revelation and punishment-stage structure are critical moderators (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

**Indirectly or Sparsely Informed Dimensions:**
- **chat**: Few studies enable chat or systematically vary its presence.
- **default_contrib**: Framing is manipulated in select papers.
- **show_n_rounds**, **show_other_summaries**, **show_punishment_id**: Sometimes manipulated, but often bundled with other dimensions.
- **Reward-related variables**: More evidence for the impact of reward as an alternative or in conjunction with punishment, but less consensus on their interaction.

**Missing or Only Contextually Discussed:**
- Several papers provide only a general discussion or theoretical consideration of other dimensions (e.g., reputation, social capital, tradition) with minimal experimental manipulation.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Predominance of Laboratory Settings:** Most evidence is from artificial, tightly controlled lab games with typical undergraduate samples, which may not generalize to real-world or field environments; several field/ethnographic papers find weaker or null effects.
- **Heterogeneity of Mechanisms:** Many adjacent or variant games (bargaining, trust, coordination, CPRG) are structurally distinct from PGGs, and results may not transfer directly.
- **Sparse Direct Efficiency Reporting:** Many behavioral studies focus on contributions or norm compliance, not net group efficiency, and must be extrapolated.
- **Moderators Not Fully Systematized:** While key design features (punishment structure, ID, retaliation, network density) strongly moderate effects, not all are systematically varied or reported across studies; interaction effects are often unclear.
- **Contextual Moderators Understudied:** Cultural, societal, and group composition moderators (e.g., prevalence of norm-keepers vs. reciprocators) are identified but poorly operationalized for prediction.
- **Limited Coverage of Dimension Space:** Some prediction dimensions (e.g., chat, opt-in framing, reward technology) are underrepresented or confounded with others, making isolated inference difficult.
- **Potential Publication Bias:** Positive (efficiency-increasing) effects of punishment are well-represented; negative, null, or context-dependent results (especially from field, naturalistic, or ethnographic research) are sparser.
- **Ambiguity and Disagreement:** Substantial disagreement remains, especially in adjacent games: punishment can both increase or reduce efficiency depending on cost structure, observability, group structure, and usage coordination.

---

**In sum:** The corpus contains robust, nuanced evidence supporting the general expectation that enabling punishment in PGGs increases efficiency under certain common lab-game designs and parameters, but with critically important caveats tied to punishment cost, institutional structure, possibility of retaliation, group openness, and group composition. Where punishment structures differ (e.g., allow retaliation, are highly costly, or are implemented in field or non-PGG games), efficiency gains may be negated or even reversed. Prediction of treatment efficiency relative to control should therefore *always* be conditioned on these factors. Behavioral outcomes (contributions, punishment rates, cooperation) are not reliable proxies for efficiency unless net payoff is reported or can be inferred under the design in question.
