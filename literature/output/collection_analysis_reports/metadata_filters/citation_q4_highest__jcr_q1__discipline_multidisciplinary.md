# 1) Evidence Base

**Composition and Breadth:**  
This paper set is unusually broad and deep for the prediction task: it consists of 155 papers, combining a large body of **empirical lab and field experiments**, as well as **theoretical and simulation studies**. There is a strong empirical core focused on standard (linear) Public Goods Games (PGG) and closely related repeated or one-shot group social dilemmas, with a substantial number of theory papers modeling wider variants and mechanism designs (e.g., punishment, reward, reputation, institution choice, partner selection).

**Empirical vs. Theoretical Coverage:**  
- **Empirical studies** are the clear majority and directly test punishment effects under varied PGG-like conditions. Lab experiments with control vs. punishment treatments are frequently used to report efficiency and payoff-based outcomes.
- **Theoretical papers** provide mechanism explanations, predictions for parameter regimes, and treatment of institutional design, often supporting or challenging empirical findings.
- Some chosen papers are adjacent to PGGs (e.g., dyadic games, trust/ultimatum/prisoner’s dilemma games), or focus on behavioral outcomes rather than group payoff/efficiency.

**Relevance for Prediction:**  
Taken together, the set provides a strong foundation for predicting the efficiency impact of peer punishment in public-goods settings, with dense evidence directly relevant to efficiency as a function of game design and control efficiency.

# 2) Task Relevance

Synthesized relevance, using the required categorical labels:

**a) pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact:** Many empirical and theoretical studies are set in standard linear PGGs or direct extensions (e.g., Rand et al., 2009; Gürerk et al., 2006; Gächter et al., 2017; Fehr & Gächter, 2002).
- **Close:** Several studies expand to collective-risk, maintenance/provision dilemmas, spatial and reputation-augmented PGGs. Some studies are threshold games, multigenerational, or continuous partner choice games.
- **Adjacent/Weak:** A minority use dyadic PD/trust/ultimatum games, evolutionary agent models, or field settings that are not strict PGGs.

**b) punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact:** Many studies toggle peer, pool/institutional, or hybrid punishment mechanisms, with clear ‘punishment on/off’ experimental contrast.
- **Close:** Reward, exclusion, social/reputation-based sanctions, and ostracism mechanisms are sometimes tested or modeled alongside or instead of pure punishment.
- **Adjacent/Weak:** A few focus on the correlates or motives of punishment (not its effects), or on related norm enforcement.

**c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact:** A substantial subset report efficiency as group payoff relative to maximum possible payoff, total surplus, or group welfare—often the primary outcome.
- **Close/Adjacent:** A smaller group uses contribution rates or group success as a proximate marker; some provide individual payoffs, not normalized to efficiency.
- **Weak/None:** Numerous studies focus on cooperation, norm compliance, or punishment behaviors, not efficiency.

**Summary:**  
Despite heterogeneity in approaches, the literature base is at least ‘close’ in all three dimensions for a sizeable segment, with a core of **exact-exact-exact** studies forming a strong evidential foundation for the efficiency-prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (directly compatible with prediction):**
- **Group efficiency (payoff relative to cooperative optimum), total group payoff/welfare, or surplus:** Frequently primary outcomes (Rand et al., 2009; Gürerk et al., 2006; Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gächter et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016).
- **Average individual earnings or group earnings:** Reported as direct measures of efficiency.
- **Institutional cost per unit cooperation:** In some institutional models (Duong & Han, 2021; Chen et al., 2015).
- **Group achievement (fraction of groups reaching a payoff target):** Used as a proxy for efficiency in collective-risk models.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (distinguished by instructions):**
- **Contribution/cooperation rate/persistence:** Often measured, sometimes erroneously equated with efficiency (explicitly cautioned against in some reviews).
- **Punishment frequency and targeting, antisocial punishment, second-order free riding, norm compliance, ostracism rates, expectation and preference measures.**
- **Motivational and emotional correlates (e.g., norm salience, perception of fairness, neural activations).**

**Linkage between behavior and efficiency:**  
Several papers explicitly argue that increased cooperation does **not always** translate to higher efficiency, particularly when punishment is costly or misapplied (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Barrett, 2016; Herrmann et al., 2008).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the strongest and most relevant studies:

**a) Effect Direction and Robustness**
- **Standard PGGs, no complex institutional features:** The introduction of peer punishment reliably increases efficiency relative to no-punishment controls, provided antisocial punishment and counter-punishment are rare (Gürerk et al., 2006; Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gächter et al., 2017; Andrighetto et al., 2013).
- **Magnitude of effect:** Punishment often boosts efficiency from decaying/low control values up to 80–95% of the maximum possible, particularly in repeated, small-group, non-anonymous settings without strong antisocial punishment or corruption, and where initial cooperation is not too low.
- **Reward options:** When reward is possible, it is often equally or more efficient than punishment in maintaining cooperation (Rand et al., 2009; Yang et al., 2018).

**b) Moderators and Mechanism Effects**
- **Cost/effectiveness of punishment:** The efficiency impact of punishment depends critically on the cost to the punisher and the punishment magnitude (e.g., 1:3 cost-impact is common). Ineffective or excessively costly punishment may reduce or fail to improve efficiency (Barrett, 2016; Wu et al., 2016).
- **Institutional context (peer vs. institutional punishment):** Both can increase efficiency, but institutional designs (pool punishment, second-order punishment) can be more stable—though sometimes less efficient if costs are high (Hilbe et al., 2014; Sigmund et al., 2010).
- **Reputation, information, and communication:** The positive efficiency effect is amplified when reputation mechanisms, norm-signaling, or communication channels are available (Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006; Andrighetto et al., 2013; Sigmund et al., 2001). Anonymous and uninformative environments see smaller efficiency gains.
- **Antisocial punishment/cultural context:** In some societies or subgroups with high antisocial punishment, the positive effect of enabling punishment can be erased or reversed (Herrmann et al., 2008; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
- **Corruption/bribery and leader incentives:** Institutional punishment can be undermined if corruption is possible, with stronger punishment backfiring (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
- **Group size, rounds, and dynamic features:** Larger groups, longer games, and more complex (e.g., spatial, dynamic) environments can alter the effect, with evidence that voluntary participation, centralization of power, or dynamic partner selection moderate efficiency outcomes (Gross et al., 2016; Hauert et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2012).
- **Threshold games/collective-risk:** For threshold-type group dilemmas, punishment increases the probability of success but doesn't always raise average efficiency if punishment costs are high (Lohse & Waichman, 2020).

**c) Limitations and Null/Negative Effects**
- **When punishment is ineffective or antisocial:** In several studies, punishment fails to increase and sometimes decreases efficiency, either because costs outweigh the cooperative gains (Wu et al., 2016; Janssen et al., 2010; Herrmann et al., 2008), punishment is mutualized among groups with different strategies (Gross & De Dreu, 2019), or cultural context produces counter-punishment (Wu et al., 2009; Dreber et al., 2008).
- **Punishment without other key mechanisms (e.g., reputation):** Without reputation or communication, the effect is weaker or non-existent (Sigmund et al., 2001).
- **Punishment’s effect can be null in dynamic, context-rich or networked settings, or when other mechanisms (partner choice, exclusion) dominate (Liu et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2012).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should this literature inform prediction of efficiency with punishment enabled (given game dimensions and control efficiency)?**

- **Baseline expectation:** Enabling peer punishment in a standard, repeated PGG with small groups, moderate punishment cost (e.g., 1:3 ratio), and no major cultural or antisocial confounds can be expected to increase group efficiency substantially, often raising it closer to the social optimum compared to control (without punishment) (Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gächter et al., 2017; Gürerk et al., 2006).
    - *Quantitatively*, control efficiency may be in the 40–60% range; with punishment, efficiency can rise into the 80–95% range, but this is modulated by parameters and context.
- **Critical moderators for prediction:**
    - **Punishment cost and impact** are primary—high costs or low impact reduce or eliminate the efficiency gain (Barrett, 2016; Rand et al., 2009).
    - **Presence of reward:** If both reward and punishment are enabled, reward usually gives a stronger efficiency boost (Rand et al., 2009; Yang et al., 2018).
    - **Reputation, communication, or centralization mechanisms** further increase the efficiency yield of punishment (Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006; Gross et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2013).
    - **Cultural context and group composition** must be considered if antisocial punishment or corruption is plausible (Herrmann et al., 2008; Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
    - **Game structure:** In one-shot, small-group, or threshold/collective-risk settings, punishment's effect can be smaller, null, or even negative if punishment costs are high or targeting is poor (Wu et al., 2016; Lohse & Waichman, 2020).
- **Control efficiency as a moderator:** Higher control efficiency (from high MPCR, default contributions, etc.) can shrink the marginal gain from punishment, sometimes making punishment less efficient on net, especially if baseline cooperation is already high (Duong & Han, 2021).
- **Indirectly,** design features that enable centralization of punishment power, voluntary participation, or norm-signaling (e.g., chat, visibility of actions, or explicit norm messaging) are expected to increase the efficiency benefit of punishment (Gross et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2013).
- **If relevant dimensions are missing or ambiguous (e.g., unknown cultural context, unclear punishment cost/tech),** prediction intervals should widen to reflect the literature’s evidence for both positive and negative or null effects.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- **player_count:** Effects of group size on punishment’s efficiency impact are frequently tested and theorized (Gürerk et al., 2006; Gächter et al., 2017; Hauert et al., 2002).
- **num_rounds:** Many studies are repeated games; number of rounds influences both baseline decay and the benefit of punishment.
- **all_or_nothing, mpcr:** Core design features, with experimental manipulations showing that higher MPCRs increase baseline efficiency and can render punishment less necessary (Kümmerli et al., 2010; Barrett, 2016).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Crucial; cost/impact ratio is a dominant moderator in most experiments and models.
- **chat, show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds:** Communication and information visibility are shown to increase the positive effect of punishment via norm salience and coordination.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Reward is sometimes manipulated alongside punishment (Rand et al., 2009; Yang et al., 2018; Duong & Han, 2021).
- **show_punishment_id:** Non-anonymity is found to enhance effectiveness by harnessing reputation effects (Sigmund et al., 2001; Hilbe & Traulsen, 2012).
- **default_contrib:** Framing alters control efficiency and the ease with which punishment deters free riding (Gross et al., 2016).

**Indirectly Informed or Only Contextually Discussed:**
- **punishment_tech:** Some studies provide nuanced theory only (e.g., implicit vs. explicit punishment effectiveness, technological implementation).
- **reward_cost, reward_tech:** Not always systematically varied, but important where discussed.

**Effectively Missing or Sparse:**
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Some information is present but often as a background feature, not systematically investigated.
- **default_contrib:** Typically default to opt-in; variations are rare.
- **Group composition (diversity, prior experience) and cultural context:** Not systematically incorporated but shown to be crucial in multi-country or field studies.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavioral vs. payoff measures:** Many studies report contribution rates, norm compliance, or punishment frequency, not efficiency/payoff. Prediction models must not conflate these outcomes.
- **Cultural/contextual moderators underreported:** The prevalence of antisocial punishment, corruption, and norms varies and is often a stronger moderator than game structure; these factors are rarely available in advance for prediction (Herrmann et al., 2008; Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
- **Complex or dynamic game variants:** Dynamic, spatial, threshold, or networked environments can generate exceptions or even negative efficiency effects from punishment (Janssen et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2016; Gross & De Dreu, 2019).
- **Fine-grained design features may not generalize:** Some dimensions (e.g., chat, identity disclosure) are poorly explored across enough parameter space to support fine-tuned predictions.
- **Sparsity for novel combinations:** Multi-dimensional treatments (e.g., punishment plus reward, centralization plus chat) are not systematically varied; interactions may be under-represented.
- **Assumptions about implementation errors or real-world complexity:** Most lab studies assume reliable implementation; field and more ecological studies suggest more fragile or highly moderated real-life effects.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature robustly supports the expectation that enabling peer punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment control in standard, small-group, repeated PGGs—*providing* that punishment is not so costly as to outweigh cooperative gains, and that the social context limits antisocial punishment and corruption. Game design dimensions—especially punishment cost/tech, player count, rewards, reputation mechanisms, and communication—moderate the effect, but the exact quantitative outcome depends heavily on contextual factors and is not always positive. For modelers, control efficiency, punishment cost/effectiveness, and contextual features should jointly anchor predictions, but uncertainty is warranted when parameters or the social environment are outside the well-mapped regions of past research.
