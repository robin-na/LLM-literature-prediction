# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is both broad and rich, comprising 143 papers with a strong predominance of empirical, laboratory-based experimental studies, with some field experiments and observational studies. The set is highly focused on public goods games (PGGs) and close variants. A significant subset of the literature targets the core prediction task: the effect of enabling peer or institutional punishment on group efficiency in controlled, payoff-transparent environments. The evidence base includes numerous canonical multi-round linear PGGs, as well as collective-risk and threshold variants, and games with leadership/punishment institutions and additional design features (e.g., monitoring, transparency, team decision rules).

There is a moderate balance between studies that report direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes and those that focus on behavioral markers of cooperation (contribution rates, punishment frequencies) or institutional choice, psychological mechanisms, or reputational outcomes. A substantial portion of the literature (including major meta-analyses) also distinguishes between payoff-based outcomes and behavioral or attitudinal outcomes, though only a subset reports efficiency in the precise form required for prediction.

Most of the highest-relevance empirical evidence is based on randomized lab experiments with full digital records, allowing close mapping from design dimensions to observed outcomes. There are also synthetic/meta-analytic papers that aggregate across studies.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact:** The majority of papers use repeated linear public goods games or direct variants, matching the target environment exactly (e.g., Jiang & Wang, 2024; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Salahshour et al., 2022).
- **close:** A large subset uses close variants—collective-risk threshold games, CPR extraction games, or trust games with public-good-like features.
- **adjacent/weak/none:** Some adjacent evidence comes from prisoner's dilemma, dictator, or trust games, which are less directly relevant.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact:** Many studies examine institutionally enabled, peer, or leader punishment (exact match).
- **close:** Papers on collective punishment, exogenous (automatic) punishment, or multi-level punishments are close but not always peer-based.
- **adjacent/weak:** Some studies report reward only, norm/nudge mechanisms, or non-strategic forms of punishment, lessening direct relevance.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact/close:** A significant portion report group efficiency (total payoff/maximum), group income, welfare, or surplus (Jiang & Wang, 2024; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Nhim et al., 2023; Eichenseer, 2023). Some only report payoff in adjacent forms (e.g., group welfare in threshold games).
- **adjacent/weak/none:** Many papers focus on behavioral metrics, norm compliance, or mechanism variables without direct payoff analysis. For some, payoff data exist but are interpreted via intermediary variables like PLA (probability of loss avoidance), not direct surplus.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes:**
- **Directly reported:** Group efficiency (total/group payoff vs. optimal); net profits/earnings; welfare/surplus; final group income.
- **Closely related:** Probability of reaching a collective target (threshold games), average earnings per round, surplus created.
- **Sometimes reported only as differences/relative effects** (e.g., efficiency gain from adding punishment relative to control).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- Contribution rate/cooperation rate
- Punishment frequency and magnitude; antisocial vs. prosocial punishment
- Institutional choices (voting for punishment/reward institutions)
- Communication frequency; norm compliance signals
- Punishment or cooperation rates by subgroup, identity, or treatment
- Normative expectations, trust, emotions (anger, guilt)
- Choice of institution/commitment/entry

**Distinction:**  
While high contributions and low punishment costs typically map to higher efficiency, the relationship is not always linear—especially when costly or antisocial punishment is common, or when design elements (e.g., punishment noise, subgroup structure) interfere.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Punishment typically increases efficiency, but with major contingencies:**
- **Canonical PGGs with standard peer or institutional punishment:** Enabling punishment often increases efficiency over control, as sustained cooperation offsets punishment costs (Jiang & Wang, 2024; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Eichenseer, 2023; Kamei et al., 2023).
- **Conditional and context effects:**
    - **Group structure:** Efficiency gains may be lost or reversed with group heterogeneity, pluriformity, or prior conflict, due to discriminatory or maladaptive punishment (Molenmaker et al., 2023; Gross et al., 2022).
    - **Monitoring and transparency:** Efficient punishment requires accurate individual-level monitoring and transparent endowments. Punishment fails—or is counterproductive—when actions or wealth are opaque (Eisenkopf & Walter, 2022; Chen et al., 2025).
    - **Noise and antisocial punishment:** Stochastic punishment, high punishment noise, or frequent antisocial punishment sharply reduces or reverses efficiency gains (Salahshour et al., 2022; Angelsen & Naime, 2024).
    - **Punishment cost/efficiency:** Low-cost punishment may encourage wasteful or antisocial punishment, undermining payoffs (Chen et al., 2025; Nhim et al., 2023). Appropriately costly punishment reduces antisocial punishment and improves efficiency.
    - **Institutional framing and leadership:** Centralized or prosocial leaders, especially under responsibility framing and via election, markedly amplify efficiency gains. Team decision rules with unanimity reduce destructive punishment and increase efficiency (Jiang & Wang, 2024; Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Eichenseer, 2023).
    - **Group experience/order:** Effectiveness of punishment in multilevel games is contingent on prior experience with successful norm coordination (Otten et al., 2024).
    - **Type of punishment:** Collective and pre-committed punishment (with opt-in) can outperform peer-to-peer punishment, especially under noisy or imperfect monitoring (Duell et al., 2024).
    - **Competitive/attack forms:** When punishment is structured as costly competition/attack, or when the institutional context is adversarial, enabling punishment can **reduce** efficiency (Romano et al., 2024; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022).
    - **Reward interactions:** Adding reward mechanisms can interact with punishment in complex ways; sometimes combined sanctioning (reward and punishment) is necessary for maximal gains (Makovi et al., 2025).
    - **Minimum contribution/tax regimes:** Minimum contribution (tax) mechanisms paired with punishment tend to yield greater net efficiency than strict, high-cost punishment regimes (Nhim et al., 2023).

**Diminishing or negative effects:**
- Settings with high punishment costs, weak enforcement, punitive inefficiency, or dominant antisocial punishment can yield no net efficiency gain or even efficiency losses (Nhim et al., 2023; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022; Grimalda et al., 2022; Romano et al., 2024).
- Voluntary, opt-in punishment and/or avoidance of costly institutions can limit the realized efficiency improvement (Del Ponte et al., 2025).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Baseline (control) efficiency is informative, but not sufficient:**  
  Studies show that control efficiency (no-punishment) is a necessary input for prediction, but the efficiency boost from introducing punishment is **strongly moderated** by game design features—including group composition, punishment cost and technology, monitoring transparency, institutional structure, norm clarity, and presence of reward.
- **Enabling punishment generally increases efficiency,** especially in canonical repeated linear PGGs with moderate group size, clear individual monitoring, and moderate MPCR. Expect substantial gains if:
    - Punishment is costly enough to deter antisocial use but not so costly as to erode gains from higher contributions.
    - Monitoring and feedback are individualized and transparent.
    - Institutional context supports norm coordination (leaders can signal or align group goals, or group discussion/facilitation is present).
    - Group is homogeneous or social context enables effective norm enforcement.
    - Antisocial punishment is rare or design mitigates its frequency.
    - Punishment is deterministic; stochastic/noisy punishment reduces effectiveness.
    - In collective-risk games, efficiency gains depend on high punishment credibility and risk.
- **Punishment may fail or backfire** (producing no efficiency gain or even net loss) when:
    - Group is heterogeneous or there is pre-existing conflict or identity salience.
    - Punishment is low-cost, poorly targeted, or allows for antisocial use.
    - Punishment technology is noisy or poorly enforced.
    - Institutional framing cues status, competition, or attack, rather than norm enforcement.
    - Transparency about contributions or endowments is lacking.
    - Punishment costs are high enough to outweigh cooperation gains.
- **Specific meta-analytic effect sizes:**
    - Addition of peer punishment: increases efficiency by ~0.47 (on a 0-1 scale, Eichenseer, 2023).
    - Centralized/leader punishment: increases efficiency by ~0.26.
    - Effect sizes may need to be adjusted downward in real-world field settings or with larger or highly diverse groups.
- For games with multiple forms of punishment/reward or unique institutional designs, interpret findings in context; transfers from one design to another should be made cautiously.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**
- *player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, show_other_summaries, chat, reward_exists*
    - These are core dimensions in most experimental PGG designs and are routinely specified and analyzed as moderators of treatment effects (e.g., group size, round number, marginal return, cost-impact ratio of punishment, presence/absence of communication and feedback, ability to reward as well as punish).

**Indirectly informed/contextually discussed:**
- *default_contrib*, *show_n_rounds*, *show_punishment_id*
    - Sometimes specified/controlled for, less often experimentally varied; some studies discuss the importance of default framing or information on round horizons or punishment identity but rarely as primary treatment variables.

**Sparse/missing:**
- *reward_cost, reward_tech*
    - Detailed analyses of reward mechanisms exist, but are less thoroughly linked to efficiency effects compared to punishment.
- *punishment_exists* (as a dichotomous variable): Virtually every study involves an explicit punishment/no-punishment comparison, but the nuanced design features (type, cost, tech) are more often analyzed as moderators.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Heterogeneity in reporting:** Not all studies report efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, even when contribution or cooperation rates are high. This can obscure cases where costly punishment erodes efficiency despite raising contributions.
- **Design constraints:** Most studies keep core parameters (e.g., punishment cost, MPCR, group size) fixed; few systematically vary multiple dimensions to map generalizable effects.
- **External validity:** Lab-based PGGs, while tightly controlled, may not transfer directly to field or large-group, high-heterogeneity environments. Complex real-world cooperation contexts are underrepresented.
- **Interaction effects:** Relatively few studies explore interactions among game design dimensions (e.g., cost × transparency × group structure), making prediction in novel or hybrid designs less certain.
- **Nonlinear effects and edge cases:** Efficiency effects can reverse when antisocial punishment is common, punishment profit motives are introduced, or monitoring is noisy. Detailed prediction may require careful attention to underlying mechanisms (prosocal vs. antisocial use, credibility, enforcement quality).
- **Treatment definition ambiguity:** Some punishment mechanisms (taxes, collective fines) have different efficiency effects than peer-initiated or leader-driven punishment. Not all "punishment" treatments are equivalent for downstream prediction.
- **Sparse coverage:** Specific features (e.g., *show_punishment_id*, *reward_tech*) are rarely varied or isolated, limiting inference about their effect.
- **Reporting gaps:** Some otherwise high-relevance studies do not report control group efficiency, only relative improvement, complicating calibration.

In summary, the literature provides robust empirical and meta-analytic support for a **positive, but conditional** effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, tightly moderated by game design features, group composition, punishment cost/tech, monitoring clarity, and cultural or institutional context. Efforts to predict treatment efficiency from design dimensions plus baseline control efficiency should attend closely to these moderating factors and remain cautious in generalizing beyond canonical repeated linear PGGs with clear, prosocially motivated punishment.
