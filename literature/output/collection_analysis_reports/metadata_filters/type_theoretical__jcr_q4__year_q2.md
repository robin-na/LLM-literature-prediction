# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set consists entirely of theory papers (eight in total), with no direct empirical or experimental studies included. The coverage is relatively broad in its theoretical reach, incorporating game theory models ranging from population ecology and marketing cooperatives to general Bayesian games, as well as canonical and variant public goods game (PGG) frameworks. Several papers (e.g., Thöni, 2014; Spitzer, 2016) ground their theory in insights from prior empirical and experimental PGG research but do not themselves report new empirical outcomes or treatment effects on efficiency. The majority of the papers focus on formal models of mechanisms or equilibria relevant to punishment and cooperation, with only limited direct attention to quantitative efficiency outcomes in environments closely matching downstream prediction tasks for public goods games with peer punishment.

# 2) Task Relevance

For the three target-relevance dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant:**
    - **exact:** Three papers (Thöni, 2014; Spitzer, 2016; Lee & Iwasa, 2014) address public goods games or direct analogues.
    - **close/adjacent:** Most other papers model structurally similar or nearby social dilemmas (Voelkl, 2015; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Forges et al., 2016; Beraldo & Sugden, 2016; Golman, 2016).
    - **none:** None of the included papers are wholly unrelated.

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**
    - **exact:** Five papers discuss punishment mechanisms as a central component (Thöni, 2014; Spitzer, 2016; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Forges et al., 2016).
    - **adjacent:** One paper frames punishment in the context of signaling and institutional design but is not directly about peer punishment in PGGs (Golman, 2016).
    - **none:** One paper does not address punishment at all (Beraldo & Sugden, 2016).

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
    - **exact/close:** Four papers directly analyze or are highly relevant to efficiency or welfare payoffs (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015; Forges et al., 2016; Beraldo & Sugden, 2016).
    - **adjacent:** Others discuss impact on behavior, cooperation, or model efficiency as a constraint or boundary but do not calculate or forecast actual efficiency outcomes (Thöni, 2014; Spitzer, 2016; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Golman, 2016).
    - **none:** N/A (all papers at least indirectly engage with group outcomes as relevant for efficiency).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

The set is overwhelmingly focused on **theoretical outcomes**. No new empirical data is reported.

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (efficiency, welfare, group payoff):
    - Explicitly modeled or discussed in Lee & Iwasa (2014), Voelkl (2015), Forges et al. (2016), Beraldo & Sugden (2016).
    - Addressed as implications or constraints in Spitzer (2016), Blonski & Spagnolo (2015), and partially in Thöni (2014), but without direct values or predictions.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (contributions, cooperation rates, punishment frequency, voting, signaling):
    - Theoretical analyses are presented in Thöni (2014), Spitzer (2016), Blonski & Spagnolo (2015), and Golman (2016).
    - Several papers (Thöni, 2014; Golman, 2016) discuss mechanisms by which antisocial punishment or communication affects cooperation and the use of punishment, though not efficiency per se.

- **Efficiency** (as defined as actual ratio of achieved to possible payoffs) is addressed in the abstract or as an intended result in four papers, but with only qualitative or theoretical, not quantitative, conclusions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the set, the following guidance emerges for the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments:

- **Theoretical consensus** exists across several papers (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015; Forges et al., 2016) that introducing or strengthening (peer) punishment can, under suitable conditions, stabilize higher cooperation and increase group efficiency compared to control games without punishment.
    - Key moderating factors include the cost and magnitude of punishment (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015); punishment must be large/effective enough to deter defection but not so harsh as to be counterproductive.
    - **Graduated punishment schemes**, which scale with the harm caused, have theoretical efficiency advantages over severe/blunt punishment (Lee & Iwasa, 2014).

- **Structural features and boundary conditions**:
    - The credibility and uniform availability of punishment is necessary for sustaining efficient or welfare-maximizing outcomes, particularly in games with incomplete information (Forges et al., 2016).
    - The structure of repeated interaction, patience/discoutnt factor, and strategic risk (Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015) all affect whether harsh/forgiving punishment is required to sustain efficient cooperation.

- **Limitations and risks**:
    - The presence of antisocial punishment (punishment of cooperators or non-defectors), as discussed by Thöni (2014), is theoretically and empirically recognized as detrimental to efficiency and can potentially reverse the hypothesized positive effects of punishment on group welfare.
    - Motives for antisocial punishment may not be reducible to inequality aversion and can be context-dependent or related to revenge/harm motives.

- **Institutional and contextual moderators**:
    - The details of institutional design—such as whether chat is enabled, framing of contributions, visibility of actions or outcomes, and complexity of the mechanism—are acknowledged as potentially important moderators (Spitzer, 2016; Golman, 2016), but are not quantified or modeled with respect to efficiency outcomes.
    - Opportunities for communication or signals (Golman, 2016) may substitute for direct punishment, indirectly mitigating its necessity or effectiveness.

- **Empirical generalizability is not established** in this set, as all results are theory-driven, and quantitative shifts in efficiency are unreported. All effect sizes and predictions are qualitative or parametric in nature.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based on this literature, the following implications are relevant for predicting average efficiency when peer punishment is enabled, given game design and control efficiency:

- **Directional Prediction:** Theory robustly supports the expectation that enabling peer punishment, when it is sufficiently effective but not excessively harsh, will generally raise group efficiency relative to control in PGG-like social dilemmas—provided antisocial punishment is limited (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015; Forges et al., 2016).
- **Qualifications:**
    - The **magnitude of efficiency gain** is not provided; actual effect sizes must be extrapolated from elsewhere.
    - Contexts with high likelihood or frequency of antisocial punishment (e.g., certain subject pools or design features) may see null or negative effects on efficiency (Thöni, 2014).
    - The structure and severity of the punishment mechanism, the possibility of graduated versus blunt punishments, and the balance of punishment magnitude relative to benefits of defection are critical (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).
- **Use of control efficiency:** No paper directly links control efficiency to treatment efficiency post-punishment in a predictive model, but theory suggests that where control efficiency is already high (e.g., due to high MPCR), the *incremental* efficiency gain from punishment may be lower.
- **Applicability of design dimensions:** Theoretical findings call attention to punishment cost and magnitude, player count, number of rounds, strategic risk (discount/patience), and communication, but precise mapping to the 14-dimension space is incomplete.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed design dimensions:**  
- **player_count:** Modeled in almost all papers (Thöni, 2014; Voelkl, 2015; Forges et al., 2016; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Beraldo & Sugden, 2016; Spitzer, 2016).
- **num_rounds:** Modeled as repeat play or stages in several papers (Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Forges et al., 2016; Spitzer, 2016; Thöni, 2014).
- **mpcr:** Explicitly acknowledged as a key incentive parameter (Thöni, 2014; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Spitzer, 2016).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Modeled in all theory papers analyzing punishment (Thöni, 2014; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).
- **all_or_nothing:** Noted in Thöni (2014), Voelkl (2015), Blonski & Spagnolo (2015), Beraldo & Sugden (2016).
- **reward_exists:** Considered as a parallel or contrast to punishment in Voelkl (2015), Golman (2016), Spitzer (2016).
- **chat:** Theory notes role of communication as a moderator (Spitzer, 2016; Golman, 2016).

**Indirectly/contextually discussed:**
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id:** Not directly modeled, but implicit in general discussions of information structure (Golman, 2016; Spitzer, 2016).
- **default_contrib:** Framing/allocation discussed in Spitzer (2016), but not explicitly analyzed for efficiency.
- **reward_cost, reward_tech:** Addressed conceptually by Golman (2016).

**Effectively missing:**
- Several information dimensions are sparsely or not at all discussed (show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id).
- No empirical or parameterized predictions incorporating the full 14-dimension design space.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical limitations:** No paper provides new or directly applicable empirical data on efficiency or group payoff shifts due to the introduction of peer punishment in PGG environments.
- **Quantification gap:** Theoretical results support *relative* (qualitative) predictions about the direction of efficiency effects but provide no estimates or functional forms for predicting treatment efficiency from game features and control efficiency.
- **Behavioral and context moderators:** The theory recognizes moderators like antisocial punishment, communication, and institutional complexity, but does not systematically map them to specific design dimensions or quantify their effects on efficiency.
- **Generalizability:** Several models apply most directly to simplified or adjacent game structures (e.g., two-person Bayesian games, marketing cooperatives, or repeated-move games) rather than laboratory PGGs with peer punishment, so mappings may be imperfect.
- **Missing dimensions:** Key potentially relevant design features (information feedback, anonymity, contribution method) are not parameterized.
- **Ambiguity and disagreement:** The body of work is ambiguous regarding circumstances where efficiency gains may be reversed (e.g., due to antisocial punishment), and does not resolve strategic boundary cases.
- **No direct modeling of control efficiency dependence:** No study provides evidence on how control condition efficiency predicts efficiency under punishment.

---

**In summary:**  
This theory-based literature converges on the directional prediction that enabling sufficiently effective, non-antisocial peer punishment in PGG-like games should increase efficiency relative to control, but the lack of empirical results, effect sizes, and precise mapping to all relevant design dimensions limits value for parameterized or highly contextualized prediction. Important contextual factors such as punishment structure, social preferences, antisocial punishment, information structure, and communication options are theoretically recognized, but not operationalized with numerical outcome data.
