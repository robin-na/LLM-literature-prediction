# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large (57 papers) and comprises both **empirical (particularly laboratory experiments)** and **theoretical/simulation studies**, with a smaller number of field experiments and review/meta-analytical pieces. The evidence base is **broad with respect to mechanisms and variations on the public goods dilemma and sanctions**—covering canonical Public Goods Games (PGGs), various coordination and trust games, institutional and peer punishment, reward, reputation, exclusion, and endogenous group formation. While many papers report **direct payoff (efficiency/welfare) outcomes**, a significant subset focuses on **non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (e.g., contribution rates, norm compliance) or theoretical equilibria. Some experiments provide **precise mappings to the 14 game design dimensions** and analyze treatments where punishment is enabled versus disabled, as needed for downstream efficiency prediction. However, substantial evidence comes from games or interventions **adjacent to, but not exactly matching, the canonical PGG with and without punishment**.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) PGG or Variant**  
- **Exact**: A core group of studies (both empirical and theoretical) directly study standard linear public goods games or very close institutional variants—these provide the strongest evidence for task-relevant predictions.  
- **Close/Adjacent**: A substantial number analyze games with punishment mechanisms in related but not identical settings (minimum effort games, CPR games, trust/ultimatum/delegation games, repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma, or threshold collective action games).  
- **Weak/None**: Some studies investigate different strategic dilemmas (e.g., resource sharing, budget reporting) or focus on non-punishment mechanisms.

**b) Punishment or Sanctions**
- **Exact**: Many studies manipulate explicit, monetary, peer, or centralized punishment mechanisms corresponding to the “punishment enabled” design dimension.
- **Close/Adjacent**: Several papers explore closely related forms (exclusion, refusal, third-party sanctions, reward-based metanorms). Others consider punishment in adjacent games or non-monetary social sanctions.
- **Weak/None**: A subset only discusses monitoring or social feedback or does not manipulate punishment.

**c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**
- **Exact/Close**: About half of the studies report direct efficiency or total payoff outcomes; some additionally report surplus, welfare, or aggregate coin generation.
- **Adjacent/Weak**: Many others focus primarily on **contribution rates, cooperation, norm compliance, or punishment use**, with efficiency only inferred (not measured). Explicit **distinction between behavioral and payoff outcomes** is frequently noted in these papers.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based Outcomes**:  
    - *Efficiency* (group payoff as a proportion of maximum possible, average total earnings, surplus, group welfare, etc.)—directly reported in many key studies (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - *Related proxies*: In adjacent games, “net profits,” “aggregate wealth,” or “social cost minimization” are used.
    - *Some studies provide only indirect indicators* or require inference from increases/decreases in group earnings.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**:  
    - *Contribution/cooperation rates* (frequently the primary outcome in lab experiments—not to be conflated with efficiency).
    - *Punishment assignment and frequency* (who punishes whom, antisocial punishment).
    - *Norm compliance, exclusion/replacement rates, reward use, honesty (budget slack)*.

- **Explicit Distinction**:  
    - Multiple papers caution that increased cooperation does **not always translate to increased efficiency** due to the costs of punishment (e.g., Zhang & Pei, 2022; Abbink et al., 2004).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Across the most relevant literature—especially empirical papers focused on (peer or centralized) punishment in PGGs—the following cross-cutting, **evidence-supported findings** emerge:

- **Typical Effect of Enabling Punishment**:  
    - *Punishment frequently increases average group efficiency* relative to a no-punishment baseline, but the magnitude of gains is highly variable and context-dependent.  
    - *Positive effects are observed when punishment deters free-riding or nudges reciprocators,* and especially when the cost-impact ratio is favorable and antisocial punishment is rare (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Deng et al., 2012; Botta et al., 2021; Bowles & Gintis, 2004).
    - *High punishment costs, multiple punishment stages, visibility of punishers, or opportunities for retaliation can offset or reverse efficiency gains*, even if cooperation/contributions rise (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Abbink et al., 2004; Kanitsar, 2021; Jaffe, 2004).
    - *Centralized punishment* (via manager or institution) tends to produce larger and more reliable efficiency gains *than informal peer punishment*, particularly in open or migration-enabled societies (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
    - *Costless or low-cost punishment* may increase efficiency substantially, whereas costly punishment often eliminates efficiency gains unless offset by large increases in cooperation (Kanitsar, 2021).

- **Mechanisms and Moderators**:
    - The *proportion of strong reciprocators* and the *absence of norm-keepers or frequent antisocial punishment* are strong predictors of whether punishment boosts efficiency (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Bowles & Gintis, 2004).
    - *Punishment technology and structure are critical*: When multiple punishment opportunities and retaliation are possible, efficiency benefits evaporate (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - *Visibility of punishment or punishment ID* can boost deterrence (sometimes enhancing efficiency) but also enables targeting and feuds (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Gueth & Otsubo, 2023).
    - *The relationship between efficiency and cooperation is not monotonic*: Efficiency gains require that increased cooperation outpaces the cost of punishment; when costs are high, even full cooperation may not result in higher efficiency (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Abbink et al., 2004).

- **Contextual Factors**:
    - *Open societies or endogenous group formation*: Punishment is more effective when group membership is endogenous (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
    - *Game design dimensions* such as player count, MPCR, punishment cost/magnitude, communication, and reward mechanisms directly moderate the effect size and even the direction (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Deng et al., 2012; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
    - *Psychological factors and norm enforcement environments* (e.g., presence of metanorms, cultural expectation) further affect outcomes (Kendal et al., 2006; Uchida et al., 2024).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the evidence:

- **Control efficiency** (no-punishment baseline payoff relative to full cooperation) provides a necessary benchmark for prediction, but it is *not* sufficient for predicting treatment efficiency: *design dimensions and contextual moderators critically shape the incremental effect of punishment* (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Kanitsar, 2021).

- **Game Design Dimensions to Incorporate**:
    - *Punishment Cost and Tech*: Efficiency gains are largest when the cost-impact ratio is favorable (low cost per unit of impact); higher costs can nullify or reverse the benefit.
    - *Player Count & Group Openness*: Larger groups may benefit more from concerted or severe punishment, and endogenous group formation increases the effect of institutional sanctions.
    - *MPCR (Multiplier/Player Count)*: Punishment interacts with marginal per capita return to shape incentive compatibility; lower MPCRs require more effective or severe punishment to achieve efficiency gains.
    - *Punishment Structure*: Multiple punishment stages, visible identities, or complex retaliation opportunities can reduce or reverse efficiency gains.
    - *Reward Exists*: The presence of complementary or competing reward mechanisms can substitute or interact with punishment.
    - *Communication/Chat*: Often omitted from direct analysis but known to modulate punishment’s necessity or effectiveness.

- **Empirical Findings for Prediction**:
    - Generally, enabling punishment increases efficiency compared to baseline, unless punishment is costly, poorly structured, or prone to antisocial use.
    - In peer punishment with standard cost-effectiveness and no complex retaliation, a *moderate efficiency gain* over control can be predicted.
    - *Centralized/institutional punishment is more reliably efficiency-increasing*, even with higher punishment cost, especially where group formation is endogenous.
    - Where game design allows for multiple punishment stages, visible IDs, or easy retaliation, *do not assume efficiency gains*—and may consider predicted efficiency to be unchanged or even reduced relative to control.

- **In cases where only behavioral (not payoff-based) outcomes are reported**, the magnitude (and even sign) of efficiency changes should be treated as informed speculation (e.g., when higher contributions are observed, but punishment is costly or frequent, efficiency gains are not guaranteed).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- **player_count**: Most experimental and theoretical papers.
- **num_rounds**: Commonly specified.
- **mpcr**: Standard and carefully manipulated in many experiments/theories.
- **punishment_cost** and **punishment_tech**: Central to both experimental and theoretical evidence.
- **reward_exists** (and sometimes *reward_cost*/ *reward_tech*): Explicitly manipulated in some studies.
- **all_or_nothing** (binary vs. continuous contributions): Studied in both laboratory and theoretical variants.
- **show_punishment_id**, **show_n_rounds**, **show_other_summaries**: Often discussed as moderators, but not universally manipulated.
- **chat**: Mentioned infrequently (few direct manipulations).
- **default_contrib**: Seldom reported or manipulated.

**Indirectly/Contextually Discussed:**
- **endogenous/exogenous group formation or migration** (related to contextual openness).
- **population and cultural context** (influences on the distribution of reciprocators, norm-keepers, or prevalence of antisocial punishment).
- **reputation and metanorms** (supporting or sustaining punishment efficacy).

**Effectively Missing:**
- Some design details such as *default_contrib*, *reward_magnitude*, or nuanced framing variables are rarely the subject of systematic, payoff-outcome–linked analysis across the literature.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Ambiguity/Disagreement Across Studies**:  
    - There is no universal causal relationship; some well-controlled empirical and theoretical papers report *neutral or negative efficiency effects for peer punishment*, especially when punishment is costly or structurally permits retaliation/feuds (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Abbink et al., 2004; Jaffe, 2004; Calabuig et al., 2024).
    - The same design can yield different results across populations or experiments due to *unmeasured group composition (e.g., strong reciprocators), cultural context, or subject pools* (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).

- **Measurement Gaps**:  
    - Many studies rely primarily on *contribution/cooperation rates* or punishment frequency, *not direct efficiency outcomes*—requiring cautious inference.
    - Few studies systematically report both control and punishment-enabled group payoffs across a full range of game design parameters.

- **Design Dimension Coverage**:  
    - *Not all of the 14 prediction dimensions are fully or jointly manipulated* in any one paper, with sparse coverage of chat, default contribution framing, and the visibility/summary dimensions.
    - *Interactivity between dimensions* (e.g., interaction of punishment cost with player count and MPCR) is suggested but not fully mapped empirically.

- **External Validity and Generalizability**:  
    - Field study evidence is rare and suggests *lab effects may not fully generalize* (e.g., peer punishment is less effective in field settings, Noussair et al., 2015).
    - Theory studies provide *necessary conditions* for efficiency gains but may not capture all real-world behavioral noise or population heterogeneity.

- **Inferences from Behavioral to Payoff Outcomes**:  
    - Increased cooperation/contribution does not always translate into higher efficiency—cost structure, frequency, and targeting of punishment are critical.
    - Studies focusing only on non-payoff outcomes (e.g., norm compliance) must not be over-interpreted as evidence for efficiency gains.

**Overall**, while the literature set provides substantial guidance for predicting payoff-based effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments, accurate prediction using design dimensions and control efficiency requires close attention to variation in **punishment structure, cost-effectiveness, group context, and game framing**; caution is warranted where direct efficiency evidence is lacking or generalizability is uncertain.
