# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set consists entirely of theoretical and conceptual papers (12 out of 13), with only one empirical observational study. Most papers build mathematical models, propose frameworks, or synthesize across domains rather than delivering laboratory or field experiments with measured payoff or efficiency outcomes. The scope is fairly broad in terms of covering mechanisms related to punishment, policing, and cooperation, drawing on diverse settings from evolutionary biology to human social systems. However, it is relatively narrow for the specific downstream prediction task: very few papers report direct empirical comparisons between control (no-punishment) and treatment (punishment-enabled) PGGs, or even directly compute group efficiency as a primary outcome.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**  
- **Exact:** Several theory papers directly model n-person public goods games or n-person generalizations of the prisoner's dilemma (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; Broom et al., 2015; POLLOCK, 1988; Nhim et al., 2019).  
- **Close/Adjacent:** Others apply their frameworks to public goods–like social dilemmas, CPR games, or repeated dyadic interactions (Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011; Bshary & Bronstein, 2011; Boehm, 2014; Sasaki et al., 2016; Aguilar & Akçay, 2018), but not always with the same structure or payoff calculus as a standard PGG.

**punishment_or_sanctions**  
- **Exact/Close:** Most papers explicitly address punishment (peer or pool, individual or institutional) as a mechanism for stabilizing cooperation (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; POLLOCK, 1988; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011; Nhim et al., 2019; Sasaki et al., 2016). Some, however, focus only on reward or do not include any punishment/sanctioning mechanism (Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; Broom et al., 2015).
- **Adjacent/Weak:** A subset discusses punishment mechanistically, neurobiologically, or as a general evolutionary force, but does not directly model or measure it in PGG-like games (Du & Chang, 2015; Singh & Boomsma, 2015; Boehm, 2014; Raihani, 2014).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**  
- **Exact:** A few theory papers calculate group payoff, total efficiency, or directly frame results as implications for efficiency (POLLOCK, 1988; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011; Sasaki et al., 2016).
- **Close/Adjacent:** More often, the primary outcome is the rate of cooperation or the fixation of cooperation, with only an inferential link to efficiency (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Nhim et al., 2019; Broom et al., 2015).
- **Weak/None:** The empirical and some conceptual papers discuss behavioral outcomes or norm compliance, not payoff (Raihani, 2014; Boehm, 2014).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (group efficiency, total payoff, welfare): Explicitly modeled in a minority of theory papers (POLLOCK, 1988; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011; Sasaki et al., 2016). Some others (Baker & Rachlin, 2002) use payoff-based reasoning but do not independently report efficiency ratios.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (contribution rate, norm compliance, fixation probability, punishment frequency): Predominate in the rest of the paper set: these include models of cooperation rates without mapping directly to group payoff, qualitative and evolutionary analyses of norm compliance, and discussion of punishment's presence or psychological drivers (Nhim et al., 2019; Broom et al., 2015; Boehm, 2014; Du & Chang, 2015; Singh & Boomsma, 2015; Raihani, 2014).
- **Empirical data:** Only one observational paper (Raihani, 2014), focusing on donation concealment behavior—not efficiency or payoff.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment typically raises cooperation rates;** by implication, this often leads to efficiency gains, but this link is mostly theoretical and not always quantified (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; POLLOCK, 1988; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011; Sasaki et al., 2016).
- **Magnitude and mechanism matter:**
    - **Effectiveness of punishment and its cost structure** (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011): Punishment is only efficiency-improving when costs are not too high relative to effectiveness, and/or when power asymmetries protect punishers from retaliation.
    - **Population/game structure** (POLLOCK, 1988): Punishment works to sustain cooperation and efficiency only in structured/multigroup populations, and fails in single, well-mixed populations.
    - **Reward mechanisms** (Forsyth & Hauert, 2011): While peer reward can raise efficiency over defection-only outcomes, it is generally less effective than punishment and unstable in the long run due to second-order free-riding.
    - **Institutional or pool punishment** (Sasaki et al., 2016): Institutional mechanisms can overcome second-order free rider problems and achieve high efficiency if well designed.
    - **Resource scarcity and variability** (Nhim et al., 2019): Under harsh, unequal, or uncertain resource environments, punishment may fail to stabilize cooperation or raise efficiency, with the system abruptly collapsing to defection.
- **Payoff versus behavioral outcomes:** Most findings about increased "cooperation" or "contribution" are not separated from the direct cost of punishment, and thus do not guarantee net efficiency gains (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Nhim et al., 2019).
- **Ambiguities/Disagreement:** Some work suggests that if punishment is too costly or ineffectively targeted, it can decrease total efficiency (Sasaki et al., 2016; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011); full benefit is contingent on balance of punishment cost and effect.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **When predicting average efficiency with peer punishment enabled (given control efficiency):**
    - Theoretical models **strongly support** the expectation of increased efficiency if punishment is effective (deductions from defectors are sizable and costs to punishers are not excessive), and there are mechanisms to avoid excessive punishment or anti-social use (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; POLLOCK, 1988; Sasaki et al., 2016; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011).
    - The effect is **strongly moderated by game structure**: The more structured or partitioned the population (into groups, separate rounds, limited overlap), the greater the stabilizing effect of punishment on efficiency (POLLOCK, 1988).
    - **Punishment cost, effectiveness, and the frequency of punishers** are key moderators—direct increases in these factors may boost contributions/efficiency, but only up to the point where direct costs do not outweigh gains (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011).
    - **In one-shot or weakly-structured games, with high punishment costs or where retaliation/punishment of punishers is easy,** introducing punishment may not raise—and could even lower—efficiency (POLLOCK, 1988; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011).
    - **Contexts with resource scarcity or high inequality** are likely to see weaker or negligible efficiency gains from punishment interventions (Nhim et al., 2019).
    - **Reward-exists treatments** (peer reward) may also increase efficiency relative to control, but less reliably than punishment treatments, and do not sustain full efficiency in the long run (Forsyth & Hauert, 2011).

- **Caveats:** The above is almost entirely based on theoretical modeling; real-world or laboratory calibration of magnitudes is essentially absent from this set. Most models do not report efficiency directly but use inferred relationships from cooperation rates or payoffs.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Discussed in several papers for how group size modulates cooperation and efficiency (Broom et al., 2015; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; POLLOCK, 1988).
- `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return): Directly appears in models where efficiency is calculated as a function of the group production (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; POLLOCK, 1988; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; Broom et al., 2015).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (how punishment is implemented): Key moderators of effect size in models (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011; Nhim et al., 2019; Sasaki et al., 2016).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: These factors feature in some models, often in contrast to punishment (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011).
- `all_or_nothing`: Some models assume binary (cooperate/defect) structure (Baker & Rachlin, 2002), mapping to this design choice.

**Indirectly or contextually discussed:**
- `num_rounds`: Repetition and iteration are acknowledged as facilitating conditions (Bshary & Bronstein, 2011; POLLOCK, 1988; Nhim et al., 2019), but not always quantitatively addressed.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Responsibility identification, summary feedback, and face-to-face context play a role in some theory and ethnographic discussions (Boehm, 2014; Raihani, 2014).
- `default_contrib`: Only tangentially touched on through framing-related discussions.

**Effectively missing or minimally addressed:**
- `chat`, `show_n_rounds`: Not directly modeled.
- Other fine-grained treatment/control details (how instructions are worded, interface controls, etc.) are not discussed or modeled.

# 7) Important Limitations

1. **Empirical scarcity:** Nearly all findings are theoretical, with little or no laboratory or field calibration of predicted efficiency changes with and without punishment. There is only one empirical study (Raihani, 2014), and it does not measure payoff outcomes.
2. **Payoff-behavior conflation:** Many claims about “positive effects of punishment” rely on increased cooperation or contributions, not net group efficiency after subtracting punishment costs—sometimes this distinction is clear, but often it is inferred rather than shown.
3. **Missing design coverage:** Critical prediction dimensions such as communication (`chat`), interface framing, and certain summary/feedback mechanisms are not systematically explored in the models.
4. **Contextual generalizability:** Some papers analyze highly abstracted or special-case settings (e.g., strong population structure, power asymmetries, indirect reciprocity) whose results may not generalize to typical laboratory PGGs.
5. **Lack of effect size estimates:** No paper in this set provides estimates on the magnitude of efficiency increase with punishment, given a control baseline; most evidence is at the qualitative or “directional” level.
6. **Neglect of possible negative effects:** While several theoretical models allow for decreased efficiency if punishment is costly or misdirected, empirical evidence for negative or null effects in real game settings is lacking here.

**Summary:** The theoretical literature robustly supports the premise that enabling punishment in structured, multi-round, public-goods-game-like settings can raise group efficiency relative to controls without punishment—but only under certain favorable conditions, and the effect size and design-contingency are not empirically established in this paper set. Prediction must rely on the directionality and moderators discussed in these models, as empirical calibration and detail are missing.
