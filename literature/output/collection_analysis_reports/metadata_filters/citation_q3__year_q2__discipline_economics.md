# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base is **broad, rich, and predominantly empirical**. Most papers are **lab experiments** focused on linear public goods games (PGGs) or very close variants, with some theory and adjacent field evidence. Many directly **contrast efficiency outcomes** (total group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum) with and without punishment. Several papers also manipulate or examine specific **game design dimensions** such as group size, feedback, network structure, punishment type/cost, heterogeneity, information, and the method of rule implementation. There are multiple studies for each major design dimension, but some features (e.g., chat, framing, all-or-nothing contributions, default contribution, explicit display of rounds, identity display) are less commonly studied. Theories supplement the empirical evidence in key areas but are less wide-ranging. Adjacent literature (e.g., on other social dilemmas, exclusion, reward, field contexts) is also present but less directly relevant.

# 2) Task Relevance

The paper set is evaluated on three relevance dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant**: *exact*.
    - A substantial majority of the papers are **exactly PGGs or extremely close linear public goods settings**. A smaller subset employs adjacent designs (e.g., CPR, trust games, repeated PDs).
- **punishment_or_sanctions**: *exact to close*.
    - Most focal papers use **exact peer punishment** (costly token-based punishment). Others employ centralized sanctions, probabilistic punishment, exclusion, non-monetary/social sanctions, or closely related reward mechanisms.
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: *exact to close, but not universal*.
    - Many papers measure and directly report **group payoff/efficiency/surplus**. Others focus on *contributions* or *behavioral outcomes* without direct efficiency reporting. Some adjacent works discuss implied efficiency but do not offer direct measurements.

In summary, **empirical PGG lab experiments with explicit peer punishment and group efficiency measures** are plentiful. Theory is supportive. Some adjacent or contextually relevant studies exist, but these are clearly distinguishable.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes (Highly Relevant):**
    - *Group efficiency*: Ratio of actual to maximal possible group payoff.
    - *Total group payoff*, *group earnings*, *welfare*.
    - *Surplus*, *per-period payoffs*.
    - Many studies (e.g., Dickinson et al., 2015; Kingsley, 2016; Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014) provide **direct efficiency comparisons with and without punishment**.
- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Less Relevant for Prediction):**
    - *Contribution/cooperation rates*: Very commonly reported, sometimes as primary outcome.
    - *Punishment frequency/intensity*, *norm compliance*, *retaliation/antisocial punishment*.
    - Perceptions, attitudes, beliefs, trust, and qualitative aspects.
    - Some papers (e.g., Visser & Burns, 2015; Cheung, 2014) report only on behavioral responses, requiring indirect inference about efficiency.
- **Combination/Derived Outcomes:**
    - Some studies give both behavioral and efficiency outcomes, allowing linkage (e.g., Engel & Kurschilgen, 2013).
    - Adjacent studies (exclusion, reward, reputation, field settings) often center on contributions or other proxies, not efficiency per se.
    - Papers focused solely on norm compliance, meta-norms, or psychological mechanisms are least relevant for direct outcome prediction.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## General Result
- **Enabling costly peer punishment in linear PGGs almost always increases group efficiency over control** (no punishment)—if design conditions are favorable. This effect can, however, be moderated or even reversed by specific game features and population context.

## Moderators and Key Design Dimensions

### **1. Institutional Details of Punishment**
- **Cost and Effectiveness:**
    - *Low-cost, high-impact punishment* leads to larger efficiency gains (Dickinson et al., 2015; Qin & Wang, 2013).
    - *High-cost punishment* can wipe out efficiency gains, especially with high frequency or anti-social use (Leibbrandt et al., 2015; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Restriction/Legitimacy:**
    - Limiting punishment to legitimate targets (only higher-contributors can punish) and providing full feedback increases efficiency relative to unrestricted punishment, which may foster antisocial or retaliatory punishment and thus lower efficiency (Faillo et al., 2013).
- **Network Structure:**
    - Efficiency gains are largest in *complete punishment networks*. Asymmetric or incomplete networks can reduce or even reverse positive efficiency effects by fostering under-punished defectors and retaliation cycles (Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
- **Punishment Mechanism Design:**
    - *Exogenous probabilistic punishment* increases efficiency, but only with moderate severity/probability; effectiveness is not simply linked to expected value (Qin & Wang, 2013).
    - Allowing *higher-order punishment* does not necessarily erode the efficiency benefit, provided first-order punishment targets free riders (Fu et al., 2017; Kamei & Putterman, 2015).
    - The design of *audit/monitoring* and centralized vs. decentralized punishment matter—more so with imperfect or noisy information (Nicklisch et al., 2016; Dai et al., 2015; Markussen et al., 2016).

### **2. Feedback and Information**
- **Full feedback** about group members' actions and punishments is necessary to realize the efficiency benefits of punishment. Noise or ego-centric feedback diminishes gains or may lead to misapplied punishment (Kamei & Putterman, 2015; Nicklisch et al., 2016).

### **3. Group Structure and Heterogeneity**
- **Homogeneity vs. Heterogeneity:**
    - With *homogeneous endowments/returns*, punishment reliably raises efficiency (Kingsley, 2016; Dickinson et al., 2015; Kube et al., 2015).
    - With *heterogeneous endowments/returns*, punishment often *fails* to raise efficiency (or even reduces it) due to normative conflict, antisocial punishment, and reduced compliance (Kingsley, 2016; Gangadharan et al., 2017).
- **Subject pool and culture** moderate outcomes: General population samples may exhibit more antisocial punishment and less payoff gain than student samples (Bortolotti et al., 2015; Vollan et al., 2017).
- **Rule endorsement and method of implementation:** Punishment is more effective if *democratically chosen* or perceived as legitimate (Gürerk, 2013; Kube et al., 2015).

### **4. Alternative Interventions**
- *Rewards* may boost efficiency as much or more than punishment, especially when costless, but effects depend on use and context (Dugar, 2013; Dai et al., 2015; Moser & Musshoff, 2016).
- *Communication* and *conditional participation/exit* can substitute for explicit punishment to raise efficiency in certain designs (Nosenzo & Tufano, 2017; Gangadharan et al., 2017).

### **5. All-or-Nothing, Chat, Other Features**
- *All-or-nothing* versus *continuous contributions*: Most positive effects are observed in continuous games; less is known about threshold variants.
- *Chat*: Usually not present; when absent, the effect of punishment is clearer and more consistent.
- *Default contributions, displays, and identifiability*: Rarely tested.

## Negative or Null Results
- In the presence of *antisocial punishment* and/or *high punishment costs*, adding punishment may **decrease efficiency** (Leibbrandt et al., 2015; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Dekel et al., 2017).
- If punishment is *costless and non-monetary*, effects on efficiency are highly variable: sometimes positive (Dugar, 2013), often null (Peeters & Vorsatz, 2013).
- *Imperfect monitoring or judicial error* can make punishment counterproductive (Markussen et al., 2016; De Geest et al., 2017).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

## General Guidance

- **If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known in a standard linear PGG**, and the game design dimensions (player count, rounds, continuous contributions, complete punishment network, direct and costly peer punishment, full feedback, homogeneous group) are similar to the canonical cases in the literature, **introducing peer punishment is expected to increase efficiency over the control**, often by a substantial amount (Dickinson et al., 2015; Fu et al., 2017; Gürerk, 2013).
- **The predicted efficiency boost is strongly moderated by:**
    - The **cost and impact ratio** of punishment (positive if cost is low and impact high).
    - The **structure of the punishment network** (positive if every player can punish all others; diminished with incomplete or asymmetric networks).
    - **Information environment**: full feedback about contributions and punishments is essential.
    - **Heterogeneity and norm conflict**: heterogeneous endowments, absence of payoff information, or cultural mismatch can nullify or reverse positive effects (Kingsley, 2016; Bortolotti et al., 2015).
    - **Antisocial punishment prevalence**: If high, efficiency gains may be erased (Leibbrandt et al., 2015; Faillo et al., 2013).
    - **Punishment legitimacy and consensus**: Democratic or socially accepted punishment increases efficiency more than imposed schemes (Gürerk, 2013; Kube et al., 2015).

**Prediction formulae or point estimates are not provided, but the direction and key moderators are clear and well-synthesized.**

## When Using Control Efficiency for Prediction

- *Control efficiency* is a **good baseline** but cannot simply be linearly adjusted for the effect of punishment.
- Adjust based on relevant dimensions (e.g., reduce positive adjustment for high punishment cost, network asymmetry, heterogeneity, lack of feedback, observed antisocial punishment, or field settings with general population pools).
- In adverse configurations (high costs, antisocial use, heterogeneity, or imperfect monitoring), *do not assume efficiency will rise*, and it may even fall (Leibbrandt et al., 2015; Markussen et al., 2016; Dekel et al., 2017).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed (Strong empirical or theoretical evidence):**
- `player_count`: Many papers vary group size directly. Positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust across moderate group sizes; extremes (very large/small) are less commonly studied.
- `num_rounds`: Repeated interaction is critical for the effect to emerge.
- `mpcr`: Variations studied; higher MPCR often means higher baseline efficiency; still, punishment improves efficiency if other features are favorable.
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`: Heavily investigated; low cost/high effectiveness is favorable.
- `punishment_exists`: Directly tested (main focus of most experiments).

**Indirectly Informed or Moderately Outlined:**
- `all_or_nothing`: Most studies use continuous, but some test binary all-or-nothing.
- `chat`: Rarely present; when absent, positive punishment effect is clearer. Few studies directly contrast chat/no-chat in combination with punishment.
- `punishment_tech` (who can punish whom): Addressed via network structure studies.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Sometimes manipulated; effects less frequently isolated.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Several studies compare reward and punishment; positive efficiency effect more consistent with punishment, but contingent on setting.

**Only Contextually Discussed or Sparse:**
- `default_contrib`: Rarely directly manipulated; only a few papers discuss opt-in/opt-out frames or default contributions.
- `show_punishment_id` (identifiability): A few studies manipulate anonymity, but direct systematic evidence on its effect on efficiency is limited.

**Effectively Missing:**
- *Systematic manipulation* of `show_n_rounds`, `default_contrib`, and `show_punishment_id` is largely absent.
- *Robust examination* of interactions between dimensions is sparse; most studies manipulate 1-2 design features at a time.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **External validity:** Most evidence comes from lab settings with student subjects, repeated play, and artificial stakes. Effects in field, high-stake, or cross-cultural settings may diverge (Bortolotti et al., 2015; Vollan et al., 2017).
- **Design sparsity:** Some prediction dimensions (chat, default settings, identity display, framing) are under-studied.
- **Efficiency not always directly measured:** Several studies report only on contributions or behavioral outcomes; in these cases efficiency must be inferred, introducing uncertainty.
- **Interaction effects:** Most studies focus on one or two design dimensions at a time; higher-order interactions are not mapped.
- **Adjacency risk:** Some studies on reward, exclusion, or conditional participation are only closely related (not exact PGGs with peer punishment), so generalizing from them to core PGG outcomes should be done with caution.
- **Mechanism ambiguity:** When efficiency declines or fails to increase, the specific mechanism (e.g., retaliation, antisocial punishment, network asymmetry, heterogeneity, legitimacy) often varies by study, leading to context-dependent results.
- **No universal quantitative model:** While the literature provides strong direction and qualitative moderation, it does not support a universally applicable quantitative prediction function for treatment efficiency.

---

**Summary**:  
**Enabling peer punishment in standard lab PGGs generally increases efficiency relative to baseline, but the magnitude and even direction of the effect depend critically on design dimensions such as cost, feedback, network symmetry, legitimacy, and group homogeneity. Control efficiency is informative, but must be adjusted for these moderators. Some dimensions are well studied, while others (notably chat, default, identifiability) lack coverage. Caution is warranted in generalizing outside well-studied parameter ranges or to non-lab/field populations.**
