# 1) Evidence Base

The supplied paper set comprises 15 papers, primarily a mix of empirical (laboratory experimental and a few field) and theoretical contributions. Of these, roughly half are experimental lab studies covering standard and variant public goods games (PGGs), some manipulating or measuring punishment/reward, and several measure group efficiency or related payoff outcomes directly. Other papers are theoretical, offering game-theoretic analysis, mechanism design, or behavioral-economic models of cooperation, punishment, and efficiency, but without providing empirical efficiency outcomes. A minority of the empirical studies are field experiments or examine related game structures (common-pool, allocation, stag hunt, repeated PDs) and outcomes, contributing mostly adjacent or contextual evidence.

Overall, for the focused prediction task—forecasting the efficiency impact of enabling peer punishment in PGG-like settings as a function of design dimensions and control efficiency—this is a moderately broad literature base in scope, but only a subset (notably Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015) offer direct empirical evidence on both punishment and payoff-based efficiency in canonical PGGs. The remaining papers inform mechanisms, behavioral outcomes, or contextual moderators, or provide supporting theory without direct measurement of efficiency.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- Prevalence: Most empirical studies are in exact or close variants of the linear PGG (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016). Some are close (framed field experiments, CPRGs) or adjacent (repeated PD, stag hunt, allocation games).
- Relevance: Several theory papers deal with repeated games, Bayesian games, or cooperation, but less often directly with the standard PGG structure.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- Prevalence: About half the empirical studies include active manipulation or measurement of punishment or sanction mechanisms (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016; Noussair et al., 2015). Others are theory papers modeling punishment, or provide mechanistic rationales (Thöni, 2014; Spitzer, 2016; Forges et al., 2016).
- Quality: Only a few studies experimentally vary specific punishment design dimensions in PGGs; several theory papers point out the critical sensitivity to punishment structure.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- Prevalence: Fewer papers directly report efficiency or related group payoff outcomes (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Becchetti et al., 2015). Several others report only behavioral outcomes (contribution/cooperation rates); a handful are explicit that efficiency is unreported (e.g., Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016; Martinsson et al., 2015; Noussair et al., 2015).
- Quality: The most predictive value for downstream efficiency is concentrated in two or three lab experimental papers; adjacent literature offers only indirect guidance.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (best matches for efficiency):**
- Direct measures of group efficiency/total payoff:  
  - *Kocher & Matzat, 2016*: experimental, efficiency and group profit measured for punishment, reward, VCM.
  - *Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015*: experimental, group earnings (aggregate over rounds) for different punishment designs.
  - *Becchetti et al., 2015*: group payoff (common-pool framing); no punishment, but efficiency impact of monitoring.

- Theoretical work focused on payoff/efficiency outcomes:  
  - *Forges et al., 2016*: efficiency in Bayesian games with punishment, but not PGG-specific.
  - *Beraldo & Sugden, 2016*: efficiency in voluntary cooperation, no punishment.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (not efficiency, but often reported):**
- Contribution rates, cooperation rates, effort, norm compliance, punishment frequency, etc.:
  - Empirical focus in *Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016*, *Martinsson et al., 2015*, *Noussair et al., 2015*, *Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014*, and others.
  - Theory/mechanisms focus on motivation for punishment, retaliation, and signaling (Thöni, 2014; Spitzer, 2016; Golman, 2016).

**Summary:** Only a subset of studies measure and report efficiency directly; most report only indirect proxies or focus on underlying behavioral mechanisms.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General effect of punishment on efficiency:**
- **Punishment often increases contributions, but not efficiency:**  
  - *Kocher & Matzat (2016)* found that enabling peer punishment increases contribution rates more than rewards or the control, but reduces group efficiency—because the costs of punishment outweigh the cooperation gain. Reward, in contrast, increases both contributions and efficiency.
- **Efficiency effects are strongly moderated by punishment design:**  
  - *Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015)* show that "simple" punishment (single, anonymous stage) can raise efficiency above control in the longer run, while "rich" punishment environments (multiple stages, fixed IDs, transparency) nullify this, with efficiency no better than control—due to costly retaliation and feuding.
- **Centralized vs. peer punishment:**  
  - *Campos-Vazquez & Mejia (2016)* (measured only contributions): Centralized punishment by a leader increases cooperation, but group member counter-punishment undermines this effect. No efficiency data.
- **Field or framed non-lab settings show weaker or absent effects:**  
  - *Noussair et al. (2015)*: In a field CPRG, enabling punishment does not raise cooperation rates nor behavioral proxies for efficiency.
- **Antisocial and counter-punishment undermine efficiency:**  
  - Theory and experimental mechanisms point out that retaliation and antisocial punishment (targeting cooperators or for non-inequity reasons) can sharply undermine efficiency from punishment-enabled environments (*Thöni, 2014*).
- **General mechanism: Design details matter:**  
  - Across theory and empirical critiques (e.g., *Spitzer, 2016*), the efficiency effect of punishment is shown to be highly sensitive to design parameters: structure of punishment, information, retaliation possibilities, and player beliefs.

**Effect of design dimensions:**
- **Punishment technology/cost:** Varies effectiveness and efficiency outcome (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Information / transparency:** Showing punishment actor identity or enabling full information allows retaliation, often worsening efficiency.
- **Number of punishment stages:** Multiple stages permit costly feuding, eroding potential efficiency gains.
- **Other design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR):** Reported, but their specific moderation on punishment’s efficiency effect is less well quantified except as context—findings suggest that results are robust to player matching (partner vs. stranger) and possibly player count/MPCR within the examined range.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct Predictive Evidence:**
- When **control efficiency is known** for a specific PGG design, and **peer punishment is enabled**:
  - If the punishment mechanism is **standard** (single, anonymous, moderate cost/impact, no identity revealed, retaliation hard), efficiency may rise above baseline (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015—SP condition), but the effect is smaller than the contribution increase, often muted, and for many parameterizations may remain below control due to punishment costs.
  - If the punishment mechanism is **rich** (multiple stages, identities shown, fixed IDs/retaliation), efficiency is likely to be **equal to or below baseline**—costly punishment feuds offset any cooperation gains.

**Indirect or Qualitative Guidance:**
- **Reward vs. punishment:** Enabling rewards (if calibrated) often increases efficiency more reliably than peer punishment for the same control efficiency.
- **Effect of communication (chat):** Theory suggests chat may sometimes substitute for punishment if used for social signaling, but empirical evidence is lacking regarding its impact on efficiency.
- **Cultural/contextual moderators:** Field experiments (Noussair et al., 2015; Becchetti et al., 2015) and subject pool/contextual effects suggest lab findings do not always generalize, especially where real-world constraints or social norms differ.

**Ambiguity & Uncertainty:**
- The direction and magnitude of efficiency change from enabling punishment are **highly sensitive to specific design features**. Even small changes—such as adding a second punishment stage, or revealing punisher identity—can shift results from positive to negative treatment effects on efficiency.
- Contribution rates are consistently higher with punishment, but this does **not** reliably translate to higher efficiency.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Tested in most lab PGGs; no strong evidence that small (3-4) vs. moderate (6) group size alone shifts punishment’s impact on efficiency—design effects are more important.
- `num_rounds`: Longer games (e.g., 30 rounds) show that efficiency dynamics can evolve over time; punishment may require sufficient rounds for effects to appear or dissipate (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Both are central; costliness and effectiveness of punishment, as well as fine structure (one stage or multiple, magnitude, etc.), are critical moderators of efficiency impact.
- `show_punishment_id`, `punishment_tech`: Identity and transparency strongly moderate risk of retaliation and subsequent efficiency loss.
- `all_or_nothing`, `chat`: Manipulated in some studies, but less often linked directly to core prediction (chat theorized to interact with signaling and norm enforcement).
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Used in feedback/monitoring studies, indirect for punishment effect.

**Indirectly/contextually informed:**
- `default_contrib`: Framing is sometimes varied but rarely analyzed re: punishment impact and efficiency.
- `mpcr`: Varies across studies, though not systematically investigated for its moderation of punishment's efficiency effect; always reported.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Discussed when a reward alternative is run; evidence is that reward can improve efficiency more than punishment, with lower cost, across several studies (Kocher & Matzat, 2016).

**Dimensions effectively missing or rarely/never varied:**
- Simultaneous manipulation of all listed dimensions is rare: Most studies manipulate just a handful at a time, leaving complex interaction effects largely unstudied.
- Several comparative-static effects (e.g., exact joint effect of MPCR * punishment cost * num_rounds * information structure) are not disaggregated.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse Direct Payoff Data:** Only a minority of studies directly report efficiency or group payoff under both control and punishment-enabled treatments in standard PGGs.
- **Design dependency:** Empirical and theory papers agree that the efficiency impact of punishment depends acutely on punishment structure—meaning that predictions based on summary literature findings may be unreliable for design variants not directly tested.
- **Behavioral versus payoff outcomes:** Many studies report only behavioral outcomes (contributions/cooperation), not efficiency—these cannot be reliably translated into efficiency predictions due to the cost of punishment actions.
- **Field and context effects:** Where tested outside lab or in close field analogs, punishment sometimes has no efficiency or cooperation benefit, or can backfire.
- **Generalizability:** Effects shown in canonical PGG lab studies may not hold in related but structurally distinct dilemmas (CPRGs, stag hunt, allocation games) or in environments with different social capital, information, or incentive structures.
- **Missing joint manipulation:** Virtually no studies jointly manipulate (or report interaction) for all 14 design dimensions—some important effects (especially cross-dimension interactions) remain empirically untested.
- **Quantitative mapping:** The literature provides qualitative, rarely quantitative prediction; rarely are effect sizes or formulas for efficiency under punishment linked parametrically to control efficiency and measured design variables.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature shows:  
- Enabling peer punishment in standard PGGs often increases contributions but does **not** reliably increase efficiency; in common configurations, efficiency is **unchanged or decreased** due to the costs of punishment.
- The efficiency effect is acutely sensitive to the fine details of design, especially **punishment structure, transparency, and opportunities for retaliation**.
- Reward mechanisms, when offered as an alternative, often outperform punishment for efficiency.
- Only a few dimensions—punishment structure/cost, rounds, feedback—are directly evidenced as moderators; others remain underexplored.
- Predictions must preserve this uncertainty and clearly cite these limitations (e.g., Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
