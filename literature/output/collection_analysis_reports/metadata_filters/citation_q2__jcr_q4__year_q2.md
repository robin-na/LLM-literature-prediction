# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set (n = 8) contains a mix of laboratory experiments, one field experiment, hypothetical scenario studies, and several theoretical/game-theoretic models. The empirical work is concentrated in laboratory public goods games (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016), one framed field common-pool resource experiment (Noussair et al., 2015), and experimental studies on related games or behaviors (Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014; Roberts et al., 2013). The theoretical component covers abstract, formal models directly analyzing punishment’s efficiency effects (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015; Forges et al., 2016).

Overall, the evidence base is somewhat **broad conceptually**, ranging from social dilemmas to Bayesian games. However, it is **more limited empirically**, with only a few studies reporting direct effects of punishment on efficiency in canonical public goods games (most notably Kocher & Matzat, 2016). Theoretical works address broader boundary conditions and mechanisms but sometimes abstract away from critical experimental details. There is some diversity in settings (lab/field), but most studies only partially match the specific design dimension set needed for downstream prediction.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**
- Four papers are **exactly** in PGG or canonical variant settings (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016; Noussair et al., 2015).
- Several others are **close** (theory papers using social dilemma or cooperative game frameworks with overlapping structure: Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015), or **adjacent** (two-person, allocation, or scenario games: Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014; Roberts et al., 2013; Forges et al., 2016).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**
- All but one paper (**exact** or **close**) analyze explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms.
- A few also discuss reward institutions in parallel (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Noussair et al., 2015; Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
- Only three of eight papers provide **exact** measures of efficiency or direct group payoff effects of punishment (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015 [theory]; Forges et al., 2016 [theory, adjacent setting]).
- Three empirical studies measure only non-payoff behavioral outcomes such as contribution/cooperation rates, punishment assigned, or participant sentiment (**adjacent** or **weak**) (Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016; Noussair et al., 2015; Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014; Roberts et al., 2013).

**Summary:**  
The paper set is **best aligned with punishment mechanisms in PGG or variants**, but only a small subset provides **exact evidence on efficiency effects**, the outcome required for the prediction task. Context, mechanism, and behavioral effects are explored more broadly.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Efficiency, Group Payoff, Welfare, Surplus):**
- **Directly measured:**
    - Kocher & Matzat (2016): reports efficiency and group profit under punishment, reward, and baseline PGG.
    - Lee & Iwasa (2014): models group welfare/efficiency as outcome under punishment in social dilemma (theory).
    - Voelkl (2015): models efficiency/group payoff as a function of punishment effectiveness (theory).
    - Forges et al. (2016): discusses feasibility and efficiency in game-theoretic solutions under the presence of punishment (theory, not empirical).
- **Not measured or only modeled parametrically:** Other studies do not report experimental efficiency or monetary group outcomes.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- Contribution/cooperation rate (Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016; Noussair et al., 2015)
- Punishment and reward assigned, approval/disapproval (Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014)
- Willingness to punish and punitive sentiment (Roberts et al., 2013)
- Behavioral proxies such as "total catch and effort" (Noussair et al., 2015)

**Explicit distinctions:**
- Only Kocher & Matzat (2016) report both behavioral and payoff-based outcomes and directly compare them.
- In several cases, increased cooperation/contributions do not translate into increased efficiency due to costs of punishment (Kocher & Matzat, 2016).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Evidence (Lab and Field Experiments):**

- **Punishment increases cooperation but often reduces efficiency:**  
  In lab PGGs, enabling punishment increases contributions; however, the cost of punishing often exceeds the gains from elevated cooperation, ultimately reducing overall group efficiency (Kocher & Matzat, 2016).

- **Reward may be more efficient than punishment:**  
  Reward mechanisms yield the highest group efficiency and are most preferred by players, though they may not increase contributions by as much as punishment (Kocher & Matzat, 2016).

- **Field or real-world context may alter punishment's effect:**  
  In a framed field experiment in a resource extraction dilemma, both punishment and reward opportunities had no effect on behavioral proxies of cooperation (catch/effort), suggesting punishment’s positive effect in laboratory PGGs may not generalize (Noussair et al., 2015).

- **Contribution effects do not guarantee efficiency gains:**  
  Studies frequently measure only contribution rates or cooperation, but these do not always translate to higher efficiency because the process of punishing can be costly (Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016; Kocher & Matzat, 2016).

**Theoretical and Mechanistic Evidence:**

- **Graduated/targeted punishment increases theoretical efficiency:**  
  Models show that introducing (especially graduated) punishment can stabilize cooperation and support high efficiency if the punishment is proportional, effective, and not excessively harsh (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).

- **Credibility and scope of punishment matter:**  
  The existence of a credible uniform punishment strategy can, in theory, implement efficient outcomes in repeated or Bayesian games (Forges et al., 2016).

- **Too weak a punishment regime fails to support efficiency:**  
  In models, if punishment is not sufficiently strong or is too costly, efficient cooperative equilibrium is not supported (Voelkl, 2015). Conversely, excessive punishment becomes counterproductive if its costs dominate.

**Interplay with Design Dimensions:**

- **Punishment effectiveness (punishment_tech), strength, and cost (punishment_cost) are critical:**  
  Predictive value depends on whether punishment is strong enough to deter defection but not so costly as to erode group surplus (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Voelkl, 2015).

- **Other institutions and context (reward_exists, chat):**  
  Reward (when present) can outperform punishment in efficiency (Kocher & Matzat, 2016). Communication is absent from most experimental settings in the high-relevance papers.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

With only a subset of studies directly informing **efficiency outcomes** in PGGs, the following guidance for predicting *efficiency under punishment* (given the 14 design dimensions and control efficiency) can be supported:

- **Default expectation:** Enabling peer punishment in a standard linear PGG will often increase contributions, but will most likely reduce average efficiency compared to control, due to the direct cost of punishment actions — unless the punishment mechanism is highly effective, not overused, or is redistributive in a way that mitigates efficiency loss (Kocher & Matzat, 2016).

- **Control efficiency is a useful baseline:** In the absence of extremely high levels of initial free-riding (i.e., when control efficiency is already moderate or high), adding costly peer punishment is unlikely to increase efficiency (Kocher & Matzat, 2016).

- **Critical role of punishment parameters:** Prediction should attend closely to the settings of `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`. Theory and empirical data agree that punishment only improves efficiency in a limited parameter range: when it deters free-riding without substantial overuse or misuse (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).

- **Graduated punishment is preferable to harsh, uniform punishment:** If the punishment technology is designed to be proportional to the deviation or harm, theoretical models predict an increased likelihood of efficiency improvement (Lee & Iwasa, 2014).

- **Context and external validity are important:** Effects seen in laboratory PGGs may not generalize to field or high-noise environments (Noussair et al., 2015).

- **Do not infer efficiency from contributions alone:** Many studies show increases in contribution rates without evidence for improved payoff outcomes; use only direct efficiency measures to predict efficiency.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited direct measurement of efficiency:** Efficiency or group profit outcomes under punishment are reported in detail only by Kocher & Matzat (2016), and inferred from stylized models elsewhere (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015). Studies focusing on contribution/cooperation rates are not direct substitutes for efficiency outcomes.

- **Sparse coverage of key design dimensions:** Several prediction dimensions (e.g., default contribution framing, reward tech and costs, punishment/reward visibility) are either not manipulated or not discussed in outcome-relevant terms.

- **Potential lack of generalizability:** The positive or negative effects of punishment observed in laboratory public goods games may not extrapolate to field or real-world dilemmas (Noussair et al., 2015). Theoretical models, while mechanistically informative, may not account for empirical phenomena such as anti-social punishment, retaliation, or parameter sensitivity.

- **Behavioral vs. payoff outcome mismatch:** Many studies emphasize contributions, norm compliance, or punitive sentiment, which may be dissociated from efficient group outcomes (Kocher & Matzat, 2016).

- **No effect size guidance and limited parameter sensitivity:** Even theory papers provide limited quantitative guidelines for prediction across the range of empirical game designs.

- **No joint manipulation across all dimensions:** None of the studies systematically explore efficiency outcomes as a function of the full range of all 14 prediction dimensions; many focus on punishment cost or effectiveness only.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature is most informative for standard laboratory PGGs with clear reporting of punishment parameters, but provides limited coverage for several prediction dimensions and only partial, often theoretical, evidence regarding the efficiency effects of enabling punishment. The prevailing empirical finding is that enabling punishment typically reduces efficiency unless implemented under narrowly favorable conditions. Prediction of treatment efficiency from design dimensions should prioritize the details of punishment cost/effectiveness and control efficiency, but cannot be fully specified on the current literature alone.
