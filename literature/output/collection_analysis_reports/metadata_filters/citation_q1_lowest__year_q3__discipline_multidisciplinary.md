# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is relatively broad, spanning 18 papers with a mix of **empirical experimental studies** (lab experiments) and **theory/modeling papers**. About half of the papers are directly concerned with public goods games (PGG) and peer punishment, while others address adjacent topics: cooperation in related games (team contest, stag-hunt, Hawk-Dove, Prisoner's Dilemma), social sanctions (exclusion, rewards), and influences on cooperation (emotion, personality, biological factors). Only a subset of papers directly analyze **payoff-based efficiency outcomes** when punishment is enabled vs. disabled in a group environment that structurally resembles a PGG.

A **core group** of papers (Harrell 2019; Heine & Strobel 2020; Liao et al. 2021–retracted; Berger & De Silva 2021; Riedl et al. 2021) is most empirically and/or theoretically relevant to the direct efficiency prediction task. Several modeling papers (Okada et al. 2021; Battu 2021) provide deeper mechanistic or evolutionary arguments but rarely report explicit group efficiency outputs. Many remaining papers discuss related behavioral outcomes, individual motivations, or non-PGG settings and hence provide only contextual or indirect relevance.

# 2) Task Relevance

**(a) PGG or Variant**:  
- **Exact Relevance**: Several empirical and theory papers study standard PGGs or direct formal variants (Harrell 2019; Clark & Dickinson 2020; Okada et al. 2021; Battu 2021).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Other papers address adjacent group cooperation games (Heine & Strobel 2020, in a team contest; Riedl et al. 2021, in stag-hunt; Berger & De Silva 2021, two-player deterrence; Behnk et al. 2018, sender-receiver; others address Prisoner's Dilemma, Hawk-Dove, or real-effort tasks).
- **Weak/None**: Some studies are biologically or contextually remote (Neilands et al. 2020, on dogs; Loch-Temzelides 2021, on fungi trade).

**(b) Punishment or Sanctions**:  
- **Exact Relevance**: Multiple papers implement peer punishment as a treatment (Harrell 2019; Clark & Dickinson 2020; Okada et al. 2021; Battu 2021; Heine & Strobel 2020; Liao et al. 2021; Berger & De Silva 2021).
- **Adjacent**: Some use alternative sanctions (Riedl et al. 2021, exclusion; Behnk et al. 2018, reputation-based deterrence; others discuss but do not manipulate punishment).
- **Weak/None**: Several papers do not manipulate or analyze punishment/reward at all (Wu et al. 2021; Hackel et al. 2021).

**(c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**:  
- **Exact Relevance**: A limited set report efficiency, group payoff, or a directly comparable measure (Harrell 2019; Heine & Strobel 2020; Berger & De Silva 2021; Riedl et al. 2021).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Several report outcomes like probability of public goods provision, success rates, or inferable welfare changes (Liao et al. 2021–note retracted; some theoretical works).
- **Weak/None**: Most others focus on behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, cooperation, honesty) rather than efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes**:  
  - *Efficiency/group earnings*: Directly measured in Harrell (2019), Heine & Strobel (2020), Berger & De Silva (2021), Riedl et al. (2021).
  - *Total coins generated, welfare, mean income*: Occasionally reported, typically interchangeable with efficiency for group-based payoff outcomes.
  - *Probability of public goods success / threshold achievement*: Used as a proxy for efficiency in some threshold PGG studies (Liao et al. 2021–retracted).

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**:  
  - *Contribution or cooperation rates*: Frequently measured as proxies for norm compliance or group cooperation.
  - *Punishment and reward frequency, negative reciprocity, honesty/trust*: Often the only reported outcomes in studies with tangential relevance.
  - *Exclusion votes/partner choice*: In social sanction literature (Riedl et al. 2021).
  - *Emotional/psychological measures*: Honesty-Humility, intuition, effort perception, fear/anger.

Importantly, **behavioral outcomes are only proxies for efficiency**, and increased cooperation does not always translate into higher group earnings if punishment is costly or induces welfare loss (Heine & Strobel 2020).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Peer Punishment Effects on Efficiency

### Empirical Results:
- **Peer punishment typically increases contributions and can raise group earnings relative to a no-punishment baseline in classic PGG (Harrell 2019),** but the effect on efficiency is **modulated by punishment costs and institution structure.**
    - *Peer punishment can actually lead to lower efficiency than centralized/leader punishment*, due to uncoordinated application and costs of counter-punishment.
    - *Efficiency gains from peer punishment are smaller than from leader punishment, and effectiveness depends on punisher cooperativeness.*

- **In structured contest environments (Heine & Strobel 2020), punishment does not increase efficiency or can even reduce it**.  
    - Over-contribution and costly punishment together create inefficiency (rent dissipation).  
    - *Punishment lowers mean income compared to reward or baseline.*

- **Threshold PGGs with third-party punishment (Liao et al. 2021–retracted)** report large increases in thresholds met and implied efficiency, but the retraction status and indirect nature of outcomes (group investment rate, not explicit payoffs) weaken confidence.

- **Social sanctions other than direct punishment, particularly exclusion or endogenous partner choice, can boost efficiency** (Riedl et al. 2021), but these mechanisms are somewhat distinct from peer punishment.

### Theoretical and Modeling Insights:
- **Local vs. global punishment and interaction structure strongly affect cooperation and, by inference, efficiency (Okada et al. 2021).** Local punishment is more robustly beneficial for cooperation.
- **Punishment is most effective when frequent and when the cost inflicted (on target) is high (Battu 2021);** targeting persistent defectors enhances cooperation.

### Contextual and Mechanistic Moderators:
- **Design dimensions such as player count, rounds, and especially information structure (Berger & De Silva 2021) moderate the efficiency impact of punishment.**
    - *Detailed, low-cost information on others’ history can undermine deterrent effects and eliminate efficiency gains from punishment.*
    - *Minimal, costly information preserves deterrence and yields higher efficiency.*

- **Sleep deprivation, individual emotion, and personality factors may moderate behavioral responses to punishment** (Clark & Dickinson 2020; Chierchia et al. 2021), but these studies report only behavioral outcomes and not efficiency.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Enabling peer punishment in standard PGG settings usually raises group efficiency relative to baseline**, but **the effect size is highly sensitive** to specific design dimensions:
    - *Punishment institution structure (peer vs. leader/centralized):* Peer punishment generally produces lower efficiency gains than leader punishment, primarily due to higher punishment costs and inefficiencies from uncoordinated action (Harrell 2019).
    - *Punishment cost and magnitude:* High inflicted cost (on target) and frequent opportunities are necessary for punishment to sustain cooperation and maintain efficiency benefits (Battu 2021).
    - *The contest/competition structure:* If the underlying control game is already inefficient (e.g., due to over-contribution), enabling punishment can worsen efficiency through additional welfare loss (Heine & Strobel 2020).
    - *Information structure:* Minimal, costly access to reputation information allows punishment to deter defection and enhance efficiency, but rich, low-cost information can undermine this deterrence (Berger & De Silva 2021).
    - *Type of sanction:* Social sanctions such as partner exclusion can have even greater positive efficiency effects than direct economic punishment (Riedl et al. 2021), but are mechanistically distinct.

- **When only non-payoff behavioral outcomes are reported (e.g., increased cooperation), prediction of efficiency effects must be cautious:**
    - *Increased contributions do not guarantee higher group efficiency if punishment costs are substantial or disproportionately incurred.*

- **If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already near optimal, enabling punishment may yield little or even negative marginal efficiency gains due to punishment costs (Heine & Strobel 2020).**

- **Prediction should account for the game’s similarity to standard repeated PGG:** For close variants or games with strong contest or exclusion elements, literature support is less direct, and efficiency effects of punishment may differ.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions** (with evidence connecting to efficiency or payoff under punishment):
- `player_count` and `num_rounds`: Most empirical and theory papers specify and sometimes manipulate these.
- `chat`: Presence/absence often reported; generally not found to be a key moderator in punishment/efficiency effects.
- `all_or_nothing`: Discussed in several papers; binary vs. continuous contribution treatments occasionally analyzed.
- `mpcr` (marginal per capita return): Core parameter in nearly all PGG studies; higher MPCRs often sustain cooperation more easily.
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`: Central to many theoretical and empirical analyses (Harrell 2019, Battu 2021, Heine & Strobel 2020). Costly punishment often reduces net efficiency gains.
- `show_other_summaries` and `show_punishment_id`: Information structure (especially regarding past actions and punisher identities) is identified as a crucial moderator by Berger & De Silva (2021).

**Indirectly or Contextually Informed**:
- `default_contrib`: Rarely manipulated directly, but framing effects and default rules are occasionally contextualized.
- `punishment_tech`: The distinction between peer vs. leader (Harrell 2019), third-party (Liao et al. 2021–retracted), or exclusion (Riedl et al. 2021) sanctions.
- `reward_exists`/`reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Less frequently analyzed; Heine & Strobel (2020) compare reward and punishment, finding that reward is less damaging to efficiency or welfare.

**Sparse or Missing**:
- `show_n_rounds`: Occasionally mentioned as a control variable, but rarely analyzed for direct effects.
- `show_punishment_id`: Only one or two papers contrast anonymous vs. identified punishers, with limited formal analysis.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Where rewards are tested, the emphasis is usually on behavioral responses (cooperation) rather than efficiency.
- `default_contrib`: Framing effects are infrequently isolated.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Few papers report both control and punishment-enabled group efficiency for the same parameterizations**, which directly constrains quantitative prediction accuracy.
- **Direct evidence is thin on several prediction-relevant design dimensions** (information displays, chat, default contribution framing, reward parameters, identity visibility), limiting precise mapping from design to predicted efficiency effects.
- **Most behavioral studies substitute cooperation/contribution rate for efficiency/welfare**, which can be misleading when punishment is costly or induces counter-punishment, over-punishment, or retaliation, thus reducing group payoff despite higher cooperation.
- **Institution structure and information environment are critical moderators, but only sporadically manipulated and reported**. For instance, peer vs. leader punishment, public vs. private identities, and history/summary displays often differ between studies but are not systematically compared.
- **Results from adjacent or variant games (contest, stag-hunt, exclusion, sender-receiver) may not translate directly** to canonical PGG efficiency outcomes, especially regarding the net benefit of punishment.
- **Retraction of studies (Liao et al. 2021)** and non-reporting of efficiency in otherwise well-specified experiments (Clark & Dickinson 2020, Okada et al. 2021) further weaken the evidentiary base for direct prediction.
- **Reward-based and alternative sanctioning institutions** are under-explored in terms of their differential efficiency effects vis-à-vis peer punishment.
- **Biological, emotional, and personality variation is sometimes documented as moderating behavioral outcomes,** but the impact on payoffs or efficiency is not empirically established.

---

**Summary**: The literature supports a *conditional expectation* that enabling peer punishment in PGG-like environments will often—but not always—increase group efficiency compared to a no-punishment baseline. The net effect is **strongly determined by design details**: punishment structure, costs, information, and the efficiency of the baseline game. **Prediction should remain guarded** in contexts where design dimensions depart from canonical lab PGGs, and where only behavioral, not payoff, outcomes are reported.
