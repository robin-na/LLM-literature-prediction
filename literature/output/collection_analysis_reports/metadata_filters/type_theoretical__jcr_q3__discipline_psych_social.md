# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is composed entirely of theory papers (N = 39), with some providing model-based comparative statics or parameterized equilibrium analysis and many offering qualitative mechanism discussions or reviews of prior experiments. No strictly empirical or laboratory experimental studies are present in the digest. The coverage is broad across the conceptual territory around public goods games (PGGs), punishment, cooperation, reputation, and institutional design, but relatively narrow in direct payoff-based, quantitative evidence strictly within PGGs with and without peer punishment. Many papers generalize or focus on adjacent collective action or social dilemma environments, sometimes with substantial structural differences (e.g., Prisoner's Dilemma, commons management, trust games, sender-receiver games).

A minority of papers provide formal models with explicit predictions for group efficiency or total payoff under different punishment regimes (e.g., Gintis, 2003; Bednar, 2006; Corriveau, 2012; Camera & Gioffré, 2014; Antoci & Zarri, 2015). Others provide strong theoretical synthesis or review of experimental regularities (e.g., Brandts & Fatas, 2012; Gintis, 2003), but most center on mechanisms, motivation, or normative structure rather than on direct, game-dimension-to-efficiency mappings. There is considerable discussion of moderators (punishment cost/effectiveness, monitoring quality, group size, reward options, population heterogeneity, institutional vs. peer punishment) and some conflicting claims about when punishment increases, decreases, or leaves efficiency unchanged.

Overall, the evidence base provides strong theoretical scaffolding and some parameterized predictions for efficiency changes when enabling punishment, but is limited by the lack of quantitative empirical results and by coverage of payoff outcomes in precisely the prediction context.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessed by key dimensions relevant to the prediction task:

- **pgg_or_variant (Relevance to PGG or Close Variant):**
  - **Exact:** Several papers model or discuss standard public goods games or games with very similar incentive structures (Gintis, 2003; Bednar, 2006; Brandts & Fatas, 2012; Akdeniz & van Veelen, 2021; Chen & Perc, 2014; Prétôt et al., 2024).
  - **Close/Adjacent:** Many additional papers focus on games that are social dilemmas but are adjacent to PGGs (e.g., repeated prisoner's dilemma, trust games, CPR, send-receive games).
  - **Weak/None:** A few papers are only peripherally related or model distinct environments (e.g., network structure, solidarity).

- **punishment_or_sanctions:** 
  - **Exact:** The majority explicitly center on punishment or sanctioning (peer or institutional), including the design, mechanism, and consequences of enabling punishment (Gintis, 2003; Bednar, 2006; Prétôt et al., 2024).
  - **Close/Adjacent:** Others discuss mechanisms that are analogous or related, such as reward, ostracism, or policing, without modeling punishment as an explicit design variable.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** 
  - **Exact:** Direct modeling or calculation of group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare is delivered in a few key theory papers (Gintis, 2003; Bednar, 2006; Antoci & Zarri, 2015; Nakao, 2009; Camera & Gioffré, 2014).
  - **Close/Adjacent:** Many papers focus primarily on cooperation/contribution rates, punishment frequency, or norm compliance, treating efficiency by implication if at all.
  - **Weak/None:** Some treat only psychological, motivational, or network-structural outcomes with no group payoff or efficiency reporting (e.g., Bianchi et al., 2020; Takács, 2022).

**Summary:**  
A subset of the literature is exactly directly relevant across all three dimensions, supporting inference for PGGs with and without punishment and efficiency as the outcome. However, much of the set is only indirectly or contextually informative and lacks direct treatment of efficiency as a function of game design variables.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Relevant for Prediction):**
- **Directly reported and modeled**:  
  - **Group efficiency** (payoff relative to full cooperation): Appears in Gintis (2003), Bednar (2006), Prétôt et al. (2024), Corriveau (2012), Camera & Gioffré (2014), Antoci & Zarri (2015), Nakao (2009).
  - **Total group payoff/earnings**: Sometimes discussed as welfare, surplus, or efficiency, as in the above.
- **Others** (Close):  
  - Group-level welfare or mean payoff (sometimes in adjacent games like trust games).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Not Directly Predictive of Efficiency):**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**: Most theory reviews and mechanism models focus on these (e.g., Brandts & Fatas, 2012; Kido & Takezawa, 2024; Morsky & Akçay, 2021).
- **Punishment frequency/intensity**: Who punishes whom, how often, and with what cost/effectiveness.
- **Norm compliance**, reputational dynamics, social power, institutional emergence, and solidarity.

**Separation:**  
Several papers explicitly note the distinction, emphasizing that contributions or cooperation rates may increase with punishment even when group efficiency does not (due to costly punishment, antisocial punishment, or counter-punishment) (Brandts & Fatas, 2012; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical/Theory Synthesis

- **Punishment generally increases cooperation and can increase group efficiency—**  
  - When punishment of defectors is feasible, not prohibitively costly, and antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is rare, **theoretical and some empirical reviews find a strong tendency for group efficiency to rise relative to control** (Gintis, 2003; Bednar, 2006; Corriveau, 2012; Prétôt et al., 2024).
  - **Full efficiency is generally not attained** unless punishment is highly effective, cost is low, and monitoring is (nearly) perfect (Bednar, 2006; Prétôt et al., 2024). Otherwise, a positive but incomplete increase is expected.
  - **Diminishing returns**: Beyond a certain point, increasing punishment severity or cost has less effect, and can even reduce efficiency via excessive norm enforcement or costly errors (Nakao, 2009; Bednar, 2006; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).

- **Design dimension moderators identified by the literature:**
  - **Punishment costs and technology** (effectiveness): Central. If cost is high or punishment is inefficient, efficiency gains are attenuated or reversed (Gintis, 2003; Antoci & Zarri, 2015; Nakao, 2009).
  - **Monitoring observability and noise:** Imperfect information reduces the impact; more informative monitoring increases the efficiency effect of punishment (Bednar, 2006; Camera & Gioffré, 2014).
  - **Institutional vs. peer punishment:** Collective institutions can escape second-order free-rider problems, making large efficiency gains more robust than peer-based punishment alone (Prétôt et al., 2024).
  - **Population heterogeneity and the presence of non-punishing cooperators:** The robustness of the efficiency effect depends on the types present; second-order free-rider problems loom large (Antoci & Zarri, 2015).
  - **Reward alternatives:** The joint or competitive presence of reward can undermine, enhance, or even reverse the impact of punishment on efficiency (Antoci & Zarri, 2015; Esmaeili et al., 2022).
  - **Social/institutional context:** Commitment, norm agreement, and institutional design mediate whether punishment sustains cooperation and translates to efficiency gains (Akdeniz & van Veelen, 2021; Smith, 2020; Barrett, 2020).

- **Risks and negative effects:**
  - **Antisocial punishment, counter-punishment, and high monitoring cost** can lead to scenarios where enabling punishment reduces efficiency, especially if punishment is misdirected or revenge/counterstrategies proliferate (Brandts & Fatas, 2012; Barrett, 2020; Nakao, 2009; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).
  - In extreme parameterizations (e.g., symmetric punishment/reward, high cost, high noise), punishment can destabilize cooperation and lower payoffs compared to the no-punishment baseline (Antoci & Zarri, 2015).
  - **Group size**, length of interaction (num_rounds), and availability of communication (chat) are secondary moderators, with larger groups and longer run lengths generally making efficiency gains from punishment harder to sustain unless punishment is institutionalized (Prétôt et al., 2024; Toelstede, 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

The cumulative literature provides the following guidance for predicting the efficiency effect of enabling punishment, given game design dimensions and control efficiency:

- **Positive directional effect, conditional:**  
  Enabling peer punishment is expected to increase efficiency relative to punishment-disabled controls—**unless punishment is extremely costly, ineffective, or misdirected (e.g., prevalent antisocial punishment)**. The effect is typically positive but strictly less than full cooperation unless monitoring is nearly perfect and/or punishment institutions are collectively funded and effective (Gintis, 2003; Bednar, 2006; Prétôt et al., 2024).

- **Use of control efficiency:**  
  Prediction should be anchored in the observed efficiency of the no-punishment control, with upward adjustment reflecting the theoretical effect size due to punishment—**tempered by punishment cost, effectiveness, monitoring quality, and population composition** (Bednar, 2006; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).

- **Key dimension guidance (as identified in the literature):**
  - **Punishment cost/punishment tech:** Lower costs and more effective punishment yield greater efficiency gains. High costs or weak punishments often wash out benefits.
  - **Monitoring quality (show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds):** More informative monitoring makes punishment more effective; noise and ambiguity reduce the efficiency effect.
  - **Institutional vs. peer punishment:** Institutional mechanisms are more robust, especially in large groups, while pure peer punishment is fragile (Prétôt et al., 2024).
  - **Reward exists/reward cost:** Co-presence of reward complicates inference (may enhance or compete with punishment).
  - **Group size and number of rounds:** Generally, efficiency effect is stable in small groups and short/medium runs, but may erode in large, complex, or long-running groups without institutional support.

- **Ambiguity and risk:**  
  Where population composition is unknown and/or in presence of substantial antisocial or counter-punishment, effects are uncertain and may be negative.

- **Limitations:**  
  Quantitative effect sizes are scarce; most papers provide qualitative or comparative predictions, and non-payoff behavioral outcomes are often surrogates but not direct proxies for efficiency.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- `player_count` (group size): Multiple papers show effect modulation.
- `num_rounds` (interaction length): Discussed in repeated vs. one-shot equilibrium analysis.
- `mpcr` (marginal return): Central to payoff structure and cooperation incentives.
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech` (cost/effectiveness of punishment): Heavily theorized as key moderators.
- `show_n_rounds` / `show_other_summaries`: Monitoring/observability discussed as information structures in several models.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost` (if relevant): Modeled in some papers as co-present incentive mechanisms.

**Indirectly or Contextually Informed Dimensions:**  
- `all_or_nothing`, `default_contrib`: Sometimes appear as boundary conditions (continuous vs. binary action).
- `chat`, `show_punishment_id` (communication, reputation): Occasionally modeled as exogenous moderators but not center stage.
- `reward_tech`, `reward_magnitude`: Only treated in papers that model joint reward/punishment (more often as qualitative mechanism).

**Effectively Missing or Minimal Treatment:**  
- Specific implementation subtleties (e.g., how exactly punishment decisions are made, interface design, contribution framing) are rarely modeled in detail.
- Details of punishment assignment algorithms or variation in preference heterogeneity are usually not formally parameterized.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of Empirical Quantitative Data:**  
  Nearly all papers in the digest are theoretical or conceptual. Empirical laboratory studies and meta-analyses of experimental PGGs with/without punishment, offering effect sizes or direct mappings from design to group efficiency, are **absent**.

- **Efficiency outcome secondary in much of the literature:**  
  Many papers focus on cooperation/contribution rates—a behavioral proxy, not equivalent to efficiency, especially when costly punishment is involved. Thus, behavioral improvements do not always translate to efficiency gains.

- **Contextual generalization risk:**  
  Several core papers model environments adjacent to but not identical with the target PGG setup (e.g., Prisoner's Dilemma, trust games, sender-receiver games, CPR). Transfers must be cautious.

- **Treatment of population heterogeneity and antisocial punishment is underdeveloped:**  
  While noted as risks, most papers do not provide robust predictions for the effect of punishment when population composition varies (Antoci & Zarri, 2015 is a partial exception).

- **Sparse coverage of some design dimensions:**  
  Communication (`chat`), feedback and summary features, and identity exposure are rarely modeled in a way that yields predictions on efficiency treatment effects.

- **Ambiguity on the net effect in conflicted or complex institutional settings:**  
  Several papers show the direction/magnitude of the efficiency effect depends intricately on cost, misalignment, noise, and the nature of social norms. Prediction uncertainty remains high except in "well-behaved" parameter regions (low cost, high transparency).

- **No direct mapping for all 14 prediction dimensions:**  
  While some key dimensions (player count, punishment cost, MPCR) are well-covered, others are only touched upon or ignored.

---

**In summary:**  
This literature set provides strong theoretical support and partial quantitative guidance for predicting that enabling peer punishment in public-goods-game-like environments increases group efficiency, **conditional on low punishment cost, high effectiveness, and informational transparency**. The effect size will depend on monitoring quality, the risk of antisocial punishment, presence of cooperative institutions, and the initial efficiency of the control. Mapping from all 14 design features to treatment efficiency is only possible via inference, not direct evidence. The absence of experimental data and sparse reporting of efficiency in many papers are key constraints.
