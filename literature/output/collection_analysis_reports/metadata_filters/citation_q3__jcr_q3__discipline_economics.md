# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set comprises 13 sources, with a mix of empirical experimental (mainly lab experiments, n=7) and theoretical (n=6) contributions. The coverage is anchored by several high-quality experimental studies using standard linear public goods games (PGG) or close variants, with a subset examining direct payoff-related outcomes (efficiency or group earnings) and the remainder addressing behavioral outcomes such as cooperation and contribution rates. The theoretical papers tend to focus on mechanism arguments and modeling of public-goods-like environments, occasionally providing direct implications for efficiency, but more often focussing on behavioral or institutional factors.

The evidence base is relatively broad in its conceptual framing (including trust games, conflict games, reputation/gossip mechanisms, and commercialized cooperation), but only a subset of papers deliver direct, quantitative efficiency effects for peer punishment in repeated PGGs. Nonetheless, the empirical papers that do target these central features of the prediction task (punishment-on efficiency in repeated PGGs) provide detailed and methodologically strong evidence.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:  
- **exact**: Several papers use standard linear PGGs or close formal analogs (Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014; Greiff & Paetzel, 2016; Figuières et al., 2013).
- **close/adjacent**: Other papers analyze related settings: trust games (Fehr & Sutter, 2019), conflict games (Lacomba et al., 2014), repeated PD with termination options (Wilson & Wu, 2017), or Cournot duopolies (Bigoni et al., 2019). Theoretical analyses sometimes generalize beyond the classic PGG (De Silva et al., 2010; Zhang & Hofbauer, 2016; Shibayama, 2015).
- **none/weak**: Only one or two papers have very weak or no relevance.

**punishment_or_sanctions**:  
- **exact**: Multiple papers deliver strong evidence on punishment or sanctions: peer punishment in PGGs (Faillo et al., 2013), centralized punishment (Kamijo et al., 2014), and various theoretical expositions of punishment (De Silva et al., 2010; Zhang & Hofbauer, 2016).
- **adjacent/close**: Some analyze social sanctions (gossip: Fehr & Sutter, 2019), termination/walk-away (Wilson & Wu, 2017), or destruction as punishment in conflict (Lacomba et al., 2014).
- **weak/none**: Some focus on reputation or communication without explicit material punishment (Greiff & Paetzel, 2016; Bigoni et al., 2019).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- **exact**: Papers with measured efficiency, group payoff, or welfare directly: Faillo et al. (2013), Kamijo et al. (2014), De Silva et al. (2010), Fehr & Sutter (2019), Lacomba et al. (2014), Wilson & Wu (2017).
- **adjacent**: Several theory papers and experiments focus on behavioral outcomes absent efficiency comparisons (contribution rate, cooperation rate, norm compliance).
- **none/weak**: Some have little to no measured payoff outcomes (Clavien & Klein, 2010; Bigoni et al., 2019).

Overall, the most relevant evidence comes from a limited subset of sources, but these address the core prediction task directly.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related Outcomes (Relevant for Efficiency Prediction):**
- **Efficiency** (ratio of observed to maximal group payoff): Explicitly measured or discussed in Faillo et al. (2013), Kamijo et al. (2014), De Silva et al. (2010), Fehr & Sutter (2019), Lacomba et al. (2014), Wilson & Wu (2017).
- **Total group payoff/earnings**, **welfare**, and **surplus**: Operationalized analogously to efficiency in key experiments.
- **Mean payoff**: Used in both experimental and theoretical settings.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Not Directly Interpretable as Efficiency):**
- **Contribution rate**, **cooperation rate**, **punishment frequency**, **norm compliance**: Frequently reported in behavioral economics studies (Greiff & Paetzel, 2016; Bigoni et al., 2019; Figuières et al., 2013).
- **Destruction frequency** (as punishment proxy): Observed in conflict experiments (Lacomba et al., 2014).
- **Communication, gossip, or observation behaviors**: Focus of Fehr & Sutter (2019) and Greiff & Paetzel (2016).

There is a clear distinction: Only some papers measure efficiency or closely related payoffs, while others focus exclusively on behavior, cooperation, or beliefs.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Peer punishment can increase, decrease, or have no effect on efficiency, depending on its structure and context**. Unrestricted punishment may allow antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators), which lowers efficiency; restricting punishment to "legitimate" targets (free-riders) and providing full contribution feedback robustly increases efficiency over both baseline and unrestricted punishment (Faillo et al., 2013).
- **The design of punishment institutions (absolute vs. relative) matters**. When sanctions are not fully deterrent, relative punishment (targeting only the worst violators) yields higher efficiency than absolute punishment (Kamijo et al., 2014).
- **Both theory and experiment highlight the necessity of credible monitoring and information feedback**. The positive efficiency effect of punishment depends on the ability to identify and punish low contributors (Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014).
- **Punishment's efficiency impact is moderated by additional game design features**: voluntary participation leads to higher efficiency with punishment; when participation is compulsory, efficiency may not increase (De Silva et al., 2010). The value of the outside/punishment option and its distribution of payoffs can create non-monotonic efficiency effects (Wilson & Wu, 2017).
- **Indirect or social sanctions (e.g., gossip, reputation) can increase efficiency, sometimes rivaling or exceeding material punishment in effectiveness** (Fehr & Sutter, 2019), but these are adjacent to peer punishment as specified in classic PGGs.
- **Punishment employed as retaliation or without proper behavioral targeting can reduce overall efficiency** (Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014). The effectiveness and cost structure of punishment are critical for predicting whether efficiency rises or falls.
- **Communication (chat) and information** can have large positive effects on cooperation, but only indirectly inform efficiency unless paired with explicit payoff reporting (Bigoni et al., 2019; Greiff & Paetzel, 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **When prediction data includes control efficiency and design dimensions, the literature suggests:**
    - If **punishment is enabled in a standard PGG** without restriction on who may punish, *do not assume efficiency will increase*—antisocial punishment can reduce or offset gains.
    - **Efficiency gains are most likely when punishment is targeted (legitimate)** (e.g., only higher contributors punishing lower) *and* when all players have full information on contributions; partial/obscured feedback greatly reduces or nullifies gains (Faillo et al., 2013).
    - **Institutional details regarding who is punished and the severity/cost of sanctions** drive the quantitative effect on efficiency (Kamijo et al., 2014; Wilson & Wu, 2017).
    - **Voluntary participation amplifies punishment's positive efficiency effects** (De Silva et al., 2010); compulsory participation can yield little or no efficiency benefit despite punishment.
    - If **punishment options are too costly or poorly structured**, they may be over- or under-utilized, creating non-monotonic and sometimes negative effects on group payoff (Wilson & Wu, 2017; Lacomba et al., 2014).
    - **Indirect and social punishment mechanisms (e.g., gossip, reputation)** can yield substantial efficiency improvements, but should not be treated as direct peer punishment.
- **Quantitative effect magnitudes** depend critically on whether antisocial punishment is possible, the informational environment, the severity and cost of punishment, and baseline efficiency in the control.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (empirical or formal analysis):**
- **player_count**: Explicit in most studies, often with n=2–4.
- **num_rounds**: Standard in repeated games (typically 10–20 rounds).
- **mpcr**: Treated as an experimental variable or controlled parameter.
- **punishment_cost**: A primary variable in theory and experiment.
- **punishment_tech**: Institutional details about who can punish, punishment magnitude, and structure are key moderators (Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014).
- **all_or_nothing**: Some games use binary decisions; others, continuous contributions.
- **chat**: Directly manipulated in some experiments (Bigoni et al., 2019; Fehr & Sutter, 2019).
- **show_other_summaries/full feedback**: Strongly moderated effect of punishment (Faillo et al., 2013).
- **show_n_rounds**: Sometimes specified; not a primary moderator.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Addressed mainly in theoretical (Shibayama, 2015; Zhang & Hofbauer, 2016), not empirical, studies.

**Indirectly informed:**
- **default_contrib**: Framing addressed in a few studies, but rarely a manipulated variable.
- **show_punishment_id**: Discussed contextually but not explicitly manipulated.
- **reward_magnitude, punishment_magnitude**: Sometimes discussed as part of punishment or reward effectiveness.

**Contextual/rarely discussed:**
- Several parameters are only lightly or contextually referenced, especially those specific to user interfaces or experimental framing. 

- **Dimensions effectively missing:**  
  - **show_punishment_id**: Not analyzed as a main treatment.
  - **default_contrib**: Not empirically tested for effect.
  - **Some fine-grained parameters combining multiple institutional features** are not systematically varied.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical studies directly addressing PGG efficiency under peer punishment are limited in number and diversity**; generalizability outside lab contexts is somewhat constrained.
- **Most direct evidence comes from small group sizes (2–4) and short time horizons (10–20 rounds)**; less is known about larger, longer, or more complex environments.
- **Antisocial punishment and institutional structure are major, sometimes underexplored, moderators**—treatment efficiency varies dramatically depending on whether antisocial punishment is possible.
- **Several relevant design dimensions (e.g., punishment_id, default_contrib framing, nuanced information visibility) are not systematically studied**.
- **Theoretical models assume rationality or specific learning dynamics that may diverge from empirical behavior**, limiting predictive accuracy in real-world or poorly controlled lab scenarios.
- **Efficiency is sometimes inferred from contributions or cooperation rates, particularly in adjacent or theoretical papers**, which may not map cleanly to actual payoff outcomes.
- **Contextual moderators like culture, social distance, and prior group experience are acknowledged in reviews but not quantified for prediction**.
- **Rewards, indirect sanctions (e.g., gossip), and communication effects may not be additive or directly substitutable for punishment**; findings from these studies are only adjacent for classic peer punishment predictions.
- **Quantitative estimation remains challenging**: Only a small number of studies provide effect sizes or directly usable coefficients for mapping from control efficiency and design dimensions to treatment efficiency with punishment.

---
**Bottom line**:  
The literature base offers strong qualitative (and sometimes quantitative) guidance for predicting the direction and moderators of efficiency change when peer punishment is enabled in repeated PGGs, but the mapping from the full set of design parameters to quantitative efficiency outcomes remains incomplete, especially outside the empirically dominant parameter ranges and institutional structures.
