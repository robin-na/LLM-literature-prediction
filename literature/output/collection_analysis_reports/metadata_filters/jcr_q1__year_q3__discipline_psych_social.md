# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is relatively broad in terms of methods and conceptual scope, including both empirical (lab/field/observational) and theoretical papers. Of the 76 papers, a substantial fraction are experimental lab studies employing standard linear public goods games (PGG) or close variants, while others use adjacent paradigms or purely theoretical/agent-based modeling approaches. A material proportion of the papers analyze or discuss punishment mechanisms, but only a smaller subset directly reports efficiency or related payoff-based outcomes (group/total earnings, welfare, surplus, or coins generated) comparing punishment-enabled and control (punishment-disabled) treatments. Many other papers focus on behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation or contribution rates, sanctioning frequencies) or on underlying social and psychological mechanisms, without directly addressing efficiency.

The evidence base is therefore rich for exploring how design dimensions, punishment, and social context shape behavior in PGGs. However, when strictly limited to empirical findings concerning efficiency or related payoff outcomes in actual PGG or close variants, the evidence narrows significantly—particularly regarding systematic coverage of all 14 specified design dimensions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:  
- *Relevance*: The paper set is weighted toward **exact** and **close** PGG designs, including many standard and repeated linear public goods games, generalized exchange, common-pool resource games, and some partner-choice or dyadic trust games.  
- *Summary*: There is a robust core of directly relevant (exact/close) PGG evidence, though a substantial number of adjacent-context or only contextually relevant studies are included.

**punishment_or_sanctions**:  
- *Relevance*: For many papers, relevance is **exact** (studying peer, democratic, or central punishment as a treatment). Others investigate adjacent institutions (e.g., monitoring/fines, exclusion) or focus on reward mechanisms without punishment (**none** for those). Some models discuss punishment conceptually but without direct implementation.  
- *Summary*: There is a strong presence of studies with **exact** or at least **close** punishment relevance.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- *Relevance*: Only a minority report **exact** efficiency (ratio of total group payoff vs. full cooperation baseline), though many report **close** outcomes (e.g., aggregate group profit, earnings, welfare, total coins, or surplus), and a larger group focus on non-payoff behavioral proxies.  
- *Summary*: Payoff-related findings are present but not universal; much analysis is on behavior, not efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes**:
- *Directly measured*: Group efficiency (as defined by ratio to full cooperation) is reported in a small number of studies (e.g., Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Nockur et al., 2021; Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).
- *Closely related*: Most "efficiency" findings in the literature refer to group profit, welfare, average total earnings, or aggregate surplus—outcomes that are close proxies for efficiency but sometimes lack explicit normalization to a full-cooperation baseline.
- *Less direct*: Some studies report payoffs only as means or distributions not explicitly mapped onto efficiency; others only report changes, not absolute values.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**:
- *Common*: Contribution/cooperation rates, frequency or targeting of punishment/antisocial punishment, behavioral compliance, and subjective satisfaction or fairness are widely reported.
- *Caveat*: While higher contributions are often assumed to increase efficiency, costs of punishment (to both target and punisher) can negate or outweigh the gains from increased cooperation.

**Other Outcome Types**:
- Inequality (e.g., Gini coefficient).
- Satisfaction ratings and perceived fairness.
- Reputational or attitudinal measures.
- Norm compliance and willingness to punish.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Findings (Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes):**

- **Centralized/Institutional Punishment**:  
  - Enabling centralized punishment usually increases group efficiency (profit) versus no-punishment baseline, unless punishment is very costly or poorly designed (Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Kosfeld, 2020).  
  - Efficiency gains persist across variants of centralized authority (democratically elected or exogenous), with little added effect from selection/accountability specifics (Castillo & Hamman, 2021).
  - Democratic or central punishment avoids excessive punishment costs and thus preserves or increases efficiency, while peer punishment often reduces profit due to overuse and retaliation (Nockur et al., 2021).

- **Peer Punishment**:  
  - Enabling peer punishment increases cooperation rates and often increases group efficiency, but the costs of punishment can outweigh the benefits unless use is minimal or context limits antisocial punishment (Nockur et al., 2021; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
  - In designs where information is full and all parties are subject to sanction, even inefficient peer punishment can induce high efficiency via deterrence, as use of punishment is low (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).
  - However, in many contexts, peer punishment reduces group profit or leaves efficiency unchanged due to high punishment-related losses and retaliation cycles (Nockur et al., 2021; Ahmad & Loch, 2020).

- **Communication & Framing Effects**:  
  - Communication alone can have a larger effect on efficiency than monetary punishment (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019).
  - The framing of enforcement mechanisms matters: penalty framing increases efficiency more than market-based cost mechanisms (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019).

- **Contextual Moderators**:  
  - Transparency of endowments and incomes alters the net efficiency gains from punishment/reward: visible inequality reduces antisocial punishment and increases aggregate contributions and welfare (Hauser et al., 2021).
  - Baseline control efficiency (incentive structure) matters: punishment increases efficiency when baseline is low (temptation to defect high), but can decrease it when baseline is high (Nair et al., 2018).
  - The presence of an immune individual or an unchecked dominant type can offset efficiency gains from punishment (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).

- **Negative or Mixed Effects**:  
  - Peer punishment can reduce efficiency when too frequent or directed for antisocial reasons (Nockur et al., 2021; Ahmad & Loch, 2020).
  - Retaliation cycles (punishment–counterpunishment) can lead to net losses (Ahmad & Loch, 2020).
  - In settings with mutual punishment/retaliation options, efficiency can decline below control levels (Bolton et al., 2018).

**Mechanistic/Theoretical Insights:**
- Effectiveness of punishment depends on normative constraints, prevalence of antisocial punishment, cost-to-impact ratio, monitoring, and information structure (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Kosfeld, 2020; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021).
- Reputation and transparency interact positively with punishment to support high efficiency but only if antisocial use is contained (Hauser et al., 2021; Kosfeld, 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How to use this literature for prediction:**

- **When punishment is enabled…**
  - **Centralized or democratic punishment:** Predict a neutral to positive change in efficiency relative to control, especially if the punishment institution is well designed, not overly costly, and normatively constrained to target defectors (Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Nockur et al., 2021; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021).
  - **Peer punishment:** Predict an ambiguous or slightly negative change in efficiency unless design features or social norms ensure that punishment is used sparingly, targeted correctly, and antisocial punishment is rare. The expected gain in cooperation may not translate to higher group efficiency due to punishment costs (Nockur et al., 2021; Ahmad & Loch, 2020; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
  - **Reward frameworks (without punishment):** Not informative for the punishment effect prediction; see separate guidance.

- **Control (no-punishment) efficiency is necessary as a baseline**, but the marginal effect of punishment depends strongly on:
  - **Type/cost of punishment** (centralized vs. peer, and punishment cost/impact ratio)
  - **Information structure** (visibility of actions/endowments, full feedback)
  - **Normative/Ecological context** (presence of opportunities for antisocial punishment, group heterogeneity)
  - **Baseline efficiency** (when control is already efficient, adding punishment may lower efficiency).

- **Critical design moderators**:
  - Democratic or central punishment structures are more likely to sustain or increase efficiency without high collateral costs.
  - Low cost-to-impact ratio for punishment enhances positive efficiency effects—if costs are too high, gains from cooperation are offset.
  - Full transparency and the absence of 'immune' group members facilitate efficiency gains.
  - High temptation to defect (low control efficiency) increases the likelihood that punishment interventions will yield net efficiency gains.

- **Ambiguity & Caution**:
  - Published effects can vary substantially with subtle design variation; some contexts report strong positive efficiency effects, others negative or null (Nockur et al., 2021; Ahmad & Loch, 2020; Nair et al., 2018).
  - Many studies do not report or analyze efficiency directly, so inferences must be made with caution where only group profit, welfare, or contributions are reported.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- **player_count**, **num_rounds**: Explicitly manipulated and reported across many studies. Several findings note robustness or non-robustness of effects to group size and round number (Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Nockur et al., 2021; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).
- **chat**: Presence/absence and effects of communication are clearly tested (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019).
- **mpcr**: MPCR is explicitly reported; effects on baseline efficiency and marginal returns to cooperation are addressed (Nockur et al., 2021; Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; Gërxhani et al., 2021).
- **punishment_cost**: Cost-to-impact ratio is a central design parameter in efficiency analysis (Nockur et al., 2021; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).
- **punishment_tech**: Structure/type of punishment (peer, centralized, democratic) is key for understanding efficiency effects (Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Nockur et al., 2021; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021).
- **all_or_nothing**: Binary vs. continuous contributions are noted in multiple experiments; this dimension is often explicitly controlled.
- **show_other_summaries**, **show_punishment_id**: Some studies manipulate information about others’ actions and the anonymity of punishers, affecting antisocial punishment and efficiency (Hauser et al., 2021).

**Indirectly Informed Dimensions:**  
- **reward_exists**, **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**: The effect of punishment may sometimes be compared to reward (Hauser et al., 2021), but direct efficiency comparisons with rewards are rare.
- **default_contrib**: Only very rarely manipulated; most studies use neutral framing.
- **show_n_rounds**: Sometimes varied.

**Only Contextually Discussed/Missing:**  
- The framing of default contributions, details of reward mechanisms, and the explicit revealing of round numbers or punishment identities are less frequently analyzed in direct relation to efficiency outcomes. Some papers discuss them as potential moderators or mechanisms but do not test them directly.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited direct efficiency evidence**: Few studies report efficiency as the ratio of group payoff to the fully cooperative optimum; most use total profit or earnings, which may understate/overstate actual efficiency effects depending on group behavior and punishment costs.
- **Heterogeneity in design/context**: Results sometimes conflict when moving from peer to centralized punishment, small to large groups, or low- to high-MPCR contexts.
- **Outcome conflation**: Increased cooperation rates do not necessarily equate to efficiency gains—punishment costs can offset or reverse the benefits of higher contributions, especially in peer punishment designs.
- **Sparse coverage of some dimensions**: Reward mechanisms, contribution defaults, and certain information structure dimensions (e.g., round visibility, punishment anonymity) are less thoroughly covered.
- **Lack of external validity**: Several review/theory papers and observational studies urge caution in generalizing lab findings to field or real-world contexts—punishment effects can differ systematically outside the lab (Pedersen et al., 2020; Naar, 2020).
- **Absence of systematic quantitative meta-analysis**: While several high-quality lab studies exist, there is little meta-analytic synthesis across the full set of target prediction dimensions.
- **Potential for overestimating punishment effects**: Evidence suggests third-party punishment is less common and less effective in real-world, high-social-distance settings than laboratory analogs may suggest.

---

**In summary**, strong empirical support exists for the prediction that well-designed, centralized, or normatively constrained punishment systems tend to increase treatment efficiency relative to control in standard PGGs. Peer punishment, while increasing cooperation rates, often does not yield efficiency gains due to overuse and high punishment costs, unless the context ensures minimal use and targets defectors effectively. The magnitude and even direction of the punishment effect is highly sensitive to institution type, cost/impact parameters, information structure, and initial baseline (control) efficiency. Reward mechanisms and other design dimensions are less well covered for predicting punishment effects on efficiency per se. Prediction algorithms should incorporate control efficiency and these moderating factors, and treat marginal efficiency effects as highly context-dependent, with null or negative effects plausible for peer punishment in cooperative baselines or with high antisocial punishment.
