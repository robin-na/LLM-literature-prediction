# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is entirely theoretical, with no new empirical or experimental studies. It contains nine papers: several analyze public goods games (PGGs) or very close relatives (e.g., N-person Prisoner's Dilemma, shirker's dilemma, donation game, stag-hunt, trust game), while others model adjacent collective action contexts. The set leans toward broad conceptual modeling and mathematical proofs, including some review/synthesis work. Theoretical coverage is robust for mechanisms, group structure, and incentives, but narrower for directly modeling the specific peer punishment PGG scenario and for payoff-based, efficiency-relevant, empirical generalization.

Overall, the evidence base is broad in modeling social dilemmas and the effects of punishment/rewards but somewhat limited in direct, quantitative, predictive guidance for average efficiency outcomes under peer punishment in public goods games.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *exact:* 2 papers (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Zhang & Pei, 2022) model PGG or N-person PD directly.
- *close/adjacent:* 5 papers employ adjacent models: repeated PDs, donation games, threshold games, investment/trust games, and innovation games.
- *weak/none:* 2 papers (Lütz et al., 2023; Bellucci, 2022) are conceptually related but not direct PGGs.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *exact:* 4 papers include explicit punishment or describe it theoretically (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Zhang & Pei, 2022; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Uchida et al., 2024).
- *adjacent:* 3 model functional analogues (partner switching, external incentives) or weak sanctions (Kurokawa, 2022; Lütz et al., 2023; Peña et al., 2024).
- *none:* 1 paper (Bellucci, 2022) does not address punishment.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *exact:* 6 papers analyze payoffs/efficiency directly (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Uchida et al., 2024; Kurokawa, 2022; Lütz et al., 2023; Peña et al., 2024).
- *close:* 2 papers report on proxies or are ambiguous (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Bellucci, 2022).
- *adjacent/none:* 1 paper (Zhao & Zou, 2025) examines behavioral but not efficiency outcomes.

**Summary:**  
The paper set provides moderate-to-strong theoretical relevance for understanding punishment effects in PGG-like environments and for mapping design dimensions onto payoff-based outcomes, but direct, task-specific, payoff efficiency predictions for peer punishment in standard PGGs are only partially available.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes:**  
    - Group efficiency (ratio of realized payoff to full-cooperation payoff), group welfare, or total group earnings: explicitly modeled in Ishikawa & Fontanari (2025), Gioffré & Tampieri (2025), Uchida et al. (2024), Kurokawa (2022), Lütz et al. (2023), Peña et al. (2024).
    - Adjacent measures of system performance (e.g., group payoff, expected provision of the public good): sometimes used as proxies (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Zhao & Zou, 2025).

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
    - Cooperation rate, norm adoption, stability of cooperation, punishment frequency, trust dynamics, and evolutionary stability: prevalent in nearly all papers.
    - These outcomes are frequently discussed alongside, but are not the same as, payoff-based efficiency.

- **Explicit Task Outcomes:**  
    - Only some papers cleanly distinguish between changes in cooperation frequency and actual impact on efficiency (e.g., Zhang & Pei, 2022 note that greater cooperation via punishment does not always yield higher group payoff due to punishment costs).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Punishment Effect Is Conditional:**  
- Theoretical models predict that punishment—institutional or peer—can move group efficiency from defection/low levels to near-cooperative maxima. However, this effect is conditional:  
    - *Cost Sensitivity:* The benefits depend critically on whether the cost of punishing (to punishers) is low compared to the penalty imposed or to the benefit of increased cooperation (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).
    - *Bistability & Thresholds:* Both high- and low-efficiency equilibria can coexist; initial conditions (fraction of punishers, prior trust/cooperation levels) and group size thresholds are often decisive (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Uchida et al., 2024).
    - *Cognitive Factors:* Prospect theory (overweighting rare punishment events) can make mild punishment surprisingly effective for raising efficiency (Uchida et al., 2024).

**Peer vs Institutional Punishment:**  
- *Peer punishment* (direct assignment by players) often achieves higher cooperation but incurs costs that may offset payoff gains, leading to possible efficiency losses or ambiguous net effects (Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- *Institutional punishment* (fixed fines/costs) expands high-efficiency domains if implemented with low per capita maintenance costs or effective cost sharing (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
- Models generally suggest that peer punishment can cause more antisocial punishment and second-order free-riding, further complicating its efficiency impact (Zhang & Pei, 2022).

**Group Size and Game Structure:**  
- Larger groups sometimes require a higher critical mass of punishers and face stiffer coordination problems (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Peña et al., 2024).
- In threshold or all-or-nothing settings, increasing group size tends to reduce payoff-based efficiency unless incentives are carefully calibrated (Peña et al., 2024).
- Mechanisms like cost-sharing can counteract group size pitfalls by making punishment more scalable (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).

**Reward and Combined Incentives:**  
- Rewards generally promote cooperation less powerfully than punishment, unless rewards are large (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Zhao & Zou, 2025).
- Layering rewards and punishments often produces a synergistic effect, boosting both behavioral cooperation and system stability (Zhao & Zou, 2025).

**Communication, Feedback, and Partner Choice:**  
- Communication (chat), information about others, and partner switching are theorized to boost efficiency, sometimes serving as functional analogues to explicit punishment (Kurokawa, 2022), but are not empirically tested or parameterized here.

**Payoff Versus Behavior Distinction:**  
- Several models stress that more cooperation does not guarantee higher efficiency; the costliness of punitive actions and potential retaliation can sometimes even reduce group payoff relative to no-punishment controls (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Nature and Source of Guidance:**  
- The literature provides strong theoretical—but not empirical—guidance that enabling punishment will often raise efficiency in public goods-like experiments, but this effect depends on a favorable balance of punishment cost versus gain, as well as the population's ability to reach the cooperative equilibrium.

**Interpretation for Downstream Prediction:**  
- *If control (no-punishment) efficiency is low:* The literature suggests that enabling punishment (especially if costs are modest) will likely yield an efficiency gain in most parameterizations (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Uchida et al., 2024).
- *If punishment is expensive, group is large, or cooperation cost is high:* Effects may be neutral or negative; efficiency may not rise, and could even fall due to punishment costs (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Peña et al., 2024).
- *Critical Mass, Initial State:* Transition to high efficiency may require a certain number/mass of punishers or behavioral cooperators at the outset.
- *Peer punishment settings (versus institutional):* Results are more ambiguous; net efficiency gain is not guaranteed, and parameterization is crucial.
- *Presence of reward or combined incentives:* Can amplify punishment’s positive effects.
- *Cognitive/psychological factors (e.g., prospect theory):* When players overweight rare punishment, even minor punishment interventions may drastically improve efficiency.

**Limits:**  
- Because evidence is theoretical, not empirical, most predictions should be interpreted as upper bounds conditional on appropriate parameter configuration.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed:**  
    - `player_count` (group size): Explicitly modeled in several papers (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Peña et al., 2024; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025)—informs efficiency and critical mass for punishment.
    - `mpcr` (cost/benefit ratio): Central to all payoff models; sensitivity to cost/benefit ratio or marginal return is modeled (Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Peña et al., 2024).
    - `num_rounds` (repeatedness): Modeled in repeated games (Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Kurokawa, 2022).
    - `all_or_nothing`: Modeled in many (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Peña et al., 2024).
    - `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`: Core in all punishment models.
    - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Considered in a subset (Zhao & Zou, 2025; Lütz et al., 2023).
  
- **Indirectly Informed/Contextual:**  
    - `chat`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Discussed or translated into concepts (partner knowledge, observability, communication effects), but not explicitly modeled.

- **Effectively Missing:**  
    - `default_contrib` (contribution framing): Not discussed or modeled.
    - `reward_magnitude`, `punishment_magnitude`: Sometimes interchangeable with cost variables, but finer details are not always specified.
    - `show_punishment_id`: Rarely modeled for identity, but occasionally for informational structure.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **No Empirical Results:**  
    - Guidance is entirely theoretical; no new experimental data on efficiency effects of peer punishment.
- **Peer Punishment Versus Institutional Punishment:**  
    - Most direct efficiency predictions pertain to institutional punishment; real-world peer punishment may involve more ambiguity, retaliation, and costs, tempering generalizability (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- **Adjacent Game Structures:**  
    - Several results are from repeated PDs, donation games, or collective action settings, not literal PGGs—risk of inferential overreach.
- **Limited Coverage of Prediction Dimensions:**  
    - Dimensions like `default_contrib`, `chat`, detailed observability/identity rules, and fine gradations of `reward_magnitude` or `punishment_magnitude` are not empirically or even robustly theoretically parameterized.
- **Ambiguity in Non-Payoff Outcomes:**  
    - Many models equate increased cooperation with increased efficiency, but as noted, costliness of sanctions means these can diverge.
- **No Quantitative Estimates:**  
    - No study supplies quantitative, predictive estimates for average efficiency lift from enabling punishment given baseline control efficiency and design dimensions.
- **No Test of Interaction Effects:**  
    - Most models analyze one or a handful of design dimensions at a time, making combinatorial predictions about multi-dimensional designs difficult.

---

**Summary:**  
For the downstream prediction task, the literature provides strong, mechanism-level theoretical evidence that enabling punishment (particularly institutional, and when cost-benefit ratios are favorable) can move a PGG system to higher efficiency, potentially dramatically so if the control is stuck in a low-efficiency state. However, in peer punishment with nontrivial costs, ambiguous retaliation, or poorly calibrated parameters, average efficiency gains are not guaranteed. Prediction models can confidently use group size, MPCR, punishment cost, and incentive structure as moderators, but should be cautious about using theory as a substitute for absent empirical data or for less-studied dimensions (e.g., communication, observability, reward framing). Model ambiguity and the absence of direct empirical measurement are the main limitations for reliable point prediction.
