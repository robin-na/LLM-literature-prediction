# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set consists of **24 papers** spanning both **theoretical models** (including evolutionary game theory and agent-based modeling) and **empirical studies** (mostly laboratory experiments, with some observational and quasi-experimental field work). The coverage is **moderately broad** for PGG-like environments, but **much narrower** for the specific prediction task—i.e., effect of enabling peer punishment on group efficiency (payoff-based efficiency) as a function of detailed game design dimensions and control efficiency.

- **Direct laboratory PGG evidence** with explicit efficiency/earnings outcomes under punishment and control is found in **only a few papers** (notably Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014; Zhang & Cao, 2020).
- **Most theory papers** address cooperation, punishment, and norm compliance **adjacent** to public goods games, often offering mechanism insights or qualitative inferences without empirical payoff results.
- **Behavioral outcomes** (e.g., cooperation rates, norm compliance, conditional cooperation) are frequently reported, while **efficiency or group payoff as the primary outcome** is relatively rare.

# 2) Task Relevance

Synthesized across the set, the **task relevance** on three axes is as follows:

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - **exact relevance**: Several papers (notably Zhang & Cao, 2020; Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014; Bell et al., 2016; Quan et al., 2023) are direct experimental or theoretical studies of public goods games.
  - **close/adjacent**: Many others apply to PGG-like social dilemmas (common-pool resources, threshold games, moral dilemmas) with analogous structures but do not precisely match standard PGG assumptions.
  - **weak/none**: A small subset examines related but structurally different contexts (e.g., norm interventions, waiting in line).

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - **exact**: Most papers theorize or manipulate punishment as a treatment or as a dynamic variable.
  - **close/adjacent**: In several papers, "punishment" occurs through exclusion, reputation loss, or indirect sanction, which may not map directly to PGG peer punishment as defined in experimental economics.
  - **none**: A few papers (e.g., norm feedback interventions) do not involve punishment mechanisms.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - **exact or close**: Only a minority (e.g., Zhang & Cao, 2020; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014; Baker & Choi, 2018; Zhang & van der Schaar, 2013) directly model or empirically report group efficiency or aggregate payoff.
  - **adjacent/weak**: Many report only on contribution rates or strategy distributions, using efficiency as background or in explanatory discussions, but rarely as a primary or explicitly measured outcome.
  - **none**: Some measure only behavioral or norm adherence variables, not payoff-relevant metrics.

**Conclusion:** Task-relevant **direct evidence is available**, but **limited to a subset** of the paper set. Most remaining papers provide context, mechanisms, or adjacent insights rather than direct, quantitative efficiency effects.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes** (relevant to efficiency, i.e., total group payoff relative to social optimum):
- **Direct efficiency or total payoff**:  
  - *Zhang & Cao, 2020*: Explicit modeling of efficiency as a function of game parameters.
  - *Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014*: Group profit/earnings reported; efficiency can be inferred.
  - *Baker & Choi, 2018; Zhang & van der Schaar, 2013*: Theoretical models focus on social welfare/efficiency with punishment mechanisms.
  - *Li & Jiang, 2023*: Efficiency (in a broader governance setting) is explicitly modeled.

**Closely related but not direct efficiency:**
- *Quan et al., 2023; Bell et al., 2016; Li, 2017; Liu & Yang, 2018*: Report cooperation prevalence, not group payoff.
- *Bruner, 2013; Janssen, 2015*: Discuss group welfare conceptually, but do not report or quantify payoffs.

**Strictly non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- Most other theory and empirical papers report **contribution rate, cooperation prevalence, norm compliance, conditional cooperation**, etc., without mapping these to payoff-based efficiency.

**Summary:** **Direct measurement or reporting of efficiency is uncommon.** Behavioral outcomes dominate, and explicit group payoff evidence under punishment (relative to control) is limited.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the papers with high to moderate task relevance:

- **Punishment increases efficiency—conditional on severity/cost and context:**  
  - **Experimental and theoretical PGG studies** find that introducing punishment, if sufficiently strong (either via peer administration or centralized authority), shifts behavior from defection toward cooperation, stabilizing higher payoffs / efficiency (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014; Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Janssen, 2015). However, the effect depends on the **cost to punishers, group size, fines/severity, and baseline cooperation**.
  - **Insufficient or excessively costly punishment may have little or no effect**, or can even reduce efficiency if punishment costs outweigh cooperation gains (Zhang & Cao, 2020) or if "crowding out" occurs (Janssen, 2015).

- **Critical moderators identified:**
  - **Punishment cost and fine size**: There are explicit thresholds for when punishment produces full cooperation versus none (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Quan et al., 2023).  
  - **Group size (player count)**: Effectiveness of punishment can decline with large groups, unless punishment is sufficiently scalable or centralized (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Sripada, 2005).
  - **Punishment mechanism**: Graded or probabilistic punishment can outperform fixed strategies, but only under favorable cost/probability regimes (Quan et al., 2023; Zhang & van der Schaar, 2013).
  - **Centralized vs. peer punishment**: Centralized punishment and public sanctioning may produce greater or more sustainable efficiency than peer-based punishment, particularly where reputational effects are strong (Janssen, 2015; Baker & Choi, 2018).

- **Role of baseline (control) efficiency:**  
  - **Efficiency gains from punishment are largest when control efficiency is low** (i.e., high baseline defection). When baseline cooperation is strong, punishment may add little or may be unnecessary.

- **Rewards/combinations**:  
  - Several models find **joint effects of punishment and reward** to be larger than either alone, though effectiveness is parameter-dependent (Li & Jiang, 2023; Liu & Yang, 2018).
  - **Credible enforcement and information transparency** also moderate the effectiveness of punishment (Baker & Choi, 2018).

- **Behavioral vs. efficiency outcomes:**  
  - Many papers show that **punishment reliably increases cooperation rates**; in most models, this is directionally associated with increased efficiency, but the mapping is not always direct, especially if punishment is overused or if cost is high.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based on the above synthesis, the literature provides these primary guides for predicting **treatment efficiency** (efficiency with punishment enabled), given control efficiency and game design dimensions:

- **Punishment Mechanism and Parameters Are Key**:  
  The presence, **cost**, and **severity** of punishment are critical. **Punishment increases efficiency if its expected effect (fines, probability of assignment) is sufficient to deter defection but not so costly that it erodes group surplus** (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014).

- **Marginal Effect Varies by Baseline Efficiency:**  
  **Biggest efficiency gains appear in settings where the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low** (defection is prevalent). If voluntary cooperation is already high, enabling punishment yields smaller additional efficiency.

- **Group Size Moderates Effect:**  
  For **larger groups**, effectiveness of peer punishment may decline unless institutional or centralized mechanisms are in place, or unless the punishment can be coordinated or made automatic (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Sripada, 2005; Janssen, 2015).

- **Interaction With Other Design Dimensions:**  
  - **Framing and feedback (e.g., chat, information on others' outcomes) can increase or decrease punishment efficacy** by modulating social context (Janssen, 2015).
  - **Presence of rewards and information transparency** strengthens effects; enabling both (punishment and reward) may yield the largest efficiency upgrades (Li & Jiang, 2023; Liu & Yang, 2018).

- **Limits and Boundaries:**  
  - Punishment may fail or backfire if **costs are high, implementation is unreliable, or "crowding out" effects exist**.
  - There are **explicit regime boundaries**: below a certain threshold of punishment severity or likelihood, **no efficiency gains** are expected, even with punishment present (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Quan et al., 2023).

- **Qualitative Prediction Valid; Quantitative Uncertain:**  
  The direction of efficiency change is usually clear (increase), but the **magnitude of improvement as a function of control efficiency and design parameters is not robustly quantified across papers**.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Scarcity of payoff-based outcome data**: Most studies prioritize behavioral measures (cooperation, norm compliance) rather than group efficiency or earnings, limiting direct calibration of efficiency changes.

- **Parameter and context specificity**: Apparent effects of punishment are often **highly sensitive to exact values** (costs, fines, group sizes, baseline efficiency), but **quantitative generalizability is lacking**; theoretical threshold results may not hold in empirical data or in the presence of noise, heterogeneity, or learning.

- **Design dimension gaps**: Key prediction dimensions such as **reward presence/cost, information display (identity of punishers, round summaries), and default contribution framing** are under-explored or unmeasured as moderators of efficiency effects.

- **Ambiguity about peer punishment vs. centralized mechanisms**: Several papers conflate or interchange peer and centralized punishment; **efficiency effects may differ** and the literature does not always clarify which is studied.

- **Limited direct bridge from behavioral to payoff outcomes**: While increased cooperation is often inferred to improve efficiency, this mapping is not always validated, especially when punishment is costly or overused.

- **Applicability to real-world or field settings is weakly supported**: Most field and applied observational studies are only adjacent to standard PGG structures and use behavioral, not efficiency, outcomes.

- **Potential negative or null effects insufficiently explored**: Some mechanism papers note conditions under which punishment can fail or backfire, but few empirical studies systematically report null or negative effects on efficiency.

**In conclusion:**  
This literature set provides **strong theoretical and moderate empirical support** for the expectation that enabling punishment in PGG-like games can increase efficiency, especially when punishment is well-calibrated relative to cooperation incentives. **Predictions of efficiency change should be sensitive to the punishment parameters, baseline cooperation/efficiency, group size, and institutional context. Quantitative estimates are poorly constrained by direct data, and several predictor dimensions remain largely unaddressed in the literature.** The evidence base is thus **useful for conditional direction and qualitative moderation effects, but limited in precision for quantitative prediction across diverse game designs.**
