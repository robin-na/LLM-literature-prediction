# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence set comprises **72 theory papers**, with **no new empirical data or experimental analyses** included. All claims and findings are rooted in mathematical models, simulations, and theoretical syntheses (as stated or implied in the digests). The coverage is **broad with respect to theoretical mechanisms for punishment, efficiency, and cooperation**, including direct models of PGGs and adjacent variants (e.g., repeated prisoners' dilemmas, common pool resource games, group reputation, and networked games).  
However, the **evidence is almost exclusively theoretical**, meaning all outcome estimates, mechanisms, and moderating effects are derived from structural models and simulations, not from new or meta-analyzed empirical effect sizes.  

Within the supplied set, there are:
- Several papers that provide **exact theoretical constructs for predicted efficiency under punishment** in public goods games (e.g., Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kranz, 2010; Hwang & Bowles, 2012).
- Many that address **adjacent games or punishment forms** (e.g., repeated prisoners' dilemma, ostracism, reputation, or implicit sanctioning).
- Some that focus primarily on **behavioral outcomes** (e.g., willingness to punish, norm compliance) rather than group payoff or efficiency.
- A portion that only refer to PGGs, punishment, or efficiency conceptually—without integrating all three.

This **breadth of mechanisms and design dimensions** is a strength for theoretical coverage, but the **lack of direct empirical corroboration or calibration** means prediction for real-world or experimental settings must rely on the validity and applicability of underlying theoretical assumptions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact:** A core subset of papers model standard linear public goods games with or without punishment (e.g., Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kranz, 2010; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Hwang & Bowles, 2012).  
- **close:** Many more treat variants like common pool resource games, group production, games with exit, spatial PGGs, or repeated games with public goods-like structure.  
- **adjacent/weak/none:** About one-third focus on repeated prisoners' dilemma, reputation, group formation, or market-like settings (adjacent but not literal PGGs).

**b) punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact/close:** Most papers analyze peer punishment or formal sanctions, including their cost, technology, and strategic implications.  
- **adjacent:** Some deal with exclusion, reputation mechanisms, or social pressure rather than costly peer punishment per se.  
- **weak/none:** A minority discuss only social norms or monitoring without explicit punishment options.

**c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact/close:** A strong subset (15–20 papers) provides explicit predictions or analytic solutions for efficiency, group payoff, or welfare as a function of design and punishment.  
- **adjacent:** Others predict or simulate cooperation rates, norm compliance, or the prevalence of cooperative equilibria, using these as proxies for efficiency.  
- **weak/none:** Several focus only on behavioral drivers, motivation, psychological mechanisms, or social norm dynamics without measuring payoff or efficiency directly.  
- **Summary:** The **most relevant subset** includes theory papers with explicit payoff or efficiency outcomes for PGGs with/without punishment; however, **much of the evidence is indirect** or exact only for adjacent game structures.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based outcomes (used for prediction):**
    - **Efficiency**: Ratio of actual to optimal group payoff (most directly used in the prediction task)
    - **Total earnings, average payoff, group surplus, welfare**: Several papers supply explicit formulas or equilibrium values for these
- **Non-payoff (behavioral) outcomes (not to be conflated with efficiency):**
    - **Contribution or cooperation rates**: Frequently modeled or simulated (often as an intermediate outcome)
    - **Punishment/reward assigned frequency**
    - **Norm compliance or psychological measures (e.g., willingness to punish, intention attribution)**

Key distinction: **Some models demonstrate that punishment increases cooperation but may not increase efficiency—especially when punishment is costly or when over-punishment leads to net losses (explicitly separated in Anderies et al., 2011; Ostrom, 2000; Anderies et al., 2011).**

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesized across high-relevance papers:**

- **Punishment as an efficiency enabler (conditional):**
    - Introducing punishment options in PGGs can move equilibria from low-contribution, low-efficiency outcomes to higher or near-optimal efficiency, provided punishment is not excessively costly or ineffective, and provided a minimal share of punishers exist in the population (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kranz, 2010; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Hwang & Bowles, 2012).
    - The efficiency gain is **moderated by group composition**: If the group is largely composed of unconditional altruists, willingness to punish is reduced, so the efficacy of punishment as an enforcement tool is weakened—a **non-monotonic relationship** (Hwang & Bowles, 2012).

- **Boundary conditions and moderators:**
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness**: Lower cost and higher punishment impact per unit are associated with greater efficiency gains (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kranz, 2010). If punishment is too costly, its deterrent effect drops and efficiency gains disappear or reverse (Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Sethi & Somanathan, 2003; Heller & Sieberg, 2008, 2010).
    - **Group size**: The effect of punishment on efficiency may be non-monotonic in group size. Efficiency can initially rise with size (punishment is more credible) but falls for very large groups due to increased anonymity and reduced monitoring or willingness to punish (Tarui et al., 2008; Saak, 2012; Kritikos & Bolle, 2004).
    - **Information and monitoring**: Efficiency gains from punishment depend critically on the observability of actions and the structure/timing of information release (Abreu et al., 1991; KANDORI, 1992; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007).
    - **Strategy structure (e.g., duration and targeting of punishment)**: Targeted and forgiving punishment strategies (as opposed to grim-trigger or all-or-nothing punishment) support higher efficiency under a broader range of conditions (Lippert & Spagnolo, 2011; Chassang & Takahashi, 2011).
    - **Baseline efficiency of the no-punishment condition**: If baseline cooperation is already high due to social preferences or trust, adding punishment may not further increase, and can even reduce, group efficiency by crowding out intrinsic motivation (van der Weele, 2012; Orr, 2001).

- **Special mechanisms:**
    - **Voluntary participation or deposit commitment**: Efficiency gains from punishment are strongest when participation is voluntary or when players can self-sanction via commitment mechanisms, as these can replace or amplify the effect of formal punishment (De Silva et al., 2010; Gerber & Wichardt, 2009).
    - **Spatial and network effects**: Local punishment in spatially structured games enhances efficiency, especially with clustering of cooperators and enforcers (Noailly et al., 2009, 2007; Lippert & Spagnolo, 2011).
    - **Reputation, ostracism, and exclusion**: Punishment in the form of reputation loss or exclusion can substitute for costly direct sanctions and can yield similar efficiency effects in certain networked or repeated settings (Saak, 2012; KANDORI, 1992; Takahashi, 2010; Evans & Thomas, 2001).

**Disagreement/Ambiguity:**
- Some models argue that punishment can reduce efficiency under certain conditions—e.g., when punishment is too costly and thus decreases group payoff, or when it crowds out intrinsic motivation in highly cooperative groups (Orr, 2001; Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Anderies et al., 2011).
- The effect of punishment is sometimes **non-monotonic** or **context-dependent**: in groups with high baseline efficiency, punishment can stifle voluntary cooperation ("crowding out"), while in groups with low initial cooperation, punishment has more room to increase efficiency (van der Weele, 2012; Sethi & Somanathan, 2003).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Directly Supported Cases (Exact/Close PGG and Payoff Evidence):**
    - For standard PGG designs (with conventional parameters: 3–6 players, repeated rounds, observable contributions, no communication, moderate MPCR), **enabling peer punishment is expected to shift efficiency markedly upward**—from low (often zero or near Nash) in the control, to near the full-cooperation benchmark—even if most players are selfish (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kranz, 2010; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010).
    - This uplift is **conditional on punishment not being prohibitively costly, the effectiveness of punishment for deterring defection, and the presence of at least some willing punishers**. Theoretical formulas connect efficiency shifts to `player_count`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, and punishment/reward `tech`.
    - For groups with already high efficiency without punishment, or in which intrinsic or norm-based cooperation is strong, **the marginal benefit of punishment is reduced or can even be negative** (van der Weele, 2012; Orr, 2001).

- **Moderators and Exceptions:**
    - **Group size and monitoring:** Larger groups dampen the effectiveness of peer punishment unless monitoring is scalable (Tarui et al., 2008; Kritikos & Bolle, 2004).
    - **Punishment cost/technology:** Lower punishment cost or stronger impact leads to greater likelihood of efficiency gains. High punishment cost or low punishment effectiveness can erase gains or even reduce efficiency (Sethi & Somanathan, 2003; Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Heller & Sieberg, 2008).
    - **Information structure:** When contributions or free riding are not observable, punishment's deterrence effect is weakened or less targeted, thus efficiency gains are lower unless mechanisms for aggregate or group punishment exist (Abreu et al., 1991; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007).
    - **Voluntary participation, spatial or reputation mechanism:** If the design includes voluntary participation, grouped structures, or reputation, efficiency may be sustained even in absence of peer-punishment, or may be further increased by enabling punishment (De Silva et al., 2010; Saak, 2012).

- **Design sensitivity:** The **marginal increase in efficiency from enabling punishment is greatest in control games with low efficiency**, with smaller or ambiguous effects in settings with already high baseline cooperation.

- **Empirical inference:** Because the evidence is **entirely theoretical/simulation-based**, actual effect sizes for efficiency change under punishment must be **calibrated or validated empirically** if prediction is to be made quantitatively for new contexts.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Included in virtually all models as a direct parameter; affects the credibility, scale, and deterrent power of punishment (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kranz, 2010; Saak, 2012).
- `num_rounds`: Modeled explicitly in repeated games; longer or indefinite repetition generally supports higher efficiency by making punishment a credible deterrent (Kranz, 2010; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010).
- `mpcr`: The marginal per-capita return is a central parameter; higher values make cooperation easier to sustain and punishment more effective (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Kranz, 2010).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Both are repeatedly shown to moderate the effectiveness and net payoff of punishment (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Sethi & Somanathan, 2003).
- `all_or_nothing`: Used in several analytic models to distinguish contribution structures (Kranz, 2010; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999).

**Indirectly Informed / Partially Contextual Dimensions:**
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`: Some models address observability of contributions or punishment (e.g., public monitoring, identification; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007; Abreu et al., 1991), but these are more abstract than specific implementation features.
- `default_contrib`: Not explicitly modeled, but framing could shift the composition of reciprocal or conditional cooperators (van der Weele, 2012).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: A few adjacent models consider reward mechanisms and their interaction with punishment (Janus & Lim, 2009; Kranz, 2010), but these are not as commonly analyzed as pure punishment regimes.
- `chat`: Communication is reported in some cases as boosting cooperation and efficiency (Ostrom, 2000) but is not typically parameterized in formal models.
- `punishment_magnitude`: Sometimes subsumed as part of `punishment_tech` (impact per unit cost), occasionally modeled separately.

**Sparse or Effectively Missing:**
- Explicit experimental settings like user interface features, feedback granularity, UI defaults, or psychological framing are **not directly modeled** in most theoretical papers.
- Empirical calibration or interaction effects among the 14 dimensions are not established.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **The entire evidence base is theoretical.** No new empirical findings or meta-analyses are presented; direct experimental outcomes (e.g., actual observed efficiency changes in lab studies) are not included. This limits the ability to validate magnitude or context effects of punishment on efficiency.
- **Generalizability is conditional:** While many models produce explicit predictions for PGGs, their applicability depends on the fit of assumptions to real-world or designed experiments (e.g., presence of sufficient punishers, knowledge of population distribution, monitoring precision).
- **Sensitivity to unmodeled dimensions:** Factors like framing, communication, learning, and intrinsic motivation are addressed conceptually but are usually not controlled or included as formal model parameters.
- **Payoff vs. behavior:** Many papers measure effects on cooperation or contribution—not always on group payoff or efficiency. In some scenarios, increased punishment raises cooperation but lowers or leaves unchanged overall efficiency due to punishment costs (e.g., Anderies et al., 2011).
- **Ambiguity and divergence:** Some models highlight conditions under which punishment can reduce efficiency or be counterproductive, especially through crowding out, over-punishment, or excessive cost (Orr, 2001; van der Weele, 2012; Hwang & Bowles, 2012). There is no consensus about universal effectiveness.
- **Sparse coverage on some design dimensions:** Dimensions such as chat, default contributions, and intricate feedback features are not robustly or specifically analyzed in the theoretical models, which may affect the accuracy of predictions for novel or less-standard game designs.
- **Lack of empirical parameterization:** Quantitative predictions for new settings using these dimensions must rely on theoretical functional forms and may not match observed experimental effect sizes or qualitative outcomes in diverse populations.

---

**In summary:** The literature base is theoretically rich and highly relevant for predicting the direction and conditionality of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled in public-goods-like games, with the strongest inferences for canonical PGG structures and clearly specified cost/impact punishment regimes. However, the lack of empirical data, limited coverage of some game design dimensions, and model dependencies on assumptions mean that predictions should retain explicit uncertainty and be calibrated against empirical results wherever possible.
