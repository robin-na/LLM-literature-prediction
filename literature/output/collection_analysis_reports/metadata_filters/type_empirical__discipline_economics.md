# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence set comprises a very large sample (562) of laboratory and field experiments, overwhelmingly empirical, mostly with explicit efficiency or group payoff measures. The studies focus primarily on canonical public goods games (PGGs) and close CPR variants (where punishment and reward schemes are explicitly tested). There is strong coverage of key experimental dimensions: group size, rounds, information structure, matching, incentive magnitudes, cost/technology of punishment/reward, endogenous/exogenous institutions, communication, monitoring/noise, heterogeneity, and ostracism/exclusion. Many papers are high-powered, use group efficiency as a primary or direct outcome (with detailed quantitative results), and test institutional and behavioral moderators.

A substantial minority of papers are less central: adjacent games (prisoner’s dilemmas, market games, trust games, contest/appropriation/dynamic resources), theoretical/method papers, or studies emphasizing non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment use, norm compliance). These often inform mechanisms or moderators but only indirectly inform treatment efficiency outcomes.

The evidence is broad, with dense empirical coverage for the classic PGG, various punishment and sanctioning regimes, reward mechanisms, institutional design, and group diversity; some dimensions (e.g., emotional/cultural context, network structure) are covered less systematically.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**: Most papers are labeled `exact` or `close`—the great majority are canonical or very close variants (CPR, team production, trust games, contests). Adjacent games and other social dilemmas make up a minority.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**: A very large fraction have `exact` relevance—peer, centralized, delegated, exogenous, and higher-order punishment are all tested, as are exclusion/ostracism (close variant). Some studies focus on non-monetary sanctions, rewards, or other institutional levers (adjacent).

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: Most central PGG punishment papers have `exact` or `close` relevance, reporting total group payoff, efficiency (relative to full cooperation), or welfare. Some papers focus on behavior (contributions/cooperation/punishment rates) and are only indirectly informative for task outcomes (`adjacent` or `weak` relevance).

Summary: The literature is *highly* relevant overall—the majority of the evidence is directly on point for the prediction task: repeated PGGs, experimental manipulations of punishment, and explicit measurement of treatment efficiency compared to a control.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related/outcomes**: Direct measures of *group efficiency* (payoff relative to full cooperation), group earnings, total surplus, welfare, and final accumulated payoffs are the main outcomes in the central PGG punishment literature.
  - Many studies provide treatment-control deltas (Δ efficiency/payoff) under well-specified, parameterized experimental conditions.
  - Closely related CPR and threshold games use surplus, net group returns, or successful provision as payoff-based outcomes.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**: Many papers measure average contributions, cooperation rates, use of punishment/reward, norm compliance, or exclusion/ostracism events. These are important behavioral mechanisms but are not in themselves efficiency outcomes.
  - In some cases, high contributions with heavy punishment actually result in *lower* efficiency due to the cost of punishment.
  - Behavioral outcomes often serve as mechanisms or moderators for downstream efficiency.

Distinction: Conclusions about the effect of punishment on *efficiency* are sharply separated from its effect on *behavioral* outcomes (e.g., increased cooperation but not always increased efficiency).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Central empirical regularities:**
- **Punishment increases cooperation and contributions, but its effect on efficiency is highly context- and design-dependent:**
    - A substantial fraction of standard lab PGGs find that enabling peer or central punishment increases *group efficiency* (by deterring free riding and sustaining higher contributions), especially when:
      - Punishment is well-targeted (mainly assigned to low contributors/free riders)
      - The cost-to-impact ratio (punishment_tech) is favorable (high impact/low cost)
      - Anti-social punishment (punishing high contributors) is rare
      - Endogenous or democratically chosen institutions are present
      - Monitoring is accurate and norm/reward feedback is complete
      - The group has enough rounds for efficiency gains from increased contributions to outweigh early punishment costs

    - A similarly large set of studies show:
      - Punishment increases contributions, but efficiency—total group payoff—is unchanged or *decreases*, because the cost of punishment (and sometimes retaliation/counter-punishment) outweighs the gains from higher contributions.
      - This is especially likely when:
        - Punishment is frequently misapplied to cooperators/antisocially
        - The punishment is costly and not highly leveraged
        - Counter-punishment/feuding is possible
        - Norm conflict/heterogeneity is high (asymmetric/endowment heterogeneity, out-group, or status differences)
        - Groups lack ability to coordinate or communicate clear norms
        - Monitoring is noisy or incomplete

- **Reward-only mechanisms are generally less effective (or as effective) at sustaining contributions/efficiency in standard PGGs.** Net-positive-reward treatments (where reward cost is less than benefit) can reliably increase efficiency; transfer reward (1:1 cost-benefit) is much less effective.

- **Network structure, monitoring, and identifiability matter:**
    - Dense, symmetric punishment networks (everyone can punish everyone) can sustain positive efficiency effects of punishment, but these gains decline or can reverse in sparse/asymmetric networks, under incomplete monitoring, or with large group size.

- **Communication (chat), information, and institution design are powerful moderators:**
    - Communication can substitute for or dramatically amplify the gains from punishment; chat or pre-play discussion tends to boost efficiency, especially when coupled with an ability to punish.
    - Endogenous (voted, chosen) punishment/reward institutions yield larger efficiency gains than imposed ones, particularly with weaker (non-deterrent) sanctions.
    - Feedback formats (whether only contributions, only earnings, or both are shown) can moderate the efficiency effects of punishment even with fixed game parameters.

- **Contextual/confounding effects:**
    - Social identity, group composition (trust, status, in-group/out-group, culture/region), and emotional framing (e.g., anger, happiness priming) can modulate the effectiveness of punishment on efficiency, often via anti-social or underused punishment.
    - Certain environmental variables (e.g., field vs. lab, communication spillovers, prior exposure to institutions, ethnicity, culture) can either enhance or crowd out efficiency gains from punishment.

**Quantitative summary (across studies):**
- Under *canonical* lab conditions (4–5 players, 10–20 rounds, mpcr 0.4–0.5, peer punishment with 1:3 or 1:2 cost-impact, no chat, no default contribution, anonymous but full feedback), introducing punishment typically increases efficiency by 10–30% over baseline, provided anti-social punishment is rare.
- When anti-social punishment is common (due to cultural/institutional factors, group heterogeneity, or ambiguous norms), efficiency gains disappear or can reverse; efficiency is sometimes *lower* than control.
- High or low punishment cost or weak/no impact, poorly designed punishment institutions (open to anti-social punishment or feuding), or high group/monitoring asymmetry can produce *no gain or even lower efficiency than control*.

**Ostracism/exclusion in PGG-like/CPR games** often produces large efficiency gains (sometimes approaching full cooperation), but this is contingent on:
    - The threat being credible and exclusion being mainly targeted at low contributors.
    - Major costs to excluded members not overwhelming overall efficiency (e.g., if group size is critical for public good provision).

**Variants and CPR settings:** Efficiency gains from punishment are *more variable*. In dynamic/appropriation games, costly monitoring, external threats, the possibility of group collapse, or incomplete detection can render punishment *ineffective* or *welfare-reducing*.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **If control efficiency is low and group structure is standard (few players, perfect information, continuous contributions, known rounds, peer punishment with high impact/low cost and little anti-social punishment):**
    - Enabling punishment is *likely* to increase average treatment efficiency by 10–30% over control, especially in later rounds when use of punishment declines.
    - The size of this gain depends sharply on the cost-effectiveness of the punishment mechanism (punishment_cost, punishment_tech)—*higher cost/lower impact means smaller or negative gains*.

- **If punishment is costly and anti-social punishment is common (e.g., high norm conflict, high group heterogeneity, ambiguous norms, out-group, or poor institution design):**
    - *Do not* expect punishment to increase efficiency. It may *reduce average efficiency below control* due to excessive or misdirected punishment.

- **If communication is present (chat), group endogenously chooses punishment regime, or summary information about other contributions is provided:**
    - Expect *larger efficiency gains* when punishment is enabled. Communication and institution choice both tend to magnify positive efficiency effects or mitigate anti-social punishment.

- **If group is large (>8), monitoring/punishment is asymmetric, or network structure is sparse/incomplete:**
    - Efficiency gains from punishment are *more likely to disappear or reverse.* Coverage for large groups is thinner and often less positive.

- **If punishment is combined with rewards, ostracism, or group exclusion mechanisms:**
    - Gains in efficiency can be larger, but only if the cost of the sanction is low or targeted. Group size reduction or loss due to exclusion can sometimes offset gains.

- **If group has high control efficiency (close to socially optimal even without punishment):**
    - Enabling punishment often *reduces* efficiency relative to control, as the cost of unnecessary punishment is not offset by further gains in cooperation.

- **Critical moderators:**
    - **Punishment cost/tech (cost-effectiveness)**: Most consistent and direct predictor of whether enabling punishment will increase, decrease, or fail to change efficiency.
    - **Anti-social punishment prevalence** (group/cultural moderator)—high rates reliably predict poor efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled.
    - **Information and feedback format**: Showing only contributions, only earnings, or both sharply affects the efficacy of punishment.
    - **Institutional design**: Consensus mechanisms, legitimacy, and social learning/history all support higher efficiency outcomes from enabling punishment.

**Key advice for prediction:**
- *Do not* simply extrapolate efficiency gains from behavioral outcomes (e.g., higher contributions)—punishment often increases contributions without improving, and sometimes worsening, group efficiency.
- Rely primarily on studies matching the *entire design configuration*, especially on punishment cost/tech, group composition/heterogeneity, presence or absence of chat/communication, and information structure.
- Where evidence is based on non-payoff behavioral outcomes, *downgrade* its predictive weight for efficiency.
- Where studies report direct treatment-control efficiency differences for matching dimensions, *prioritize* these for model calibration.
- When the literature for a given design (e.g., all-or-nothing contributions, or exogenous/third party punishment) is sparse, interpolate cautiously—uncertainty should be reflected in prediction intervals.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (heavy empirical coverage, high predictive power):**
  - `player_count`
  - `num_rounds`
  - `mpcr`
  - `punishment_cost`
  - `punishment_tech`
  - `show_other_summaries` (who/what is seen; critical for feedback and norm targeting)
  - `all_or_nothing` (continuous/discrete contributions, less frequently but systematically manipulated)
  - `chat` (presence/absence of communication, strong moderator)

**Indirectly informed or contextually discussed:**
  - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (many studies include reward or combined punishment/reward, but fewer directly compare their effects, and effect sizes are generally smaller for rewards)
  - `show_punishment_id` (anonymity vs. identifiability; shows up in studies manipulating observed vs. unobserved punishment, and in the literature on anti-social punishment)
  - `show_n_rounds` (whether round count known, minor effect compared to others)
  - `default_contrib` (framing of contribution; infrequent explicit manipulation but sometimes a moderator)

**Sparse or effectively missing in this set:**
  - Edge or compound institutional design dimensions (e.g., highly complex network topologies, real-time or continuous-time manipulations, or hybrid/field-specific settings).
  - Some field/observational studies and "adjacent" games provide only limited design detail or only behavioral outcomes, not efficiency.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Design dependence and external validity**: Central findings about punishment's effects on efficiency apply most strongly to classic repeated linear PGGs with small group size, continuous contributions, and controlled lab conditions. Effects are more mixed or negative in larger, more heterogeneous, less transparent, or field-like contexts.

- **Moderator sensitivity**: Small changes in punishment institution design, information layout, or group composition can flip the effect of punishment from positive to negative (e.g., introduction of anti-social punishment, counter-punishment, ambiguity, or norm conflict). Aggregate models that do not account for these moderators can mispredict effect direction.

- **Ambiguity in mechanisms**: Some ambiguities persist—for example, the effect of endogenous institution selection vs. exogenous assignment, or the mechanisms driving group acceptance/learning of punishment. Some design features are correlated (e.g., group size and monitoring asymmetry), making it hard to isolate their effects cleanly.

- **Incomplete evidence for all 14 design dimensions**: Direct empirical variation for every dimension in every possible combination is lacking. Some dimensions (e.g., show_punishment_id, default_contrib) are only indirectly informed, while others (e.g., network topology beyond simple cases) have sparse evidence or unclear mapping to efficiency outcomes.

- **Differences between contribution/behavioral findings and efficiency**: A critical repeated limitation is that much of the literature reports high increases in contributions but ambiguous or negative effects on group efficiency. It is essential not to conflate behavioral change with actual welfare gains.

- **Potential for publication/context bias**: Much of the strongest efficiency effect evidence is lab-based, and many experiments are with student, WEIRD samples; studies with more diverse populations sometimes find neutral or negative effects (due to anti-social punishment or norm conflict).

- **Measurement/time horizon**: Short lab sessions vs. longer or real-world collective action may show different long-run dynamics. Initial periods often see efficiency losses from high punishment use, with gains only observed when punishment use decays as cooperation stabilizes.

**In summary**: This literature base enables highly informed, design-sensitive predictions of the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments. However, prediction accuracy depends on precisely matching game design, institution parameters, group composition, and context. Efficiency gains are not automatic: punishment often increases cooperation without improving (or even reducing) efficiency due to direct and indirect costs. Key moderators—especially punishment cost/tech, prevalence of anti-social punishment, communication, and institution legitimacy—must always be considered. Unmeasured design dimensions and ambiguity between behavioral and payoff effects remain important limitations.
