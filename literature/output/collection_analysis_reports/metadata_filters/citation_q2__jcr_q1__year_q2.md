# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set (67 items) is diverse, drawing from theory, laboratory and field experiments, and simulation models. About 10–12 papers provide **direct empirical or theoretical evidence** for PGGs with punishment and explicit efficiency/payoff outcomes (e.g., Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Roberts, 2013; Ye et al., 2016; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013; van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Farjam et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015 [VPEF]; Ozono et al., 2016). Many additional papers are **adjacent** in scope: they use PDs, trust games, common-pool resource games, or indirect/institutional punishment models, or focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes.

The evidence base is **broad** regarding theoretical approaches and adjacent social dilemma models, but **relatively concentrated** for the precise task of predicting efficiency in classic or close-variant PGGs when peer-punishment is enabled. Quantitative, empirical estimates of efficiency change due to punishment in standard lab PGGs are **moderately represented**, while edge-case mechanisms (strategic competition, antisocial punishment, network structure, noise, second-order punishment, etc.) are well-covered in theory and simulation. Evidence from reward mechanisms, ostracism, and collective/institutional punishment is also included, expanding the design context.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **PGG or variant:**  
  - **exact:** ~8–12 papers model or experimentally study classic or near-classic PGGs (e.g., Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Roberts, 2013; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013).
  - **close:** Many papers use related social dilemmas—common-pool resource games, threshold games, public goods with institutional punishment, trust games, or spatial PD—but not always with standard PGG payoff structure.
  - **adjacent/weak:** A substantial number of papers study only dyadic PDs, trust games, or theoretical models not directly mapping to PGGs.
  
- **Punishment or sanctions:**  
  - **exact:** Peer punishment in PGGs is well-covered, with both theory and empirical studies.
  - **close:** Papers address centralized/institutional punishment, ostracism, social disapproval, milestone penalties, or collective protection.
  - **adjacent:** Sanctioning in non-PGG games or models is common.
  - **none:** Some studies analyze only reward, sharing, or no sanction mechanisms.
  
- **Efficiency or related payoff outcomes:**  
  - **exact:** Direct group payoff or efficiency reported in a moderate number of empirical/theoretical PGG studies (e.g., Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
  - **close:** Several papers allow strong inference about efficiency, even if reported as group profit, surplus, or welfare.
  - **adjacent/weak:** Many studies focus on contribution rates, cooperation frequency, or norm compliance but do not directly report efficiency or payoff outcomes.

**Conclusion:** The literature contains **direct, highly relevant evidence** on the prediction question, but coverage is uneven. Considerable adjacent evidence informs about moderators and boundary conditions, while many studies focus on behavioral outcomes without payoff/efficiency data.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (high relevance):  
  - **Efficiency** (group payoff/maximum potential): Directly measured in a subset of theory papers and some experiments (Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013).
  - **Total group welfare/earnings** (synonyms for efficiency): Reported or inferred in several studies.
  - **Surplus, average profit, or aggregate returns:** Used as efficiency proxies, especially in simulation and network models (e.g., Ozono et al., 2016).
  
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (lower for prediction task but inform mechanism):  
  - **Average contribution rate or cooperation rate:** Very commonly reported; important for understanding mechanism, but not equivalent to efficiency.
  - **Norm compliance, punishment frequency, type of punishment (prosocial vs. antisocial):** Often reported, especially in larger or cross-cultural studies.
  - **Trust, reputation, group formation, coalition dynamics:** Reported in trust games, spatial models, threshold games, and observational studies.
  
**Distinction maintained:** Several papers show that increases in cooperation do not always translate into increased efficiency, especially when punishment is costly or antisocial punishment occurs (Handfield et al., 2016; Kubena et al., 2014).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Core empirical and theoretical findings for PGGs with punishment:

- **Enabling peer punishment in repeated PGGs often increases efficiency**, provided that:
  - The cost of punishment is sufficiently low and its impact sufficiently high (Roberts, 2013; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013).
  - The group is not too large and monitoring is not too noisy (Boyd et al., 2014; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
  - Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is rare or institutionally minimized (Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013; Farjam et al., 2015).
  - Defectors can be reliably identified, and second-order free-riding is controlled (Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Toriumi et al., 2016).
  - Resource environment (MPCR): punishment increases efficiency especially when MPCR is low (resources are scarce); when MPCR is high, enabling punishment can be neutral or even negative for efficiency (Farjam et al., 2015).
  - The design does not allow or mitigate retaliation/counter-punishment (Noussair & van Soest, 2014).
  
- **Effect size and direction are**:
  - **Substantial and positive:** In laboratory PGGs with standard design and effective punishment, efficiency with punishment can be much higher than the control (Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013).
  - **Mixed or negligible:** When monitoring is imperfect (noisy), enabling punishment can reduce efficiency below control because of misdirected (inefficient) punishment (van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
  - **Negative:** In environments with frequent antisocial punishment, high punishment cost, or strategic/tournament incentives, enabling punishment can fail to improve or may decrease efficiency (Handfield et al., 2016; Paál & Bereczkei, 2015).
  
- **Punishment structure and institutional details matter:**
  - Institutionally structured, rule-based, or collective punishment (including S-IR: institutionalized reciprocity) can outperform individual peer punishment in achieving high and stable efficiency (Ozono et al., 2016).
  - Voluntary participation and entry fees, when coupled with punishment, can result in near-maximal efficiency (Wang et al., 2015 [VPEF]).
  - The possibility of reward often complements or outperforms punishment alone in maximizing efficiency (Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013).
  - Ostracism or exclusion mechanisms, though not peer punishment per se, also robustly increase efficiency (Sääksvuori, 2014).
  
- **Empirical support for critical parameter dependencies:**
  - Efficiency effects of punishment are strongest when control (no-punishment) efficiency is low (Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Roberts, 2013; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013).
  - Where baseline cooperation is already high, the marginal gain from punishment may be modest or negative (Freytag et al., 2014).
  - Group size and relatedness (assortment) interact: in small, stable, related groups, punishment is more effective at increasing efficiency than in large, anonymous, or low-relatedness populations (Boyd et al., 2014).
  
- **Nonlinearities and exceptions:**
  - In public goods games with **increasing returns to scale** (IRS), punishment is especially effective at increasing efficiency; in standard linear PGGs, punishment may have low or unstable impact without IRS (Ye et al., 2016).
  - The relation between contribution increases and efficiency is **not always monotonic**: antisocial punishment, misapplied punishment, and high punishment frequency can lead to resource wastage and reduce efficiency, even as average contributions rise (Kubena et al., 2014; Handfield et al., 2016).
  - **Noise** (imperfect monitoring) sharply limits the positive effect of punishment; with high noise, punishment can be wasteful and reduce total welfare (van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
  
- **Findings from adjacent models:**
  - Network structure, heterogeneity, and institutional mechanisms can moderate or even substitute for peer punishment in achieving high efficiency (Xu et al., 2014; Sääksvuori, 2014; O'Connor, 2016; Ohdaira, 2017).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

## General guidance based on the literature:

- **Punishment-enabled efficiency is typically markedly higher than control efficiency,** **provided**:
  - Punishment is not prohibitively costly.
  - Defectors can be detected with low error/noise.
  - Antisocial punishment is absent or rare.
  - Group size is moderate (as group size increases, effect can be weaker or require institutional support).
  - The punishment technology is effective (cost-to-impact ratio is favorable).
  - MPCR is not so high that cooperation is already stable without punishment.

**Quantitative guidance:**  
Unlike some domains, laboratory and theory studies here routinely report substantial gains in group efficiency, sometimes approaching the cooperative optimum, when effective punishment is enabled (Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Roberts, 2013; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013; Wang et al., 2015 [VPEF])—but efficiency can stagnate or decline in edge cases involving misapplication, noise, or strategic antisocial use.

**Dimension for prediction models:**
- For **typical lab PGGs** (e.g., 4–6 players, 10–20 rounds, continuous contribution, full information, low-cost, high-impact punishment), **expect efficiency to shift from low/moderate in control to high in treatment** (Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Roberts, 2013). Use explicit cost/impact thresholds if parameters are available.
- In settings with peer identification, visible punishment, competition for rank, or potential for antisocial punishment, **downweight the positive expectation**; in some competitive or misaligned contexts, enabling punishment may have little or even negative efficiency impact (Kubena et al., 2014; Paál & Bereczkei, 2015; Handfield et al., 2016).
- **Noise in monitoring** is a strong negative moderator: with significant monitoring error, efficiency may fall **below control** when punishment is enabled due to wasted resources (van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
- When **communication/chat** is enabled, or when reciprocity/ostracism are possible mechanisms, these can **enhance or sometimes substitute for punishment** in driving efficiency (Noussair & van Soest, 2014; Sääksvuori, 2014).
- **Reward mechanisms**, especially when combined with punishment or voluntary participation (exit), can sometimes exceed the efficiency gains of punishment alone (Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013).

## For **downstream prediction**:  
- **Use design parameters** (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/impact, monitoring/noise, antisocial punishment risk, communication, default contribution, etc.) to modulate the predicted efficiency gain from punishment.
- **When control efficiency is already high** (due to high MPCR, strong communication/social norms, or other factors), enabling punishment will **usually yield small or no marginal efficiency gains**.
- **In atypical or competitive settings** (rank-based incentives, high antisocial punishment), **do not assume positive effects**; model risk of negative or null effect.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed**:
- **player_count**: Nearly all studies; clear theoretical and empirical dependence.
- **num_rounds**: Repeated vs. one-shot games are distinguished; repetition usually enhances punishment efficacy.
- **mpcr**: Central to nearly all models; strong moderator of efficiency.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Critical; many papers specify direct formulas or operational thresholds.
- **all_or_nothing** (vs. continuous contributions): Identified as moderator of punishment's impact (more pronounced in continuous games).
- **reward_exists** (and reward_tech/cost): Explicitly addressed in several papers, highlighting complementarities and sometimes superior efficacy.
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds**: Feedback/information available to players—covered in both theory and experiments.
- **chat**: Explicitly manipulated in some studies; robust positive effect on efficiency.

**Indirectly/contextually informed**:
- **default_contrib**: Framing effects addressed indirectly (e.g., opt-in vs. opt-out), though not as primary moderator of punishment’s impact.
- **show_punishment_id**: Covered in theoretical argument and some experimental designs, esp. in antisocial punishment or reputation contexts.

**Effectively missing or only sparse evidence**:
- **reward_cost, reward_tech**: Mentioned or manipulated only in a subset, more often as contextual rather than predictive dimensions.
- **show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: While some studies examine feedback and identification, quantitative evidence on their effect on efficiency with punishment is sparse.
- **Default contribution mechanisms**: Discussed (Fosgaard & Piovesan, 2015), but not tied directly to punishment effects on payoff.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited empirical quantification:** Only a subset of papers report both control and treatment efficiency with standard punishment, limiting the direct calibration of predictive models for efficiency change.
- **Parameter heterogeneity:** Empirical efficiency outcomes are highly sensitive to specific parameter values (punishment cost/intensity, group size, monitoring/noise, MPCR), but not all parameter combinations are covered; interpolation may be necessary.
- **Context-dependency:** Many theoretical and simulation-based findings may overstate or understate effect sizes relative to actual human behavior in the lab (e.g., perfect monitoring vs. real errors, availability of antisocial punishment).
- **Behavioral vs. efficiency outcomes:** Many studies measure only contribution/cooperation, not efficiency; in cases of costly or antisocial punishment, high cooperation may not produce high efficiency.
- **Boundary cases:** Efficiency gains from punishment do not generalize to all PGG variants—threshold games, noisy monitoring, dynamic or networked settings may yield mixed or negative results.
- **Adjacent evidence:** Several key moderators (e.g., reward, ostracism, institutional punishment) come from games not strictly PGGs.
- **Sparse coverage on some design dimensions:** Especially in reward cost/tech, identification, information feedback, and non-standard institutional mechanisms.

**Summary:**  
The literature provides strong, albeit context-dependent, evidence that enabling peer punishment in repeated, moderately sized PGGs typically increases efficiency, but this effect is highly sensitive to the details of game design and the control group’s baseline efficiency. Empirical generalizability is limited by gaps in direct measurement, parameter coverage, and the potential for boundary-case effects such as noise, antisocial punishment, or competitive incentives. Predictions should always account for these moderators and treat edge-case designs with appropriate caution.
