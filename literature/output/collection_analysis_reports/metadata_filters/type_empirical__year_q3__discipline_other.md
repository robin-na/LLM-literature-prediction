# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set comprises 10 empirical studies, with a moderate mix of laboratory experiments and field/observational studies. Of the set, four are laboratory experiments focused on public goods games (PGGs) or close variants, some including explicit punishment mechanisms (Engel, 2019; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Gomez-Ruiz & Sánchez-Expósito, 2020; Zhang et al., 2019). The rest are field/observational or contextually adjacent studies addressing collective action, social dilemmas, or related mechanisms (Ferguson, 2021; Qirko, 2020; Oria et al., 2018; Berger, 2021; Albergaria & Saes, 2018; Chang et al., 2021). 

Only a small subset directly analyzes efficiency (i.e., group-level payoff or welfare) as an outcome in environments with both punishment and public goods. Most experiments measure contributions or related behavioral proxies, with efficiency/earnings often secondary or only described. Several studies lack a direct PGG, formalized punishment/sanctioning, or payoff-based outcomes. Overall, the evidence base is relatively narrow and fragmented for making strong, direct predictions about the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like designs.

In summary, only one study (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021) provides exact-relevance evidence for all three task dimensions. Others have partial or adjacent relevance, particularly lacking direct analysis of efficiency effects tied to punishment in (variant) PGGs.

**Payoff-related outcomes (efficiency, group/individual earnings, welfare, total coins):**
- Directly and systematically measured as main outcome: De Geest & Kingsley (2021); Zhang et al. (2019) *(but no punishment)*
- Secondary/descriptive reporting of earnings: Engel (2019)
- Not measured: most other studies

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, cooperation rate, norm compliance, punishment frequency):**
- Primary/only outcome in most studies (Engel, 2019; Gomez-Ruiz & Sánchez-Expósito, 2020; Ferguson, 2021; Berger, 2021; Qirko, 2020; Albergaria & Saes, 2018; Oria et al., 2018; Chang et al., 2021)
- Often used to infer likelihood of efficiency changes, but are not direct measures

Distinction: Only a minority of studies measure actual group efficiency or earnings as the reported, primary outcome; the majority infer effects from contribution/cooperation rates or punishment behaviors.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Findings on Efficiency:**
- **Punishment and Efficiency Contingent on Context (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021):**  
  - In a PGG-variant with a nonlinear common-pool resource (CPR), peer punishment reduced group extraction. However, efficiency consequences depended on endowment inequality.  
    - *Equal endowments:* Punishment reduced average earnings (lower efficiency) due to high punishment expenditures and poor coordination.
    - *Unequal endowments:* Punishment led to higher efficiency for disadvantaged members and did not lower average group earnings, as it enabled better norm targeting and coordination.
  - Therefore, the efficiency effect of punishment is **not uniformly positive** and can be negative without salient inequality or coordination focal points.

- **Transparency/Feedback Effects (Engel, 2019):**  
  - When individual-level feedback on both contributions and punishment is provided, contributions (and descriptively, earnings) are lower than with aggregate or less detailed feedback.
  - Signals about what others contribute ("descriptive norms") matter more than punishment per se for sustaining cooperation.
  - High-visibility punishment can backfire by undermining cooperation.
  - Efficiency is not directly analyzed, but implication is that punishment does **not** necessarily improve group payoff when transparency is high, especially in the context of centralized punishment.

**Behavioral Effects and Boundary Conditions:**
- **Coordination, Norms, and Informal Mechanisms:**  
  - Consistent contributors (Zhang et al., 2019) and strong team identity among men (Gomez-Ruiz & Sánchez-Expósito, 2020) can sustain high contribution rates and efficiency in the *absence* of punishment, highlighting the impact of informal motivators.
  - In observational studies of real-world groups (Qirko, 2020; Oria et al., 2018), the existence of punishment mechanisms does not guarantee their use or an efficiency gain; trust and context determine effectiveness.

- **Preferences for Punishment vs. Compensation (Ferguson, 2021):**  
  - Individuals often prefer compensation over punishment as a norm-enforcing tool, and the mere presence of punishment options does not ensure their use or positive welfare effects.

- **Norm Feedback, Social Dynamics, and Tipping (Berger, 2021):**  
  - Regular normative feedback can cause substantial shifts—positive *or* negative—in group behavior, depending on initial norms, independent of punishment.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct Guidance:**  
- When using this literature base to predict the *absolute* effect on efficiency of enabling peer punishment in a PGG-like environment, *do not assume a uniform increase in efficiency*. 
  - If the baseline (control) efficiency is already relatively high, or if groups lack salient heterogeneity/coordination cues, punishment may lead to <u>no improvement or even a decrease in efficiency due to over-spending on sanctions and failed norm targeting</u> (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Engel, 2019).
  - If endowment inequality or another salient norm-focusing feature is present, punishment is more likely to improve efficiency, primarily by protecting the welfare of disadvantaged group members and focusing norm enforcement (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).
  - High transparency (very visible punishment) may undermine benefits, potentially reducing efficiency compared to less detailed feedback, even if punishment mechanisms are available (Engel, 2019).
- Baseline rates of cooperation sustained by informal means (team identity, role models, feedback alone) may substitute for the effect of punishment; the incremental efficiency gain from punishment may be limited when these features are present (Gomez-Ruiz & Sánchez-Expósito, 2020; Zhang et al., 2019).

**Indirect Guidance:**  
- Contextual factors such as group trust, size, leadership, and motivation shape punishment's effectiveness (Oria et al., 2018; Qirko, 2020).
- The *use* of punishment, not just its availability, may be infrequent or context-dependent; models should be sensitive to the possibility that enabling punishment will not translate into active enforcement (Qirko, 2020).
- Predicting from design dimensions should account for the social and informational environment, not only formal parameters.

**Payoff vs. Behavior:**  
- Predictions based solely on changes in cooperation or punishment assignment are not good proxies for group payoff or efficiency, as excessive punishment can erode any efficiency gains from higher cooperation rates.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

The following game design dimensions are directly or indirectly informed by the literature:

**Directly Informed:**
- `player_count` (most studies)
- `num_rounds` (most studies)
- `mpcr` (many lab experiments)
- `all_or_nothing` (binary or continuous contribution analyzed in multiple labs)
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Engel, 2019; Ferguson, 2021)
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` (Engel, 2019)

**Indirectly Informed / Contextually Discussed:**
- `chat` (occasionally manipulated as part of feedback or communication, Engel, 2019; Gomez-Ruiz & Sánchez-Expósito, 2020)
- `reward_exists` and related parameters (considered in a few adjacent studies)
- Endowment inequality (not a column per se but a salient moderator—closely parallels elements like `default_contrib` and player role heterogeneity)

**Missing or Sparse:**
- `default_contrib` (rare/no direct discussion of default framing)
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `reward_exists` (not addressed by included lab studies)
- `show_n_rounds` (occasionally mentioned as a manipulation but not as a main focus)
- Some technical/implementation details (timing, interface, etc.) absent

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Scarcity of Direct Evidence on Efficiency:** Most studies measure behavioral outcomes (contribution, norm compliance), not group payoff or efficiency as a primary outcome, especially under punishments.
- **Heterogeneity of Game Design:** Very few studies investigate the full range of the 14 design dimensions; most manipulate only a small subset, limiting the granularity of prediction for parameter changes.
- **Limited Evidence for Peer Punishment (vs. Centralized):** Some studies use centralized punishment or third-party punishment rather than true peer punishment, which may alter predicted efficiency effects.
- **Context-Dependence and External Validity:** Findings from lab games may not generalize to real-world collective action problems, where factors like trust, enforceability, and group heterogeneity are crucial (Oria et al., 2018; Qirko, 2020).
- **Lack of Factorial/Interaction Evidence:** Most studies do not explore interactions (e.g., between group size, visibility, and punishment technology), making it difficult to infer combinatorial effects.
- **Ambiguity or Mixed Results:** Where efficiency outcomes are measured, the effect of punishment can be positive, negative, or neutral depending on context (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021), and highly sensitive to game parameters and group characteristics.
- **Sparse Evidence on Rewarding, Defaults, and Interface Features:** Most design dimensions involving rewards, default contributions, or user interface visibility are not systematically studied in relation to efficiency effects.

**Summary:**  
This literature set supports prediction that the effect of enabling peer punishment on group efficiency in PGG-like settings is highly contingent and context-dependent. It cannot provide generalizable, parameter-specific efficiency uplift estimates; most evidence points to non-monotonic, moderator-dependent punishment effects, with possible efficiency reductions under some conditions and improvements under others—especially when punishment becomes well-targeted through group heterogeneity or salient norms (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021). Direct predictions from design dimension changes to efficiency outcomes should therefore be made with caution, and always with attention to context and mechanism.
