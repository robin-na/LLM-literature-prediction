# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set is **broad and rich**, spanning 157 papers—primarily **theoretical models** but including a substantial number of **empirical laboratory experiments** (most with high internal validity). There is a heavy emphasis on evolutionary game theory, agent-based simulations, and analytical decision models, with close mapping to **public goods game (PGG) structures** featuring variants in population structure, punishment and reward mechanisms, and production technologies. Among empirical studies, most manipulate punishment in lab PGGs, varying parameters like group size, rounds, and cost structures. **Payoff/efficiency outcomes** are measured or modeled in a large portion of these papers, although many others focus primarily on cooperation rates or behavioral strategies rather than direct measures of group payoff or efficiency.

The set is **well-suited** to address the downstream prediction task: most papers directly manipulate the presence/absence or structure of punishment and link it to payoff-based outcomes. Nonetheless, several papers use models or experimental designs *adjacent* to PGGs—such as repeated PDs, indirect reciprocity, or common-pool resource environments—requiring caution in mapping their results to canonical PGG prediction. There is a strong representation of mechanism-focused work, including discussions of anti-social punishment, retaliation, group structure, and institutional features; however, nuances in communication, information structure, and certain game design dimensions are treated sporadically.

# 2) Task Relevance

### a) pgg_or_variant
**Relevance:** _exact/close_ for the majority of the set. Most studies are framed around canonical linear PGGs or minimal variants (e.g., threshold/weakest-link games, structured populations). Some important evidence comes from closely related settings (e.g., common-pool resources, iterated PD, or indirect reciprocity); such papers are labeled as _close_ or _adjacent_ but remain informative when mapping outcomes to PGGs.

### b) punishment_or_sanctions
**Relevance:** _exact_ or _close_ for nearly all central studies. Papers either manipulate _directly_ the presence or structure of peer or institutional punishment or analyze closely related sanctioning mechanisms (e.g., reward, exclusion, reputation loss). Coverage of anti-social punishment, retaliation, and counter-punishment/second-order punishment is substantial and exact; however, some models rely on exclusion or partner selection as functionally analogous to punishment, which is _adjacent/intermediate_ in relevance.

### c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome
**Relevance:** 
- _exact:_ Many papers directly report group efficiency (payoff ratio relative to full cooperation), group payoff, or welfare outcomes.
- _close:_ Some report contribution rates or the abundance of cooperative strategies as a proxy for efficiency, which is a caveat since contributions are *not* equivalent to realized efficiency when punishment is costly or when antisocial punishment occurs.
- _adjacent/weak:_ About a third of the set focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation frequency, punishment assigned) or theoretical stability states without directly reporting efficiency or total payoff.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

### a) Payoff-Related Outcomes
- **Measured directly:** Many theory and empirical papers focus on *efficiency* (group total payoff relative to the fully cooperative optimum), group earnings, welfare, surplus, or total resource generated.
- **Measured indirectly:** Some studies infer efficiency from average contribution rates, but this only maps directly to payoff when punishment is absent or pro-social and not costly; in the presence of costly punishment or antisocial punishment, efficiency may be *lower* even if contribution rates remain high.
- **Missing:** Several behavioral and mechanism papers analyze only strategy frequencies, evolutionary stability, or the prevalence of punishment/defection, without reporting group payoffs.

### b) Non-payoff (Behavioral) Outcomes
- **Cooperation/contribution rates:** Ubiquitous and detailed across the set, often reported alongside, but not replacing, efficiency.
- **Punishment metrics:** Frequency, magnitude, target (pro-social/anti-social), and counter-punishment are reported in many studies.
- **Norm compliance, exclusion, preference data:** Frequent, especially in social norm, neural, and institutional design studies.
- **Reputation, information processing:** Featured in adjacent models, especially in work on indirect reciprocity, exclusion, and signaling.
- **Psychological/neural correlates:** A minority focus, but distinctly separated from payoff outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Synthesis of Cross-Paper Findings (Focusing on Payoff/Efficiency Where Available):

- **Typical (linear, repeated PGGs):** Most *theoretical* and *experimental* PGG papers find that enabling peer punishment increases cooperation and, when antisocial punishment is rare and punishment is not too costly, increases long-run group efficiency relative to no-punishment baselines [(Gintis, 2000; Sparks et al., 2024; Eldakar et al., 2007; Fischer et al., 2016)].
- **Punishment Cost/Effectiveness:** Efficiency gains from enabling punishment strongly depend on the punishment cost and effectiveness parameters [(Okada & Bingham, 2008; Wang & Lv, 2019; Gintis, 2000)]. Low-cost, high-effectiveness punishment produces the strongest and most robust efficiency gains.
- **Anti-social Punishment:** When anti-social punishment (punishing cooperators) is possible and prevalent, or when enforcement institutions are corrupt/untrustworthy, enabling punishment can result in *no gain* or a *loss* in efficiency [(Rand et al., 2010; Hauser et al., 2014; Fatas & Mateu, 2015)].
- **Game Structure Effects:** The production function (e.g., linear vs. threshold/weakest-link) and population structure modulate punishment's effect: in threshold or complementary production games, punishment has a larger positive effect on efficiency, while in standard linear games, antisocial punishment can offset gains [(Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Archetti & Scheuring, 2013)].
- **Retaliation and Counter-Punishment:** Retaliation by punished individuals (second-order punishment) can suppress the efficiency benefit of punishment, particularly in repeated games with easy punisher identification [(Wolff, 2012; Janssen & Bushman, 2008)].
- **Information Structure/Communication:** Efficiency effects of punishment are amplified when players can communicate about or coordinate on norm enforcement; communication reduces efficiency loss due to counter-punishment and supports higher group earnings [(Andrighetto et al., 2016)].
- **Population Structure and Institution Type:** Efficacy of punishment depends on whether the institution is peer-based or centralized: centralization can reduce perverse punishment, but doesn't guarantee higher efficiency. Population structure (well-mixed vs. spatial/group-based) can amplify or dampen punishment’s effects [(Fischer et al., 2016; Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017)].
- **Synergies with Reward, Reputation, and Visibility:** Combined use of punishment and reward, or the addition of reputational mechanisms and public feedback about cooperation and punishment, yields greater efficiency than punishment alone in most designs [(Cressman et al., 2012; Milinski & Rockenbach, 2012)].
- **Probabilistic or Graduated Punishment:** Graduated or probabilistic punishment schemes (not always punishing, or scaling with defect severity) can achieve high efficiency at lower collective cost, especially in heterogeneous or error-prone populations [(Jiao et al., 2020; Iwasa & Lee, 2013; Couto et al., 2020)].
- **Corruption, Insurance, and Enforcement Failures:** When incentive-avoiding strategies (insurance, bribery, corrupt enforcement) are available and effective, the efficiency gain from punishment is sharply reduced or eliminated unless these loopholes are closed [(Huang et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2013)].
- **Parameter Extremes (Size, Cost):** Very large group sizes, extremely high punishment cost, or one-shot interactions sharply diminish or even reverse the efficiency effect of enabling punishment [(Eldakar et al., 2007; Powers et al., 2012; Kurokawa, 2023)].

## Disagreement and Ambiguity
- **Some empirical studies find that enabling punishment increases efficiency only in the long term, as initial punishment costs outweigh gains (Sparks et al., 2024); in others, punishment’s cost never recoups the efficiency lost to punishment itself [(Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Fischer et al., 2016)].**
- **Anti-social punishment is highly variable across contexts/cultures, leading to substantial heterogeneity in efficiency outcomes with punishment [(Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Rand et al., 2010)].**
- **In group-structured or spatial models, punishment may increase, decrease, or have no net effect on efficiency, depending on the precise interaction of local structure, update rules, and cost structures [(Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Wolff, 2012)].**

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For predicting the *average efficiency* with peer punishment enabled, given the game parameters and the efficiency of the control (no-punishment) condition:**

- **Enabling peer punishment in standard, repeated, small-to-medium group public goods games typically increases efficiency relative to control, especially when:**
  - Punishment is sufficiently effective (high impact per cost).
  - Punishment is not prohibitively costly for punishers.
  - Anti-social punishment is rare or not possible.
  - The information structure supports accurate targeting (but not easy retaliation) and possibly public feedback.
  - The group is not extremely large (where diffusion of responsibility and collective action problems undermine punishment's efficacy).
  - Communication is allowed, further amplifying positive efficiency effects.

- **The efficiency boost from punishment is *modulated* by:**
  - Punishment cost and effectiveness (dimensions: punishment_cost, punishment_tech).
  - Group size (player_count), with diminishing returns or even net efficiency losses in large groups unless other institutional supports exist.
  - Number of rounds (num_rounds): longer repeated games allow punishment to have cumulative efficiency gains; single-shot or very short games show little/net-negative effects.
  - Production function (mpcr): higher MPCRs reduce the need for punishment; threshold/weakest-link functions amplify gains from punishment.
  - Information structure and communication (chat, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id): communication and feedback about actions and outcomes support efficiency gains.
  - Structure and institution type (peer vs. centralized punishment, corruption/bribery possibility): centralized and honest enforcement can increase or stabilize efficiency, but only if corruption is low and institutional design prevents anti-social or arbitrary punishment.
  - Probability and magnitude of anti-social punishment or retaliation: efficiency gains are negated or reversed when anti-social punishment or easy retaliation occurs.

- **Baseline (control) efficiency is an important predictor of treatment efficiency:** If efficiency in the control game is already high, the marginal gain from adding punishment may be small or negative (due to the punitive costs), especially if antisocial punishment occurs. If control efficiency is low, and the above moderators support effective prosocial punishment, the efficiency gain is typically larger.

- **Additional institutional features, such as the presence of rewards or combined reward-punishment schemes, almost always further raise efficiency if implemented at reasonable cost.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `all_or_nothing`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`
  - Extensively modeled: Effects of group size, rounds, cost/tech of punishment on efficiency are frequently quantified.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`
  - Modeled in many theory and some empirical papers, especially in contrast/combination with punishment.
- `chat` (communication): 
  - Directly manipulated in several experiments—communication generally enhances efficiency, especially in punishment-enabled games.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`
  - Information and feedback structures are modeled or manipulated in some studies; punishment effectiveness is often higher with greater transparency (but sometimes more vulnerable to retaliation).

**Indirectly/contextually discussed:**
- `default_contrib` (opt-in/opt-out framing):
  - Rarely modeled directly; a few papers comment on the psychological framing’s effect on cooperation but do not link it robustly to efficiency under punishment.
- `punishment_exists`
  - Universally manipulated across the main set as a treatment variable.

**Sparse/missing:**
- Empirical/explicit comparisons across: 
  - Variations in default contribution framing (`default_contrib`), detailed punishment magnitude structure (`punishment_magnitude` beyond tech/cost), nuances in multi-dimensional feedback (`show_punishment_id` with anonymity effects).
- Interactive effects between multiple simultaneous dimensions (e.g., chat and identification, or combined reward and nuanced punishment in large, real populations) are infrequently addressed.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Dependence on Mechanism Models:** Many findings are theoretical or agent-based simulations; empirical generalization may be constrained by unmodeled social, psychological, or institutional variables (e.g., real-world norm enforcement complexity, transparency, retaliation capacity).
- **Payoff-Behavior Distinction:** Several studies measure only cooperation/contribution/punishment frequencies, *not efficiency*; in cases of costly or antisocial punishment, behavioral increases do not map directly to payoff gains.
- **Anti-social Punishment and Cultural Variability:** Observed strong cultural and contextual heterogeneity in the prevalence and effectiveness of antisocial punishment, with some populations/institutions showing net efficiency losses with punishment.
- **Game Structure Generality:** Most robust positive efficiency effects from punishment occur in linear, small-group, repeated PGGs without corruption or strong anti-social punishment. Effects may be absent, reversed, or ambiguous in:
  - Large groups.
  - Threshold or highly nonlinear production games.
  - Institutional environments with corruption, bribery, insurance against punishment, or strong anti-social punishment.
  - One-shot or very short games.
- **Limited Multi-Dimensional Interaction:** Sparse coverage of:
  - How multiple game design dimensions interact in non-additive ways to determine treatment efficiency.
  - Real-world noise, communication, and unmeasured psychological factors.
- **Incomplete Dimension Coverage:** Some predictor dimensions (e.g., default contribution framing) are effectively missing.
- **Mapping from Behavior to Payoff:** In several adjacent or mechanism-focused works, efficiency must be inferred from strategy prevalence, which can mislead if punishment is frequent and costly or if sanctioning is misaligned.
- **Ambiguity and Ambivalent Evidence:** Where empirical and theory results conflict (e.g., punishment’s effect under intermediate noise or varying institutional centralization), guidance must be interpreted as context-sensitive.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature strongly supports the prediction that, under commonly studied PGG conditions, enabling peer punishment increases group efficiency relative to control—but this effect is tightly moderated by punishment cost/effectiveness, population structure, institutional and information design, the possibility of anti-social punishment, game repetition, and to a lesser extent, communication and feedback. Prediction should treat these moderators explicitly, and, where efficiency is not directly measured, carefully separate behavioral from payoff-based outcomes. Some key prediction dimensions are thoroughly covered (costs, group size, rounds), while others are sparse or contextually discussed only. In application, ambiguity should be preserved when the evidence base is equivocal, especially regarding anti-social punishment, retaliation, and institutional failures.
