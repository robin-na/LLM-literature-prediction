# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 48 papers with a mix of empirical (primarily experimental) and theoretical works. The empirical studies are dominated by lab experiments, with some field and naturalistic studies, while the theoretical contributions use analytical models and computational simulations. The coverage is relatively broad in terms of game structures, contexts (lab, field, social dilemmas, trust games, common-pool resources), and intervention types (punishment, reward, communication, monitoring), though the majority center on public-goods game (PGG) or very close variants. Some papers discuss adjacent environments (e.g., Prisoner's Dilemma, trust games, minimum effort/coordination games), with mechanisms closely related to punishment and cooperation. The evidence base includes both direct tests of punishment's effect on group efficiency and indirect analyses focusing on behavioral outcomes or moderators of efficiency.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**  
- **exact**: Many papers address standard public goods games or CPR games that are strategically equivalent (e.g., Gürerk et al., 2009; Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Wang et al., 2010; Dejong et al., 2008).
- **close/adjacent**: Several papers use close analogues (n-person Prisoner’s Dilemma, minimum effort, trust games), offering contextually relevant findings but sometimes with non-identical incentive structures (e.g., Dugar, 2010; POLLOCK, 1988; Masclet & Pénard, 2012).
- **none/weak**: A small number are more distantly related (e.g., ultimatum game, firm cooperation models).

**punishment_or_sanctions**  
- **exact**: Most directly informative works employ some form of peer or institutional punishment as an explicit design element.
- **close/adjacent**: Some studies analyze alternative enforcement or social sanction mechanisms (monitoring, shaming, reputation, reward), which often function similarly to punishment.
- **none**: Control or baseline studies without punishment primarily inform on the baseline (no-punishment) condition.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**
- **exact**: A significant subset of papers directly measures efficiency, group earnings, welfare, or surplus (e.g., Dejong et al., 2008; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; Wang et al., 2010; Noailly et al., 2009).
- **close/adjacent**: Many papers focus primarily on cooperation/contribution rates or strategy frequencies rather than efficiency per se, but discuss payoff implications or provide enough structure to map to efficiency in PGGs (e.g., Kocher et al., 2012; Gao et al., 2012).
- **none**: Several papers, while potentially informative about underlying behaviors, do not provide payoff or efficiency outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (directly informative):**
- *Efficiency*: Ratio of actual to maximum possible group payoff given full cooperation (e.g., Gürerk et al., 2009; Dejong et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2010; Fatas et al., 2010).
- *Group Payoff / Earnings / Surplus*: Reported directly as experimental or simulated group earnings or total coins generated (e.g., Noailly et al., 2009, 2007; Guillen et al., 2007).
- *Related welfare measures*: Sometimes deduced from provision rates or resource extraction (e.g., Hayo & Vollan, 2012; Masclet & Pénard, 2012).

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (adjacent/indirect):**
- *Contribution/Cooperation rate*: Most common, reflecting the proportion contributed to the public good (often used as a proxy, but distinct from efficiency).
- *Punishment frequency, norm compliance, etc.*: Frequency and patterns of punishment or sanctions, expectations about others' behavior (often linked to efficiency but not a substitute).
- *Provision rates*: For step-level games, rate at which the public good is provided (proxy for efficiency only in threshold games).

_Some papers explicitly caution that increased contributions may not yield higher efficiency due to the direct costs of punishment (e.g., Kocher et al., 2012; Fatas et al., 2010)._

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesized across papers with exact or close relevance:**

- **Punishment can increase efficiency:**  
  - Multiple sources (theoretical and empirical) consistently report that enabling punishment in PGG or CPR games increases efficiency/group payoff, especially relative to Nash equilibrium in the absence of punishment (Dejong et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2010; Noailly et al., 2009; Sigmund et al., 2011).
  - The effect is especially pronounced in repeated games, if baseline cooperation/efficiency without punishment is low, or if structural conditions (e.g., local interactions, group partitioning) facilitate effective punishment (POLLOCK, 1988; Noailly et al., 2009).

- **Efficiency gains are not universal and are context/moderator-dependent:**  
  - If punishment is costly and/or poorly targeted, efficiency gains may be limited or even negative. In some cases, punishment increases contributions but reduces group earnings due to the cost of punishing (Kocher et al., 2012; Fatas et al., 2010).
  - The structure of the punishment/reward mechanism critically shapes outcomes: centralized leader punishment, peer punishment, pool (institutional) punishment, and their cost/impact ratios have distinct effects (Gürerk et al., 2009; Sigmund et al., 2011).

- **Baseline (control) efficiency is a strong moderator:**  
  - When efficiency is already high in the control, adding punishment may yield little or even negative marginal benefit due to punishment costs and the risk of antisocial or misdirected punishment.
  - If control efficiency is low (near Nash), punishment tends to yield larger relative increases in efficiency.
  
- **Role of social, psychological, and structural moderators:**  
  - Social preferences (altruism, reciprocity), patience (discount factor), group size, network structure, observability, and voluntary participation all moderate the effectiveness and efficiency of punishment (Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Haag & Lagunoff, 2006; Sigmund et al., 2011).
  - Strong evidence exists that the network or spatial structure (local vs. global punishment) and the possibility of group partitioning enable higher efficiency via punishment (POLLOCK, 1988; Noailly et al., 2009, 2007).
  - Inefficiency can occur if punishment is used antisocially (e.g., punishing cooperators) or if high costs of punishment are not offset by sufficiently increased contributions (Kocher et al., 2012).

- **Reward and mixed incentive mechanisms:**  
  - In several cases, reward leads to equal or higher efficiency than punishment, since rewards do not entail a destruction of resources (Gürerk et al., 2009; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011).
  - Net positive reward (where the recipient's gain exceeds giver’s cost) can sometimes increase efficiency more than punishment (Vyrastekova & van Soest, 2008); the reverse is true if punishment more effectively sustains cooperation.

- **Empirical-mechanism arguments:**  
  - The path-dependence of incentive schemes: history and prior experience (switching from reward to punishment, order effects) matter for outcomes (Gürerk et al., 2009; Guillen et al., 2007).
  - Implementation details (e.g., cost of punishment, visibility, identity of assignors) and the presence of communication or public information modulate the net efficiency effect.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design:**  
  - **If control efficiency is low** (i.e., high levels of defection): enabling peer punishment is likely to increase efficiency, potentially substantially, as punishment deters free-riding and supports high-contribution equilibria (Dejong et al., 2008; Noailly et al., 2009).
  - **If control efficiency is high** (i.e., contributions already high): enabling punishment may have little effect or may even reduce efficiency if punishment is misdirected or if the direct costs of punishment outweigh marginal gains in cooperation (Kocher et al., 2012; Fatas et al., 2010).
  - **Cost and effectiveness of punishment**: Lower punishment cost/greater impact magnifies gains (up to a point), but excessive or antisocial punishment negates benefits. Reward mechanisms may sometimes match or outperform punishment in producing high efficiency without resource destruction (Gürerk et al., 2009).
  - **Game structure and context**: Structural moderators such as player count, number of rounds, MPCR, voluntary participation, observability, network structure, and communication can all shift the impact. Pool punishment and voluntary participation tend to yield higher and more stable efficiency than peer punishment with compulsory participation (Sigmund et al., 2011).
  - **Behavioral vs. efficiency outcomes**: While most studies find increased cooperation/contribution when punishment is enabled, this does not always translate into increased efficiency—the cost and distribution of punishment matters critically. Direct measures of efficiency should be prioritized, not inferred from contributions alone.

- **Quantitative inferences**: Several models provide explicit prescriptions or formulas linking design parameters (e.g., MPCR, punishment cost/tech) to expected efficiency (Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Wang et al., 2010; Dejong et al., 2008).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions** (supported by multiple empirical/theoretical studies):
- **player_count**: Effects of group size; larger groups tend to need more structured punishment or monitoring to sustain efficiency (Dejong et al., 2008; Sigmund et al., 2011).
- **num_rounds**: Repetition is vital for punishment to work as threat; longer horizons enable more effective punishment equilibria (Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Wang et al., 2010).
- **mpcr**: Explicitly analyzed in most models/experiments as a core moderator (Gürerk et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2010).
- **punishment_cost and punishment_tech** (cost-to-impact): Multiple papers analyze sensitivity to these parameters (Wang et al., 2010; Kocher et al., 2012; Sigmund et al., 2011).
- **all_or_nothing**: Several models analyze binary vs. continuous contributions (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Dejong et al., 2008).
- **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech**: Explored especially in contrast to punishment (Gürerk et al., 2009; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; Vyrastekova & van Soest, 2008).
- **show_other_summaries/show_n_rounds**: Addressed in both experimental controls and theoretical models (Bolle & Vogel, 2011; Cadigan et al., 2011).

**Indirect/contextually informed dimensions**:
- **chat**: Presence/absence of communication, explored as a moderator (Guillen et al., 2007; Kocher et al., 2012).
- **show_punishment_id**: Punisher anonymity/visibility is discussed, though not always systematically varied.
- **default_contrib**: Some studies consider opt-in/opt-out and framing (Rebers & Koopmans, 2012).

**Sparse or missing**:
- **Punishment technology detail**: Some models specify exact punishment functions, but many experiments use simple 1:3 or 1:1 cost-benefit ratios.
- **Complex institution or hybrid mechanisms**: While pool and peer punishment are compared, finer variations are less systematically explored.
- **Social context variables**: Not formally encoded in prediction dimensions but acknowledged as significant moderators (Kocher et al., 2012).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Distinction between contributions and efficiency**: Many experimental papers report increased cooperation or contributions but do not always report corresponding payoff or efficiency outcomes. This can mislead if punishment is costly or misapplied.
- **Parameterization gaps**: Some relevant design dimensions (especially nuanced punishment/reward tech, social context, individual heterogeneity) are only partially mapped; direct empirical exploration of these dimensions is sporadic.
- **Social and institutional context effects**: Strong evidence exists that social background, group norms, and cultural context shape the efficiency impact of punishment but these are not captured in the main prediction dimensions.
- **Antisocial punishment and misdirected sanctions**: Several papers highlight the risk that punishment reduces efficiency when applied antisocially or when baseline cooperation is already high, yet these effects are not always systematically measured.
- **Pool vs. peer punishment, centralized vs. decentralized institutions**: Differentiation exists but is not always cleanly mapped to prediction dimensions.
- **Adjacency of some studies**: A notable subset of the literature is based on games adjacent to—but not structurally identical with—PGG, requiring careful transfer of conclusions.
- **Absence of clear quantitative effect sizes across full design space**: While several papers offer explicit predictions for specific parameter regimes, the literature does not cover all combinations of the 14 dimensions, leaving gaps for new or complex game designs.
- **Insufficient attention to long-term dynamics and learning**: Learning, history, and path-dependence are acknowledged, but few studies systematically study their impact on efficiency effects.
- **No direct coverage** of some prediction dimensions (e.g., default_contrib, show_punishment_id) or intersectional moderators.

**Conclusion:**  
The literature robustly supports the qualitative expectation that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments generally increases group efficiency when baseline efficiency is low and punishment is well-targeted and not excessively costly. However, the effect is contingent on the exact game structure, baseline efficiency, cost/effectiveness of punishment, and broader social or network context. Several design dimensions are well covered (player count, rounds, mpcr, punishment cost/tech), while others remain sparse or unaddressed. Prediction for new designs should prioritize mapping to directly measured efficiency outcomes rather than inferring from increased contributions alone.
