# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large (N=162) and encompasses a wide array of both **empirical (laboratory and field experiments)** and **theoretical** studies focused on cooperation, punishment, and efficiency in **public goods games (PGGs)** and closely related social dilemmas. The majority of the most directly relevant evidence comes from lab experiments and theoretical modeling on PGGs with and without punishment. The set is comprehensive for canonical PGGs and institutional/punishment mechanisms, though field studies and some variants (CPRs, reward games, mutualisms) are also included.

Most empirical studies directly measure **efficiency/earnings/welfare** or close proxies (group profit, surplus). Our prediction task—estimation of **average group efficiency with peer punishment enabled vs. disabled**—is therefore well-anchored by this literature, especially for standard PGGs and peer punishment. However, **some important design dimensions** are rarely or only indirectly addressed, and there is noted ambiguity and mixed findings around moderators such as cultural context, group competition, and antisocial punishment.

# 2) Task Relevance

## a) `pgg_or_variant`

- **Exact relevance:** The bulk of the evidence comes from public goods games or very close variants (CPR, threshold PGGs, institutional PGGs), directly matching the prediction context.
- **Adjacent/weak relevance:** Some studies model related social dilemmas (Prisoner's Dilemma, Trust Game, Indirect Reciprocity), which are cited for mechanistic insight but not direct quantitative prediction.

## b) `punishment_or_sanctions`

- **Exact relevance:** Many papers manipulate the presence/absence and technology of punishment, often peer-to-peer, but also institutional or pool punishment. These match the prediction task directly.
- **Close/adjacent:** About 10–20% address only reward, reputation, ostracism, or indirect sanctions, useful for mechanism context but not direct effect sizes.
- **None:** A minority do not include any punishment/sanction mechanism and are not informative for treatment-control efficiency prediction.

## c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`

- **Exact/close relevance:** In the best papers, **group efficiency, group earnings, welfare, or surplus** (as a fraction of maximum possible) are primary or secondary outcomes. Many provide effect sizes for treatment-control efficiency shifts.
- **Adjacent/weak:** A subset only report cooperation/contribution rates, punishment frequency, etc., with claimable inference about efficiency where monotonic relationships exist, but only cautiously.
- **None:** Some behavioral or neural studies report only punishment allocation or motive without any connection to efficiency.

**Conclusion:** The core of the literature is highly relevant, but findings are often **moderated by design nuances, contextual features, and behavioral phenomena** (e.g., antisocial punishment, retaliation).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

## a) **Payoff-related Outcomes (directly linked to task)**
- **Efficiency**, defined as the ratio of group payoff to the fully cooperative optimum (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; O'Gorman et al., 2009; Traulsen et al., 2012; Sääksvuori et al., 2011).
- **Group/individual earnings, mean group profit, welfare, or surplus** (e.g., Egas & Riedl, 2008; Cressman et al., 2012).
- **Group achievement in threshold games or CPRs** (e.g., Pacheco et al., 2014; Gelcich et al., 2013).

## b) **Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes (distinguished from payoffs)**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates** (extensively reported, but not a substitute for efficiency except in full-information, linear PGGs).
- **Punishment frequency or allocation** (important for mechanism but not welfare/efficiency).
- **Norm compliance, reputation scores, third-party punishment/reward, strategy abundance** (adjacent, not direct efficiency outcomes).

## c) **Ambiguities**
- In some studies, only behavioral outcomes are reported, requiring **cautious interpretation** regarding efficiency (often as a hypothesis/conclusion, not a measured result).
- **Cultural studies** often infer efficiency from differences in contributions or punishment patterns but lack direct payoff reporting.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### General Patterns:
- **Enabling punishment in classic, repeated, well-mixed PGGs**:
    - **Increases efficiency** (relative to control) in most cases where antisocial punishment and retaliation are absent and punishment cost is moderate (Gächter et al., 2017; O'Gorman et al., 2009; Cressman et al., 2012; Gintis, 2000).
    - **Magnitude**: Often improvement from low-moderate (e.g., .3–.5) to high (e.g., .7–.9) efficiency when punishment is moderate/effective and used to maintain cooperation.
- **Crowding-out and net costs**:
    - In some lab and field studies, **punishment increases contributions but reduces net efficiency**, as punishment costs outweigh cooperation gains (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Guala, 2012).
    - **Efficiency gains depend** on balance of increased cooperation versus punishment costs; only if punishment is **rare, well-targeted, and effective** do net gains occur.
- **Institutional/pool punishment and second-order punishment**:
    - Pool punishment (pre-committed sanctions) can **stabilize cooperation** but sometimes at high efficiency cost (Traulsen et al., 2012).
    - **Second-order punishing** (punishing non-punishers) tends to be especially costly, exacerbating efficiency loss.
- **Key moderators (empirically/theoretically supported):**
    - **Punishment cost/effectiveness ratio** is critical: cheap, high-impact punishment increases efficiency; costly, low-impact does not (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Gintis, 2000).
    - **Group competition** (e.g., intergroup contests) dramatically increases the positive effect of punishment on efficiency (Sääksvuori et al., 2011).
    - **Group size and number of rounds**: Larger groups and shorter games reduce the positive effect, but punishment can expand the parameter range for sustaining efficiency (BOYD & RICHERSON, 1992).
    - **Possibility of antisocial punishment/retaliation**: If allowed, punishment may **reduce** or eliminate efficiency gains (Rand et al., 2010; Hauser et al., 2014).
    - **Cultural context**: Efficiency effects of punishment are variable and sometimes reversed across cultures due to prevalence of antisocial punishment (Gächter & Herrmann, 2009, 2010).
    - **Institutional design**: Crowd-in effects require self-determination; externally imposed punishment can reduce efficiency in high-trust groups (Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011).
    - **Reputation and observability**: Efficiency is maximized when punishment is reputationally visible and used as a threat (dos Santos et al., 2011, 2013), or when punishment identity is shown.
    - **Communication/chat**: Empirical evidence shows chat can substitute for, or complement, punishment in maintaining efficiency (Anderies et al., 2011).
    - **Reward presence**: When both punishment and reward are present, cooperation may be maximized with more efficient outcomes from reward (Rand & Nowak, 2013).
    - **Corruption and bribery**: Availability of bribery can reverse or eliminate the positive efficiency effect of punishment (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
    - **Ecological or dynamic constraints**: In CPRs, resource growth rate can cap efficiency regardless of cooperation/punishment (Chen & Szolnoki, 2018).

### Areas of (Dis)agreement:
- **Theoretical consensus**: In most evolutionary models, **punishment increases efficiency only if not too costly, not vulnerable to retaliation or anti-social misuse, and is normatively/targetedly used**.
- **Empirical heterogeneity**: Some contexts, especially in field/real-world or high-trust settings, yield **neutral or negative efficiency effects** of punishment, especially if not participatory or imposed externally.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given a **control efficiency** and full design dimensions, the literature suggests:
- **Punishment is more likely to increase treatment efficiency when:**
    - Punishment cost is low–moderate and impact high.
    - Punishment is targeted at defectors (prosocial only), with little or no antisocial punishment.
    - Group competition or repeated interaction is present.
    - Norms, communication, or self-governance make punishment **rare but credible**.
    - Reputation or identifiability of punishment is possible.
    - The game is not all-or-nothing or threshold with very high coordination risk.

- **Punishment may not improve or could reduce efficiency when:**
    - Punishment is costly relative to gains from cooperation or is overused.
    - Antisocial punishment or retaliation against punishers is possible.
    - The group is unstructured, or norms are weak/contested (esp. cross-cultural variation).
    - Corruption opportunities (e.g., bribery) exist in institutional punishment roles.
    - The resource is ecologically limited (CPR/slow growth rate) or the environment is high-trust and externally imposed punishment crowds out cooperation.

- **Dimension-based predictions:**
    - **Control efficiency is a strong baseline**, but the effect size and direction of punishment depend heavily on: **punishment cost/tech, player count, rounds, possibility of chat/communication, presence of reward/alternate incentives, group context, and the visibility/identifiability of punishment**.
    - Design features that make punishment **efficient, targeted, and sparingly used** (e.g., single designated punisher, use of chat, low cost, or visible threat) are associated with the highest increases in efficiency.
    - Conversely, **pool punishment with second-order enforcement** or high-cost, diffuse peer punishment in unstable groups can lead to substantial **net efficiency loss**.

- **Ambiguity/reminders:**
    - **Behavioral increases in cooperation/contribution** do not guarantee efficiency increases; punishment costs must be netted out.
    - When relevant moderators (e.g., antisocial punishment, cultural context, group voting on rules) are entangled with design dimensions, predictive accuracy will be limited unless these are explicitly encoded.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Best-informed (direct empirical/theoretical support):**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `all_or_nothing`
    - Frequently manipulated and analyzed; their effects on control and treatment efficiency (and the marginal effect of adding punishment) are abundantly documented.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` (as proxies for transparency and reputational consequences)
    - Sometimes manipulated; evidence supports their importance in modulating punishment effectiveness and, in turn, efficiency.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`
    - Examined more often in theoretical models or as comparative treatments.

**Indirectly informed/contextually discussed:**
- `chat` (communication)
    - Often included as a treatment or background variable; known to facilitate high efficiency regardless of punishment, but interaction effects with punishment are sometimes sparse.
- `default_contrib` (framing: opt-in/opt-out)
    - Rarely manipulated but occasionally discussed as affecting baseline cooperation and possibly moderating punishment needs.

**Sparse/effectively missing:**
- `show_punishment_id` (punisher identity display) is discussed as important for reputational consequences and retaliation risk but is rarely experimentally varied explicitly.
- **Other details** (e.g., within-group heterogeneity, network structure, reputation systems beyond simple observation, ecological/field design variables) have limited coverage.

**Design features consistently highlighted as **critical moderators** include:
- Cost-to-fine (punishment cost/impact) ratio.
- Presence/absence of antisocial punishment and mechanisms to limit it.
- Punisher anonymity (retaliation risk).
- Group decision process (voting vs imposed rules).
- Contextual moderators (culture, ecological dynamics, baseline trust).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency vs. Contribution/Cooperation:** Many studies **report only behavioral outcomes**. Predicting efficiency from these requires assuming all increased cooperation translates into net payoffs—often false when punishment is costly, used antisocially, or retaliation is possible.
- **Design Dimension Confounds:** Some important moderators (e.g., culture, trust, legitimacy of punishment, corruption) are not cleanly captured by standard game design variables, limiting predictive power from design dimensions alone.
- **Antisocial Punishment and Retaliation:** When possible, these frequently **neutralize or reverse** efficiency gains from enabling punishment, but effective design dimensions for blocking such behavior are not always specified/measured.
- **Crowding-out Effects:** In high-trust, self-governing, or context-specific populations, externally imposed punishment can **reduce efficiency** (Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011).
- **External Validity:** Field experiments (CPR, real-world social dilemmas) often find more **ambiguous or context-dependent efficiency effects** than laboratory canonical PGGs.
- **Parameter Ranges:** Extreme parameterizations (very high or low cost/effectiveness, very large groups, short games) are underrepresented, and most evidence clusters around small groups and moderate punishment settings.
- **Interaction Effects:** Combinations of mechanisms (punishment + communication, punishment + reward, punishment + reputation) can have **non-additive or counterintuitive effects** not easily modeled without direct evidence.
- **Indirect/Contextual Evidence:** Many theoretical and adjacent studies inform the **mechanisms** or provide baseline expectations but do not supply **quantitative effect sizes** for the central prediction task.

**Summary:**  
Prediction of punishment effects on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments is best when direct empirical/theoretical evidence exists for **all relevant design dimensions** and control efficiency. However, efficiency effects of punishment are often **heterogeneous, context-sensitive, and non-monotonic**, hinging crucially on **punishment cost/effectiveness, presence of antisocial punishment, contextual legitimacy, and observability**. In the absence of direct effect sizes for designs with certain features (e.g., large groups, communication/chats, institutional corruption), prediction accuracy declines and must embrace uncertainty and known boundary/moderator effects.
