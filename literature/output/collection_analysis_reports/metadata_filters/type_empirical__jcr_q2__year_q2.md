# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large (n=114), drawing primarily on **empirical, laboratory experimental studies** of repeated, multi-person social dilemmas—mainly public goods games (PGGs) and close variants, with some coverage of common-pool resource (CPR) games, trust/investment games, and related team production or oligopoly structures. The evidence base is *broad within the domain of lab-based social dilemma experiments*, but narrows rapidly when focusing specifically on the effects of **peer punishment** on **efficiency** (payoff- or welfare-based outcomes), especially as predicted by concrete game design dimensions plus control efficiency.

A substantial portion of the literature provides *direct or close analogs* to the core prediction scenario: linear PGGs or CPRs with well-described punishment/reward stages, parametric variation in institutional and informational design, and clear reporting on group payoff, efficiency, or surplus. However, many papers focus on *non-payoff behavioral outcomes* (e.g., cooperation rates, punishment frequency), or on adjacent domains (trust games, third-party punishment, exclusion, communication), which do not always translate directly to predictions for efficiency with punishment enabled.

There is a **mix of highly relevant empirical findings, theory-driven mechanism arguments**, and studies with only *indirect/contextual* relevance. Some research delivers quantitative comparisons of efficiency with and without punishment under controlled experimental manipulations of critical design dimensions, while others highlight the *mechanistic moderation* (e.g., by network structure, cost of punishment, information structure), or report only on underlying behavioral or emotional responses.

# 2) Task Relevance

## pgg_or_variant

- *Relevance label: `exact`* — The majority of central studies implement a standard PGG or very close variants. About one quarter of the set is dedicated to *close* analogs such as CPR games, helping games, trust games, or team production frameworks, usually with only modest changes to underlying social dilemma structure.
- Some studies are *adjacent*: e.g., pure trust games, one-shot PDs, third-party punishment, or biological/animal models, which are less structurally aligned to the multi-player, repeated PGG format.

## punishment_or_sanctions

- *Relevance label: `exact`* — Most key studies include explicit treatments enabling peer, centralized, or exogenous punishment as the experimental manipulation. The forms of punishment range from costly, monetary, and peer-assigned, to centralized, probabilistic, or via exclusion.
- There is considerable richness in design (peer vs. exogenous, network structure, cost/magnitude, information about identity, etc.), but also a subset of studies where “punishment” covers non-monetary or symbolic sanctions (e.g., disapproval points, exclusion, public feedback) with weaker payoff salience (thus, sometimes labeled `close`).
- Some papers focus more on reward, communication, reputation, exclusion, or voluntary exit, hence are *adjacent* or *weak* in relevance.

## efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome

- *Relevance label: `exact` to `close`* — About half of the most relevant set report **efficiency** directly (group earnings as share of full cooperation); others report primary outcomes as total group payoff, welfare, or earnings, which can usually be linked to efficiency. Some highly relevant papers report only contribution rates or cooperation rates and are labeled `adjacent` or `close`, requiring care when making efficiency inferences.
- The remainder focus on behavioral, psychological, or social outcomes only, with no efficiency or payoff metric (`weak` or `none`).

**Summary:** For the core prediction scenario (enabling peer punishment in a lab PGG and predicting efficiency given design and baseline), the *literature is strong and directly relevant*, but only across a subset of papers (not the whole set).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related Outcomes:** Group efficiency (payoff as % of social optimum), total group earnings, average individual payoffs, welfare/surplus, frequency of reaching efficient states, and, occasionally, inequality metrics. High-compliance experiments often directly report efficiency gains/losses with punishment versus control. Some studies use group profit, coins generated, or earnings per round, which can usually be converted to efficiency.
- **Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:** Contribution/cooperation rates, frequency and targeting of punishment/reward, norm compliance, retaliation/antisocial punishment, punishment acceptance, emotional responses, perceptions of fairness, beliefs and expectations, decision strategy profiles, and institutional choice rates.
- Studies using only behavioral outcomes sometimes *impute* efficiency effects by noting contribution levels near social optimum or via indirect mechanisms, but do not quantify aggregate payoffs.
- A subset focuses on *mechanistic* drivers (targeting, motivation, retaliation, network effects), sometimes without direct payoff reporting.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Synthesis of Efficiency Effects of Peer Punishment in PGGs

**1. Enabling peer punishment in standard PGGs reliably increases contribution rates.** However, the *translation to efficiency is conditional*.

- *Efficiency increases* when: 
  - Punishment is targeted at free-riders,
  - Cost of punishment is moderate/low,
  - Punishment is symmetric and universally available,
  - There is sufficient “coverage” for monitoring and punishment (complete networks),
  - Social learning or history information is provided,
  - Group is homogeneous in endowments or capabilities,
  - Retaliation/antisocial punishment is limited, and
  - Baseline (control) efficiency is not already high due to alternative coordination mechanisms (Page et al., 2013; Reif et al., 2017; Wang & Qin, 2015; Gürerk, 2013; Fu et al., 2017; Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Grieco et al., 2017; Qin & Wang, 2013; Dugar, 2013; Kamei & Putterman, 2015; Kölle, 2015).

- *Efficiency gains can be zero or negative* when:
  - Punishment is costly and mis-targeted (e.g., antisocial punishment is high),
  - Network for punishment is incomplete/asymmetric (some individuals can’t be punished or punish only others),
  - The motivational structure is undermined by group heterogeneity and lack of norm consensus,
  - The punishment mechanism requires costly entry or effort to punish (participation in punishment is limited),
  - The design allows significant retaliation (anti-social punishment),
  - Monitoring or information about others’ contributions is missing, or
  - Baseline efficiency is already high (i.e., little to gain via punishment, sometimes efficiency even decreases due to punishment costs) (Kingsley, 2016; Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Ramalingam et al., 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015; Robbett, 2016; Javaid et al., 2015).

**2. Institutional and informational design features are major moderators.**
- Symmetry of monitoring and punishment, punishment cost/magnitude, identifiability of punishers/punished, ability to coordinate or exclude, heterogeneity (endowment, value, capability), network structure, and provision of history/social learning *condition the efficiency effect* of punishment (see dimensions below).

**3. Some punishment mechanism modifications can boost efficiency above standard peer punishment.**
- *Redistributive punishment* (where fines are recycled, not burned) turns punishment into a net resource-conserving process, enhancing efficiency relative to both baseline and standard punishment (Page et al., 2013).
- *Centralized authority or third-party punishment*, under some conditions, yields higher efficiency, especially when personal incentives are minimized (Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017).

**4. Costless, non-monetary, or social punishment (e.g., disapproval points, emoticons) can sometimes increase efficiency, but not always.**
- With purely symbolic, costless punishments, large efficiency gains are possible if social cues are salient (Dugar, 2013), but immaterial or weak signals (smilies, disclosure) often have little or no effect (Peeters & Vorsatz, 2013).

**5. Reward mechanisms and exclusion often function as substitutes or complements to punishment, sometimes producing equal or greater efficiency gains (Choi & Ahn, 2013; Drouvelis et al., 2017; Croson et al., 2015).**
- The efficiency effect of exclusion and reward depends on the production function and group structure.

**6. Effects are not universal—context, especially subject pool and local social norms, crucially moderate punishment’s efficiency effects.**
- For example, antisocial punishment prevalence in general population samples can neutralize or entirely reverse efficiency gains seen in student samples (Bortolotti et al., 2015).

**7. Prediction based only on control efficiency is unreliable: the *magnitude and direction* of the treatment effect depend strongly on the interaction with design features.**
- In designs with high baseline efficiency (e.g., strong informal norms, transparency, social learning), punishment may not further boost—and can sometimes reduce—efficiency (Javaid et al., 2015).
- In designs with low baseline efficiency and incomplete or costly punishment, enabling punishment may produce little or negative effect.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should this literature inform prediction of treatment efficiency from design parameters plus control efficiency?**

**A. Control efficiency is informative but not determinative.**
- *If control efficiency is low*, enabling well-designed, symmetric, moderately costly peer punishment is likely to provide a *large efficiency boost*, provided design pitfalls (see below) are absent.
- *If control efficiency is high* due to strong endogenous coordination (communication, social information, small homogeneous groups), enabling punishment adds little and may even reduce efficiency if punishment costs are not offset by further gains.
- The *marginal efficiency gain* from punishment can be small, zero, or negative in high baseline conditions or where punishment is poorly targeted.

**B. Key design dimensions act as powerful moderators:**
- *Player count (player_count):* Most findings are from 3-5 player groups. Some evidence (Roux & Thöni, 2015) suggests positive punishment effects persist in larger groups if mechanisms allow for targeted punishment.
- *Number of rounds (num_rounds):* Most experiments use 10–20 rounds; learning and decay effects can moderate efficiency in later rounds; effect size is generally stable across repeated play if punishment is well designed.
- *Punishment cost & impact (punishment_cost, punishment_tech):* Lower cost and higher impact (e.g., 1:3 or above ratios) maximize efficiency gains; high cost/low impact can destroy gains (Masclet et al., 2013; Kingsley & Brown, 2016).
- *Network structure (punishment_tech/coverage):* Complete/symmetric networks support positive efficiency effect. Incomplete/asymmetric networks (where some can punish or be punished unequally) reduce or neutralize efficiency gains (Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
- *Information provision (show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id):* Full information and punishment transparency often support more effective, prosocial punishment and reduce antisocial punishment and retaliation (Kamei & Putterman, 2015; Khadjavi et al., 2017).
- *Endogeneity of punishment institution (punishment_tech, punishment_cost):* Requiring a cost to join the institution reduces effectiveness; at least two persistent punishers are often needed.
- *Group heterogeneity (endowment, value, capability):* Heterogeneity in value or endowment reduces punishment’s effectiveness; capability heterogeneity can increase efficiency gains (Kölle, 2015; Kingsley, 2016).
- *Communication (chat):* Communication boosts contributions in the absence of punishment, and can substitute for punishment, but is typically *not present* in punishment-plus designs.
- *Reward and exclusion (reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech):* Rewards can support or substitute for punishment. Exclusion is highly effective in some public goods variants.

**C. Special cases:**
- *Redistributive punishment* is more efficient than standard “burning” punishment (Page et al., 2013).
- *Probabilistic or weak punishment* (where expected penalty is constant but probability/severity varies) can show non-monotonic effects on efficiency (Qin & Wang, 2013).
- *Endogenous group formation and exclusion* can yield very high efficiency (Charness & Yang, 2014), but this is a different mechanism than pure peer punishment.

**D. Negative/Null/Mixed Results:**
- In heterogeneous groups, or when punishment is easy to misuse, or antisocial, or overly costly, enabling punishment may *not* improve efficiency, and can reduce it.
- High baseline efficiency (e.g., from pre-existing norms or disclosure) can mean that enabling punishment triggers efficiency-reducing miscoordination or costly “overkill” (Javaid et al., 2015).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr` (linear, interior-solution PGGs are well covered for 3-5 players, 10–20 rounds).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (cost-to-impact ratios, effectiveness, network structure, who can punish whom).
- `all_or_nothing` (most experiments use continuous; a minority use all-or-nothing, some with threshold or best-shot designs).
- `chat` (mostly disabled when punishment present; a few studies vary this).
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` (information about contributions and punisher identity is experimentally manipulated in several studies).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (manipulated in studies comparing reward/punishment or their interaction).
- `default_contrib` (rarely manipulated, but occasionally varied via opt-in/out framing).
- `show_n_rounds` (often shown; a few studies test uncertainty).

**Indirectly/contextually informed:**
- *Endowment heterogeneity* and type (`player_count`, group composition); some studies use unequal endowments or productivity, but most standard PGGs are homogeneous.
- *Voluntary group formation, exclusion, or merger* (only a few studies).
- *Communication* (often as a baseline or in CPR settings).

**Missing/sparse:**
- Little direct variation or reporting on `default_contrib` (framing), or on `num_rounds` above 20.
- Little use of dynamic or variable MPCR.
- Few studies with large groups (N > 10) or real-time/continuous-play PGGs.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **External Validity:** Almost all evidence is lab-based and with undergraduate student samples; effects can differ in field-contextualized settings or with non-student/complementary samples (Bortolotti et al., 2015).
- **Generalizability Across Institutional Forms:** Most prediction-relevant results are from *linear PGGs with peer punishment*; designs with more complex externalities, thresholds, or real-world messy features (e.g., asymmetric CPR, stochastic outcomes) may show different patterns or even inverse effects (Javaid et al., 2015).
- **Reporting Gaps:** Not all studies swap in and out all design dimensions independently; thus, evidence may not fully span higher-dimensional parameter combinations.
- **Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes:** Several key patterns (e.g., increased cooperation from punishment) are well established for behavior but not always for payoffs; translation to efficiency requires either direct reporting or careful inference.
- **Heterogeneity in Punishment Behavior:** Variability in antisocial punishment and retaliation is common (driven by sample, context, design), making efficiency effects less predictable in some scenarios.
- **Mechanism Dependence:** Efficient punishment requires well-targeted, moderate-cost, appropriately covered, and symmetric structures. Deviations from this (asymmetric networks, high cost, requiring effort to join the institution, low baseline trust) degrade or reverse efficiency effects.
- **Crowding-Out and Overkill:** In some contexts (high baseline efficiency, concurrent communication or observation, strong local norms), enabling formal punishment can crowd out informal cooperation or worsen coordination, reducing efficiency.
- **Non-Linear or Complex Social Dilemma Structures:** Production functions beyond the linear (e.g., weakest-link, best-shot, threshold public goods) can show different punishment effects—positive or null (Croson et al., 2015).
- **Not all design dimensions are equally supported:** Some dimensions are only contextually varied or not manipulated in isolation.

---

## Final Summative Statement

**For the prediction task—estimating treatment (punishment-on) efficiency given game design and control (punishment-off) efficiency—the literature is rich and, for standard lab PGGs, provides robust guidance: enabling peer punishment generally increases efficiency *if* institutional, attentional, and social moderators are favorable. Predictive accuracy increases when models incorporate not only control efficiency and core parameters, but also details of punishment mechanism design, group composition, information structure, and social context. However, predictability is weaker under heterogeneous, incomplete, or high baseline-efficiency conditions, and unsupported design configurations (e.g., high punishment cost, incomplete networks, strong antisocial punishment) may yield non-monotonic or negative efficiency changes.**
