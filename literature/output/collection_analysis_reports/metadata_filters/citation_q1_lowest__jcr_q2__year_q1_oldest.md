# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is composed of 13 papers, with a mix of empirical (4 experimental, 1 observational) and theoretical (8) works. Most empirical studies use laboratory experiments; theoretical contributions often employ formal game-theoretic or evolutionary models. While the set spans a variety of social dilemma environments (public goods games, repeated prisoners’ dilemmas, collective action, resource dilemmas, and even analogies to family organization), only a subset of papers deals with exact public goods games and directly measures payoff-based efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled versus disabled. Many works are adjacent or close rather than exact to the public goods game (PGG) paradigm, and several do not report or analyze efficiency outcomes explicitly. Overall, the evidence base for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in PGG-like environments is broad in context but relatively narrow for exact, directly empirical, payoff-based predictions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:
- **Exact**: Only a small subset (e.g., Webb & Foddy, 2004) investigates games structurally equivalent to PGGs.
- **Close/Adjacent**: Several papers use variants (e.g., voluntary contribution mechanism, repeated PD, common-pool-resource dilemmas, team-production), with varying degrees of similarity to standard PGGs.
- **Weak/None**: A minority are more distantly related (e.g., family organization analogies in Wittman, 2005).

**punishment_or_sanctions**:
- **Exact**: Approximately half the papers focus specifically on peer punishment or formal sanctioning systems—either experimentally or via explicit model mechanisms (e.g., Webb & Foddy, 2004; Janus & Lim, 2009; Matsushima, 2012; Heller & Sieberg, 2008).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Others discuss related concepts (peer sanctions via exclusion/reputation, third-party enforcement, retaliation, or threat rather than explicit costly punishment).
- **None**: Several purely baseline or observation studies (e.g., Rosenbaum et al., 2012) do not manipulate or model punishment.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
- **Exact/Close**: Some theory papers and a few empirical works directly analyze “efficiency” as group payoff relative to cooperative maximum, or report comparable metrics (total group profit, welfare).
- **Adjacent/Weak**: Many focus on cooperation rates or norm compliance; some discussions of efficiency are only theoretical or inferred, not directly measured.
- **None**: Several studies report behavioral motives for punishment, spatial organization, or other outcomes with no direct connection to total payoff or efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes**:
    - Efficiency (group payoff as proportion of maximum possible): directly reported or theoretized in a subset of papers (e.g., Webb & Foddy, 2004; Janus & Lim, 2009; Matsushima, 2012; Heller & Sieberg, 2008; Aramendia, 2006; Ishida, 2009).
    - Total group profit, welfare, surplus, or coins earned: reported or discussed in close relation to efficiency in several theory papers.
    - Resource preservation (as a proxy for efficiency in common-pool resource dilemmas).

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**:
    - Contribution rate/cooperation rate (common in experimental papers).
    - Punishment frequency or distribution (who punishes whom, retaliation, spite).
    - Norm compliance, fairness, intentions.
    - Observational behavior (spatial structure, exposure to risk).
    - Voting for institutional change (Webb & Foddy, 2004).

- **Distinction**: Payoff-based outcomes are directly informative for predicting efficiency; non-payoff outcomes are only inferentially or contextually informative unless directly tied to group earnings in the studied design.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant to Prediction

- **Overall Direction**:
    - Most theoretical models predict that enabling (peer) punishment or sanctions tends to increase efficiency by sustaining cooperation, provided the punishment mechanism is credibly and appropriately designed, and provided that the costs of punishment do not outweigh its benefits (Janus & Lim, 2009; Matsushima, 2012; Aramendia, 2006; Heller & Sieberg, 2008).
    - Efficiency effects are not guaranteed and depend on detailed rules: sanction type, cost, group heterogeneity, monitoring efficacy, and the possibility of strategic response or manipulation.

- **Empirical Variability**:
    - Webb & Foddy (2004) find heterogeneity: targeted (individual) sanctions better preserve resources, while shared sanctions maximize group profit, with efficiency outcomes highly dependent on group access structure and the definition of efficiency.
    - Some theoretical models (Whitmeyer, 2004; Ishida, 2009) warn of non-monotonic or even catastrophic effects: under some parameters, introducing punishment can reduce efficiency or lead to undesirable equilibria (e.g., compliance collapse, excessive punishment costs, or deadweight loss).

- **Dependence on Design Dimensions**:
    - Repeated/interated play substantially enhances the effectiveness of punishment for achieving high efficiency (Janus & Lim, 2009; Matsushima, 2012; Aramendia, 2006).
    - The cost of punishment is critical: if punishing is too costly, efficiency gains are lost (Heller & Sieberg, 2008).
    - Punishment mechanism design (individual vs. shared sanctions; finite vs. infinite punishments; observability; timing and asymmetry) are important moderators.
    - Empirical data (Webb & Foddy, 2004) show that details such as resource access distribution interact with punishment mode to determine who gains from efficiency improvements.

- **Behavioral Outcomes versus Efficiency**:
    - Several studies report increases in contribution/cooperation rates or decreased defection with punishment, but do not always translate these findings into efficiency or group payoffs, especially when punishment costs are high.
    - Motives for punishment (retaliation, fairness, spite) can affect who is punished, but not necessarily efficiency (Klempt, 2012).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

From this literature, the following insights should inform any prediction of the average efficiency of a game with punishment enabled, given its design features and baseline efficiency:

- **General Expectation**: When punishment is enabled in group dilemma games similar to PGG—with sufficiently low punishment costs and credible implementation—average efficiency is expected to increase relative to the control, especially in repeated or indefinitely repeated games (Janus & Lim, 2009; Matsushima, 2012; Aramendia, 2006; Heller & Sieberg, 2008).

- **Moderators**:
    - **Punishment Cost & Magnitude**: Low-cost, high-impact punishment mechanisms are most efficiency-enhancing. If the cost of punishing is high, net efficiency gains may vanish or reverse (Heller & Sieberg, 2008).
    - **Sanctioning Structure**: Whether punishment is targeted/individual, shared/collective, or contractually contingent (e.g., only for last-period deviations) materially affects the efficiency impact, sometimes in opposite directions (Webb & Foddy, 2004).
    - **Heterogeneity and Access**: If group members have unequal access or ability, the efficiency effect of punishment can be unequally distributed and may depend on the group’s structure (Webb & Foddy, 2004).
    - **Repeated Play & Observability**: The more repeated the game and the more observable the actions, the more likely punishment is to sustain high efficiency (Janus & Lim, 2009).
    - **Ambiguity**: Some models show parameter regions where punishment fails or reduces efficiency (Whitmeyer, 2004; Ishida, 2009); predictions should not assume monotonic improvement.

- **Where Limited**: For game designs with high cost of punishment, weak or ambiguous sanctioning rules, one-shot interactions, or highly heterogeneous access/power, the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is likely small, ambiguous, or even negative.

- **Quantitative Guidance**: No study in the set provides a closed-form or empirical regression mapping the full set of design dimensions and control efficiency to treated (punishment-enabled) efficiency. Thus, only qualitative, theoretically grounded predictions (and some conditional expectations based on contextual matches) are supported by this literature.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed**:
- **player_count**: Commonly manipulated or varied (empirical and theory papers).
- **num_rounds**: Repeated/interated versus one-shot play is a central moderator.
- **punishment_cost**: Rules specifying cost-effectiveness, central to theory and empirical inference.
- **punishment_tech**: Types (individual, shared, contract-based), directly discussed.
- **mpcr**: Variation appears in several models or experiments.
- **all_or_nothing**: Some games use binary action spaces, others continuous.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost**: Co-discussed as alternatives to punishment in theoretical models.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Information structures noted in theory (e.g., observability’s effect on equilibrium), with limited empirical manipulation.

**Indirectly Informed**:
- **chat**: Communication’s effect occasionally mentioned but not always systematically manipulated.
- **default_contrib**: Framing is sometimes discussed, not primary.
- **reward_tech, reward_magnitude**: Not the focus, but sometimes sketched.
- **show_punishment_id**: Implicit in observability arguments.

**Contextually Discussed/Effectively Missing**:
- Several dimensions (especially reward- and display-related ones) are only loosely covered, if at all, or discussed in analogy and not operationally tested.

**Best-Informed Dimensions**:
- **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech**, **player_count**, **num_rounds**

**Sparse Dimensions**:
- **chat**, **default_contrib**, **reward_magnitude**, finer detail of display mechanics (e.g., **show_other_summaries**, **show_punishment_id**).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of Exact PGG Empirics**: Direct, empirical, payoff-based findings from standard multi-round PGGs with and without punishment are rare in this set.
- **Theory-Empirics Gap**: Many findings are theoretical or simulation-based and may not transfer quantitatively to lab or field PGG environments with real human subjects.
- **Ambiguity and Conditionality**: Several models highlight non-monotonic, path-dependent, or ambiguous impacts of punishment on efficiency, implying that predictions should include caveats regarding parameter-dependence and possible negative effects.
- **Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes**: Much of the behavioral data concerns cooperation/contribution rates or punishment targeting, which may not map directly onto group efficiency if punishment costs are large.
- **Limited Dimensional Coverage**: Not all 14 prediction-relevant game design dimensions are equally covered; key moderators such as details of player observability, chat, framing, or reward mechanism are under-examined.
- **No Quantitative Effect Sizes**: The literature does not contain effect size estimates, regression coefficients, or closed-form mappings for translating control efficiency and design features into expected treatment efficiency.
- **Contextual Mismatches**: A portion of the literature addresses adjacent or analogous games (PD, common-pool resources, teams, family, fish shoals) that differ in mechanics and may not generalize cleanly to PGGs.

---

In summary, the literature supports the expectation that enabling well-designed, appropriately-costed punishment generally increases efficiency over control in repeated, observable, multi-player social dilemmas. However, the effect is strongly design-dependent and not guaranteed, especially when punishment is costly or poorly structured. Several game design prediction dimensions are directly supported, while others are minimally discussed. Predictions should be qualitative, highlighting both positive baseline expectations and key failure modes. The lack of direct, quantitative, exact PGG outcomes with measured efficiency restricts precise forecasting.
