# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is relatively **broad** in its coverage of punishment and payoff outcomes in public-goods-game (PGG) and adjacent environments, with an **emphasis on empirical lab experiments and formal theory**. Roughly half of the papers produce direct empirical evidence (by experimental lab studies) on efficiency or group payoff with and without punishment in classic and variant PGGs. The other half develop theory or mechanism arguments, sometimes calibrated to canonical games (e.g., Fehr & Gächter), and sometimes extending to adjacent domains (e.g., repeated PD, CPR, network games). Mechanistically adjacent work often models or tests ostracism, endogenous partner choice, network effects, institutional formation, and alternative sanction/reward systems.

Many empirical studies report **quantitative treatment effects (efficiency/payoff change)**, and several theoretical works provide **explicit comparative statics or closed-form solutions** linking design dimensions and expected efficiency with/without punishment. Some empirical studies report only behavioral effects or are primarily distinguished by non-PGG structure or outcomes. Most of the highly relevant papers are **PGG or close variants**, with tightly controlled information about design parameters.

Overall, the **evidence base is strong for certain parameter regimes** (e.g., 4-player, 10-round, linear MPCR, standard peer punishment), but **patchier outside these domains**—large groups, complex networks, or highly endogenous institution formation are more sparsely covered.

# 2) Task Relevance

### pgg_or_variant:
- **exact relevance**: The core of the evidence set comprises **PGG and voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM) studies** (Nicklisch & Wolff, Zheng & Nie, Dannenberg, Kamei, Rockenbach & Wolff, etc.), as well as several theory papers explicitly modeling public goods provision with punishment (Dutta et al., Kranz, Hwang).
- **close/adjacent relevance**: Many papers use **adjacent environments** (CPR games, repeated PDs, partner-choice, networked games) that share essential features with PGGs but may not match technical details (e.g., Akpalu & Martinsson; Shreedhar et al.; Gallo et al.).
  
### punishment_or_sanctions:
- **exact relevance**: Empirical and theoretical studies **directly manipulate or model peer punishment, centralized punishment, or sanctioning institutions** (Nicklisch & Wolff, Zheng & Nie, Dannenberg, Chang et al., etc.).
- **close relevance**: Some papers model or test **endogenous ostracism, exclusion, reputation-based sanctions, or indirect punishment/reward equilibria**—these are adjacent but often serve a comparable strategic role (Rob & Yang, Fujiwara-Greve et al.).
  
### efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:
- **exact relevance**: Many empirical studies **explicitly measure efficiency or group payoff** (Nicklisch & Wolff, Zheng & Nie, Rockenbach & Wolff, Dutta et al., Kranz, Shreedhar et al., Karakostas et al., etc.). 
- **close/adjacent relevance**: Some studies **mainly report non-payoff outcomes (e.g., contribution rate, norm compliance)**, sometimes with payoff outcomes inferred but not formally analyzed; others focus on intermediate welfare, insurance, or risk outcomes (Akpalu & Martinsson, Gatiso & Vollan, etc.).
- **weak/none**: A minority report only **behavioral outcomes** without efficiency or payoff-related data.

**In summary:** Direct relevance is high for all three dimensions, especially for standard PGG with peer punishment and efficiency as outcome; relevance declines for adjacent design settings or non-payoff outcomes.

---

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (central to prediction):**
  - **Group efficiency** (explicitly, or as group total payoff relative to the social optimum): Measured or modeled in a majority of core papers (Nicklisch & Wolff, Zheng & Nie, Dannenberg, Dutta et al., Hwang, Kranz, Shreedhar et al., Karakostas et al., Colombier et al., Rockenbach & Wolff, etc.)
  - **Net earnings, welfare, or surplus**: Sometimes reported as alternative summary statistics, occasionally as the main dependent variable.
  - **Aggregate/individual earnings comparisons across treatments**.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - **Contribution or cooperation rates:** Common in almost all studies, even when efficiency is not the primary outcome.
  - **Punishment frequency, type, legitimacy, or assignment:** Often as mechanisms or mediator variables.
  - **Norm compliance, voting for agreements/rules, preference for monitoring type, ostracism rates, etc.**
  - **Reputation, network link formation/removal, exclusion rates.**

- **Mixed outcomes:**
  - Some papers infer efficiency gains/losses from significant shifts in cooperation, or report that contributions increase yet net efficiency does not (especially when punishment costs offset gains).

**Distinction:** The best evidence for prediction comes from papers reporting explicit efficiency or group payoff measurements pre/post punishment implementation.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### 1. **Punishment generally increases efficiency in standard PGGs, but there are important boundary conditions:**
   - With **classic lab parameters** (4-player, 10-round, linear MPCR, well-targeted peer punishment), **enabling punishment reliably raises efficiency** above the no-punishment control, sometimes dramatically (Nicklisch & Wolff, Dutta et al., Kranz, Kamei, Dannenberg—if high agreement).
   - **The magnitude and sign of the effect can reverse or decline** in other settings—especially **larger groups, institutional design with high punishment cost, or poorly targeted punishment** (Zheng & Nie, Rockenbach & Wolff, Colombier et al.).

### 2. **Efficiency gains from punishment are context-dependent:**
   - **Group size:** Gains are clearest in small groups. In large groups, anonymous punishment can **reduce efficiency** compared to no punishment if legitimacy or coordination falters (Zheng & Nie, Hwang).
   - **Punishment institution:** **Legitimacy, identifiability (revealing punisher ID), and monitoring regime (peer vs group, exogenous vs endogenous)** all moderate the efficiency impact (Zheng & Nie, DeAngelo & Gee, Chang et al., Shreedhar et al.)
   - **Cost and effectiveness:** Lower punishment cost and higher effectiveness predict larger efficiency gains (Kranz, Dutta et al., Hwang).
   - **Production technology:** Nonlinear technologies (best-shot, weakest-link) alter the effect; allocation/reward mechanisms can outperform or underperform classic punishment (Karakostas et al.).
   - **Network and information structure:** Imperfect monitoring, sparse punishment networks, and reputational uncertainty undermine the efficiency benefit of punishment (Shreedhar et al., Gallo et al., Balmaceda & Escobar).

### 3. **Punishment can backfire or be dominated by alternatives:**
   - **If the direct costs of punishment exceed cooperation gains, efficiency can decline** (Rockenbach & Wolff, Colombier et al., Dughera, Akpalu & Martinsson).
   - **Reward mechanisms, participative rule change, and communication can achieve higher efficiency** or similar impact at lower cost (Rockenbach & Wolff, Brent et al., Greiff).

### 4. **The presence of agreements, legitimacy, or institution formation interact strongly with punishment:**
   - Efficiency gains from punishment are **much higher with clear, ambitious, and credible agreements** (Dannenberg, Gatiso & Vollan, Chang et al.).
   - Endogenous, participative, or democratically selected punishment institutions fare better than imposed/costly/illegitimate ones.

### 5. **Empirical magnitude:**
   - Several papers provide **tables of control and treatment efficiency** (Zheng & Nie; Kamei; Dannenberg), which allows for **quantitative mapping from control efficiency + design dimensions to predicted treatment efficiency**.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct mapping** from control (punishment-off) to treatment (punishment-on) efficiency is **strongly supported** for standard linear PGGs with lab-relevant parameterizations (player count 2–4, 10–15 rounds, MPCR > 0.4, peer punishment).
- **Most robust expectations:** Enabling well-designed, low-cost, peer-focused punishment in small groups will increase efficiency, often bringing it near the social optimum.
- When **group size increases, the structure of the punishment institution, and perceptions of legitimacy or targeting,** become crucial:
    - In small groups, anonymous punishment is more effective; in large groups, identified, legitimate punishment is necessary for efficiency gains (Zheng & Nie).
    - Poorly targeted or excessive punishment can offset cooperation gains, reducing or negating net efficiency.

- **Qualitative moderators to consider:**
    - **Punishment cost:** Higher cost → lower net efficiency gain (Kranz, Shreedhar et al., Hwang).
    - **Punishment technology (effectiveness):** More efficient punishments (greater impact per cost) favor larger efficiency increases.
    - **Monitoring, network, and information regime:** Efficiency gains require sufficient, accurate monitoring but not so much as to induce wasteful, redundant punishment (Shreedhar et al., Gallo et al.).

- **Key caveats:**
    - **No guarantee of monotonic effect:** In some parameterizations, **enabling punishment reduces efficiency** below the control game (Rockenbach & Wolff, Colombier et al.).
    - **Some forms of institution or alternative sanction/reward systems can either outperform punishment or render it unnecessary** for high efficiency (Brent et al., Rockenbach & Wolff, Dughera).
    - **Adjacency of evidence:** For prediction outside standard PGG (e.g., best-shot, weakest-link, dynamic networks), evidence is more mixed or theoretical and should be weighted accordingly.

- **Best-informed dimensions for prediction:**
    - **Player count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech (effectiveness), and punishment institution (anonymity, legitimacy, monitoring regime)**
    - These dimensions are often directly measured or explicitly manipulated in the core empirical and theory papers.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**  
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`: Heavily featured in both empirical and theory PGG studies (see full specification and explicit comparison in e.g., Zheng & Nie, Kamei, Dannenberg, Hwang, etc.)
- `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Almost always reported or modeled; clear evidence on their impact on efficiency and punishment effect size.
- `chat`: Sometimes manipulated; its presence as communication or joint decision-making is found to improve efficiency and interact with punishment (Kamei, Rockenbach & Wolff, Dannenberg).
- `all_or_nothing`: Most studies use continuous contribution, some use all-or-nothing; effect of this is more contextually discussed but not always disentangled.
- `reward_exists`: Less frequently present, but when available, direct comparisons are drawn (Karakostas et al., Brent et al., Greiff).
- `punishment_exists`: By construction, central to almost every included study.

**Indirectly Informed / Contextually Discussed:**  
- `default_contrib`: Not always specified as a variable, but its framing or default may be inherent in opt-in/opt-out settings.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes manipulated (Zheng & Nie on punisher ID; Kamei, Shreedhar et al. on info/reputation), often discussed as moderators or boundary conditions.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `reward_magnitude`: Only studied in subset with explicit reward comparison (Karakostas et al., Greiff).

**Effectively Missing:**  
- Some high-dimensional features (e.g., fine details of the *timing* of information, identity display, or real-time feedback) are rarely systematically compared.
- `default_contrib` as a controlled variable is seldom directly analyzed, and is often an unreported framing effect.
- `show_punishment_id` is discussed mostly in qualitative terms concerning legitimacy and counter-punishment.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Parameter specificity and generalizability:** Most strong findings are restricted to PGGs with standard lab conditions (e.g., small group size, standard punishment cost/tech, short time horizons). Predictions outside this regime (large N, nonlinear payoffs, complex networks, very high/low MPCR) are less certain.
- **Selective reporting and heterogeneity:** Many empirical studies report only significant findings, or else group payoff effects are not always primary outcomes (some infer efficiency from increased contribution, which may be confounded by high punishment cost).
- **Limited direct evidence for rare design combinations:** Several of the 14 prediction dimensions (such as opt-in framing, real-time chat, and fine-grained observability variations) are only sparsely addressed or not systematically crossed with punishment in reported experiments.
- **Behavioral vs. payoff outcome conflation:** Not all increases in cooperation/contribution translate into efficiency gains; cost of punishment can fully or partially offset contribution improvements (e.g., Dannenberg, Rockenbach & Wolff, Colombier et al.).
- **Adjacency and theoretical transfer:** Several papers provide only adjacent evidence (PD, CPR, insurance, network games) and should be given less weight for direct PGG payoff prediction, though mechanisms are often transferable.
- **Ambiguity and disagreement:** Some studies find negative or neutral effects of punishment on efficiency, especially as institutional structure, error, or social context change (Rockenbach & Wolff, Colombier et al., Gallo et al.). There is **no consensus** that enabling punishment always increases group efficiency.
- **Long-run versus short-run effects:** Efficiency gains may be transitory or require additional institutional support (e.g., agreements, legitimate authority) for their realization and maintenance (Dannenberg, Chang et al.).

---

**In sum:**  
The literature set provides **strong prediction support within parameter regions closely matching standard linear PGGs with small groups and well-designed peer punishment**, but **predictive power declines as the context diverges**. The effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is fundamentally **conditional on multiple design features**, notably group size, punishment institution, cost/tech, and the presence of cooperative agreements or legitimizing mechanisms. Prediction should account for these dimension-level contingencies and avoid assuming universal positive effects of punishment on efficiency.
