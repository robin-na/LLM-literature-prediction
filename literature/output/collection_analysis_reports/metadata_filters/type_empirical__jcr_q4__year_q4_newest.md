# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists primarily of **empirical experimental studies**, with a heavy focus on lab experiments across economic games, including some field-lab approaches. There is a **broad representation** of game-theoretic environments: roughly half are based on public goods games (PGG) or precise variants, while others study trust, delegation, bargaining, coordination, team investment, or real-world collective action settings. **Very few theoretical or purely conceptual pieces** are present; most claims derive from observed behavior in controlled or semi-controlled environments.

Regarding **task coverage**, the set is **moderately specific** to the prediction problem at hand: several papers report direct or adjacent evidence about efficiency or total payoffs in PGG or near-PGG settings with and without punishment options. However, there are also papers that:
- Do not report efficiency or payoff outcomes (focusing on non-payoff behaviors like contribution choices, norm perception, or punishment assignment),
- Do not link findings directly to the core prediction task (the effect of **enabling peer punishment** on treatment efficiency given control efficiency and design parameters),
- Provide context or mechanism discussion without quantitative evidence.

Overall, the base provides **solid but not exhaustive empirical evidence** for direct prediction about punishment effects on efficiency in PGG-like environments, and there are notable **gaps and indirectness** in some design dimensions and outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

### a) PGG or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`)
- **Exact relevance:** About a third of the papers use **standard PGGs** or only slightly modified variants (e.g., Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Pancotto et al., 2023; Peng, 2022).
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Several employ games with social dilemma structure but not canonical PGGs (minimum effort games: Lec et al., 2023; team investment: Calabuig et al., 2024; trust/delegation games: Li et al., 2023; Herne et al., 2022; bargaining: Pevnitskaya & Ryvkin, 2022; real-effort teamwork: Kamei & Tabero, 2025).
- **Weak/none:** Other studies are ethnographic, conceptual, or focus on real-world observations without experimental game structures.

**Summary:** The set as a whole is **moderately strong** in PGG or very-close-variant evidence, but weaker or missing on highly idiosyncratic or real-world collective action contexts.

### b) Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`)
- **Exact:** Most lab-experimental studies manipulate the presence/absence or details of **peer punishment or formal/informal sanctions** (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023; Herne et al., 2022; Pevnitskaya & Ryvkin, 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024, etc.).
- **Close/adjacent:** Some include **reward** as an alternative comparator, or model group choice over institutions with or without punishment (Peng, 2022; Buso et al., 2025; Kamei & Tabero, 2025).
- **None:** Certain papers discuss punishment/sanctions only contextually (Bharti & Malik, 2023; Clark, 2022), or do not include manipulation of punishment.

**Summary:** The **majority of experimental papers directly inform punishment as a manipulated treatment**, providing actionable evidence for the prediction task.

### c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`)
- **Exact:** Roughly half provide **explicit group efficiency or total payoff data** (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Peng, 2022; Li et al., 2023; Herne et al., 2022; Lec et al., 2023; Pevnitskaya & Ryvkin, 2022).
- **Close/adjacent:** Some report contribution rates or coordination measures and infer likely efficiency impact, but do not directly calculate payoff ratios (Pancotto et al., 2023; Buso et al., 2025; Kamei & Tabero, 2025).
- **None:** Several focus solely on non-payoff outcomes (behavioral norms, punishment assignment, qualitative reports).

**Summary:** **Direct efficiency reporting is somewhat limited**—several relevant studies only infer efficiency effects from behavioral data. Some potentially important design dimensions (e.g., continuous vs. binary contributions, visibility features) are less frequently studied for their direct impact on efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**: 
  - *Efficiency*: Measured as average payoff as a proportion of the social optimum, or directly as group/individual earnings (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Peng, 2022; Li et al., 2023; Herne et al., 2022; Lec et al., 2023; Pevnitskaya & Ryvkin, 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).
  - *Total welfare / joint surplus*: Total earnings net of punishment/reward costs.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
  - *Contribution/cooperation rate*: Individual or group contributions as proportion of possible maximum (common in PGG studies: Pancotto et al., 2023; Buso et al., 2025).
  - *Punishment frequency, norm enforcement, or sanction choices*: Who punishes whom, frequency of anti-social punishment, determinants of punishment (Wiederhold & Bühren, 2025; Dato & Friehe, 2025).
  - *Norm perception and qualitative resolution of unfairness*: How people perceive fairness and resolve conflict (Sequeira, 2023; Clark, 2022).
- **Mechanism/qualitative outcomes**: 
  - Endogenous institutional choice, preference for (non-)punitive institutions, social/cultural context for punishment.

**Note:** Several studies **infer efficiency outcomes by linking behavioral changes (like increased contribution) to likely payoff impact**, but caution is required since increased costly punishment can sometimes lower net payoff even as contributions rise (Herne et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the literature:

- **Enabling punishment often (but not always) increases efficiency relative to no-punishment controls**, particularly in PGGs/variants *if* the punishment is effective, not excessively costly, and group structure enables enforcement (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Lec et al., 2023; Gueth & Otsubo, 2023; Li et al., 2023).
    - Example: Formal sanctions (centralized punishment) in open groups yield **substantially higher efficiency** than informal punishment, particularly when migration is allowed (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
    - Repeated, large-group coordination games exhibit robust increases in efficiency over time after introducing costly peer punishment (Lec et al., 2023).

- **The cost and targeting of punishment are crucial moderators**:
    - When punishment is costly and used frequently, **efficiency gains from increased cooperation may be offset or outweighed by punishment costs**, resulting in negligible or even net negative effect on group efficiency (Herne et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).
    - Efficient (rare, well-targeted) punishment is most likely to increase payoffs; frequent or poorly coordinated punishment can diminish group welfare.

- **Game design and context shape the treatment effect**:
    - Open communities, endogenous group selection, and visibility of punishment effectiveness **amplify the positive effect of punishment** on efficiency (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023).
    - Binary-contribution/team investment settings show **less or no efficiency gain from punishment**, especially when costs are high or punishment must be coordinated (Calabuig et al., 2024).
    - Peer reward—especially by majority vote—can also boost efficiency, sometimes more robustly than punishment, though this is outside the direct “punishment effect” focus (Peng, 2022).

- **Non-payoff behavioral changes often track, but do not exactly predict, efficiency gains**:
    - Increases in contribution rate with punishment are common but **may not guarantee net efficiency improvement** (Pancotto et al., 2023; Herne et al., 2022).

- **Mechanisms for norm enforcement or institutional choice interact with efficiency effects**, but sometimes via indirect or ambiguous pathways—such as through voluntary partnerships or democratic procedures (Buso et al., 2025; Kamei & Tabero, 2025).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the evidence:

- **Treatment efficiency with peer punishment enabled is likely to be higher than control efficiency when:**
    1. The punishment cost is moderate (fine-to-cost ratio > 1), and punishment is not used indiscriminately.
    2. The group structure allows for monitoring/enforcement (fixed groups, repeated interaction, visible punishment).
    3. Group openness (voluntary entry/exit) or visibility of punishment enhances deterrence.

- **Efficiency gains are less likely, or even reversed, when:**
    1. Punishment is very costly relative to its impact, or is frequently used (leading to net loss for group).
    2. Contribution decisions are binary and asymmetric, or when multiple punishers cannot coordinate (Calabuig et al., 2024).
    3. The norm or institution does not support or value punitive enforcement (as per real-world ethnographic contexts or partnership mechanisms).

- **Control (no-punishment) efficiency is a strong baseline:** If it is already high, punishment may have little room to improve efficiency, or may lower it if punishment is misapplied or costly.

- **Predictions about efficiency gains should adjust for:**
    - **Player count and round structure:** Effects may be more robust in larger, repeated groups.
    - **Punishment technology:** Cost and magnitude of punishment are critical; details must be matched with prediction scenario.
    - **Information features:** Visibility of punishment, public summary, revelation of rounds may moderate punishment’s effect.
    - **Presence of rewards or alternative mechanisms:** Peer reward, partnership formation, and democratic procedures can independently boost efficiency—and may outperform or interact with the effect of punishment.
    
- **Where only behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rate) are reported:** Predictive confidence in efficiency effects should be lower, especially if punishment is used frequently or is costly.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed Dimensions:**
    - **player_count**: Frequently varied and explicitly studied (groups from 2 up to 8).
    - **num_rounds**: Reported and manipulated across studies (from 1 to 30 rounds).
    - **all_or_nothing**: Both continuous and binary contribution settings observed.
    - **mpcr**: Marginal per-capita return is specified in many designs.
    - **punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Explicit treatment in most papers (cost per punishment point, fine/cost ratio, targeting/coordination structures).
    - **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Well addressed in some adjacent studies (Peng, 2022; Pevnitskaya & Ryvkin, 2022).
    - **chat**: Communication channel presence/absence is a common variable.
    - **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries**: Occasionally specified, though rarely isolated as focal variables.

- **Indirect/Contextual Dimensions:**
    - **default_contrib**: Only contextually addressed (e.g., voluntary participation opt-out, as in Pancotto et al., 2023).
    - **show_punishment_id**: Punisher/rewarder identity visibility is occasionally manipulated (Li et al., 2023), but usually not the primary focus.

- **Sparse or Missing Dimensions:**
    - **Detailed structural and information features** (e.g., summary information, interface cues, identity revelation) are rarely isolated for main effect analysis.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Payoff measurement gaps:** Several experiments **do not directly report efficiency or group payoff**, requiring inference from behavioral outcomes, leading to limitations in quantitative prediction accuracy.

- **Partial coverage of design space:** Some design dimensions crucial for prediction (e.g., information visibility, punishment identity) are not systematically manipulated or reported.

- **Game structure generalizability:** While there is strong coverage of PGG and variants, **results from adjacent games (trust, delegation, coordination, bargaining)** may not always map cleanly onto PGG settings, especially for multiplayer or continuous action spaces.

- **Idiosyncratic or real-world observations are weakly informative** for prediction tasks involving controlled experimental games, and cannot be extrapolated for quantified efficiency predictions.

- **Heterogeneity in punishment institutions:** Effects differ depending on whether punishment is formal/centralized, peer-to-peer, visible, or requires coordination, and these distinctions are sometimes not cleanly aligned with the 14 prediction dimensions.

- **Ambiguity or disagreement among findings:** Some settings indicate net positive efficiency effects of punishment, while others suggest **no effect or even a negative effect** due to punishment costs or coordination failures (Herne et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).

- **Behavioral versus payoff outcomes:** Outcomes such as contribution rate and norm compliance are only proxies for efficiency; **treatment efficiency predictions must be modified when only such behavioral outcomes are reported**.

- **External validity:** Many lab studies use simplified or stylized environments—applicability to field or diverse real-world collective action remains uncertain.

In sum, **while there is reasonable empirical support for certain prediction dimensions and contexts, important information gaps, indirectness, and ambiguities mean that prediction of efficiency effects from enabling punishment in PGG-like games will remain probabilistic and context-dependent, rather than deductive or uniform**.
