# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is **large and diverse** (n=100), drawing on a rich mix of **experimental and theoretical research**. There is a substantial core of **empirical laboratory experiments** on standard public goods games (PGG) and close variants that directly test the impact of enabling punishment mechanisms on group efficiency (e.g., Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011). Alongside these are numerous **theory/modeling papers**, simulation studies, and adjacent research spanning evolutionary biology, economics, political science, and ecology. While the core evidence is robust for standard repeated PGGs with and without punishment, findings are more heterogeneous in adjacent or real-world contexts (e.g., CPR, dyadic negotiation, ostracism, or networked games). A notable portion of papers focus primarily on **behavioral, normative, or sociopsychological outcomes** rather than payoff-based efficiency.

**Empirical evidence** is strongest in standard linear or binary PGGs with well-specified design parameters. Theoretical and simulation work often extends to spatial, networked, or evolutionary models, sometimes aligning closely with experimental PGGs, other times extrapolating to broader social dilemmas or institutional settings. Overall, the evidence base is broad for standard PGGs, but **diversifies and attenuates** as focus shifts to adjacent settings, non-payoff outcomes, or complex institutional environments.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. PGG or Variant**
- **Exact**: A significant subset deals directly with **standard or close variant public goods games** (e.g., linear or binary repeated PGGs). These include both empirical lab studies and formal theoretical models (e.g., Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Kranz, 2010).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Many papers cover **adjacent settings**—common pool resource (CPR) games, prisoner's dilemmas (PD), trust/ultimatum games, or networked/dynamic/social dilemma games—which share mechanisms but differ from the PGG in group size, payoff structure, or interaction protocol (e.g., Abbink et al., 2004; Congleton & Vanberg, 2001; Akpalu & Martinsson, 2012).
- **Weak/None**: Some papers focus on **real-world policy, psychological traits, or high-level mechanisms** without modeling PGG structure directly.

**b. Punishment or Sanctions**
- **Exact**: Core experimental and theoretical PGG papers **directly test or manipulate punishment options** (peer or institutional), often with detailed manipulation of punishment cost, magnitude, and assignment (e.g., Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Zhuang et al., 2012).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Others examine **ostracism, exclusion, reward, or reputation** in similar roles, or contextualize punishment in norm enforcement, policing, or evolutionary terms, **but do not always implement PGG punishment mechanics** (Akpalu & Martinsson, 2012; Brandts & Fatas, 2012).
- **Weak/None**: A smaller set discuss punishment conceptually, or study games without any punishment mechanism.

**c. Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**
- **Exact**: Select studies provide **quantitative measures of group efficiency or average payoff**—the target prediction variable (e.g., Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Many report related but non-equivalent outcomes (e.g., group quality, welfare, compliance, group earnings), or imply efficiency changes via cooperation/contribution or extraction rates.
- **Weak/None**: A substantial proportion focus on **non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (contribution, punishment frequency, norm adherence)—useful moderators, but not directly the prediction target.

In summary, **most directly informative evidence comes from experimental and theoretical studies in standard or close PGGs that explicitly measure group efficiency or average payoff under treatment and control.** Outcomes from adjacent or mechanistically similar environments should be interpreted cautiously and distinctly.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (directly relevant):**
  - **Group Efficiency / Average Payoff / Welfare:** Ratio of actual to maximum group payoff (e.g., Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012)
  - **Total Group Earnings / Surplus / Coins Generated:** Sometimes labeled as welfare or total output
  - **Success Ratio / Quality Score:** Sometimes in variant group tasks

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (moderators, not outcomes):**
  - **Contribution or Cooperation Rate:** Frequently measured but not equivalent to efficiency (Brandts & Fatas, 2012; Goette & Huffman, 2007)
  - **Punishment Frequency or Intensity:** How often, and how much, players assign punishment
  - **Retaliation/Counter-punishment Rate**
  - **Compliance with Norms / Target Achievement** (e.g., emission reduction compliance)
  - **Voting to Exclude / Ostracism Use**
  - **Mean Offer Rate / Trustworthiness** in dyadic bargaining
  - **Feedback, Social Pressure, Reputation/Exclusion Measures**

- **Mixed or implied outcomes:**
  - In some studies, improved cooperation or compliance is **interpreted as improved efficiency**, especially where punishment costs are low or negligible.
  - Some model studies aggregate "welfare" as total output, sometimes equating higher cooperation rates with efficiency but omitting punishment costs.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**a. Baseline:**
- In **standard repeated PGGs**, enabling strong, externally imposed punishment consistently raises group efficiency from low or Nash-equilibrium levels to near the cooperation optimum—**but only if punishment is effective, exogenous, and not prohibitively costly** (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Kranz, 2010; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010).
- **Endogenous, weak, or costly punishment institutions achieve much smaller (sometimes null or negative) efficiency gains**, especially in low-cooperation groups or those prone to retaliation (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Wolff, 2012).

**b. Moderators from Game Design Dimensions:**
- **Number of Rounds:** Efficiency gains from punishment are strongest in longer repeated games with stable groups (Frey & Rusch, 2012); short games or unstable groups undermine effectiveness.
- **Punishment Cost and Effectiveness:** High punishment impact per cost (punishment tech) is associated with higher efficiency gains. Very costly punishment can lead to efficiency losses if costs outweigh cooperation gains (Rosas, 2008; Kranz, 2010).
- **Retaliation/Counter-punishment:** Presence of retaliation can neutralize or even undermine the positive efficiency effects of punishment (Wolff, 2012).
- **Social Structure / Network:** Structured (non-random) populations, high "viscosity," or partner choice support greater punishment effectiveness and efficiency gains (Grimm & Mengel, 2011; Zhuang et al., 2012).
- **Peer vs. Pool (Institutional) Punishment:** Both can promote efficiency, but pool punishment may be needed to overcome second-order free rider problems (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012).
- **Reward vs. Punishment:** Reward is sometimes more effective than punishment in raising efficiency, given the same expenditure (Zhuang et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2011; Colombier et al., 2011).
- **Baseline Cooperation:** Baseline (control) efficiency is a strong predictor—**punishment has the greatest effect in low-efficiency/no-punishment baselines** (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Wang et al., 2011; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010).
- **Procedural Justice/Accuracy, Information, and Feedback:** Accurate, just, and well-communicated punishment systems work better (De Cremer et al., 2012).

**c. Nonlinearities and Threshold Effects:**
- There are sharp **phase transitions**: once punishment exceeds a threshold in coverage, cost-effectiveness, or severity, efficiency rises rapidly; below the threshold, gains are negligible (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012).
- **Overuse or misapplication** of punishment (especially at moderate cost, or "antisocial" punishment) can reduce efficiency by imposing unnecessary costs (Abbink et al., 2004; Colombier et al., 2011).

**d. Settings Where Punishment Decreases Efficiency:**
- In some CPR, bargaining, or nonstandard settings, enabling punishment increases cooperation but **reduces total efficiency** due to high punishment costs or misdirected punishment (Colombier et al., 2011; Abbink et al., 2004; Bolle et al., 2011; Tan & Xiao, 2012).
- Contexts with **third-party approval, procedural hurdles, or peer evaluation among friends** can lead to **reduced efficiency** compared to equal split or unrestricted peer punishment (Corgnet, 2012; Tan & Xiao, 2012).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**a. When control efficiency is low, and punishment is strong (low cost, high impact, externally imposed, little/no retaliation):**
- **Enabling peer punishment is highly likely to increase efficiency**—sometimes up to the full-cooperation benchmark, especially when punishment is externally imposed or pre-specified (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).
- Most robust in **small-to-medium-sized, repeated groups with stable membership**, clear information, and well-calibrated punishment parameters.

**b. When punishment technology is weak, costly, endogenous (decided by group), easily retaliated, or groups are unstable:**
- **Gains in efficiency are much smaller**, sometimes negligible; in rare cases, **punishment may lower efficiency** by wasting resources or provoking counter-punishment.
- **Game features** such as high punishment cost, ineffective punishment, or open retaliation pathways predict weaker or negative efficiency effects (Wolff, 2012; Kranz, 2010; Rosas, 2008).

**c. Other critical moderating factors:**
- **Social context (networking, cohesion, partner choice):** Structured/repeated interactions boost efficiency gains from punishment (Grimm & Mengel, 2011; Zhuang et al., 2012).
- **Type of punishment (peer vs. institutional):** Pool/institutional punishment can overcome second-order free riders, but only if contribution to the punishment pool is not too costly (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012).
- **Combination with rewards:** Some environments see even greater efficiency gains from well-designed reward systems or reward-punishment combinations (Zhuang et al., 2012; Colombier et al., 2011).

**d. Relationship to baseline (control) efficiency:**
- **Higher control efficiency reduces the possible gain** from punishment—diminishing returns apply (Kranz, 2010).
- **When voluntary participation, communication, or other norm-promoting mechanisms already sustain high efficiency, punishment is less crucial and may have small or null effects.**

**e. Cautions:**
- Do not infer efficiency effects from cooperation/contribution rates alone unless punishment costs are also measured and accounted for.
- For game designs or contexts not covered by the experimental or well-mapped theoretical literature, predictions should be treated as **extrapolations with greater uncertainty**.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- **player_count:** Widely manipulated and discussed; group size often moderates the effect of punishment (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).
- **num_rounds:** Critical moderator; longer games with stable partners promote larger efficiency gains from punishment (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
- **mpcr (Marginal Per Capita Return):** Key factor in almost all payoff-relevant studies (Kranz, 2010; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Central moderators (cost and impact of punishment per unit), often directly manipulated (Kranz, 2010; Rosas, 2008).
- **punishment_exists:** The core treatment manipulation.

**Indirectly Informed:**
- **all_or_nothing:** Binary vs. continuous contributions sometimes moderate efficiency effects, but less often explicitly tested.
- **chat:** Presence/absence of communication is mentioned as a moderator (communication can reduce the need for punishment).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Present in some models/experiments, typically as comparisons or moderators.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries:** Observability, feedback, and information about group performance sometimes included, especially in studies of procedural justice, reputation, or third-party punishment.

**Only Contextually Discussed:**
- **default_contrib:** Framing of default endowment/contribution is rarely the primary experimental variable for payoff-based results.
- **show_punishment_id:** Identity of punishers/rewarders is less frequently manipulated in efficiency-focused studies, though sometimes relevant for norm enforcement.

**Effectively Missing for Prediction:**
- No substantial evidence mapping **every design dimension** to efficiency effects; some dimensions (e.g., default_contrib, chat, show_punishment_id) lack comprehensive treatment in efficiency-focused studies.
- **Complex institutional details, strategic uncertainty, or real-world political/institutional context** are present only in select papers and often in adjacent literature.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Outcome consistency:** While experimental and theoretical studies generally support the positive effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in standard PGGs, **not all studies report efficiency directly**; many rely on contributions or behavioral proxies.
- **Generalizability:** Findings are strongest for **standard, small-group, repeated PGGs**—caution is needed when generalizing to CPR settings, one-shot games, dynamic networks, or policy contexts.
- **Scope of moderators:** Some critical moderators (e.g., retaliation, antisocial punishment, group heterogeneity, social structure) are not present or systematically varied in every study.
- **Ambiguity at boundary conditions:** For **weak or costly punishment, or in the presence of retaliation or antisocial punishment,** the effect on efficiency can be null or negative, and studies disagree about the circumstances for these outcomes.
- **Sparse evidence for rarely manipulated dimensions** (e.g., show_punishment_id, default_contrib) means that their impact on efficiency must be interpolated with caution.
- **Payoff vs. non-payoff outcomes:** Many papers focus on non-payoff outcomes or sidestep explicit group efficiency/welfare, which limits the direct evidence available for the target prediction.
- **Adjacent and real-world contexts:** Papers on ostracism, policing, exclusion, third-party punishment, or evolutionary/real-world institutions are sometimes only **loosely analogous** to experimental PGGs and should be applied to prediction with appropriate caution.
- **Potential publication and reporting bias:** Most attention is given to cases where punishment increases cooperation or efficiency; **less is known about environments where punishment fails, is misapplied, or is rendered unnecessary by other mechanisms.**

---

**In summary:** The literature strongly supports positive efficiency effects from enabling peer punishment in standard PGGs when punishment is strong, well-calibrated, and external. Moderators such as group size, time horizon, punishment cost/effectiveness, social/network structure, and the baseline level of cooperation are critical. Negative or null effects emerge when punishment is weak, costly, open to retaliation, or when other norm reinforcement mechanisms are strong. Several design dimensions are well-covered; for others, evidence is sparse or indirect. Prediction models should prioritize directly measured efficiency findings in close PGG designs, use behavioral outcomes only as moderators, and extrapolate cautiously in under-evidenced domains.
