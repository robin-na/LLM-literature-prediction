# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises a mix of empirical and theoretical work, with strong representation of both laboratory experiments and formal models. Of particular note:
- **Empirical Experimental**: Multiple high-quality lab studies directly manipulate peer punishment in canonical PGGs, often reporting both behavioral and payoff-based outcomes. There are also studies using close variants, such as prisoner's dilemma, ROSCA, or common-pool-resource games.
- **Theory/Simulation**: There is a broad range of analytical models and simulations addressing punishment in PGGs, commonly focusing on payoff evolution, critical thresholds for punishment effectiveness, and the interplay of punishment with other mechanisms (e.g., reward, group structure).
- **Breadth/Narrowness**: For the target prediction task—estimating efficiency changes from punishment enabled—the direct evidence base is **reasonably broad and deep** for standard, repeated linear PGGs with peer punishment. However, coverage is sparser or more ambiguous for variants (e.g., collective punishment, noisy punishment, nonlinear returns, network structure) or games with design features less typical in lab PGGs.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessed by three dimensions:

**a) PGG or Variant**  
- **Exact**: Many studies are true public goods lab experiments or theoretical analyses (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Several papers use close variants, such as prisoner's dilemma in groups, resource dilemmas, contest games, or ROSCA (e.g., van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Koike et al., 2018; Romano et al., 2024).
- **None/Weak**: A set use only adjacent games or extract behavioral analogies from non-economic domains.

**b) Punishment or Sanctions**  
- **Exact**: Core lab PGG studies and most theory papers manipulate peer punishment directly and analyze its effects (e.g., Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013).
- **Adjacent/Weak**: Some studies examine social exclusion, centralized sanctions, or reward only, or treat punishment distally (e.g., Ozono et al., 2016; Koike et al., 2018).
- **None**: Studies without any punishment manipulation.

**c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**  
- **Exact**: Several empirical and theory papers report efficiency (total payoff relative to optimum), group payoff, or welfare (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Papers reporting average earnings, proportion of optimum, or comparing total group payoffs—without full normalization.
- **Weak/None**: Behavioral outcome only (e.g., contribution rate, punishment frequency), or exclusive focus on psychological/motivational measures.

**Summary**: The highest task relevance is in canonical lab PGGs with peer punishment and group efficiency measured (exact/exact/exact). For other design features or close game variants, relevance decreases.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-based Outcomes (Directly Inform Prediction)**
- **Group Efficiency**: Total realized payoff as a proportion of the group maximum, i.e., efficiency (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020).
- **Group Payoff/Average Profit**: Total or average group profit (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).
- **Welfare/Surplus**: Occasionally labeled as welfare or surplus, conceptually same as efficiency.
- **Earnings**: Sometimes reported raw; occasionally normalized.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Important Moderators, Not Direct Predictors)**
- **Contribution/Cooperation Rate**: Frequency or amount, but not accounting for punishment costs.
- **Punishment Frequency/Assigned Punishment**: Used for mediation, but not equivalent to efficiency.
- **Norm Compliance, Perceptions, Strategic Use of Punishment**: These inform mechanism but not payoff outcomes.
- **Network Effects, Reputation Dynamics**: Adjacent, rarely directly calculating efficiency.

**Explicit distinctions in the literature**: Several studies highlight the risk of inferring efficiency from increased contributions, noting that costly punishment can offset or outweigh gains from higher cooperation (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Kubena et al., 2014).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Patterns**
- **Punishment increases efficiency**: In standard linear repeated PGGs with deterministic, efficient peer punishment, adding punishment typically leads to a substantial **increase in group efficiency** relative to control/no-punishment—especially when baseline cooperation is low (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Roberts, 2013; Hintze & Sornette, 2013).
- **But: Effectiveness is conditional**:
    - **Punishment parameters**: Low punishment cost and high impact are critical (Roberts, 2013; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Hintze & Sornette, 2013).
    - **Noise/Antisocial punishment**: Efficiency gains are undone or reversed if punishment is noisy/stochastic, leading to frequent errors or antisocial punishment (Salahshour et al., 2022; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
    - **Weak punishment or competitive environment**: If punishment is too weak, non-costly, or used for rivalry/status, efficiency does **not** increase and may decline (Kamijo et al., 2020; Paál & Bereczkei, 2015; Romano et al., 2024; Honjo & Kubo, 2020).
    - **Baseline efficiency**: If the control game were already near optimum (e.g., prosocial-type populations), punishment might add little or nothing (Honjo & Kubo, 2020).
    - **Collective/centralized punishment**: Effectiveness varies; collective or impersonal punishment may fail or perform worse than peer punishment (Chapkovski, 2021; Koike et al., 2018).
    - **Returns to scale/Production function**: In games with increasing returns or non-linearities, punishment can dramatically increase efficiency, but not so in standard linear PGGs unless parameters are favorable (Ye et al., 2016).
    - **Reward vs. punishment**: In inefficient PGGs (MPCR < 1), reward is much more effective at raising efficiency than punishment (Kamijo et al., 2020).
    - **Information structure/network**: Efficacy of punishment can be boosted (or heavily moderated) by network/intergroup structure, monitoring accuracy, and whether partners are fixed or changing (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Galan et al., 2011).
- **Empirical vs theory**: Theory papers consistently predict strong efficiency gains from punishment under favorable conditions (Roberts, 2013; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013), but some empirical studies show mixed or null effects, especially with noisy, antisocial, or misapplied punishment (van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Kamijo et al., 2020).
- **Adjacent and mixed findings**: In adjacent settings or with institutional variations (e.g., exclusion vs. direct punishment), exclusion tends to outperform simple punishment (Koike et al., 2018), and in competitive, status-driven or fragmented environments, punishment may foster inefficiency (Romano et al., 2024; Honjo & Kubo, 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**What The Literature Supports About Predicting Treatment Efficiency:**

- **Substantial efficiency gains** from enabling peer punishment are a robust empirical and theoretical expectation in standard linear repeated PGGs with:
    - **Sufficiently low-cost/high-impact** punishment mechanisms,
    - **Deterministic and accurate** punishment (minimal noise),
    - **No strong social, competitive, or group-heterogeneity distortions**,
    - **Baseline (control) efficiency** not already near the optimum.
    - Example: If a control PGG yields low efficiency (<60%), enabling efficient peer punishment may bring groups near social optimum (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
- **Conditional effect or even declines in efficiency** are predicted if:
    - **Punishment is noisy/stochastic**, causing errors or antisocial punishment (Salahshour et al., 2022; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
    - **Antisocial/competitive uses** of punishment dominate (Paál & Bereczkei, 2015; Romano et al., 2024).
    - **Punishment is collective/centralized**, misaligned, or not well-targeted (Chapkovski, 2021; Koike et al., 2018).
    - **Punishment cost is too high** or impact too low (Roberts, 2013).
    - **Baseline efficiency is already high/players are prosocial**, so added punishment gives little marginal gain (Honjo & Kubo, 2020).
- **Efficiency may not rise even with increased cooperation** if punishment costs are large: Some studies warn that the costliness of punishment can eat up efficiency gains from extra cooperation (Kamijo et al., 2020; Hintze & Sornette, 2013).
- **Prediction in non-linear or threshold environments**: If the PGG has increasing returns to scale, punishment can yield non-linear jumps to high efficiency (Ye et al., 2016); if returns are too low, effects may be small or null.
- **Incentive structures matter**: Reward alone can often outperform punishment in inefficient PGGs (Kamijo et al., 2020).

**Overall**, when using control efficiency and design dimensions for prediction:
- Use empirical effect sizes if the new (treatment) game matches the canonical PGG designs (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment ratio, etc.).
- If design dimensions differ (e.g., high noise, group heterogeneity, collective punishment, nonlinear returns), moderate or even reverse the expected efficiency boost accordingly.
- Do **not** infer efficiency improvements solely from higher contributions unless net payoff is reported (Kamijo et al., 2020).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly/Best Informed Dimensions**
- **player_count**: Frequently and explicitly varied; group size effects modelled or tested, though not always as a primary moderator.
- **num_rounds**: Standard lab PGGs range from 4–30 rounds; theory demonstrates importance of repeated play.
- **mpcr**: Central parameter; many papers analyze efficiency as a function of MPCR.
- **punishment_cost & punishment_tech (cost/impact ratio)**: Nearly all studies stress the importance; some provide explicit boundaries or phase transitions in effect.
- **all_or_nothing**: Varied across studies, though less often analyzed as a moderator.
- **chat**: Manipulated in control for communication effects.
- **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech**: Sometimes contrasted with punishment; reward can outperform punishment in certain environments (Kamijo et al., 2020).
- **show_other_summaries**: Feedback is almost always present in lab PGGs; level of feedback about others' choices sometimes manipulated.
- **punishment noise/stochasticity** (a variant of punishment_tech): Strong empirical evidence on efficiency reversal if punishment becomes noisy (Salahshour et al., 2022).

**Indirectly or Occasionally Informed Dimensions**
- **default_contrib**: Only a few papers manipulate framing of default vs. opt-in contributions.
- **show_punishment_id**: Sometimes present, but rarely varied as a moderator for efficiency.
- **show_n_rounds**: Sometimes discussed as a feature, but little evidence on its impact on efficiency.
- **Reward mechanisms**: Usually presented in comparison; findings suggest enabling both reward and punishment provides different, often additive effects.

**Contextually Discussed or Missing**
- **Sanctioning institutions, collective punishment, centralized punishment**: Evidence exists, but studies are fewer or diverge from PGGs, so generalizability is lower.
- **Network structure/inter-group migration**: Several theory papers; empirical evidence sparse and sometimes conflicting.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Generalizability to Non-Canonical Settings**: Strong evidence is available for standard, small-group, repeated linear PGGs with deterministic, observable peer punishment. Findings for large groups, threshold goods, networked/fragmented populations, or more complex institutional designs are less secure.
- **Efficiency vs. Contribution**: Many studies and especially older literature confound efficiency with contribution. The best papers warn against inferring efficiency gains from higher cooperation rates if punishment cost is high.
- **Lack of Parameter Sweeps in Lab Experiments**: Many empirical studies only present results for a fixed set of punishment parameters (cost, impact), limiting ability to extrapolate effects as these parameters vary.
- **Ambiguity in Nonlinear or Noisy Settings**: Empirical results are sometimes mixed in non-linear or high-noise environments, with efficiency sometimes decreased by enabling punishment.
- **Treatment of Antisocial Punishment**: Not all studies distinguish norm-enforcing from antisocial uses of punishment, which can be critical moderators of outcomes.
- **Sparse Evidence for Some Design Dimensions**: Several predictors (default_contrib, show_punishment_id, reward_cost/tech) are underexplored as moderators of efficiency impacts.
- **Lack of Field Evidence**: Most evidence is from laboratory settings; field and naturalistic evidence is sparse and largely descriptive.
- **Limitations in Close Variants**: Papers using close (but not exact) variants—such as prisoner's dilemma, contest, or ROSCA—show that distinctions in game structure and sanctioning mechanisms can meaningfully alter conclusions.
- **Heterogeneity of Baseline Efficiency**: Prediction accuracy depends crucially on control condition efficiency, which itself is highly sensitive to e.g. group composition, framing, prior experience—often not systematically reported.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature base provides strong, convergent evidence that enabling effective, peer-administered, deterministic punishment in repeated linear public goods games generally increases efficiency relative to control, unless punishment is poorly targeted, highly noisy, inefficient, or used for rivalry/status. Key design features—group size, MPCR, punishment parameters—moderate this effect, and when control efficiency is already high, marginal gains are limited. Some dimensions and institutional features, notably network structure, collective punishment, and behavioral heterogeneity, are less well covered and can sometimes reverse the expected pattern. Predictions should be grounded using direct efficiency outcomes, not proxy behavioral measures, and contextualized to the closest available experimental findings on the relevant design dimensions and baseline efficiency.
