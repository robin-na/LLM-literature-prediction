# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is **broad and deep**, containing 142 studies covering empirical lab and field experiments as well as theoretical models. The majority are laboratory public goods games (PGGs) or close variants, with strong representation of games with and without peer punishment, and a substantial subset reporting efficiency or directly comparable payoff outcomes. Theoretical contributions complement the empirical literature, clarifying mechanisms and boundary conditions. The set includes both classic symmetric PGGs and numerous variants—adding heterogeneity, leadership, institution/voting, reward, communication, and various forms of punishment (peer, leader, formal, majority, central, ostracism, quota, etc.).

Papers range from tightly controlled lab studies to field and artefactual field experiments and simulation/modeling studies. While some papers focus only on behavioral outcomes (cooperation, contribution rates), a large number report group payoff, efficiency, or closely related outcomes, facilitating analysis relevant to the downstream prediction task.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessing the literature against three target-relevance dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant**
  - **exact**: The majority of experimental papers are standard linear PGGs or direct laboratory analogues (e.g., Kamei, 2024; Chen, 2022; Krügel & Maaser, 2025). Many theory papers explicitly model standard or simplest PGGs.
  - **close**: Some studies employ CPR (common-pool resources), trust, or minimum-effort (weakest-link) games—with similar social dilemma structure but not literal PGGs (e.g., Ntuli et al., 2023; Schaefer, 2023).
  - **adjacent/weak**: A minority focus on dyadic games (PD, trust), static allocation, or market-like settings.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**
  - **exact**: Many experiments introduce peer or centralized costly punishment, ostracism, or formal sanctions (e.g., Kamei, 2024; Botelho et al., 2022).
  - **close**: Quota enforcement, tax-based punishment, or institutional punishment variants (e.g., Dughera, 2022; Libois, 2022).
  - **adjacent or weak**: Some only feature reward, reputation, or partner switching, rather than true payoff-reducing punishment.
  
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**
  - **exact**: A substantial subset report efficiency as the ratio of group payoff to the cooperative optimum (e.g., Kamei, 2024; Chen, 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024).
  - **close**: Studies using total group earning, welfare, resource conservation, or reduction in extraction (e.g., Ntuli et al., 2023).
  - **adjacent or weak**: Others only report behavioral outcomes, necessitating inference about efficiency (e.g., contribution rates only).

**Summary:** Overall, the literature set provides high direct relevance to the downstream prediction task, with many exact matches on game form, punishment treatment, and efficiency outcomes. Some subliteratures (CPR, trust games, complex institutions) slightly diverge but retain conceptual proximity.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

The papers distinguish between two categories:

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (**directly relevant**):  
  - *Efficiency (fraction of possible group payoff)*, average group payoff/earnings, total welfare, resource conservation (analogous to efficiency in CPR), surplus generated, total coins.
  - Example: "Efficiency increases from 0.42 to 0.71 under punishment" (Ntuli et al., 2023), or "Average per-period earnings are lower with punishment than without" (Chen, 2022).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (**supportive but less direct**):  
  - Group or individual *contribution rates*, cooperation rates, norm compliance, punishment assigned, monitoring/voting patterns, compliance rates, retaliation incidence.
  - Example: "Punishment increases average contributions but does not increase efficiency" (Peng, 2022b), or "Punishment raises compliance but reduces group payoff" (Deng et al., 2025).

**Explicit distinction:** Several findings note that punishment may increase cooperation (contributions) but *fail to increase* or may *decrease* group efficiency due to the direct costs of punishment (e.g., Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical synthesis:**

- **Punishment often but not always increases efficiency:**
  - *Positive effects:* Enabling punishment reliably increases efficiency when:
    - The punishment mechanism is well-calibrated (not excessively costly, effective in deterring free riding) (Kamei, 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Ntuli et al., 2023; Joseph et al., 2025).
    - Punishment is institutionalized/formal, especially in open or dynamic group settings (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Kamei et al., 2023).
    - Punishment is minimal but targeted (punish only the lowest contributor), as shown theoretically (Huang et al., 2024).
    - Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is rare, or its frequency is reduced by institutional design (Peng, 2022b; JARUNGRATTANAPONG, 2022).
    - Group size is moderate (3–5), MPCR is not too low, and communication is absent or controlled.

  - *Negative or null effects:* Punishment fails to increase, or reduces, efficiency when:
    - The punishment is costly relative to the marginal per capita return, so costs offset gains (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Peng, 2022b).
    - There is substantial antisocial or retaliatory punishment, especially in heterogeneous/higher-MPCR groups (Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022b).
    - Network structure leads to diffusion of responsibility, reducing punishment effectiveness (Peng & Fan, 2023).
    - The opportunity for punishment is avoidable (e.g., voluntary participation, as in Del Ponte et al., 2025).
    - Punishment mechanisms are collective or probabilistic rather than targeted, or involve significant second-order punishment (Schaefer, 2023; Jiang & Villeval, 2024).

- **Contextual and interaction effects:**
  - *Leader or central punishment*: Effectiveness can depend on the leader’s gender, selection method, and framing (Jiang & Wang, 2024).
  - *Type of punishment*: Formal (centralized) punishment is often more effective than informal/peer punishment, especially in open communities (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
  - *Magnitude/credibility*: Larger and more credible punishment increases efficiency more (Ye et al., 2023; Alt et al., 2023).
  - *Repeated vs. one-shot*: Punishment increases efficiency in both settings, but effects are larger and more robust in repeated games (Kamei, 2024; Joseph et al., 2025).
  - *Group heterogeneity*: In heterogeneous MPCR settings, punishment often fails to boost efficiency, and sometimes makes it worse due to antisocial/ineffective targeting (Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022b; Bühren et al., 2025).
  - *Institutional design*: Majority-voting or third-party punishment can reduce antisocial punishment and help efficiency (Peng, 2022b; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Zong et al., 2025), but the effect is highly context-specific.

- **Mechanisms:**
  - Efficient punishment is:
    - Targeted (at defectors only; little anti-social use).
    - Minimal (just enough to deter, but not overused or vengeful).
    - Backed by high probability or certainty of enforcement.
    - Either formal (strong, credible institution) or peer-based with strong shared norms against anti-social use.

  - Inefficient punishment is:
    - Used frequently, including antisocially or in retaliation.
    - Costly (relative to public good return).
    - Applied in complex or diffuse networks, which can undermine its effect.

**Theory synthesis:**

- *Punishment increases efficiency only above certain thresholds of severity, credibility, and when targeted appropriately* (Huang et al., 2024; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).
- *Punishment’s efficacy is diminished in large groups or with commitment types unless it is involuntary and targeted* (Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023).
- *Where reward instruments are available, efficient use of reward in tandem with minimal punishment maximizes welfare* (Huang et al., 2024).

**Ambiguity/disagreement:**

- Even in highly controlled settings, some studies find net *negative* or *null* efficiency effects due to the direct destruction of resources by costly punishment (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Calabuig et al., 2024), especially when anti-social punishment or ineffective targeting is common.
- Other studies emphasize that *procedural legitimacy* or *cultural context* can moderate the effect, such that similar institutions differ in outcome across experimental settings or populations (Kamei et al., 2025; Kamei, 2024).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should this literature inform prediction of treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency?**

- **General principle:**  
  - **Punishment is most likely to increase efficiency when:**  
    1. The baseline (no-punishment) efficiency is low (i.e., considerable free-riding).
    2. The punishment is not excessively costly.
    3. Design details support precise, targeted, anti-defector punishment, and the anchoring effect of anti-social punishment is minimized.

- **Prediction model inputs:**  
  - Control (no-punishment) efficiency reliably predicts the upper bound for achievable efficiency under punishment: if the control game has high efficiency, additional gains from punishment will likely be small or even negative (Botelho et al., 2022).
  - The effect size of adding punishment is strongly modulated by:
    - **player_count:** Moderate-sized groups (3–5) are most conducive to positive effects; large, anonymous groups may weaken punishment’s effect.
    - **mpcr:** Higher MPCR makes cooperation more individually rational; in low-MPCR games, costly punishment is less likely to yield efficiency gains.
    - **punishment_cost & punishment_tech:** High-impact, low-cost punishment is more effective; anti-social or retaliatory punishment (common with low MPCR or high heterogeneity) can offset gains.
    - **num_rounds:** More rounds enhance learning and can stabilize efficient cooperation under punishment, but endgame effects (declining efficiency) are common.
    - **all_or_nothing:** Continuous action spaces (not binary) provide more room for marginal nudges by punishment.
    - **institutional design:** Centralized, formal, or majority-voting punishment schemes often outperform decentralized peer schemes, especially in reducing anti-social punishment.
    - **reward_exists:** If rewards are available, using both minimal punishment and maximal reward is more efficient (Huang et al., 2024).

- **Adjustment based on indirect or context-only evidence:**  
  - Where only contribution or compliance data are present, infer that efficiency will move in the same direction as contribution *unless* punishment costs are high enough to absorb surplus.
  - Where anti-social punishment or inefficiency due to institutional complexity is evident, be cautious: predicted efficiency may not improve and could worsen.
  - If the punishment institution is avoidable, non-mandatory, or subject to exclusion, expect treatment efficiency to be little changed from control.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (reported/mechanistically explored in multiple papers):**
- **player_count**: Most studies use 3–5; effects of group size systematically examined (e.g., Kamei et al., 2023; Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023).
- **num_rounds**: Strong evidence on finite vs. infinite/unknown round effects.
- **chat**: Most studies enforce no chat; some test and show its effects (e.g., Kamei et al., 2023).
- **all_or_nothing**: Binary vs. continuous contributions examined; continuous action spaces facilitate marginal deterrence.
- **mpcr**: Key moderator; higher MPCR increases baseline efficiency and reduces marginal gain from punishment.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Central for both empirical and theoretical results; directly quantified in numerous studies.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Some studies analyze joint or alternative use of reward and punishment.
  
**Indirectly informed (occasionally varied or discussed):**
- **default_contrib**: Explored in a few studies; non-defaults (opt-in/opt-out) affect baseline cooperation.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries**: Manipulated in a minority; information structure moderately influences learning and norm adherence.
- **show_punishment_id**: Rarely manipulated, but presence/absence of anonymity is sometimes discussed as affecting retaliation/antisocial punishment.

**Contextually addressed (not main focus but mentioned):**
- **heterogeneity** (in returns, endowments): Directly connected to the likelihood of anti-social punishment or reduced efficiency gains from punishment (Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022b).
- **institutional structure** (complete vs. incomplete punishment networks, formal vs. informal punishment).

**Effectively missing or sparse:**
- Precise details on *real-world* external validity (most papers are laboratory studies with abstract framing).
- *Edge-case* design features: dynamic/changing network structures, large population games, integrated second-order punishment.

# 7) Important Limitations

**Scope and boundaries:**

- **Cost of punishment is critical:** In several high-quality studies, costly punishment increases contributions but *reduces or fails to improve efficiency*, especially under low MPCR or frequent anti-social use.
- **Heterogeneity issues:** When group members have different returns or endowments, punishment can induce retaliation or anti-social use, blunting or reversing efficiency gains (Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022b).
- **Anti-social punishment:** Not all contexts reliably prevent punishment of higher contributors, diluting the institution’s effect.
- **Institutional complexity:** More complex punishment institutions (e.g., need for coordination, incomplete networks, second-order punishment) can muddy predictions, sometimes reducing efficiency unpredictably.
- **External validity:** Most evidence is from laboratory or framed field experiments; real-world (field) representation and high-level institutional mechanisms are less common.
- **Interaction with communication/mainstreaming:** Papers with multiple overlapping mechanisms (e.g., chat, coalition formation, rewards, leader selection) make it harder to isolate punishment effects.
- **Outcome reporting:** Some otherwise high-quality studies only report behavioral outcomes, requiring indirect inference for efficiency.
- **Edge-case or parametric sensitivities:** Prediction for games with large groups, multiple interacting institutions, dynamic resource feedback, or uncertain round numbers is less secure; some theoretical models identify parameter thresholds where standard intuitions fail.
- **Cultural/contextual moderators:** Some effects are highly dependent on norms, procedural legitimacy, or cultural context, potentially limiting universal generalizability (Kamei et al., 2025; Kamei, 2024).

---

**In sum:** This literature base provides strong, nuanced, and partially context-dependent guidance for predicting the efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments using game design dimensions and control efficiency as inputs. The direction and magnitude of the effect depend systematically on cost/tech of punishment, group size, MPCR, institution design, and baseline efficiency, with important caveats around anti-social use, heterogeneity, and context. Predictions must attend to these moderating factors and preserve justified uncertainty when control group efficiency is high, punishment is costly, punishment is abused, or institutional structures are complex.
