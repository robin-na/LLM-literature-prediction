# 1) Evidence Base

The supplied evidence base consists predominantly of high-quality empirical laboratory experiments, with a substantial concentration on standard linear public goods games (PGGs) and close variants. The data set is both broad and deep regarding the central prediction task—estimating efficiency shifts following the introduction of peer punishment—though a sizeable subset of studies address adjacent designs (e.g., trust games, CPR games, contest games, dyadic interactions) or focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes. Most papers feature clearly specified experimental parameters, with outcomes that include efficiency or group payoff (often defined as total earnings or average profit relative to the cooperative optimum), though some studies report only on contribution rates or punishment behavior. The evidence base includes a small number of field experiments and observational studies, which enrich the understanding of external validity but rarely provide direct efficiency estimates.

# 2) Task Relevance

### a) PGG or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`)
- **Exact relevance**: The majority of key studies employ standard linear PGGs or the voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM) (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sutter et al., 2010; Masclet et al., 2003). Many adjacent studies cover CPR dilemmas or contest environments that deviate from canonical PGGs (e.g., Ostrom et al., 1992; Abbink et al., 2010).
- **Relevance label coverage**: Most informative studies are `exact` (for PGG), with a meaningful subset rated `close` or `adjacent`, providing cross-checks on mechanism generalizability.

### b) Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`)
- **Exact relevance**: Numerous studies enable direct peer punishment consistent with the prediction task (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Rand et al., 2009), including both monetary and non-monetary forms, as well as alternative punishment technologies (ostracism, delegated punishment).
- **Variation**: A portion of studies implement institutional (centralized) punishment or examine the effect of rule-based (e.g., tax/fine) systems, and some analyze reward or mixed institutions, all of which are relevant for model comparisons.

### c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`)
- **Exact or close relevance**: Scores of studies provide *direct efficiency or group payoff* measures (e.g., ratio of actual to maximum possible earnings), or closely related welfare outcomes.
- **Limitations**: Some highly-cited studies, especially those focused on behavioral or psychological mechanisms, report only on contribution rates, norm compliance, or punishment frequency, limiting their predictive use for efficiency.

**Conclusion**: The set is highly relevant, especially in the intersection of exact PGG designs, peer punishment, and explicit efficiency outcomes, although the scope for generalization beyond standard lab PGGs is best supported with caution.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`):**
- Explicit measures of group efficiency (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000); total group earnings (e.g., Gürerk et al., 2006); welfare or surplus (e.g., Andreoni & Gee, 2012).
- Occasionally, related proxies such as average contribution per group, but only when mapped quantitatively to payoffs.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- Contribution/cooperation rates, punishment/reward frequency, anti-social punishment rates, norm compliance.
- Psychological and attitudinal outcomes, e.g., willingness to punish (Morality/anger/retaliation), trust, belief updating.
- Some studies provide detailed evidence on behavioral mechanisms or motivations (e.g., Herrmann et al., 2008; Seip et al., 2009), which are contextually useful but cannot substitute for payoff-based results.

**Clarity in reporting:**
- Many canonical studies provide both behavioral (contribution) and payoff-based outcomes, but only efficiency or payoff outcomes are valid for the target prediction.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**A) Peer Punishment Increases Efficiency—But Moderated by Key Contexts**

- **Standard finding**: Enabling costly peer punishment in repeated linear PGGs generally increases efficiency compared to the control with no punishment, as it deters free riding and sustains contributions over time. Efficiency gains are typically in the range of 10–40% over control, especially as control contributions decay in repeated-play (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Masclet et al., 2003; Gintis et al., 2003).
- **Magnitude and limits**: The net efficiency gain is often less than the gain in contributions due to the cost paid for punishing; some studies show that in the short run, or if punishment is frequent, efficiency can be below control (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Ambrus & Greiner, 2012).

**B) Moderators That Shape the Efficiency Effect of Punishment**

- **Monitoring/Information Quality:** Punishment only increases efficiency if contributions are accurately observable. Under noisy monitoring, punishment is often mistargeted, increasing antisocial punishment and reducing efficiency below control (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Grechenig et al., 2010).
- **Group composition and culture:** The efficiency benefit of punishment is highly context-dependent; in societies or groups with high antisocial punishment, efficiency does *not* increase and may decline (Herrmann et al., 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Barclay, 2004 [group/ethnicity moderation]).
- **Cost and Leverage of Punishment:** The cost-to-impact ratio critically moderates outcomes: if punishment is relatively cheap and/or highly effective, efficiency gains are largest; with high punishment costs or low effectiveness, efficiency gains are reduced or reversed (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Sutter et al., 2010; Ertan et al., 2009).
- **Alternative Punishment Technologies:** Delegated or centralized punishment, targeted ostracism, and institutionalized (voted, formal) sanctioning can yield larger efficiency gains than uncoordinated peer punishment (Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Gürerk et al., 2006; Masclet, 2003). Designating a single punisher can reduce redundant punishment costs and improve efficiency (O'Gorman et al., 2009).
- **Pool punishment and second-order punishment:** Pool punishment (institutional, group-funded) and especially second-order punishment mechanisms can reduce efficiency due to high fixed costs—even as cooperation is stabilized (Traulsen et al., 2012).
- **Combined Mechanisms:** Allowing both reward and punishment, or integrating communication and reputation, often leads to higher efficiency than punishment alone (Rand et al., 2009; Bochet & Putterman, 2009; Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006; Milinski et al., 2002).
- **Match type and sorting:** Partner (fixed group) matching supports higher efficiency than stranger (random) matching, especially with punishment (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Masclet et al., 2003).
- **Group size:** Larger groups often see reduced effectiveness of exclusion/ostracism punishment (Kerr et al., 2009; Camera & Casari, 2009), though peer punishment efficacy per se is variably reported.

**C) Conditions Where Punishment Reduces Efficiency**

- **Antisocial and counter-punishment:** Where punishment is used against high contributors or escalates into vendettas (counter-punishment), group efficiency falls, sometimes below control (Nikiforakis, 2008; Fehl et al., 2012; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).
- **Noisy, contest, or competitive environments:** In contest-like public goods or where between-group competition is introduced, punishment may drive unproductive expenditures, wasting resources and sharply reducing efficiency (Abbink et al., 2010; Leibbrandt & Sääksvuori, 2012).
- **Cultural and institutional settings:** In some non-Western contexts, Russia, or where trust/self-determination is high, punishment crowds out cooperation, producing negative efficiency effects (Vollan, 2008; Gächter et al., 2008).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given well-specified design dimensions and observed control efficiency, the following predictions are best justified:

- **General rule (standard, repeated linear PGG):** Enabling peer punishment will, on average, increase efficiency compared to control if baseline efficiency is low, information is accurate, and antisocial punishment is limited. Typical gains are in the 10–40% range over control.
- **If the control efficiency is already high (due to communication, social learning, or other non-punitive interventions), the effect of enabling punishment is reduced or can become neutral/negative**, as punishment costs are unnecessary.
- **If information about others' contributions is noisy, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency below the control outcome.**
- **If punishment is unrestricted (i.e., can target anyone), or if counter-punishment is possible, enabling punishment can lead to efficiency losses. Restricting punishment to clear free riders and minimizing opportunity for counter-punishment enhances efficiency gains.**
- **When enabling punishment via centralized mechanisms, or by designating a single punisher, efficiency is improved relative to diffuse peer punishment, due to coordinated targeting and reduced redundant punishment costs.**
- **When both reward and punishment options exist, the greatest efficiency is achieved by groups that emphasize reward or combine both, especially when reward is highly effective.**
- **Cultural/social context and group composition must be factored for accurate prediction. In settings prone to antisocial punishment or where strong norms exist against punishment, enabling punishment may have little or negative effect on efficiency.**
- **Group size and matching protocol are nontrivial moderators. Efficiency gains from punishment are strongest in small, stable groups with repeated interaction.**
- **Effect sizes can be calibrated (with uncertainty) using quantitative results from well-matched studies. For canonical PGGs: enabling punishment typically shifts final-period efficiency from ~70% (control) to ~85–90% (with peer punishment) under best-case conditions (Fehr & Gächter, 2000). In edge cases, or with poorly designed punishment, the effect may be null or negative (Nikiforakis, 2008; Egas & Riedl, 2008).**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count` and `num_rounds`: Routinely manipulated; group size (3–16), round count (1–50), with effects on learning, decay, and other dynamics.
- `mpcr`: Manipulated in many studies; shown to moderate baseline efficiency and interact with punishment.
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech` (impact/leverage): Well explored; cost/impact ratio is consistently a strong moderator.
- `punishmentExists`: Central treatment in the majority.
- `all_or_nothing`: Both continuous and binary designs tested; some all-or-nothing studies suggest similar or slightly weaker effects for punishment vs. continuous.

**Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
- `chat`: While direct communication is rare in canonical punishment studies, many studies contrast chat versus no-chat conditions, typically showing communication alone can substitute for punishment (Ostrom et al., 1992; Bochet & Putterman, 2009).
- `reward_exists` and related: Discussed as alternative or complement, with many studies showing reward can be as or more effective than punishment for efficiency.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Feedback, information structure, and identity revelation are discussed primarily as moderators of targeting and antisocial punishment.
- `default_contrib`: Not commonly manipulated directly, but some studies (e.g., Messer & Zarghamee, 2007) explore framing effects.

**Effectively Missing or Sparse:**
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (except in studies directly comparing punishment and reward).
- `show_punishment_id`: While some examine anonymous vs. public punishment, most canonical studies use anonymous peer punishment.
- Fine-grained manipulation of summary info, group migration, network structure (aside from a few recent network studies).

# 7) Important Limitations

**a) Scope of Laboratory Experiments**
- Most core results come from small, stylized lab PGGs. Predictions in complex, real-world, or field settings (with endogenous social context, high baseline efficiency, or non-standard incentives) are less certain. Evidence from field/large-group settings is mixed and context-contingent.

**b) Context Dependency and Cultural Moderators**
- Effectiveness of punishment on efficiency is not universal; it is strongly context-dependent (e.g., prevalence of antisocial punishment, local norms, trust, cultural background). Many studies demonstrate large between-society and within-group variation (Herrmann et al., 2008; Barclay, 2004).

**c) Dependence on Information Quality**
- Many positive results require accurate, timely, and transparent information about others' contributions. Under noisy or incomplete monitoring, punishment can become inefficient or destructive.

**d) Assumptions about Punishment Targeting**
- Predictions based on studies where punishment is effectively targeted at free riders may break down when opportunities for antisocial or counter-punishment are present.

**e) Generalizability Across Designs**
- Findings from canonical PGGs with continuous contributions and monetary punishment may not generalize with high confidence to all-or-nothing, contest, or CPR-like games, or to varying technology (e.g., institutionally imposed vs. peer sanctions).

**f) Temporal Dynamics**
- Short-term versus long-term effects can diverge. Punishment may reduce efficiency in early rounds (due to immediate cost), with gains only occurring as cooperation stabilizes in later rounds (Masclet et al., 2003; Balliet et al., 2011).

**g) Literature Gaps**
- Some dimensions (such as framing, default contributions, summary info, feedback structures) remain underexplored. Few studies test combinatorial effects of all 14 prediction dimensions.

---

In summary, **enabling peer punishment in public-goods-game-like environments generally increases efficiency relative to the control with punishment disabled, but the magnitude and direction of this effect are moderated by monitoring quality, cultural/social context, punishment cost-effectiveness, and the design of the punishment mechanism**. Predictions should integrate control efficiency and design dimensions, while accounting for possible negative or null effects in cases of antisocial punishment, ineffective targeting, high punishment costs, or noisy monitoring. For maximally valid prediction, close matching to canonical lab study conditions is recommended, and caution is warranted when extrapolating outside that domain.
