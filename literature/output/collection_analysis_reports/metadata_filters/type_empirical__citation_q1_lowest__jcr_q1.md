# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is very broad and consists predominantly of empirical, experimental lab studies, with a solid mix of field experiments and a small proportion of theoretical or observational papers. For the prediction task—forecasting the efficiency effect of punishment in public-goods-game (PGG)-like environments—this set provides a rich pool of direct evidence, especially from standard PGGs and closely related social dilemmas. Roughly 20–30 papers deliver *exact* or *close* fit to the core prediction task (i.e., efficiency or related payoff in PGGs as a function of punishment/no-punishment), while many others provide adjacent or indirect information, often about contribution rates, norms, psychological drivers, institutional details, or variations in mechanism design. Very few high-quality theory papers are present, and causal mechanism discussions often build on, rather than replace, experimental evidence.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:** The literature is highly relevant, with a critical mass of studies using *exact* PGGs (linear or binary) and additional studies using close variants (e.g., Trust Game, Common Pool Resource, Threshold/Contest Games, Multilevel/Networked Social Dilemmas). However, a substantial number of papers are only *adjacent* or *weakly* relevant, employing dyadic games (e.g., Prisoner's Dilemma), allocation/ultimatum contexts, or field/real-world analogs. Thus, the relevance profile is: *many exact and close, many adjacent, few none.*

**punishment_or_sanctions:** Major attention is on *exact* punishment or sanction treatments: peer punishment, centralized/leader punishment, third-party punishment, collective punishment, exclusion, reward, and combinations thereof. The majority of relevant PGG studies manipulate punishment as a binary condition, and several explore gradations (cost, magnitude, timing, publicness). Some explore endogenous institution choice. A set of adjacent papers focus on reputation-based or informal sanctioning, punishment frequency/mechanism, or non-punishment interventions, which only *weakly* inform the key task.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** Approximately half of the directly relevant literature measures *exact* efficiency or total group payoff (relative to full cooperation). Many others use *close* payoff-based proxies: group earnings, welfare, or surplus. However, a significant number—including influential and large sample studies—focus only on *behavioral outcomes* (contribution/cooperation rate, punishment frequency) without analyzing efficiency. Where efficiency is not measured, evidence is *adjacent* or *weak* for the prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (used in prediction): group efficiency (ratio or % of benchmark), group earnings/profit, surplus, welfare, total coins/tokens/earnings—these map directly to the prediction target and are measured as primary outcomes in a large subset (e.g., Jiang & Wang 2024; Harrell 2019; Castillo & Hamman 2021; Molenmaker et al. 2023; Krügel & Maaser 2025; Nhim et al. 2023; Nicklisch et al. 2021; Duell et al. 2024; Kamei et al. 2023).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**: contribution rate, cooperation rate, punishment frequency/severity, norm compliance, convergence, response to intervention, personality/motivational correlates, strategy adoption. Many studies with these outcomes do not report efficiency, though often imply (sometimes incorrectly) that increased contribution = increased efficiency.

- **Hybrid or indirect**: some papers describe mixed or ambiguous outcomes, such as inequality reduction, subgroup payoffs, persistence of cooperation, or qualitative welfare statements without direct efficiency analysis.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### Empirical Patterns

- **Enabling punishment typically increases efficiency, but not always:** The modal finding in exact PGG lab experiments is that introducing peer/leader/third-party punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment controls (Jiang & Wang 2024; Harrell 2019; Castillo & Hamman 2021; Krügel & Maaser 2025; Nicklisch et al. 2021; Duell et al. 2024; Kamei et al. 2023). However, this effect depends crucially on the specifics of punishment design, monitoring, institution structure, information, and group context.

- **Punishment costs limit or possibly reverse efficiency gains:** If punishment is highly costly, antisocial, or poorly targeted, efficiency gains are reduced or even negated, as punishment destroys value (Nhim et al. 2023; Harrell 2019; Molenmaker et al. 2023; Milinski & Marotzke 2022; Ozono & Nakama 2022; Chen et al. 2025; Safarzynska 2020; Alam & Rai 2025; Heine et al. 2023; Praxmarer et al. 2025).

- **Institutional design moderates effects:** Centralized/leader punishment is often more efficient than peer punishment due to lower redundant punishment and reduced counter-punishment (Harrell 2019; Castillo & Hamman 2021; Krügel & Maaser 2025; Duell et al. 2024). Peer punishment with poor coordination or high antisocial punishment produces limited or negative effects (Molenmaker et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2025; Angelsen & Naime 2024; Nhim et al. 2023).

- **Monitoring and transparency:** Cheap or accurate monitoring increases the positive effect of punishment on efficiency; expensive, noisy, or missing monitoring/memory results in small or null efficiency effects (Nicklisch et al. 2021; Eisenkopf & Walter 2022; Chen et al. 2025).

- **Group heterogeneity, anti-social punishment, and context:** Efficiency gains from punishment are undermined in heterogeneous/pluriform groups, or when antisocial punishment is common (Molenmaker et al. 2023; Angelsen & Naime 2024; Chen et al. 2025). In some settings, punishment reduces efficiency, especially when motivated by rent extraction, profit motives, or applied indiscriminately (Milinski & Marotzke 2022; Alam & Rai 2025; Safarzynska 2020; Cagala et al. 2019).

- **Effect of reward and mixed sanction regimes:** Reward, when available, can substitute or complement punishment, sometimes producing higher efficiency and less welfare destruction (Makovi et al. 2025; Heine et al. 2023).

- **Baseline (control) efficiency as a moderator:** Games with already high baseline efficiency may show weak or negative marginal efficiency improvement from punishment, due to small room for gain and higher risk that punishment costs outweigh cooperation gains (Ozono & Nakama 2022; Engel 2019; Heine et al. 2023).

### Theoretical Mechanism Arguments

- Efficient punishment requires accurate targeting, minimal redundancy, cost-effectiveness, and monitoring (Nicklisch et al. 2021; Castillo & Hamman 2021).
- Centralized punishment, coordination mechanisms, and leadership can resolve the second-order free-rider problem and reduce efficiency losses compared to decentralized peer punishment (Harrell 2019; Krügel & Maaser 2025).
- Information feedback influences norm formation and the effectiveness of punishment (Engel 2019; Chen et al. 2025).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Where control efficiency is low and punishment is designed to be cost-effective, adding (peer or leader) punishment is likely to *increase* efficiency, often substantially, especially in small groups with centralized/leader sanctioning, accurate monitoring, and high transparency.**
- **Where punishment is costly, antisocial, poorly coordinated, or occurs in highly heterogeneous groups or voluntary/avoidable regimes, efficiency gains are reduced, null, or negative—even as cooperation rates may rise.**

- **The difference in efficiency between control and punishment treatments is *not* mechanically predictable from control efficiency alone—critical moderators include:**
    - *Punishment cost* and *impact* (if cost is high, gains shrink or reverse)
    - *Punishment institution* (peer vs. leader/centralized; commitment/coordination)
    - *Monitoring* (individual vs. group, noisy vs. accurate)
    - *Group structure* (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous)
    - *Visibility and feedback* (transparency can strengthen or in some cases reduce efficiency via psychological/strategic spillovers)
    - *History and learning/institutional experience* (facilitation, prior failures, learning periods enhance or diminish gains)

- **Design settings with democratic, responsible leaders, centralization, or explicit commitment/participation seem to robustly maximize positive effects.**
    - Efficiency improvements from punishment are highest where leadership is prosocial, elected, and responsibility-framed (Jiang & Wang 2024), where commitment institutions exist (Duell et al. 2024), or with competitive and monitored higher-order institutions (Krügel & Maaser 2025).
    - In contrast, peer punishment with high antisociality or where monitoring is expensive/proxy-based yields little or negative efficiency change compared to control (Molenmaker et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2025; Ozono & Nakama 2022).

- **Reward-only or mixed (reward/punishment) mechanisms can sometimes achieve similar or better efficiency without costly norm enforcement.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Strongly Informed Dimensions** (direct or high-quality, efficiency-relevant data):
- `player_count`, `num_rounds` — most experiments detail these; group size and horizon are known moderators of punishment efficacy.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` — critical in shaping efficiency effects; papers directly test various cost/tech profiles.
- `mpcr` — nearly always specified; affects the baseline tradeoff between private and public benefit.
- `all_or_nothing`, `default_contrib` — less frequently manipulated but often reported, with binary vs. continuous contributions affecting strategy space and norm formation.
- `show_other_summaries`/`show_n_rounds` (feedback/visibility) — several studies manipulate feedback and transparency, showing mixed or strong effects (e.g., Engel 2019; Chen et al. 2025).
- `chat` — less often a main treatment, but when manipulated (e.g., Duell et al. 2024), shown to affect coordination, commitment, and norm formation.

**Moderately Informed or Contextual Dimensions**:
- `show_punishment_id` — considered in some studies (identity/reputation effects), often reported but rarely manipulated alone.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` — mixed coverage, informative mainly in papers with symmetric or mixed intervention arms.

**Sparse or Missing Dimensions**:
- Indirect coverage for `default_contrib` (framing/opt-in), rarely main treatment.
- Very few papers manipulate complex feedback structures (e.g., partial/success-based visibility) or systematically explore combinations of `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`, and endowment transparency.
- Some real-world and online studies lack precise reporting of design parameters, though most lab studies are explicit.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency not always reported:** Many otherwise-ideal PGG papers focus solely on behavioral/psychological outcomes (contributions, punishment rates) without reporting efficiency or group payoff explicitly, requiring inferential leaps that may not hold when costs are high (Nakagawa et al. 2022; Clark & Dickinson 2020; Langenbach & Tausch 2019).
- **Design parameter gaps:** Some dimensions crucial to prediction (information structure, ID feedback, baseline heterogeneity) are only partially explored or reported, limiting fine-grained prediction.
- **Generalization ceiling:** Where results stem from stylized lab settings (student samples, small N), larger group, field, or highly asynchronous/online settings may show weaker or reversed patterns, and external validity to e.g., CPR or contest environments is uncertain.
- **Context sensitivity and interaction effects:** Many efficiency outcomes are strongly moderated by group structure (heterogeneity), pre-existing norms, institution formation histories, and even sample culture/nationality; universal directionality of effect cannot be assumed.
- **Few long-term/dynamic studies:** Most results come from single-session, short-horizon experiments; persistence of efficiency gains with punishment over repeated/sustained interaction is underexplored.
- **Non-standard environments:** For variants—CPR, contest, trust, or threshold settings—effects of punishment on efficiency can be neutral or negative, especially under voluntary, opt-in/avoidable regimes or when punishment is affordable to antisocial types.

---

### **In summary:**  
The literature provides robust, high-quality empirical evidence that, in standard repeated PGGs, enabling cost-effective, coordinated punishment generally increases efficiency over a no-punishment baseline, especially with centralized/leader designs, cheap accurate monitoring, and homogenous groups. However, these gains are not universal—punishment can reduce efficiency where its costs, antisocial use, or group structure negate cooperation gains. Prediction from design dimensions requires accounting for these moderators. Many non-payoff studies provide only indirect support; efficiency must be directly checked, and contextual dependencies carefully considered.
