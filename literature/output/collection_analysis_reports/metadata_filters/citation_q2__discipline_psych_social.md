# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is extensive (164 papers) and spans a broad mix of **empirical lab and field experiments**, **theory/modeling papers**, and **observational/contextual studies**. A **substantial core** of the evidence base consists of **empirical lab experiments** using standard and variant public goods games (PGGs), usually with clear manipulation of punishment mechanisms and **direct measurement of group payoff or efficiency**. These are complemented by theory papers that analyze key mechanisms (punishment cost, information, group size, etc.) and modeling studies (ABMs, equilibrium models, simulations) that generalize or qualify empirical findings. 

Some papers cover **close variants** such as threshold PGGs, common pool resource (CPR) games, or adjacent social dilemmas. Several studies examine **field or naturally occurring environments**, though these often lack detailed measurement or dimensional control. 

Empirical coverage is **rich for many design dimensions** (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost, information/feedback, chat/communication) but **thin or indirect** for some others (e.g., reward mechanisms, default contributions, technological variations in punishment/reward, metanorms, or display settings). **Theoretical and simulation papers** often fill in structural or limiting-case gaps but sometimes lack direct mapping to standard experimental conditions or efficiency outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

## a) `pgg_or_variant`
- **Exact relevance**: The core of the paper set (especially lab experiments and many theory papers) studies standard linear PGGs or canonical variants (threshold, all-or-nothing, weakest-link, voluntary contribution mechanism, etc.).
- **Close/adjacent relevance**: Some studies use social dilemmas that substantially deviate from canonical PGGs (e.g., CPR, PD, allocation/bargaining games), providing mechanism insights but not always exact outcome comparability.
- **Weak/none relevance**: Some papers discuss adjacent cooperation phenomena (reputation, reciprocity, prosociality) but not PGGs or their main analogues.

## b) `punishment_or_sanctions`
- **Exact relevance**: Most empirical and theory papers manipulate peer or centralized punishment, and report results with or without punishment.
- **Close**: Several studies use adjacent sanctioning mechanisms (ostracism, exclusion, reputation, anti-social punishment, milestones as de facto punishment, etc.).
- **Adjacent/weak**: Some examine only metanorms, rewards, or natural non-monetary sanctions; or analyze related norm enforcement without explicit group-sanctioning actions.

## c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
- **Exact relevance**: A strong subset reports **efficiency** (payoff as fraction of cooperative optimum), group earnings, welfare, or coins generated.
- **Close/adjacent relevance**: Many report only **contributions** or **compliance rates** as main outcomes, which are not, but may be proxies for, efficiency—sometimes with punitive costs not properly accounted for.
- **Weak/none relevance**: Numerous behavioral studies report only psychological, neural, or reputational outcomes, or choices in scenarios unrelated to group payoff.

**Overall, the literature provides** robust and direct evidence **for prediction about punishment effects on efficiency in repeated, linear PGGs and their close variants. Relevance is lower for complex/field settings, adjacent games, or when only behavioral compliance is measured.**

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based outcomes**: Many core papers measure **group efficiency, total earnings/payoff, surplus, welfare, or coin generation**. These are most relevant for predictive modeling of efficiency effects. Several meta-analyses and summary papers also use contribution rates as an efficiency proxy, which can be valid if punishment costs are minor, but not otherwise.
- **Behavioral (non-payoff) outcomes**: Many studies report **contribution/cooperation rates, compliance with rules, punishment frequency, norm compliance/violation, fairness decisions**, and **psychological or reputational responses**. These outcomes are often related to but not identical with efficiency; high cooperation may not map to high efficiency if punishment is costly or misapplied.
- **Combined reporting**: Some studies report both, allowing for mapping between contribution increases and net efficiency.
- **Rare or missing**: For some dimensions (e.g., default contribution framing, punishment/reward information display), neither behavioral nor payoff outcomes are commonly reported.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Cross-paper synthesis** reveals several empirically robust findings and important qualified results:

- **Enabling peer or centralized punishment in standard, repeated linear PGGs almost always increases group efficiency over control (no punishment), provided anti-social punishment and punitive costs are not overwhelming** [(Lo Iacono et al., 2023); (Wang & Qin, 2015); (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021); (Andrighetto et al., 2016); (Eichenseer, 2023)].
    - The efficiency gain is often large—contribution rates (and thus approximated efficiency) can double, and group earnings approach the cooperative optimum when punishment is available and sufficiently targeted.
- **However, the positive efficiency effect is **not universal**:
    - If **anti-social punishment**, **excessive punishment costs**, or **maladaptive punishment patterns** (e.g., feuds, unstable power) emerge, efficiency gains can be small, null, or negative [(Fatas & Mateu, 2015); (Vollan et al., 2019); (Dorrough et al., 2017); (Gross et al., 2022); (Abbink et al., 2004)].
    - In **nonlinear, threshold, or field CPR settings**, punishment often fails to improve (sometimes reduces) efficiency, especially if communication is available [Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Vollan et al., 2019].
    - **Cultural and social context** can moderate antisocial punishment's prevalence.
- **Critical moderators** (with direct evidence) include:
    - **Information/feedback structure**: High visibility of actions and clear link between punishment and behavior support efficiency gains. Lack of feedback or noisy environments reduce efficiency effects, even with punishment enabled [(Waichman & Stenzel, 2019); (Fischer et al., 2016)].
    - **Communication/chat**: Presence of chat or even minimal communication (normative or accountability) reliably increases efficiency, both with and without punishment; communication can substitute or enhance punishment effects [(Andrighetto et al., 2016); (Morgan et al., 2019); (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019); (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016)].
    - **Group size**: Some theory and empirics indicate that punishment's efficiency gains are stronger in smaller groups; in large groups, unless networks are dense or information is robust, punishment's effectiveness diminishes [(Boyd et al., 2014); (Harrell & Wolff, 2023)].
    - **Punishment institution design** (centralized, selected, restricted vs. decentralized, unstable, or unrestricted): Centralized punishment and stable, equal punishment power generally increase efficiency; instability, unequal power, or peer revenge cycles reduce it [(Castillo et al., 2021); (Dorrough et al., 2017); (Grieco et al., 2017)].
    - **MPCR (marginal per-capita return)**: Punishment is more effective at increasing efficiency when MPCR is low (scarce resources); at high MPCR, the marginal gains from punishment shrink or may turn negative [(Boyd et al., 2014); (Farjam et al., 2015)].
- **Theoretical studies**: Mechanism models reinforce these empirical findings, especially regarding:
    - The necessity of low punishment cost/high effectiveness, the benefits of strong but short-lived/corrective punishment (e.g., CTFT vs. self-punishing or mutual-revenge schemes) [(Langlois & Langlois, 2004); (Nakao, 2009)].
    - The importance of repeated interaction, patience (discount factor), and ability to credibly threaten punishment for sustaining high efficiency [(Camera & Gioffré, 2014); (Melkonyan et al., 2022)].
- **Key exceptions**: In settings with (a) high anti-social punishment, (b) high-cost/low-impact punishment, (c) high noise/poor information, and (d) threshold or nonlinear reward structures (not continuous efficiency gains), punishment either does not improve, or may worsen, efficiency.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **When punishment is introduced to a repeated, linear PGG with standard lab parameters (n=4-12, 10–30 rounds, visible contributions, no communication), **treatment efficiency is reliably higher than control efficiency** whose magnitude is well-predicted by meta-analytic effect sizes [(Eichenseer, 2023): +0.47 peer, +0.26 centralized on contribution ratio/efficiency scale].
    - **Control efficiency** (with punishment disabled) is a strong linear baseline; shifts are in the positive direction, unless parameter regimes align with the exceptions above.
    - **Design dimensions modulate the effect**: 
        - Larger player count, lower MPCR, imperfect information, and decentralized, high-cost punishment **reduce** the efficiency gain.
        - Chat/communication, centralized or selected punishers, and robust feedback **increase** the efficiency gain.
        - In threshold or weakest-link variants, punishment's effect depends on the production technology: dramatic efficiency gain in weakest-link, little-to-no gain (can be negative) if anti-social punishment is prevalent in linear VCMs [(Fatas & Mateu, 2015)].
    - **If anti-social punishment, revenge cycles, or high punishment costs are features or likely emergent outcomes, expect efficiency to improve less, not at all, or at worst decrease compared to control** [(Abbink et al., 2004); (Vollan et al., 2019); (Handfield et al., 2016)].
- **If feedback linking punishment to norm violation is absent/delayed, or if noise is high, punishment often increases cooperation (behavior) but not efficiency (payoff)**—i.e., more is spent on punishment than is gained in public goods contributions.
- **When predicting treatment efficiency from design + control efficiency, adjust upwards for:**
    - Centralized/stable/equal punishment power.
    - Presence of communication or chat.
    - Endogenous or credible reputation mechanisms.
    - Threshold/weakest-link technology (if antisocial punishment is rare).
- **Adjust downward or anticipate little/no change (possibly negative) if:**
    - Unstable or unequal punishment power (esp. if roles are performance-based or changeable).
    - High baseline anti-social punishment.
    - High noise, imperfect information.
    - Field environment or CPR game with complex or nonlinear payoffs.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed** (empirical or theory, efficiency tested):
- `player_count` (group size): Heavily studied, with both empirical and theoretical treatments; strong effect on efficiency and scaling of punishment effectiveness.
- `num_rounds`: Standardized in many experiments (mostly 10–30 rounds); long games allow punishment to have stronger effects.
- `chat`: Strong, repeatedly demonstrated moderator; chat boosts efficiency, often more so combined with punishment.
- `mpcr`: Marginal per-capita return is a core moderator; efficiency gains from punishment larger at low MPCR to overcome temptation.
- `punishment_cost`/`punishment_tech`: Key direct variables; high-cost or low-impact punishment can nullify or invert expected efficiency gains.
- `all_or_nothing`: Both all-or-nothing (discrete) and continuous designs represented; effect sizes and anti-social punishment prevalence can differ.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Feedback and transparency are critical—salient, persistent feedback supports higher treatment efficiency.
- `reward_exists`: Less frequently included, but some studies demonstrate rewards are less effective than punishment (unless rewards are large/net-positive; see Stoop et al., 2018).
- `punishment_tech` (centralized/peer, delayed/immediate, etc.): Institution design is a core focus—a direct moderator in several studies.

**Indirectly Informed**:
- `default_contrib`: Framing effects (opt-in/out) are rarely manipulated directly in the context of payoff outcomes, but some field/framing studies suggest possible main effects.
- `reward_cost`/`reward_tech`: Mechanism-level findings, less commonly matched to efficiency outcomes, but reviews suggest limited effect unless reward is substantial.
- `show_punishment_id`: Very few direct tests; some studies mention anonymity or identity effects for punishers but lack systematic efficiency data.

**Contextually Discussed/Effectively Missing**:
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Fewer direct empirical manipulations than punishment; reviews and some simulation/theory papers discuss their possible roles.
- `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id`: Largely missing from efficiency-focused literature.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Generalizability to non-lab or field settings is limited**: Some field experiments show weaker or absent efficiency effects of punishment compared to lab settings; contextual factors (real stakes, norms, population diversity) can moderate both cooperation and punishment [e.g., Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Noussair et al., 2015].
- **Design dimension interactions are underexplored**: Most studies systematically manipulate 1–2 dimensions, so higher-level interactions (e.g., how chat × group size × punishment structure interact) are inferred from synthesis, not directly tested.
- **Sparse evidence for default contribution framing, reward structure, some display/information settings**: Many design features critical for platform implementation are rarely directly tested with efficiency as the main outcome.
- **Behavioral versus efficiency outcomes**: Many studies report only contribution/cooperation rather than efficiency; in high-punishment-cost regimes, these can diverge.
- **Anti-social punishment, retaliation, and cultural context**: These remain major moderators and sources of ambiguity in efficiency predictions. The frequency, drivers, and control of anti-social punishment are not always observable ex ante.
- **Punishment institution design (centralized vs. decentralized vs. peer selection), and endogenous institution choice remain under-represented in many samples.**
- **Nonlinear or threshold game structures**: Applicability of canonical results to CPR, all-or-nothing, or weakest-link variants is supported only when studies intentionally explore these structures.
- **Long-run dynamics and feedback**: Most experiments are relatively short. Simulations and theory cover longer time scales and adaptive punishment, but empirical confirmation is scarce.
- **Some prediction dimensions (e.g., reward mechanisms, meta-punishment, information display settings) are insufficiently varied to support strong inference on their moderating role for efficiency.**

---

In sum: 
**The literature strongly supports predictions that enabling peer or centralized punishment in canonical repeated linear PGGs increases treatment efficiency over control, with effect sizes modulated by group size, communication, information, penalty structure, and anti-social punishment propensity**. However, **these positive effects are not guaranteed outside standard lab regimes, especially when high punishment costs, anti-social dynamics, complexity, or structural variations (e.g., nonlinear/threshold payoffs) are present**. Robust prediction must **adjust for these key design dimensions, rely more heavily on direct efficiency findings (rather than behavioral proxies), and acknowledge uncertainty where the literature is sparse or divided**.
