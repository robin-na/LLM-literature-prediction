# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set (n = 1,442) includes an exceptionally large, multidisciplinary mix, but the overwhelming majority of core, high-relevance evidence for the requested prediction task comes from a concentrated set of laboratory experimental economics papers, agent-based/theoretical modeling studies, and empirically grounded reviews. The set is narrow with respect to our specific focus—predicting average efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments with or without peer punishment—but contains abundant direct, close, and adjacent evidence for the precise mapping from punishment-enabled/disabled (and key game parameters) to efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes.

- **Empirical vs. Theory:** A significant portion of the evidence is from high-powered, lab-based experimental studies closely matching the canonical public goods game (PGG) design. These provide direct, empirical estimates of how enabling punishment (peer or institutional) affects group efficiency. There is also a substantial number of theory/simulation/model papers, many of which directly analyze the payoff consequences of enabling punishment versus not, including effects of game dimensions (e.g., MPCR, group size, punishment cost, punishment tech). There is good triangulation between empirical observation and model predictions, which reduces bias from methodological limitations.

- **Breadth and Coverage:** The empirical base covers a wide but uneven array of game design dimensions. There is strong direct evidence for the effect of punishment in canonical PGG settings (moderate group size, repeated rounds, standard MPCR, continuous contributions, anonymous/no-chat, classic punishment cost/tech ratios). Evidence is sparser or more model-based for extreme values and for interaction/moderation effects with communication, partner choice, reputation, network/population structure, and less common incentive schemes. The literature is much narrower on non-western field evidence, crowding-out, antisocial punishment, and institutional complexity.

# 2) Task Relevance

Relevance is best assessed along three axes: (1) *public goods game or variant* (`pgg_or_variant`), (2) *punishment or sanctions manipulation* (`punishment_or_sanctions`), and (3) *efficiency or closely related payoff-based outcome* (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`).

- **pgg_or_variant:** The set contains a high density of studies with `exact` or `close` match to classic repeated PGGs or canonical CPR/collective-risk games. Many `adjacent` studies use repeated prisoner's dilemma, volunteer's dilemma, weak-link/coordination, or trust games, which are structurally comparable but have important differences regarding the scope and target of punishment.

- **punishment_or_sanctions:** A large cohort manipulates and explicitly codes the *enabling/disabling of peer punishment,* with others focusing on centralized punishment, ostracism/exclusion, or institutional sanctions (classed as `exact` and `close`). Numerous theoretical and empirical studies include `adjacent` evidence: sticky reputation-based exclusion, communication as norm enforcement, or indirect sanctions.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** Many studies report *exact* measures such as group efficiency (payoff relative to the social optimum), group earnings, surplus, or closely related proxies. Some report only on cooperation rates/contributions or behavioral responses (`adjacent` or `weak`), which must not be conflated with real efficiency. Overall, the task-relevant set is well-populated for the key comparison (“control” efficiency vs. “punishment-enabled” efficiency), though with gaps for certain edge cases.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based (relevant for efficiency):**
  - *Efficiency*, *group payoff*, *average/group earnings*, *total (group) surplus*, *social welfare*, or *Coins/points generated* are reported in many experimental PGG studies and some theoretical models.
  - Notably, several studies (e.g., Fehr & Gächter 2000, Dickinson et al. 2015, Masclet et al. 2003, Sutter et al. 2010, Lo Iacono et al. 2023, Gruereck & Rockenbach 2006) provide the exact mapping from control to treatment (punishment) efficiency.
  - *Partial/overlapping outcomes*: In some field/CPR studies, welfare is proxied by resource level, group survival/success, or average earnings.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (not equivalent to efficiency):**
  - *Cooperation/contribution rates*, *punishment rates/frequency*, *trust/trustworthiness*, *reputation scores*, *emotion/self-reported affect*.
  - *Inequality outcomes* (e.g., Gini coefficient) and norm perceptions are sometimes reported but are not payoff-based efficiency.
  - *Punishment* and *reward behavior* are often measured, but these must be interpreted separately from their net impact on efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Direction and Magnitude of Punishment Effects

- **Punishment can increase efficiency,** but *only* under certain conditions—mainly when the control efficiency is low (contributions/efficiency decay in no-punishment baseline), punishment is not too costly relative to impact, monitoring is reliable, group size is moderate, and antisocial/counter-punishment is minimal (Fehr & Gächter 2000; Dickinson et al. 2015; Kosfeld et al. 2009; Gürerk et al. 2006).

- **Punishment increases cooperation/contributions more than it increases efficiency:** In many lab PGGs, punishment boosts contributions but the costliness of punishment itself can offset much of the welfare gain (Rand et al. 2009; Simpson et al. 2017; Egas & Riedl 2008). Direct efficiency gains are typically 10-30% over a decaying-control, not a full jump to the social optimum.

- **Punishment can be welfare-destroying,** leading to lower efficiency than baseline, *especially* when:
    - Monitoring is noisy/imperfect (Ambrus & Greiner 2012; Grechenig et al. 2010),
    - Antisocial punishment is common (Herrmann et al. 2008; Gächter & Herrmann 2011; Dekel et al. 2017),
    - Punishment is misdirected or vindictive (Nikiforakis 2008; Janssen et al. 2010; Fehr & Rockenbach 2003),
    - Counter-punishment (escalating vendettas) is possible (Fehl et al. 2012; Gross & De Dreu 2019),
    - Cultural/country context enables punitive norm breakdown or inefficiency (Barclay 2004; Herrmann et al. 2008; Fehr et al. 2008; Nikiforakis et al. 2012).

## Moderators and Limits

- **Critical game design moderators:**
    - *Punishment cost-to-impact ratio:* Efficiency gains from punishment are greater when punishment is inexpensive and highly effective (Egas & Riedl 2008; Ambrus & Greiner 2012; Perc et al. 2017).
    - *Group size:* Punishment is most effective in small/medium groups (4–6); in large groups, the diffusion of responsibility and complexity can undermine its effect (Fehr & Schmidt 1999; Powers et al. 2023; Powers & Lehmann 2013).
    - *MPCR (marginal per capita return):* Low MPCR (high dilemma) makes punishment more valuable for efficiency gains; in high-MPCR environments, efficiency may already be high without punishment, and the cost can outweigh gains (Fehr & Gächter 2000; Fehr & Schmidt 1999; Farjam et al. 2015).
    - *Monitoring/feedback accuracy:* If feedback about others' contributions is noisy, punishment can reduce efficiency (Ambrus & Greiner 2012; Grechenig et al. 2010; Nicklisch et al. 2016; Markussen et al. 2016).
    - *Communication/interaction structure:* The presence of chat, communication, or social signaling (norm enforcement by message or observation) can substitute for or moderate punishment effects, sometimes making punishment less necessary for efficiency (Bochet & Putterman 2006; Markussen et al. 2014; Andrighetto et al. 2013).
    - *Peer vs. centralized/leader punishment*: Central/leader punishment is often more efficient than peer punishment, as redundant punishment is avoided (Harrell 2019; Andreoni & Gee 2012; Gross et al. 2016).

- **Institutional and social context:**
    - *Antisocial punishment prevalence* (punishing high contributors, retaliation): If common (in some cultures), punishment can decrease efficiency and cooperation (Herrmann et al. 2008; Ray et al. 2009; Barclay 2004).
    - *Democratic/voluntary adoption* of sanctions: Efficiency is higher when institutions are adopted democratically or voluntarily, but not when imposed (Sutter et al. 2010; Markussen et al. 2014; Kosfeld et al. 2009).
    - *Social heterogeneity/cultural differences*: Group composition, norms, and cross-cultural variation moderate the efficiency impact of punishment (Herrmann et al. 2008; Guala 2012; Gächter et al. 2010).

- **Interaction with alternative mechanisms:** Reward, communication, reputation, exclusion/ostracism can be as effective or more efficient than punishment (Rand et al. 2009; Andrighetto et al. 2013; Sutter et al. 2010; Masclet et al. 2003; Milinski 2016; Bochet & Putterman 2006). Combined mechanisms (reward plus punishment) or flexible choice can yield optimal outcomes (Andrighetto et al. 2013; Chen et al. 2015; Sun et al. 2023).

## Exceptions & Counterexamples
- **No efficiency gain from punishment,** or even efficiency loss, is observed:
    - In repeated games with high base cooperation (already efficient), adding punishment can reduce group payoff due to the cost of punishing (Egas & Riedl 2008; Sutter et al. 2010; Bahbouhi et al. 2024; Bühren & Dannenberg 2021).
    - When antisocial/counter-punishment is widespread (Herrmann et al. 2008; Gächter & Herrmann 2011).
    - Under noisy or incomplete monitoring (Ambrus & Greiner 2012; Grechenig et al. 2010; Markussen et al. 2016).
    - When punishment mechanisms are profitable for the punisher (Alam & Rai 2025; Ouss & Peysakhovich 2015), or not perceived as fair (Fehr & Rockenbach 2003; Hernandez et al. 2022).

- **Punishment is more effective when it is rare, credible, and not overused:** Low-frequency, targeted, or committed punishment is sufficient and efficient, while redundancy or frequency can destroy efficiency (Masclet et al. 2003; O'Gorman et al. 2009; Harrell 2019).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Modeling recommendations based on the literature:**

1. **If the no-punishment baseline (control efficiency) is low (i.e., efficiency decays), and the punishment institution is well-designed (moderate group size, not too costly, effective, and with accurate monitoring):**
    - Expect a *moderate positive increase* in treatment efficiency (often 10–30% above low-decaying-control in lab studies).
    - The effect is *strongest in repeated games, moderate groups (n = 4–6), moderate MPCR, continuous contributions, and anonymous/no-chat settings*.

2. **If control efficiency is already high (sustained cooperation):**
    - Do *not* expect a large efficiency increase upon enabling punishment; group payoff may remain unchanged, or even decrease if punishment is used (due to wasted costs).
    - If the group is highly cooperative, punishment is often not used or is unnecessary (Bahbouhi et al. 2024; Bühren & Dannenberg 2021).

3. **If punishment cost is high, monitoring is noisy, or antisocial punishment is prevalent:**
    - Punishment may *reduce* efficiency relative to control or have a negligible/ambiguous effect. Be especially cautious with large, heterogeneous, or distrustful groups, or settings known for antisocial punishment.

4. **Edge cases:**
    - *All-or-nothing PGGs, very large or small groups, or threshold public goods:* Model evidence is sparser but generally supports the above findings, with special attention to group size and coordination requirements (Ambrus & Greiner 2012; Powers & Lehmann 2013).
    - *Exclusion/ostracism as punishment:* Strong evidence that exclusion-based punishment (especially voluntary/peer exclusion) is very efficient (Cinyabuguma et al. 2005; Masclet 2003; Sääksvuori 2014).

5. **Indistinguishable/weak evidence:**
    - When existing studies report only on contribution rates, cooperation, or non-payoff measures, or use games adjacent but not matching PGGs, these should only affect priors or modeling assumptions when direct evidence is lacking.

6. **Interaction with other design dimensions:**
    - Inclusion of chat, communication, or visible reputation can substitute for or even crowd out the impact of punishment for efficiency.
    - Peer punishment is typically less efficient than centralized or leader-based punishment, due to redundant punishment costs and overuse.

7. **Thresholds:** Empirical and theoretical studies identify threshold effects for punishment cost-effectiveness, monitoring accuracy, and group size—below or above these, the efficiency impact of punishment can rapidly change direction (Ambrus & Greiner 2012; Farjam et al. 2015; Nikiforakis et al. 2012).

8. **Moderators to always specify in the model (per strong evidence):**
    - *player_count*, *num_rounds*, *mpcr*, *punishment_cost*, *punishment_tech*, *monitoring accuracy/feedback quality*, *institutional vs. peer punishment*, *presence/absence of communication or chat*, *group heterogeneity/composition*, *cultural context* (if known).

**Explicit Cautions:**
- *Never* infer efficiency effects from contribution rates alone.
- Punishment-induced increases in efficiency require that punishment institutions are not frequently used in wasteful ways (i.e., no excessive, misdirected, or antisocial punishment).
- If only adjacent, non-payoff, or non-PGG evidence is available, predictions must remain more uncertain.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Best informed (direct or close empirical evidence for efficiency changes):**
- **player_count** (group size): Moderates both baseline and punishment-enabled efficiency.
- **num_rounds**: Longer games increase the benefit of punishment; very short or one-shot games often show no efficiency effect.
- **mpcr**: Lower MPCR (stronger dilemma) means greater impact of punishment for efficiency improvement.
- **punishment_cost** & **punishment_tech** (cost-to-effectiveness): Most studies explicitly manipulate or report cost and impact of punishment.
- **punishment_exists** / **punishment institution details (peer, leader, exclusion, etc.)**: Peer vs. leader/centralized punishment is often compared.
- **chat**: Communication presence/absence is well-represented and shown to interact with punishment.
- **show_other_summaries**/**show_n_rounds** (feedback structure): Often reported or manipulated.
- **reward_exists** & related reward dimensions: Less central but included in many comparative studies.

**Indirectly or sparsely informed:**
- **all_or_nothing**: Sparse, but some evidence for binary/contributions/extreme cases.
- **default_contrib**: Framing effects (e.g., opt-in/out, default contribution) only occasionally studied; little direct evidence for efficiency impact.
- **show_punishment_id**: Some studies report anonymity/identifiability's effect, but as a moderator of antisocial punishment, not core efficiency.

**Missing or only contextually discussed:**
- **population structure/dynamics**, **network effects**, *hard-to-measure social norms or cultural background*.
- **Complex institutional designs**, combinations of multiple mechanisms (e.g., repeated PGGs with exclusion, chat, leader punishment, etc.) are underrepresented in direct efficiency evidence.

---

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited coverage for rare or complex designs:** There are gaps in direct efficiency evidence for threshold public goods, extremely large (>10), or small (<3) groups, all-or-nothing or dynamic contribution games, or settings with highly heterogeneous populations.

- **Cultural/contextual moderating effects:** Cultural context and the prevalence of antisocial punishment are crucial moderators, but are either missing or insufficiently parameterized in most studies (Herrmann et al. 2008; Fehr et al. 2008).

- **Reliance on behavioral proxies in some studies:** Many studies report only cooperation/contribution rates or norm compliance, but not efficiency or group earnings; these must be excluded from direct model calibration for efficiency outcome prediction.

- **Overrepresentation of laboratory/PWE samples:** Most empirical studies rely on lab settings (students in Western countries) and moderate group sizes; real-world variation and institution-level complexity are under-explored.

- **Potential publication/selection bias:** Negative or null results or complex interactions (e.g., punishment interacts negatively with other mechanisms, weakening efficiency gains) may be underreported.

- **Inadequate attention to multifactorial interactions:** Most PGG studies focus on single-dimension manipulation; true joint effects (e.g., chat × punishment × feedback accuracy × group size) are poorly mapped.

- **Ambiguity in mapping adjacent/weak evidence:** Many adjacent game types (estimation studies, trust games, etc.) and purely behavioral outcome studies do not provide enough counterfactual or quantitative structure for robust prediction, and must be differentiated clearly from direct empirical PGG efficiency studies.

---

## **Summary**

The literature directly supports the following: The effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like settings is *moderately positive and robust* only when punishment is effective, not overly costly, rarely misapplied, group size is moderate, monitoring is accurate, and baseline (control) efficiency is low. Under high efficiency baselines, costly or imprecise punishment, or high rates of misdirected/antisocial punishment, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency or have no effect. Model-based or adjacent evidence broadly triangulates with direct empirical findings, with strong caution warranted in extending results to contexts where punishment is costly, noisy, or not well targeted by design.

**For modelers:** Use direct experimental PGG studies with payoff-based efficiency outcomes for parameterization and calibration. Use adjacent or theoretical studies only for qualitative priors or in the absence of better evidence, and never substitute non-payoff outcomes (contribution, cooperation, etc.) as proxies for efficiency in model calibration. Always account for moderators—punishment cost, monitoring, MPCR, group size, feedback structure, communication, and cultural context—when making predictions.
