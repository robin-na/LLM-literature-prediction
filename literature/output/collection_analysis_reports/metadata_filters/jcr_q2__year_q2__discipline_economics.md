# Evidence Base

The paper set is large and empirically rich, dominated by laboratory experiments with a smaller but notable contingent of theory papers. Among 93 papers, there is a strong focus on **standard and variant public goods games (PGGs)** with repeated interaction and random or fixed partner assignment. Most included studies are **empirical lab experiments** (few field experiments) and several make use of theoretical modeling to clarify mechanisms. In terms of **outcomes**, a significant subset directly measure payoff-based variables—most commonly group payoff or efficiency as a ratio of the cooperative optimum—while many others report only behavioral results (e.g., contribution rates).

The **breadth of the evidence base** is substantial with respect to classic and variant PGGs, peer punishment, and the mapping from design dimensions to efficiency. However, **coverage is uneven** for some moderating design features (e.g., chat, reward systems, more unusual punishment technologies, explicit display of prediction-relevant information). There is **less empirical evidence** for non-standard environments, real-world (field) studies, and certain design dimensions such as chat, information display, and multi-level sanctioning structures.

# Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**: Relevance is high and often **exact**—dozens of papers study linear or threshold PGGs with typical structural features. Several closely related common-pool resource, team production, or oligopoly settings (Cournot, helping games) are included and marked as **close** or **adjacent**. A few are only **weakly relevant** (e.g., trust games, dictator/ultimatum games, or one-shot exchanges).
- **punishment_or_sanctions**: Nearly all papers in the focused subset are **exactly** relevant, testing costly peer punishment, exogenous punishment, or exclusion mechanisms. Adjacent mechanisms (reward, exit, exclusion, reputation, hierarchical control) receive **close** or **adjacent** relevance; non-monetary and symbolic punishments are less covered, but still represented.
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: About a third to half of studies are **exact** (direct group efficiency/payoff), with another substantial fraction reporting **close/adjacent** outcomes (monetary earnings, welfare, group surplus). Many studies focus **solely on behavioral outcomes** (contribution, punishment frequency), rated as **adjacent** or **weak**.

# Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes**: Group efficiency (payoff relative to social optimum), total/group earnings, welfare, and surplus are prioritized in many studies (e.g., [Page et al., 2013]; [Reif et al., 2017]; [Wang & Qin, 2015]; [Leibbrandt et al., 2015]; [Dugar, 2013]; [Otto & Bolle, 2016]; [Kölle, 2015]). Some papers estimate efficiency from reported contribution and payoff data even when not directly labeled.
- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**: Contribution rate, cooperation rate, norm compliance, punishment assigned, and retaliation are **more prevalent** as primary outcomes in much of the literature. These are sometimes used as proxies for efficiency but should not be conflated with it. Several papers focus explicitly on psychological, strategic, or group dynamics aspects, without payoff data.
- **Mixed/Indirect Outcomes**: Some studies use contribution data or behavioral proxies to **infer** about likely efficiency effects; causal attribution is often ambiguous when punishment costs are not reported, or when structural inefficiencies may offset cooperation gains.

# Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the evidence base:
- **Enabling costly peer punishment in standard repeated PGGs almost always increases group contribution and can increase group efficiency**—but only **when punishment is well-targeted, punishment cost is not excessive, and antisocial punishment is rare** ([Page et al., 2013]; [Reif et al., 2017]; [Fu et al., 2017]; [Wang & Qin, 2015]).
- **Punishment can reduce efficiency in the following scenarios**:
    - **High punishment costs**: If punishment costs outweigh contribution gains, efficiency can stagnate or fall ([Leibbrandt et al., 2015]; [Boosey & Isaac, 2016]; [Kingsley, 2016]; [Kingsley & Brown, 2016]).
    - **Network/monitoring asymmetries**: If some actors are under-monitored or unpunishable, they often become free riders or retaliators, undermining group efficiency ([Boosey & Isaac, 2016]; [Leibbrandt et al., 2015]).
    - **Endowment or valuation heterogeneity**: Heterogeneity in resources can promote normative conflict, leading to ineffective or even counterproductive punishment ([Kingsley, 2016]; [Kölle, 2015]; [Robbett, 2016]).
    - **Prevalence of antisocial punishment**: In some cultures or populations, punishment is misdirected at high contributors, reducing the efficacy of punishment ([Bortolotti et al., 2015]; [Sylwester et al., 2013]).
    - **Costly acquisition of punishment rights**: When it costs participants to obtain the right to punish, the positive effect on efficiency disappears ([Ramalingam et al., 2016]).
- **Alternative and hybrid mechanisms (reward, exclusion, central authority, endogenous group formation) also sometimes increase efficiency**, though effects are highly context-dependent ([Otto & Bolle, 2016]; [Charness & Yang, 2014]; [Croson et al., 2015]; [Nosenzo & Tufano, 2017]; [Drouvelis et al., 2017]). Exclusion or exit can substitute for punishment but may harm efficiency if overused ([Gaudeul et al., 2017]).
- **Information structure is crucial**: Full transparency about contributions, actions, and identities strengthens the positive efficiency effect of punishment ([Kamei & Putterman, 2015]; [Khadjavi et al., 2017]).
- **Efficiency effects are more positive with endogenous or consensual adoption of punishment institutions** and with social learning/history information ([Gürerk, 2013]).
- **Control (no-punishment) efficiency is not always a lower bound for efficiency with punishment**. In some designs (incomplete monitoring, high heterogeneity, antisocial settings), punishment reduces efficiency ([De Geest et al., 2017]).

# Prediction Guidance

**For predicting average efficiency with punishment enabled, given control efficiency and design dimensions:**

- **Expect positive or strongly positive marginal efficiency effects of peer punishment** when the following design conditions hold:
    - Homogeneous and symmetric environments (player endowments, roles)
    - Complete, symmetric punishment networks
    - Moderate or low punishment cost-to-effect ratios (e.g., 1:3 or better)
    - Clear information about others’ contributions, actions, and punishment (transparency)
    - Culture or group setting with low antisocial punishment
    - Sufficient number of participants with punishment rights (at least two in N=4 designs)
- **Expect modest or no efficiency gain—possibly negative effect—in these cases**:
    - Endowment or incentive heterogeneity, especially when not observable or not accounted for in punishment ([Kingsley, 2016]; [Kölle, 2015]; [Robbett, 2016])
    - High punishment costs for the punisher with low impact on the target
    - Incomplete or asymmetric punishment networks (i.e., not all can be sanctioned)
    - Substantial antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators)
    - Weak or no monitoring/auditing
    - Low tangibility or weak salience of punishment mechanisms
    - Acquisition cost for punishment rights ([Ramalingam et al., 2016])
- **The structure of punishment (who can punish, what triggers punishment, whether it is exogenous/automatic, and whether monetary or non-monetary)** is critical.
- **Information and history (social learning) amplify the efficiency gains from punishment** ([Gürerk, 2013]; [Kamei & Putterman, 2015]).
- **Prediction must be moderated by the structure of reward mechanisms if also available**: Rewards alone or with punishment can sometimes produce even higher efficiency, but effects are highly sensitive to context ([Dugar, 2013]; [Page et al., 2013]).
- **Do not use control efficiency as a simple baseline; the marginal effect of punishment is conditional on network structure, punishment cost/impact, and group context**. In some cases, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency below the control ([De Geest et al., 2017]; [Leibbrandt et al., 2015]; [Ramalingam et al., 2016]).

# Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (with empirical or robust theoretical evidence):**
- **player_count** (N): Evidence for N=2–8; theory suggests effect of punishment is non-monotonic in N and depends on punishment technology ([Levine & Modica, 2016]; [RouX & Thöni, 2015]).
- **num_rounds**: Across studies, more rounds allow punishment (or its threat) to sustain efficiency; short games produce weaker or less persistent effects ([Kamei & Putterman, 2015]; [Bruttel & Friehe, 2014]).
- **mpcr**: Higher MPCR supports easier cooperation, but punishment is effective even at low MPCR, especially when costs are moderate ([Choi & Ahn, 2013]; [Bruttel & Friehe, 2014]; [Levine & Modica, 2016]).
- **punishment_cost & punishment_tech**: Very well studied—the magnitude and cost-to-impact ratio of punishment strongly moderate effect on efficiency ([Kamei & Putterman, 2015]; [Qin & Wang, 2013]; [Levine & Modica, 2016]).
- **all_or_nothing** (vs. continuous): Both types covered; effect of punishment is robust to this dimension ([Fu et al., 2017]; [Page et al., 2013]; [Grieco et al., 2017]).
- **network structure** (coverage of punishment): Highly informative results on complete vs. incomplete/asymmetric punishment networks ([Leibbrandt et al., 2015]; [Boosey & Isaac, 2016]).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Some studies compare punishment and reward, showing sometimes complementary, sometimes substitutive effects ([Page et al., 2013]; [Dugar, 2013]; [Choi & Ahn, 2013]).

**Indirectly informed:**
- **chat/communication**: While not always the primary variable, several studies show chat can increase efficiency and sometimes substitute for or amplify the effect of punishment ([Brick et al., 2016]; [Oprea et al., 2014]), but direct evidence on chat × punishment interaction is sparse.
- **default_contrib**: Framing studies exist but are not the focus ([Messer et al., 2013]; sparse for direct punishment × default interactions).
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Multiple studies manipulate feedback and summary display. Transparency about identity and history increases the efficacy of punishment ([Kamei & Putterman, 2015]; [Khadjavi et al., 2017]).
- **punishment acquisition cost/participation**: Important moderator (if punishment rights require payment or endogenous acquisition, effect on efficiency can be eliminated) ([Ramalingam et al., 2016]).

**Effectively missing or very limited:**
- Interaction effects between all design dimensions are **not systematically mapped**.
- Detailed evidence for **chaotic, dynamic, or large population settings**, real-world or field experiments, and long-run sustainability is limited.
- Certain rare dimensions (chat frequency/structure, very high/low player counts, repeated exclusion, multi-level group structures beyond basic PGGs) are underrepresented.

# Important Limitations

- **Many papers report only behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment frequency) rather than efficiency or payoff.** Care must be taken not to infer efficiency gains when only contribution rates are reported—especially when punishment is costly ([Cheung, 2014]; [Kamei, 2014]).
- **Generality of findings is limited by lab settings and standard subject pools.** Several studies show that effects of punishment are not robust across subject pools (e.g., students vs. general population; [Bortolotti et al., 2015]).
- **Design features are often studied in isolation**; joint and interactive effects of multiple design dimensions are rarely tested systematically.
- **Contextual and cultural moderators are sometimes crucial**: The effect of punishment is not universal—high antisocial punishment rates, weak institutions, or high norm conflict can make punishment reduce group welfare or efficiency.
- **Adjacency and functional similarity do not always guarantee transferability**: Findings from adjacent game forms (e.g., trust games, exclusion-based mechanisms, Cournot oligopolies) may not generalize fully to classic PGGs, especially in the presence of subtle design differences.
- **Reporting and estimation of control (no-punishment) efficiency is uneven**; direct measurement is sometimes missing or must be inferred from ancillary data.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting how peer punishment affects efficiency in repeated public goods games—but only when design features such as punishment cost, network symmetry, heterogeneity, monitoring, and information structure are rigorously accounted for. The effect of punishment is typically positive but highly contingent. Efficient prediction requires careful mapping from game design dimensions and control efficiency to treatment efficiency, informed by the specific moderators that have been empirically demonstrated to shape outcomes. Limitations include over-reliance on behavioral outcomes, lack of systematic coverage of all design dimensions, and potential issues with generalizability or context dependence.
