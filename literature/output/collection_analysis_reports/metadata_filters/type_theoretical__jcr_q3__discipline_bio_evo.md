# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set is composed entirely of theoretical papers, spanning models, simulations, and analytical frameworks; there are no empirical or laboratory experimental studies included. The set is narrow in its methodological type (theory only) but broad and deep in topical coverage for public-goods (PGG) and related games, punishment and sanction mechanisms, and evolutionary game theory. Among the 139 papers, many provide direct or close evidence relevant to the prediction task—forecasting the impact of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games based on key design dimensions—while a sizable fraction are only adjacently relevant (e.g., focusing on related games, reputation systems, or indirect reciprocity), or discuss mechanisms qualitatively rather than quantitatively. There are relatively few gaps regarding design-moderator coverage, but significant gaps exist in direct, empirical treatment-effect measurement.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact:** Many papers model canonical, n-player PGGs (e.g., Cressman et al., 2012; Jiao et al., 2020; Eldakar et al., 2007; Gintis, 2000), or games with payoff structure mathematically equivalent to PGGs, sometimes with institutional or spatial modifications.
- **Close:** Some model close variants such as common-pool resource games, threshold public goods, or n-person prisoner's dilemmas (e.g., Lee & Iwasa, 2015; Cooney, 2025).
- **Adjacent:** Others study repeated dyadic games, indirect reciprocity, mutual aid, or evolutionary games with different payoff structures or enforcement mechanisms.
- **Weak/None:** A subset are about cooperation mechanisms or norm enforcement in general, with only secondary reference to PGGs.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact:** A significant number of papers directly analyze costly or institutionalized punishment as a formal, parameterized game dimension (e.g., punishment cost, magnitude, and method: Cressman et al., 2012; Eldakar et al., 2007; Henrich & Boyd, 2001).
- **Close:** Some examine non-costly or reputational sanctions, peer exclusion, or enforcement as analogous to punishment.
- **Adjacent/None:** Reviews or mechanisms papers treat punishment as part of a broader set of social controls, indirect sanctions, or partner choice.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact:** Many papers report outcomes as efficiency (group payoff relative to maximum), group welfare, or mean fitness (e.g., Cressman et al., 2012; Wang & Lv, 2019; Dercole et al., 2013).
- **Close:** Others focus on cooperation/contribution rates or prevalence of cooperative equilibria, inferring efficiency improvements indirectly.
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Some discuss behavioral outcomes or evolutionary stability, not efficiency per se.

**Summary:** The corpus is highly relevant at all three target dimensions, particularly for canonical PGGs, formal game treatments of punishment or sanctions, and efficiency-related outcomes, though empirical, laboratory, or field validation is missing.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes (Exact/Close):**  
  - Efficiency (fraction of maximum group payoff; Cressman et al., 2012; Eldakar et al., 2007).
  - Group payoff/welfare/mean fitness (Gintis, 2000; Wang & Lv, 2019).
  - Direct comparison of payoffs or earnings under punishment-enabled vs. control.
- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  - Contribution and cooperation rates (common in spatial or evolutionary models).
  - Frequency and distribution of strategies (e.g., prevalence of punishers, defectors).
  - Stability and basin of attraction for cooperative equilibria (mechanistic, not outcomes).
- **Indirect/Mixed:**  
  - Stationary abundance of strategies, or indirect proxies for efficiency (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
- **Not Measured (in Adjacent/Weak Relevance Papers):**  
  - Some discuss only the mechanism (e.g., norm enforcement), social-cognitive framing, or stability conditions without linking to payoffs or efficiency.

**Important Distinction:** Many papers caution that high contribution/cooperation rates do not necessarily yield high efficiency if the costs of punishment/reward outweigh their benefits (Wolff, 2012; Cooney, 2025).  

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **General Effect of Enabling Punishment:**
  - The dominant theoretical result is that enabling peer or institutional punishment in a PGG increases efficiency (group payoff, welfare) relative to the no-punishment baseline, **provided the cost of punishment is not too high, punishment is effective, and anti-social punishment is limited** (Gintis, 2000; Cressman et al., 2012; Henrich & Boyd, 2001).
  - Efficiency improvements are parameter-dependent: gains from punishment diminish or reverse if punishment is too costly, ineffective, prone to retaliation, or if anti-social punishment (punishing cooperators) is possible (Wolff, 2012; Rand et al., 2010; Hauser et al., 2014).
  - **Population Structure and Game Setting:**  
    - In well-mixed environments, punishment’s effect on efficiency is weaker or null unless initial conditions favor punishers (Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017).
    - In spatially or group-structured populations, and with strong between-group selection or migration, punishment can robustly promote high efficiency (Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Cooney, 2025).
  - **Type of Punishment/Avoidance of Anti-Social Punishment:**  
    - Efficiency gains are much higher when only pro-social punishment is possible.
    - Presence of anti-social punishment can destroy cooperation and reduce efficiency below the control (Rand et al., 2010; Powers et al., 2012).
  - **Distributed vs. Institutional Punishment:**  
    - Institutional or pool punishment often yields greater efficiency gains than purely peer-based systems, especially if cost is shared and systems are transparent (Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Dercole et al., 2013).

- **Moderators/Interactions with Design Dimensions:**
  - **Punishment Cost and Effectiveness:**  
    - The efficiency benefit is maximized when punishment is cheap and highly effective; if cost/fine ratio is poor, punishment can lower efficiency by direct cost drain (Wang & Lv, 2019; Gintis, 2000).
  - **Number of Rounds (Repeatedness):**  
    - Punishment is more likely to improve efficiency in repeated games with enough rounds for deterrence; in very short games or one-shot settings, benefits are small or nonexistent (Eldakar et al., 2007; Gintis, 2000).
  - **Group Size (Player Count):**  
    - Small groups benefit more from punishment; as group size increases, the stability of high-efficiency cooperation via punishment declines (Henrich & Boyd, 2001; Powers et al., 2012).
  - **Retaliation/Vengeance Possibility:**  
    - If punished players can easily identify and retaliate against punishers, efficiency benefits can disappear; **anonymity of punishers increases efficiency gains** (Janssen & Bushman, 2008).
  - **Reward Exists/Hybrid Schemes:**  
    - Combining reward and punishment often outperforms either alone; the combination can make maximal efficiency globally stable (Cressman et al., 2012; Milinski & Rockenbach, 2012).
  - **Information/Transparency:**  
    - Public visibility of punishment institutions and transparency about who is punishing increases deterrence and efficiency (Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2015).
  - **Other Moderators:**  
    - Probabilistic execution of punishment (punishing with fixed probability, not always) can improve cost-efficiency and group payoff (Jiao et al., 2020).
    - Corruption and insurance mechanisms that undermine punishment can dramatically reduce or eliminate efficiency gains (Fang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2013).
    - Heterogeneous populations (variation in punishment costs, effect, or player sensitivity) increase the range of parameter values where punishment is beneficial (de Weerd & Verbrugge, 2011; Iwasa & Lee, 2013).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **General Guidance:**  
  - Enabling punishment should, in most canonical PGG-like environments, be expected to increase group efficiency relative to control, except where costs are prohibitively high, anti-social punishment is possible, or games are too short to allow deterrence.
- **Dimension-Specific Moderation:**  
  - **Low punishment cost / high punishment effectiveness / moderate group size / sufficient rounds** → large efficiency gain.
  - **High cost, large groups, few rounds, presence of retaliation/anti-social punishment, weak information structures:** Expected efficiency gain is smaller or can be negative.
  - **Control Efficiency Baseline:**  
    - The lower the baseline efficiency (i.e., substantial free riding), the more likely punishment will yield a large treatment effect.
    - If baseline efficiency is already high (due to reputation, direct reciprocity, or other mechanisms), marginal efficiency boost from punishment may be small or negative if cost is high.
- **Use of Control Efficiency:**  
  - Theoretical models support using control efficiency as a key predictor of treatment efficiency: if the control game is near full cooperation, effect of adding punishment is small or potentially negative if it adds cost; if the control game is near full defection, adding punishment can move efficiency toward its theoretical maximum (provided optimal cost/effect ratios; Cressman et al., 2012).
- **Interactions and Nonlinearity:**  
  - The effect of enabling punishment is often non-monotonic: intermediate cost/effect values sometimes maximize efficiency due to tradeoffs (Jiao et al., 2020; Cooney, 2025).
- **Institutional Features and Edge Cases:**
  - Games allowing anti-social punishment, retaliation, or cheap insurance against punishment may see little or negative efficiency change (Rand et al., 2010; Wolff, 2012).
  - Visibility, identity revelation, or hierarchical punishment increases complexity: if corrupt or anti-social agents cannot be screened out, punishment may not increase efficiency (Lee & Iwasa, 2015).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed (modeled as parameters with efficiency outcomes):**
  - `player_count` (group size): Moderates both stability and efficiency of punishment effects (Gintis, 2000; Wang & Lv, 2019).
  - `num_rounds` (repetition): Strongly moderates the effect size (Eldakar et al., 2007; Gintis, 2000).
  - `mpcr` (synergy/returns): Higher MPCR makes sustaining cooperation easier; impacts magnitude of efficiency gains (Archetti & Scheuring, 2013).
  - `punishment_cost` & `punishment_tech` (cost/effectiveness): Central to almost all formal results (Gintis, 2000; Okada & Bingham, 2008).
  - `all_or_nothing` (binary vs. continuous contribution): Both types are modeled; effect of punishment on efficiency persists but may differ in magnitude (Eldakar et al., 2007).
  - `reward_exists` (and reward-related parameters): Joint modeling of reward and punishment yields crucial findings (Cressman et al., 2012; Milinski & Rockenbach, 2012).
- **Indirectly Informed (modeled but no direct efficiency outcome):**
  - `show_punishment_id` (punisher anonymity): Important for retaliation risk and effectiveness (Janssen & Bushman, 2008; Lee et al., 2015).
  - `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Some models discuss information feedback, but few directly link to efficiency.
  - `chat` (communication): Rarely modeled in theory papers, more relevant in experimental work.
  - `default_contrib`: Framing effects rarely addressed in efficiency terms.
- **Only Contextually Discussed or Sparse:**
  - `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Only detailed in hybrid models; some papers ignore reward mechanisms.
  - `punishment_exists` (binary switch): All theory papers compare with/without punishment, but many focus on the effect of magnitude rather than strict presence/absence.
- **Effectively Missing:**
  - Detailed modeling of `chat`, `default_contrib`, or complex real-world information feedback is rare; these dimensions are more often discussed in behavioral papers or empirical summaries.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of Empirical Data:**  
  - All findings are based on theoretical analysis or simulation; no experimental or field data directly validates the predicted size or reliability of punishment effects on efficiency.
- **Parameterization and Quantification:**  
  - Few papers provide quantitative estimates or formulas that can be directly mapped onto arbitrary empirical parameter values; results are often qualitative ('punishment helps when cost is low and group size is moderate').
- **Complex Moderation and Nonlinearity:**  
  - Many papers warn of non-monotonic, sometimes discontinuous effects: increasing punishment cost or group size can sharply reduce or reverse efficiency gains (Wolff, 2012; Cooney, 2025). Some parameter regimes yield negative effects.
- **Assumptions and Scope:**  
  - Most models assume rational updating, perfect observability (or state key limitations), and specific evolutionary or replicator dynamics; results may not generalize to settings with bounded rationality, misperception, or laboratory/human behavior with richer psychology.
- **Treatment of Anti-Social Punishment/Rare Behaviors:**  
  - Presence of anti-social punishment or spiteful behaviors is a consistent caveat undermining efficiency results; real settings may allow anti-social punishment through misimplementation or cultural drift.
- **Treatment of Information, Communication, and Social Learning:**  
  - Detailed social information, reputation updating, or communication is variably modeled or omitted, limiting guidance for games where these are prominent.
- **Sparse Evidence on Some Dimensions:**  
  - Game features like `chat`, `default_contrib`, or rich feedback structures are generally not included in the efficiency analysis, so their interactions with punishment are unclear from this literature.

---

**In summary**: Theoretical evidence robustly supports that enabling peer punishment in public-goods-game-like environments increases average efficiency compared to the no-punishment control, with the size and presence of the effect strongly moderated by punishment cost/effectiveness, group size, number of rounds, the possibility of anti-social punishment or retaliation, and the baseline efficiency of the control game. Predictions should weigh these design dimensions accordingly. However, all conclusions are model-driven and lack empirical effect-size calibration; real-world and lab outcomes may diverge due to further psychological, social, or institutional complexities not fully captured in this literature.
