# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is **large (111 papers)**, with a strong **empirical, experimental lab focus**: almost every study measuring behavioral and/or payoff effects of institutional or design changes in PGGs or close variants. There is an abundance of findings from standard public goods games (linear PGGs) and a smaller but substantial number from close-variant settings (e.g., CPRs, threshold/extraction games, resource allocation, trust games, and field experiments).

**Theory papers are nearly absent; the set is overwhelmingly empirical.** Many papers directly report treatment-control comparisons on group payoff or efficiency. Others report only behavioral proxies (contribution rates, norm enforcement, frequency of punishment/reward), which are not synonymous with efficiency as defined in the prediction task. A sizable minority cover only adjacent paradigms (e.g., trust games, dictator/ultimatum, third-party punishment, or CPRs with added features).

For **predicting the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in public goods games**, there is a **broad and diverse empirical base** but also considerable diversity in outcome measures and design details. Coverage for the key prediction task is good but—given the granularity of the desired design dimension mapping—**still sparse for some parameters** and especially when seeking generalizable, quantitative effect sizes.

# 2) Task Relevance

Evaluated across the three core relevance dimensions:

- **PGG or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`):**  
  - About half the studies are in **standard linear PGGs** with classical design (multiple players, repeated rounds, nontrivial MPCR, voluntary contribution). Many others employ **close variants** such as common pool resource (CPR) games, extraction/resource dilemmas, or networked PGGs with modifications.
  - About 20% of studies are only **adjacent** (trust/ultimatum, third-party, market games), and a minority are **weak or not relevant** for PGGs.

- **Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`):**  
  - *Exact relevance* is high in more than 30 empirical studies where peer punishment (or its institutional variants) is manipulated (enabled vs. disabled or across mechanisms).
  - *Close relevance* is seen with centralized, rule-based, or exclusion-based sanctions, and in variant designs (CPRs, reputation systems).
  - *Adjacent* or *weak* relevance appears in studies of reward/taxation, communication, or behavioral proxies of sanctioning without actual punishment.

- **Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`):**  
  - *Exact* or *close* outcome relevance (where efficiency, group payoff, total earnings, or welfare are directly measured or can be calculated) is present in about half the studies.
  - Many studies report only *adjacent* outcomes (contribution rates or norm compliance, sometimes inferring efficiency effects).
  - A significant subset reports only non-payoff behavioral data (*none* for efficiency), especially social preference or psychological studies.

In sum: **Relevance for each dimension is often exact or close, particularly for mainline PGG studies with punishment and measured group payoffs.** Some treatment comparisons are highly applicable for the downstream prediction task. However, **not all design dimensions are equally covered**, and many studies focus on behavioral mechanisms or adjacent social dilemmas, requiring caution in transfer.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related (efficiency) outcomes:**  
- **Direct efficiency measures** (group earnings/group potential, surplus/welfare, coins generated) are explicitly reported in many PGG lab experiments (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Fatas et al., 2020).
- Some studies provide **average group payoff, surplus, or welfare** (e.g., Waichman, 2020; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021). A few use related measures (e.g., "social efficiency," "mean group income," or "repayment rate").
- **Calculation of efficiency from reported data** (e.g., contribution and punishment expenditures) is possible but often not explicit.

**Non-payoff, behavioral outcomes:**  
- **Contribution rate/cooperation rate** (by round or treatment) is the most common outcome.
- **Punishment frequency, amount, or targeting** (how often or to whom punishment is assigned).
- **Norm compliance, conditional cooperation, response to punishment or reward** (e.g., effect of group structure, communication, or information).
- **Emotion, group composition, and belief effects** on punishment assignment or contribution behavior.

**Distinctions:**  
- Some studies demonstrate **decoupling between cooperation behavior and overall efficiency**: for example, punishment may increase cooperation but, due to the burden of costly punishment, not increase (or even reduce) group payoffs (Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021; Mantilla et al., 2021).
- Studies using only behavioral proxies **cannot be used to infer efficiency effects directly** unless the mapping from behavior to efficiency is validated or reported.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Effect of Enabling Punishment:**
- **Punishment often increases efficiency** relative to no-punishment control in standard linear PGG settings with anonymous, repeated rounds, and visible contribution histories—especially if punishment is not overly costly or antisocial use is rare (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021, Fatas et al., 2020).
- **But, the effect of punishment on efficiency is not uniform**; it is **highly context-dependent** and moderated by design dimensions, group composition, and informational structure.

**Conditional and Contextual Findings:**
- **Group composition and social context:**  
  - Punishment effectiveness is undermined in *ethnically mixed or diverse groups* due to in-group favoritism or antisocial punishment, often leading to *lower efficiency relative to homogeneous groups* (Mantilla et al., 2021; Drouvelis et al., 2021; Vollan et al., 2019).
- **Information structure and transparency:**  
  - Efficiency gains from punishment are achieved only when others' actions (and capacities) are observable and punishment can be well-targeted (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; De Geest & Kingsley, 2019). **Incomplete information weakens or reverses the efficiency effect.**
  - *Feedback linking punishment to behavior* is critical; without it, punishment may increase costs without efficiency gains (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).
- **Antisocial punishment and retaliation:**  
  - High levels of *antisocial punishment* (punishing cooperators/out-group members for personal or vengeful reasons) can offset or reverse efficiency gains (Fatas et al., 2020; Vollan et al., 2019; Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021).
- **Group baseline cooperativeness:**  
  - In groups with *already high cooperation* (high control efficiency), adding punishment often **reduces efficiency due to unnecessary punishment costs** (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).
  - In low-cooperation groups, *severe or well-targeted punishment with information* can increase efficiency, but only if the institution is chosen early and understood.
- **Punishment mechanism details matter:**  
  - **Centralized, automatic, or democratic punishment mechanisms** (central manager, pooling, voting-based punishment) often yield **larger efficiency gains** and **reduce antisocial punishment** compared to individualistic or dictator-like systems (Castillo et al., 2021; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019).
  - The **visibility of punishment** and identifiability of punishers can amplify prosocial targeting and improve efficiency (Kamei, 2018).

**Null, Negative, or Weak Effects:**
- In some settings, **enabling punishment does not increase efficiency**, and may even lower it due to high costs of punishment, antisocial use, or misapplied sanctions (Kamijo et al., 2020; Mantilla et al., 2021; Drouvelis et al., 2021; Vollan et al., 2019; Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021).
- In *inefficient PGGs* (MPCR < 1), punishment effects on efficiency are **weak or null**; reward mechanisms tend to have **strong positive effects** on efficiency instead (Kamijo et al., 2020).
- In designs where **punishment is group-level, not targeted** (whistleblowing, collective sanctions), punishment can reduce efficiency (Makowsky & Wang, 2018; Chapkovski, 2021).

**Moderators:**
- **Emotional context** (Lee & Min, 2021), communication/chat (Koch et al., 2021; Kamei, 2019), sanctioner selection (Castillo et al., 2021; Fatas et al., 2020), heterogeneity in endowment or punishment ability, and monitoring regime (DeAngelo & Gee, 2020) all can moderate the punishment-efficiency relationship.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct Recommendations:**
- In **mainline linear PGGs** (multiple rounds, observable contributions, reasonably symmetric players, moderate punishment cost/effectiveness), **enabling peer punishment will generally increase group efficiency relative to control**, except:
  - Where the **control efficiency is already high** (adding punishment may reduce efficiency via unnecessary cost),
  - Where **antisocial punishment or in-group favoritism is expected** (mixed or diverse groups)—here, *efficiency gains are null or negative*,
  - Where **punishment is anonymous, insufficiently targeted, or information is incomplete** (contributions or endowments are not observable),
  - In **inefficient PGGs** (MPCR < 1), where punishment's positive effect on efficiency is generally weak or null, but **rewards can improve efficiency**.

**Key Moderators to Adjust Prediction:**
- **Group composition:** If mixed/heterogeneous by ethnicity or baseline pro-sociality, *expect less or no efficiency gain* from punishment.
- **Information structure:** If contributions/capacities and punishment are observable and feedback is salient, *expect higher efficiency* from punishment. If not, *expect weak/null/negative effect*.
- **Punishment mechanism:**
  - **Centralized/democratic punishment** is more effective at increasing efficiency (especially if reduces antisocial use).
  - **Peer punishment in small groups**: costliness, frequency, and targeting matter; high cost or mis-targeting reverses gains.
  - **Group-level sanctions** (untargeted) can *reduce efficiency*.
- **Baseline control efficiency:** If control is already efficient, *punishment will likely reduce efficiency*.
- **Sanctioner immunity:** If any individual is *immune from punishment*, overall group efficiency drops.
- **Reward mechanisms:** If present, **they can substitute for or outpace punishment in boosting efficiency**, especially with net-positive reward schemes.

**Use Control Efficiency as a Baseline:**  
- **Always anchor the prediction on the reported control (no-punishment) efficiency** under matched design dimensions. The *incremental efficiency gain from enabling punishment* should be *moderated* by the above design and group context variables.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- **player_count** and **num_rounds**: Well-covered in nearly all studies; most PGG experiments report group size (2–10 typical) and round structure (5–30 rounds).
- **mpcr**: Explicitly specified in nearly all lab PGG papers; its effect as a moderator is directly analyzed in some (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Fatas et al., 2020).
- **punishment_cost** and **punishment_tech** (cost to punisher / impact on target): Explicit and variably parameterized in many studies.
- **all_or_nothing**/**continuous**: Both options are explored, though continuous contribution games are more common.
- **chat/communication**: Directly manipulated in some studies (Koch et al., 2021; Kamei, 2019; Ahn et al., 2018), found to enhance efficiency, and a strong moderator of punishment effects.
- **show_other_summaries**, **show_n_rounds**, **show_punishment_id**: These elements of information structure are often reported and manipulated, shown to affect targeting, norm enforcement, and efficiency (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Kamei, 2018).

**Indirectly or Contextually Informed Dimensions:**
- **default_contrib**: Framing as opt-in/opt-out for contributions is rarely manipulated directly. A few studies may discuss endowment framing.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Coverage is sparser than for punishment; a limited set of studies compare reward and punishment or study their joint effects.
- **punishment_exists**: Universal as a treatment variable in exact-relevance studies, but with less cross-study comparison for other dimensions.
- **group matching protocol, endowment heterogeneity, and sanctioner selection method**: Discussed as contextual moderators but not always tied to a specific prediction dimension.

**Effectively Missing or Sparse:**
- Direct manipulation or factorial coverage of:
  - **default_contrib** (opt-in/opt-out contributions)
  - **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**, and the operational details of reward mechanisms (with exception for a few well-documented studies, e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Stoop et al., 2018)
  - **show_punishment_id** (punisher/rewarder identifiability) is covered in some, but not systematically
  - **punishment_tech** other than simple cost/impact structure (e.g., probabilistic sanctioning details, institution selection) is uneven

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Context-Dependence:**  
  - The effect of punishment on efficiency is not monotonic or robust across contexts; group composition, information structure, anti-social punishment, and emotional/psychological context can *reverse* the expected direction of effect.
- **Generalizability Across Dimensions:**  
  - Most studies vary only a handful of design dimensions at a time; interactions among multiple dimensions (e.g., large groups, anonymous play, heterogeneous endowment with peer punishment) are **rarely covered in a factorial way**.
- **Measurement Gaps:**  
  - Many papers report only behavioral proxies (contributions, norm compliance), not actual group efficiency or payoff, creating uncertainty in inferring efficiency effects.
- **Reward and Mixed Institutions:**  
  - **Reward mechanisms are less studied** than punishment. Where both are present, effects are often non-additive and design-dependent (Kamijo et al., 2020).
- **External Validity and Field Evidence:**  
  - Most evidence comes from small-group lab experiments; field or naturalistic studies (especially with large groups or real-world stakes) are rare, though there are some framed field experiments and resource-use studies.
- **Ambiguity and Disagreement:**  
  - Findings conflict in certain parameter regions (e.g., high diversity, high pre-existing cooperation). Both positive and negative effects of punishment on efficiency are observed, often depending on subtle design or social features.
- **Sparse Evidence for Some Prediction Dimensions:**  
  - Some design dimensions (e.g., punishment/reward identifiability, default contribution framing) are only indirectly or not at all informed.
- **No Purely Theoretical Papers:**  
  - The set is nearly entirely empirical, so extrapolation to untested parameter combinations is limited by observed empirical range.

---

**In summary:** The literature provides **strong but context-dependent empirical evidence** for predicting the effect of enabling peer punishment on group efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. **Punishment tends to increase efficiency in standard, well-informed, homogeneous small-group PGGs with observable actions**, but this is highly moderated by group composition, information, mechanism details, baseline efficiency, and other design dimensions. Caution is required when extrapolating to settings with high diversity, high antisocial punishment, anonymous play, or inefficient public goods, and where only behavioral (non-payoff) outcomes are available. Control efficiency, design dimensions, and contextual moderators should all be used in conjunction to inform predictions, with an explicit acknowledgment of ambiguity and limitations.
