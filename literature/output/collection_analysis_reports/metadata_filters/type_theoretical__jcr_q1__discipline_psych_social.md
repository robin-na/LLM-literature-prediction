# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set is extensive and predominantly consists of theoretical and simulation modeling papers, with a strong emphasis on public-goods-game (PGG) or closely related social dilemma environments. Although primarily theoretical, these papers systematically explore key mechanisms underlying collective action, punishment, sanctioning, cooperation, and efficiency. Empirical data from direct experiments are mentioned in a few papers and frequently cited as support, but the bulk of findings stem from mathematical models, simulation studies, and integrative reviews drawing on multiple sources.

The evidence base is relatively "broad" within social-dilemma theory, but "narrow" in empirical coverage: very few papers deliver direct, experimental, or observational estimates of changes in group efficiency (as defined as group payoff relative to full cooperation) when peer punishment is enabled versus disabled in PGGs, especially as a function of all 14 prediction-relevant design dimensions. Nevertheless, mechanistic understanding of punishment effects in PGGs is well developed, with many papers analyzing parameter conditions and moderators.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) PGG or Variant —** `pgg_or_variant`
- **Exact relevance**: Many papers explicitly analyze linear or threshold public goods games, or direct analogues (e.g., Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Macy, 1993; Boyd et al., 2014; Ye et al., 2011).
- **Close/adjacent relevance**: Numerous others focus on adjacent or more general n-person social dilemmas, including repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, common pool resource games, or evolutionary simulations capturing the same basic incentive structure (e.g., Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Skatova & Ferguson, 2011).
- **Some papers analyze partner selection, club goods, or reputation signaling in games structurally similar, but not formally PGGs (e.g., Cai & Kock, 2009; Glover & Kim, 2021).**
- **Purely context/theoretical papers are also present but less directly relevant (e.g., Powers et al., 2021; Balliet et al., 2022).**

**b) Punishment or Sanctions —** `punishment_or_sanctions`
- **Exact relevance**: The bulk of the set directly examines peer punishment, institutional punishment, or sanctioning mechanisms—often as experimental, computational, or analytic additions to the control PGG.
- **Some adjacent papers analyze reward, ostracism, or information sanctions (e.g., gossip) as substitutes or complements to punishment, especially in field or naturalistic settings.**
- **Several papers examine the conditions for punishment's effectiveness, its cost structure, who punishes, and how punishment is normatively or institutionally channeled.**
- **A minority contextually reference punishment but focus more on non-punitive norm enforcement or endogenous sorting (e.g., reputation, partner choice).**

**c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcomes —** `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
- **Direct (exact/close):** A minority of papers measure or model group efficiency, i.e., total group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum, and address how this changes with punishment (e.g., Ye et al., 2011; Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Farjam et al., 2015; Skatova & Ferguson, 2011; Glover & Kim, 2021).
- **Many papers instead report on cooperation rate, norm compliance, or contribution as primary outcomes, with efficiency/group payoff inferred only indirectly.**
- **Several papers make clear that higher cooperation (contribution) does not always translate to higher efficiency—due to costly punishment, anti-social punishment, or resource destruction (e.g., Kurzban et al., 2015; Kroupa, 2014; Guala, 2012).**
- **A large proportion are adjacent or weak, discussing “welfare” or “payoff” generally, or focusing on mechanisms rather than reporting or predicting net efficiency.**

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, earnings, welfare):**
    - *Directly measured/modelled:* Efficiency (group payoff relative to max possible) is the primary outcome in a core subset (e.g., Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Ye et al., 2011; Kroupa, 2014; Farjam et al., 2015; Skatova & Ferguson, 2011).
    - *Closely related outcomes:* Surplus, “social welfare,” total coins/energy, average earnings (sometimes as function of contribution + punishment costs).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
    - *Most common are cooperation or contribution rate*: Most papers and reviews highlight improvement of average contribution or cooperation rate after introduction of punishment, but often do not assess whether gains in contribution outweigh destruction from punishment (e.g., Fehr & Fischbacher, 2004; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
    - *Punishment frequency, norm compliance, prevalence of punishment strategies*, adaptation of behavioral types—commonly measured as proxies for successful norm enforcement, not as efficiency per se.
    - *Other outcomes include norm stability, retaliation, antisocial punishment, and social selection mechanisms*.

- **Distinction is explicit:** Many reviews and models specifically note that increases in cooperation driven by punishment do **not always** correspond to increases in efficiency and that destructive or misapplied punishment (including antisocial punishment or “second-order” free riding) can reduce overall payoff.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**(A) Empirical and Theoretical Consensus/Disagreement**
- *Consensus (conditional):* Introducing punishment in a PGG **tends to increase contribution/cooperation rates**, and can—depending on parameter conditions—increase efficiency compared to the control (no-punishment) case (Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Macy, 1993; Parks et al., 2013; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021).
- *Critical caveat:* **Efficiency only increases if** punishment is not too costly, is well-targeted (prosocial, not antisocial), avoids rampant retaliation, and the design/environment allows for effective norm enforcement without excessive second-order free riding (Ye et al., 2011; Kroupa, 2014; Kurzban et al., 2015; Guala, 2012).
- *Conflict/ambiguity:*
    - Some models and reviews demonstrate that, *in many standard laboratory PGGs*, punishment **increases cooperation but reduces overall efficiency** due to destruction of resources from punishment costs (Kurzban et al., 2015; Guala, 2012).
    - Other studies and simulations show **large efficiency gains** with punishment, but nearly always under parameter combinations with low punishment cost, high effectiveness, presence of compensatory/second-order mechanisms, longer run lengths, or additional social features like communication or norm internalization.

**(B) Cross-Paper Synthesis of Predictive Moderators**
- **Punishment Parameters:**
    - **Cost:** Low-cost punishment is more likely to increase efficiency; high-cost, uncoordinated punishment often destroys value (Ye et al., 2011; Guala, 2012; Farjam et al., 2015).
    - **Effectiveness:** Punishment must be strong/credible (Cai & Kock, 2009; Evans & Thomas, 2001; Libois, 2022). Too weak and it does not incentivize cooperation; too strong and it can crowd out intrinsic motivation or provoke retaliation (Hernandez et al., 2022).
- **Auxiliary Mechanisms:**
    - **Compensation/Reward for Punishers:** Presence of reward or second-order incentives for punishers can make punishment evolutionarily stable and more efficient (Ye et al., 2011; Toriumi et al., 2016).
    - **Communication, Reputation, Leadership:** These features mitigate antisocial or excessive punishment, improve targeting, and sustain high efficiency (Kroupa, 2014; Vincent, 2017; Guala, 2012).
    - **Norm Internalization and Institutionalization:** Normatively constrained punishment (e.g., institutional, reputation-based, leader-administered) is more efficient (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021).
- **Game Structure/Parameter Sensitivity:**
    - **Player Count:** Punishment is more effective at increasing efficiency in small groups; in large groups, coordination problems and second-order free riding hamper effect (Boyd et al., 2014; Parks et al., 2013).
    - **MPCR:** In low-MPCR games (less individually lucrative to contribute) punishment can transform efficiency more dramatically; in high-MPCR games, punishment adds little or can be harmful (Farjam et al., 2015; Macy, 1993).
    - **Number of Rounds:** Effects of punishment on efficiency often become positive only in longer repeated interactions, as the need for punishment and its cost diminish over time (Kroupa, 2014).
    - **Information and Network Structure:** Efficiency gains from punishment depend on monitoring informativeness, timing of information release, and network integration for information spread (Abreu et al., 1991; Larson, 2017).
    - **Antisocial Punishment/Noise:** Efficiency is undermined when punishment targets cooperators or errors are common (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Handfield et al., 2016).
    - **Baseline Efficiency (Control):** When control efficiency is already high, punishment may add little or even reduce efficiency; when control is low, well-designed punishment is more likely to yield large positive effects (Farjam et al., 2015; Asgharpourmasouleh et al., 2017).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the evidence and limitations of this paper set:

- **Expect punishment enabled to increase efficiency relative to control** when (a) control efficiency is low, (b) punishment is not too costly, (c) punishment is normatively or institutionally constrained, (d) additional mechanisms like communication, reward/compensation, or reputation are present to support prosocial punishment and minimize antisocial or second-order free riding.
- **Do not expect a universally positive effect**: In standard lab PGGs, or when punishment is costly, uncoordinated, prone to retaliation or antisocial use, or when group size is large and communication absent, the efficiency gains are often negative or negligible—even if cooperation/contribution increases. Use baseline control efficiency as a strong moderator.
- **For continuous-contribution, one-shot, anonymous games**, or when punishment is harsh or the design lacks supporting mechanisms: the marginal effect of punishment on efficiency is likely weak or negative (Kroupa, 2014; Guala, 2012; Handfield et al., 2016).
- **Effect size of efficiency improvement depends strongly** on dimensions such as punishment cost, effectiveness, structure; group size; MPCR; number of rounds; presence of communication/reputation/leadership; and control (baseline) efficiency.
- **Predictions should explicitly account for design features** that make effective, targeted, and normatively constrained punishment possible, and for baseline efficiency. Absent such features, or when those dimensions are unfavorable, efficiency improvements should not be assumed.
- **Where evidence is behavioral (cooperation rate) and not payoff-based**, treat it as an upper bound; unless demonstrated otherwise, efficiency should be expected to increase less than cooperation rate, due to direct and opportunity costs of punishment.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Heavily modeled; smaller groups consistently show more positive efficiency gains from punishment than larger groups (Boyd et al., 2014; Macy, 1993; Parks et al., 2013).
- `num_rounds`: Explored in depth—longer games allow punishment to have a more positive effect on efficiency (Kroupa, 2014; Fehr & Gintis, 2007).
- `mpcr`: Central focus; low MPCR settings benefit most from punishment, high MPCR less so (Farjam et al., 2015; Macy, 1993).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Key moderators across many papers. Low punishment cost/mechanisms with high effectiveness generate more positive efficiency changes; misapplied or antisocial punishment is specifically modeled to destroy efficiency.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Discussed as critical moderators when present; reward or compensation for punishers can greatly improve efficiency (Ye et al., 2011; Toriumi et al., 2016).
- `all_or_nothing`: Some models focus on binary (all-or-nothing) versus continuous-contribution; findings often differ on effect magnitudes (Ye et al., 2011; Boyd et al., 2014).

**Indirectly/Contextually Informed:**
- `chat` (communication): Considered an important moderator (allows norm formation, coordination, reduces unnecessary punishment), though often not the main manipulated parameter (Kroupa, 2014; Parks et al., 2013).
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`: Addressed in the context of information availability and monitoring, mainly for the importance of transparency for targeting and deterring defection (Abreu et al., 1991; Fehr & Fischbacher, 2004; Larson, 2017; Evans & Thomas, 2001).
- `default_contrib`: Rarely independently varied in the models; discussions of framing effects are infrequent.

**Effectively Missing/Sparse:**
- `show_punishment_id`: Occasionally mentioned regarding retaliation or reputation, but not systematically varied or analyzed.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Typically discussed only tangentially as informational context.
- **No paper fully cross-tabulates all 14 dimensions in a factorial or systematic parametric way. Empirical coverage tying multivariate configurations to payoff outcomes is very limited.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Predominantly theoretical and model-based:** Direct, empirical estimates of efficiency improvements from peer punishment in PGGs (with all design features manipulated) are rare. The generalizability of simulated findings is limited.
- **Payoff vs. behavior outcomes:** Many conclusions rely on cooperation/contribution rates, not direct efficiency or group payoff. Efficiency changes are assumed or inferred, often without explicit reporting.
- **Ambiguity in design-specific prediction:** Evidence is strong for qualitative moderation (e.g., high-cost punishment is often inefficient), but effect size prediction for novel combinations of dimensions or for within-game parameter sweeps is poorly supported.
- **Conditions for positive efficiency effect are strict:** The literature documents numerous boundary conditions and failure modes (e.g., second-order free riders, antisocial/retaliatory punishment, harshness crowding out voluntary cooperation, or punishment being too weak or too costly).
- **Empirical/lab-field gaps:** Laboratory PGGs may overstate the inefficiency or frequency of punishment due to design artifacts (Kroupa, 2014; Guala, 2012), but analogues in real-world or field settings often add institutionally constrained or coordinated punishment, making translation complex.
- **Coverage gaps:** Several prediction-relevant dimensions are either not manipulated or only mentioned contextually, meaning prediction for those parameter regions is highly uncertain.
- **No consistent mapping from control efficiency + design dimensions to treatment efficiency exists** in the current literature—with strong moderators and boundary conditions, but weak quantitative calibration.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature set provides strong qualitative and mechanistic guidance, with exact, close, and adjacent coverage on core concepts and design dimensions. However, for precise, design-level quantitative prediction of efficiency change due to enabling peer punishment in a public-goods-game-like environment, evidence is mixed, conditional, and often incomplete. Key moderators (punishment cost, structure, relatedness, MPCR, group size, communication) must be considered, with the expectation that enabling punishment does not universally improve efficiency, and the predicted effect must be modulated based on the presence/absence of supportive design features and the control (baseline) efficiency.
