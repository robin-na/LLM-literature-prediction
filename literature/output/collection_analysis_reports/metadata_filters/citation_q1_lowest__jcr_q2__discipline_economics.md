# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 105 studies, with a substantive empirical core featuring controlled laboratory experiments and field experiments, supported by several theoretical contributions. Empirical coverage is particularly strong for classic and modern public goods games (PGG) and laboratory analogs, as well as for adjacent environments (CPR, market/trust games, team production, contest, and bargaining games). Theoretically, the literature includes explicit mechanism design, repeated game theory, and evolutionary models.

- **Empirical/Theoretical Mix**: The evidence base is heavily empirical (especially lab experiments) for the core PGG with and without punishment, with a smaller but meaningful representation of field experiments and formal models of punishment mechanisms.
- **Breadth**: For *punishment-enabled efficiency in PGGs*, the evidence is broad and deep in the classic lab settings and moderately broad for adjacent, CPR, or market-like environments. Theory articles extend coverage to structural and mechanism-level findings (punishment size, targeting, game repetition, etc.).
- **Limitations**: Comparative evidence for all variants of the 14 design dimensions exists, but coverage of some dimensions (communication, reward, information) and certain population contexts (e.g., cultural cross-sections) is thinner or more indirect for efficiency outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessed across three target dimensions: `pgg_or_variant`, `punishment_or_sanctions`, `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`.

- **PGG or Variant**  
  - **Relevance**: **exact to close** for the core set; a significant share of studies use canonical PGGs or very close analogs (linear, threshold, all-or-nothing, etc.). Adjacent studies focus on CPR, market, or trust games.
  - **Outliers**: A notable minority are adjacent (principal-agent, CPR, trust, or bargaining games) and must be mapped with caution.

- **Punishment/Sanctions**  
  - **Relevance**: **exact** in the core (peer, centralized, institutional, ostracism, exclusion, etc.); small set address adjacent forms (social disapproval, endogenous exit, exclusion, monitoring).
  - **Sanction Mechanism Variation**: The range includes peer-to-peer costly punishment, formal punishment, hired-gun/institutional, ostracism, monitoring/exclusion, group voting, and higher-order or targeted mechanisms (punishing punishers or non-punishers).

- **Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**  
  - **Relevance**: **exact** for many key studies (direct group efficiency or average payoff); **close to adjacent** for others (group earnings, welfare, surplus, coins generated); some studies only report non-payoff behavioral outcomes, leading to **weak/none** relevance for direct efficiency prediction.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Relevant for Prediction)**  
- *Exact/Close* Outcomes:
    - Group efficiency (ratio of actual group payoff to fully cooperative benchmark)
    - Average group payoffs, welfare/surplus, total coins/earnings
    - Group-level outcomes across experimental conditions (punishment enabled vs disabled)
- *Indirect/Related* Outcomes:
    - Group earnings relative to social optimum, resource stocks, net welfare after sanction or reward costs

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Important but Distinct)**
- *Commonly Reported:*
    - Individual and group contribution rates
    - Cooperation/compliance rates
    - Punishment frequency, severity, and targeting
    - Norm compliance, usage of punishment/reward, voting/acceptance of institutional rules
    - Propensity for exclusion, ostracism, or partner switching
    - Emotional, motivational, or psychological correlates of cooperation/punishment

**Distinguishing Note:**  
Behavioral outcomes (e.g., higher contributions, cooperation, compliance) often—but not always—correlate with increased efficiency. In many studies, higher contributions are offset by punishment costs, meaning efficiency (net payoff) does not always improve with increased cooperation.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Empirical Patterns:**

- **Punishment increases efficiency when:**
    - Punishment is well-targeted (directed at clear free-riding, well-calibrated, or based on explicit contribution norms) and not excessively costly (Kamei, 2024; JARUNGRATTANAPONG, 2022; Gürdal et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2024 [theory]; Joseph et al., 2025).
    - The punishment mechanism is institutional, centralized, or combines multiple channels (formal punishment, hired-gun, or a combination of observed/unobserved punishment) (Kamei, 2024; Glöckner et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2020).
    - The punishment cost-to-impact ratio is favorable; free or low-cost punishment is associated with the largest efficiency gains (Billinger & Rosenbaum, 2023 [meta]; Huang et al., 2024 [theory]).
    - The underlying baseline (control) efficiency is low: punishment is most effective when free-riding is prevalent in the control (Kamei, 2024; Gürdal et al., 2021).

- **Punishment does not improve or reduces efficiency when:**
    - Costs of punishing are high relative to cooperative gains, leading to net welfare loss despite more cooperation (Botelho et al., 2022; Ramalingam et al., 2019; Robbett, 2019; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Deng et al., 2025).
    - The punishment mechanism encourages anti-social punishment or is prone to abuse (Kim et al., 2025; Glöckner et al., 2018; Hopp & Süss, 2024).
    - Network structure or institutional implementation diffuses responsibility (Peng & Fan, 2023; Bühren et al., 2025), or punishment is too easy or too uncoordinated (Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017).
    - Information or game framing is unfavorable; with ambiguous norms, private types, or weak identification of defectors, punishment fails to sustain or can undermine efficiency (Robbett, 2019; Kim et al., 2025; Glöckner et al., 2018).
    - Cultural or group context does not support norm-enforcing punishment (Kamei et al., 2025; Davies & Fafchamps, 2021).

- **Conditional and Moderator Findings:**
    - The effect of punishment is typically stronger in repeated games, smaller groups (n=4–5), with well-understood, transparent rules and observable behavior.
    - Framing (Give/Take) and punishment institution (targeted, centralized, etc.) strongly moderate the effect (Ramalingam et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2020; Glöckner et al., 2018).
    - Reward mechanisms, partner choice, enforceable taxes, and commitment devices can sometimes substitute for or outperform punishment for efficiency gains (Chugunova et al., 2020; Chessa & Loiseau, 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Serdarevic et al., 2021; Huber et al., 2018).
    - The threat, rather than frequent use, of punishment often produces the largest efficiency gains (Chugunova et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2024 [theory]; Bühren et al., 2025).

**Theoretical/Mechanism Insights:**
- The minimum effective punishment threshold and targeting (punishing only the lowest contributor, or minimal level to deter defection) are key to maximizing efficiency (Huang et al., 2024; Matsushima, 2012; Aramendia, 2006).
- Heterogeneity in group size, marginal return, or player types introduces non-monotonic or context-dependent effects (Peng & Fan, 2023; Dong et al., 2024).
- Repeated game theory asserts that, with sufficient patience and observability, appropriate punishment can sustain full cooperation and thus full efficiency (Huang et al., 2024; Camera & Gioffré, 2025; Janus & Lim, 2009; Aramendia, 2006).

**Disagreement & Limits to Consensus:**
- Several studies robustly contest the idea that punishment always increases efficiency. In some parameter regimes or institutional designs, it reduces efficiency (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Robbett, 2019; Deng et al., 2025; Glöckner et al., 2018).
- Efficiency effects are population- and context-dependent: cultural norms (Kamei et al., 2025; Davies & Fafchamps, 2021), group ability, and information structure (Ye et al., 2023) play a central role.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Implications for Predicting Treatment Efficiency**

- **Directly Supported:** Where design matches lab PGGs with well-specified group size, number of rounds, continuous contributions, MPCR, no chat, clear peer punishment technology, cost, and feedback structure, the empirical evidence is robust: enabling punishment usually increases efficiency unless punishment is too costly or misused (Kamei, 2024; Gürdal et al., 2021; Joseph et al., 2025; JARUNGRATTANAPONG, 2022).
- **Moderators:**
    - *Punishment Cost/Effectiveness*: Prediction should adjust downward if punishment is costly to administer and/or if anti-social punishment is prevalent.
    - *Punishment Institution/Network*: Peer punishment in complete networks may suffer from diffusion of responsibility; incomplete or structured institutions (e.g., hired gun, centralized) often perform better (Peng & Fan, 2023; Liu et al., 2020).
    - *Magnitude and Targeting*: Large, well-targeted, and institutionalized punishment performs best; excessive, indiscriminate, or uncoordinated punishment can backfire.
    - *Behavioral/Contextual Factors*: Baseline (control) efficiency, anti-social punishment propensity, group ability, and culture are important covariates.
- **Control Efficiency as Predictor:** While correlated, baseline (control) efficiency alone is not sufficient—design dimensions (especially regarding punishment and information) are critical moderators.
- **Structural Effects:**
    - Efficiency gains are largest when baseline cooperation is low and when punishment 'threat' supports cooperation with minimal actual punishment expenditure.
    - When punishment is too costly, ambiguous, or encourages anti-social behavior, efficiency gains vanish or may reverse.
    - Institutional competition (reward vs punishment) and endogenous choice (migration, voting) can affect both the magnitude and direction of efficiency changes.

**Prediction Steps:**
1. Check whether the prediction context matches standard PGG lab parameters—if so, expect significant efficiency gain with punishment enabled, *unless cost/other design parameters suggest otherwise*.
2. If punishment is costly, network is complete, or anti-social punishment prevails, efficiency gains diminish or may turn negative; adjust prediction accordingly.
3. For institutionalized or centralized punishment schemes, especially with well-designed targeting and costs, predict stronger efficiency gains.
4. Use control (no-punishment) efficiency as a baseline, but modulate treatment estimates by game design details as above.
5. For adjacent or non-standard environments, use only contextually supported adjustments, preserving uncertainty.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed Across Multiple Papers:**
    - `player_count` (group size): Almost ubiquitous; effects of size directly tested.
    - `num_rounds`: Extensively covered (one-shot vs repeated, finitely/infinite repetition effects).
    - `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return): Generally explicit; variations tested.
    - `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Highly detailed in many studies—cost-to-impact, technology/mechanism type, targeting, network structure, observability.
    - `all_or_nothing` (binary/continuous): Both forms tested.
    - `chat`: Explicitly controlled for in many papers; effect on efficiency and punishment use assessed.
    - `reward_exists` (incentive regime): Present in several; compare effects of reward and punishment.
    - `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Information structure, round/feedback transparency commonly specified.
- **Indirectly Informed or Contextual Only:**
    - `default_contrib`: Framing (opt-in/opt-out) discussed in a few (Martinsson et al., 2019; Colasante et al., 2024).
    - `show_punishment_id`: Varies; anonymity or identity known in some, but not a primary comparison.
- **Effectively Missing or Rare:**
    - `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Less frequently varied, though some papers include reward as a counterfactual.
    - Granular punishment/reward technology (e.g., timing, selective targeting, higher-order complexity) is addressed variably; rare direct ties to prediction parameters outside core PGG studies.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Reporting Gaps:**  
  - Not all studies report both control and treatment (punishment-enabled) *efficiency*; many focus on cooperation/contribution rates only or do not tie behavioral outcomes to total payoff.
  - For some designs, only adjacent or weakly related outcome measures (CPR stock, compliance, reputation, etc.) are used.

- **Population and Context Transferability:**  
  - Cultural context, risk preferences, group heterogeneity, and cognitive ability are shown to moderate treatment effects in several studies but are often unmeasured in others.
  - Some results are derived from adjacent games (CPR, principal-agent, trust, bargaining)—careful mapping is needed to port findings to PGGs.

- **Dimension Coverage:**  
  - Several design dimensions (reward cost/tech, detailed communication protocols, identity feedback, default contribution framings) are under-explored for efficiency effects.
  - Effects of information structures (identification, context, feedback) and higher-order punishment are variably addressed, often not with direct efficiency outcomes.

- **Ambiguity and Disagreement:**  
  - Substantial disagreement remains on when and how punishment increases efficiency: several robust studies and a meta-analysis show that, under conventional costly peer punishment, group payoff often fails to improve and may decrease, especially when cost is high, punishment is misdirected, or anti-social punishment is common.
  - The magnitude and sign of efficiency changes are contextually sensitive (depends on institutional details, player ability, baseline efficiency, and group/cultural context).

- **Limits of Inference:**  
  - Causal generalizability from tightly controlled lab experiments to broader or field environments is not always supported; outcomes in group-heterogeneous, high-stakes, or naturally formed groups may diverge from lab findings.
  - Several theoretical results depend on idealizations (infinitely repeated, perfectly enforced punishment, full observability) that may not hold in experimental or real-world PGGs.

**In summary:** The literature provides powerful tools and robust patterns for predicting efficiency changes when enabling punishment, directly supporting many design dimensions. However, accurate prediction requires careful attention to the exact institutional details, cost structures, control efficiency, and context-dependent moderators, as the treatment effect of punishment on efficiency is highly design- and context-sensitive.
