# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is moderately broad for public-goods-game-like environments (PGG) but extremely mixed in its coverage of our specific prediction target: the payoff consequences (efficiency) of enabling peer punishment. It consists of both empirical lab and field experiments (including high-quality, well-controlled public goods games) and theoretical/evolutionary models. The set includes a substantial number of exact-relevance PGG experimental studies directly manipulating punishment and a number of theory papers modeling efficiency or related payoff outcomes under different punishment regimes.

However, a considerable fraction of the papers are only adjacent to PGGs, focus on behavioral cooperation/contribution rather than payoff, or center on reward, ostracism, or indirect norm-enforcement mechanisms. Only a minority of empirical studies provide exact, quantitative efficiency (welfare, group payoff) measures for the PGG-with-punishment versus PGG-without-punishment contrast essential for the prediction task. Several key design dimensions are frequently reported and manipulated; others appear only infrequently or contextually.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **PGG or Variant**:
    - `exact`: A subset of both empirical (e.g., Ozono et al., 2020; Otten et al., 2020; Molleman et al., 2019; Weber et al., 2018) and theory papers (e.g., Salahshour, 2021; García & Traulsen, 2019; Huang et al., 2018) match public goods games very closely.
    - `close`: Some papers use adjacent paradigms (e.g., repeated PD, social dilemma variants with exit or exclusion, 'innovation race' games), offering relevant mechanisms that may generalize.
    - `adjacent`/`weak`: Other studies use dictator or ultimatum games, ethnographic or field accounts, or non-PGG game models.
- **Punishment or Sanctions**:
    - `exact`: Many studies directly manipulate or theorize peer punishment mechanisms, typically with cost and impact parameters (e.g., Ozono et al., 2020; Salahshour, 2021; García & Traulsen, 2019).
    - `close`/`adjacent`: Evidence for reward, exclusion, restorative justice, and third-party punishment is also substantial but is not always peer punishment.
- **Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**:
    - `exact`: A minority of studies (notably, Ozono et al., 2020; Salahshour, 2021; García & Traulsen, 2019) report group efficiency or average payoffs as a function of punishment.
    - `close`/`adjacent`: Many other papers only report contribution, cooperation, sanctioning frequency, or inequality, and do not report efficiency or total group payoff.

**Conclusion**: The set is highly relevant for mechanisms of punishment and its effect on cooperation, but direct, quantitative data on efficiency effects of enabling punishment in PGGs are limited.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes**:
    - *Efficiency*, *group payoff*, *total earnings*, or *welfare* are measured directly in a few key studies ([Ozono et al., 2020](#), [Salahshour, 2021](#), [García & Traulsen, 2019](#)). Some theory papers model average payoff as an equilibrium property.
    - Some studies adjacent to PGG report efficiency-like outcomes (e.g., in innovation games, [Han et al., 2021](#)), total group wealth ([Tsvetkova et al., 2018](#)), or use phase diagrams of dominant strategies corresponding to average payoff ([Hooper et al., 2021](#)).
    - Many experimental studies do **not** report efficiency comparisons between punishment and no-punishment conditions, even when payoff data are available.
- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**:
    - The large majority report *contribution rates*, *cooperation rates*, *punishment frequency*, or *strategy prevalence* (e.g., [Huang et al., 2018](#); [Otten et al., 2020](#); [Molleman et al., 2019](#); [Weber et al., 2018](#); [Li et al., 2021](#)).
    - Other outcomes: *norm compliance*, *inequality* (Gini), *strategy adoption frequency*, norm perceptions, and neural responses.
    - Some adjacent outputs (e.g., distributional consequences, ostracism effectiveness, restorative justice vs. punishment) focus on behavioral or structural outcomes rather than efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis across studies focusing on efficiency and related payoffs yields these core findings:**

- **Positive Effects of Punishment on Efficiency—But With Key Moderators**:
    - Theory and some empirical evidence suggest that enabling peer punishment *can* increase group efficiency in PGGs, as it sustains cooperation ([Ozono et al., 2020](#); [Salahshour, 2021](#); [García & Traulsen, 2019](#); [Hooper et al., 2021](#); [Perry & Gavrilets, 2020](#); [Okada, 2020](#)).
    - **Crucial Moderators**:
        - *Alignment of local and global incentives*: If cooperation is locally efficient (i.e., the local group benefits), punishment increases efficiency. If not, punishment is ineffective ([Ozono et al., 2020](#)).
        - *Cost and effectiveness of punishment*: Efficiency rises when punishment is not too costly but remains effective. Too-cheap punishment can promote antisocial punishment, reducing efficiency ([Salahshour, 2021](#); [García & Traulsen, 2019](#); [Han et al., 2021](#)).
        - *Possibility of antisocial punishment or corruption*: Institutional or peer punishment can reduce efficiency if antisocial punishment spreads or if institutions are corrupt ([García & Traulsen, 2019](#); [Lee et al., 2019](#); [Fulker et al., 2021](#)).
        - *Punishment mechanism design*: Conditional or coordinated punishment often yields better outcomes. If it is difficult to coordinate, efficiency may not rise ([Huang et al., 2018](#); [Molleman et al., 2019](#)).
        - *Observability/conditionality of punishment*: Punishment systems only increase efficiency if players can observe them and condition their strategies ([García & Traulsen, 2019](#)).
        - *Scope (local vs. global punishment)*: Enabling more global targeting of punishment can reduce effectiveness by making targeted interventions harder ([Ozono et al., 2020](#)).
        - *MPCR/returns to cooperation*: Higher marginal returns make pro-social punishment more likely to evolve and more likely to increase efficiency ([Salahshour, 2021](#); [Hooper et al., 2021](#)).
- **Neutral, Negative or Context-Dependent Effects**:
    - Where punishment induces antisocial behavior or is wasteful (e.g., excessive cost for little effect, or when it aggravates inequality), group efficiency may not increase—or may even decrease ([Tsvetkova et al., 2018](#); [Fulker et al., 2021](#); [Han et al., 2021](#)).
    - In the presence of institutional corruption, efficiency gains from punishment are not robust and may oscillate or collapse ([Lee et al., 2019](#)).
    - Reward-based or restorative mechanisms may be as (or more) effective in raising efficiency, especially in small-scale settings ([Wiessner, 2020](#); [Han & Tran-Thanh, 2018](#)).
- **Control Game Efficiency Is an Important Moderator**:
    - Where baseline (control) efficiency is already high (i.e., cooperation is sustained without punishment), enabling punishment may yield little or even negative returns after accounting for the cost of sanctioning ([Ozono et al., 2020](#)).
    - If control efficiency is low (cooperation unravels), enabling punishment tends to yield larger positive effects ([Salahshour, 2021](#); [Perry & Gavrilets, 2020](#)).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **General Guidance**:
    - The effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency is *not universally positive*—it is strongly *conditional on game design dimensions* and pre-existing baseline efficiency.
    - *Positive treatment effects* (higher efficiency in the punishment condition) should be expected when:
        - Punishment is costly enough to deter antisocial use, but not too costly to deter pro-social punishment.
        - Cooperation yields a high marginal return (high mpcr); group benefit from full contribution is clear and locally salient.
        - The structure of the game makes punishment observable/conditionable, and players have sufficient information to coordinate on punishing defectors.
        - The risk of institutional corruption or antisocial punishment is low.
    - *Neutral or negative effects* may occur:
        - When punishment is too cheap (fosters spite/antisocial punishment) or too expensive (ineffective).
        - If the group structure or network enables the spread of costly antisocial punishment ([Fulker et al., 2021](#)), or if corruption is possible ([Lee et al., 2019](#)).
        - When baseline (control) efficiency is already high, in which case the cost of punishment might reduce efficiency.
    - **Prediction should take into account the specific game design dimensions available**: e.g., `punishment_cost`, `mpcr`, `player_count`, `punishment_tech`, `observability` (proxied by `show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries`), presence of reward, and whether chat or other coordination mechanisms are available.
    - When prediction input includes *control efficiency*: The higher the control efficiency, the less likely punishment will deliver large efficiency gains; low control efficiency invites larger improvements if punishment is properly tuned.
    - **Empirical effect sizes are sparse**: Theory provides strong qualitative mapping of the influence of parameter settings, but quantitative point predictions should be recognized as uncertain.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed**:
    - `player_count`: Frequently varied; smaller groups facilitate coordination on punishment, larger groups often require institution or delegation.
    - `num_rounds`: Repeated games (higher `num_rounds`) support the emergence of punishment and cooperation through reputation and learning.
    - `mpcr`: Central to predicting net efficiency—higher returns make punishment and cooperation more likely and effective.
    - `punishment_cost`: Directly manipulated and modeled; optimal intermediate levels are generally most effective for efficiency.
    - `punishment_tech`: Mode/mechanism of punishment (peer, pool, institutional, conditional, coordinated, etc.) is critical.
    - `show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries`: Observability and feedback are theorized and sometimes tested as moderators of punishment’s effect on efficiency.
- **Indirectly Informed / Contextually Discussed**:
    - `all_or_nothing`: Discussed or used in some papers.
    - `chat`: Contextually included in several lab experiments; not often isolated as a focal moderator.
    - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Some studies examine these in the context of combined reward/punish institutions; reward can substitute for punishment under certain conditions.
    - `show_n_rounds`: Round count transparency is manipulated in some papers, usually in repeated games.
    - `default_contrib`: Opt-in/opt-out framing seen only occasionally and not directly tied to efficiency outcomes.
- **Effectively Missing / Sparse**:
    - Precise parameterization of `punishment_magnitude` (impact per unit) is inconsistently reported.
    - Rich manipulation/measurement of `chat`, `show_n_rounds`, `default_contrib`, and some other interface/feedback elements are rare.
    - Several studies do not specify exact group structure details (e.g., network topology).
    - Correlated manipulation of multiple dimensions (e.g., how reward and punishment interplay under various observability or network settings) is rarely systematically explored.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse Direct Efficiency Evidence**: Only a handful of studies actually report group efficiency or total payoff as a function of enabling punishment, and there is considerable reliance on theory or indirect inferences from behavioral data.
- **Theoretical Overlap and Gaps**: Much theoretical evidence relies on evolutionary stability and dominance of strategies as proxies for efficiency; real-world details may limit applicability.
- **Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes**: Most studies report behavioral (contribution/cooperation) outcomes rather than aggregate payoffs, which might not map one-to-one onto efficiency changes, especially when the cost of punishment is high.
- **Overlooked Design Dimensions**: Several prediction inputs are insufficiently covered, and parameterization is not always precise (e.g., observability, chat, reward configuration, specific punishment technology).
- **Negative and Mixed Results**: A subset of papers reports anti-social punishment effects, efficiency reductions, or null effects, especially under low punishment cost, poor institutional design, corruption, or high baseline control efficiency.
- **Non-PGG Contexts**: Some findings are from adjacent paradigms (repeated PD, innovation races, exclusion games, social dilemmas with exit, dictator/ultimatum), and transfer to group-based, continuous-contribution standard PGGs is nontrivial.
- **Lack of Quantitative Predictive Meta-Analysis**: No meta-analytic aggregation of effect sizes or statistical models translating experimental findings into parameter-mapped predictions for the downstream prediction task.
- **Dynamic & Social Complexity**: Some theory and ethnographic work emphasize endogenous cycles or social selection processes (e.g., cycles of corruption), suggesting static predictions may miss key dynamics.

---

**In summary**:  
- The literature base provides strong qualitative guidance for predicting when enabling peer punishment will or will not increase efficiency in PGGs—but robust, quantitative evidence directly mapping design dimensions to efficiency outcomes is relatively thin.
- Key design dimensions (especially player count, rounds, mpcr, punishment cost/tech, observability) are theoretically well-mapped as moderators.
- Prediction should account for the starting baseline (control efficiency), punishment cost/effectiveness, risk of antisocial punishment, alignment of incentives, and observability.
- Expect positive efficiency effects only in conducive parameter regimes; effects can be negative or null if punishment is poorly tuned, is subject to antisocial abuse, or if cooperation is already robust in the absence of punishment.
