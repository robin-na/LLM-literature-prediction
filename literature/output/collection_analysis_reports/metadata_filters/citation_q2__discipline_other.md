# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set includes 27 papers spanning theory, empirical observational work, field experiments, and laboratory experiments; however, the majority are theoretical and conceptual, with a smaller subset providing empirical or experimental evidence relevant to punishment and efficiency in public-goods-game (PGG) environments. The evidence base is thus mixed but somewhat narrow in directly supporting the prediction task (i.e., treatment efficiency in controlled PGG-like experiments with and without punishment).

Within this set:
- **Theory papers** provide substantial modeling and mechanism-based argumentation regarding how punishment institutions affect cooperation, payoff, and efficiency (e.g., Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Vlerick, 2016).
- **Experimental and simulation-based work** is less frequent but offers some direct empirical findings, mostly on contribution, cooperation, and sometimes on payoff or efficiency (e.g., Zhang et al., 2019; Brick & Visser, 2010; Xu et al., 2014).
- **Observational and field studies** are sparse and tend to be adjacent, focusing on institutional or real-world settings rather than controlled PGGs (Montoya et al., 2015; Holubcik et al., 2023).

Overall, the base is broad in conceptual and theoretical reach but comparatively narrow in direct, empirical, payoff-based evidence for the core prediction of efficiency effects from enabling punishment in PGGs.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessing the literature’s directness to the three target-relevance dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant:**  
  - **exact:** A handful of theory and meta-analysis works (e.g., Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Vlerick, 2016) directly model PGGs or settings indistinguishable from PGGs.
  - **close/adjacent:** Many papers use structurally similar games (network trust, repeated sharing, market entry, step-level dilemmas), which are close but not always PGGs proper (Xu et al., 2014; Brick & Visser, 2010; Lim & Capraro, 2022).
  - **weak/none:** Several papers are only tangential, referencing PGGs for context but not focusing on them or reporting PGG-relevant outcomes.

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**  
  - **exact:** Multiple theory papers and some empirical studies directly manipulate or analyze punishment (peer or institutional, e.g., Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012).
  - **close:** Indirect forms of punishment or broader sanctioning systems are often modeled (e.g., exclusion, rating-based sanctions).
  - **adjacent/weak:** Some adjacent works reference punishment only in broad discussions of cooperation and institutions.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
  - **exact:** Only a few papers report or model **efficiency** precisely as group payoff over maximum possible (Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Vlerick, 2016).
  - **close/adjacent:** Several report closely related end outcomes (e.g., group/individual earnings, mean payoff, welfare), but not always normalized efficiency.
  - **weak/none:** Many studies report only non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation/contribution rates, norm compliance).

Thus, **the set provides a core subset of highly relevant theory and some empirical support, but most papers are adjacent or only indirectly informative** for the prediction of treatment efficiency outcomes in PGGs with design-dimension inputs.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

The literature distinguishes between two types of outcomes:

- **Payoff-based outcomes (the focus of prediction):**
  - **Efficiency** (group payoff as a proportion of the social optimum): Directly measured or modeled in a few theory and meta-analysis papers (Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Vlerick, 2016).
  - **Total earnings, group surplus, welfare, or mean payoff:** Sometimes reported as stand-ins when explicit efficiency isn't computed (Xu et al., 2014; Lim & Capraro, 2022; Brick & Visser, 2010).
  - **Related economic targets:** For adjacent settings, compliance with social optimality (e.g., meeting emissions targets), group-level payoff, or reduction in loss from anti-social behavior (Brick & Visser, 2010).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - **Contribution or cooperation rates:** Most frequently measured across experimental studies (Zhang et al., 2019; Rommel, 2015; Woodcock & Heath, 2002; O'Connor, 2016).
  - **Punishment frequency, norm compliance, pro-social/anti-social acts, market entry rates:** Used as proxies for social cooperation but not directly convertible to payoffs or efficiency.
  - **Psychological/strategic behaviors (guilt, apology, moral reasoning):** Present in modeling but not tied to economic efficiency (O'Connor, 2016; Forber & Smead, 2016; Handfield & Thrasher, 2019).

**Few papers connect behavioral outcomes to explicit payoff-based measures.** Where reported, payoff outcomes typically improve with effective punishment, but this is not universally quantified nor normalized to social optimum standards.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis across the most directly relevant literature:**

- **Punishment increases group efficiency when properly structured and when design parameters are favorable.**
  - Theory and meta-study evidence show that well-designed punishment institutions reliably increase both cooperation and group efficiency in PGGs, bringing group payoffs closer to the social optimum (Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Vlerick, 2016).
  - The effect is not universal: design details crucially matter (Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012).

- **The effect of punishment on efficiency is contingent and moderated by:**
  - **Institutional scale and adoption mechanism:** Collective-choice punishment is required for larger/global-scale public goods, while individual choice is effective for local public goods (Vasconcelos et al., 2022).
  - **Time horizon (# rounds) and group stability:** Longer games and stable groups allow punishment to pay off as cooperation stabilizes and costly punishment tapers off, boosting efficiency; in short or unstable group settings, punishment may remain costly and reduce overall efficiency (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
  - **Punishment cost and method:** Direct costly punishment can be inefficient if mismatched to group composition; exclusion-based or rating/institutional punishment mechanisms are more robustly efficient (Rosas, 2008; Xu et al., 2014; Lim & Capraro, 2022).
  - **Information and memory:** Strong learning, longer memory, and richer information about others’ behavior/institutional performance increase both adoption and the effectiveness of punishment-enabled high efficiency (Vasconcelos et al., 2022).
  - **Interaction/network structure:** Structured (less well-mixed) populations need less severe punishment to achieve full efficiency; networked or partner settings enhance the efficiency effects of punishment (Lim & Capraro, 2022; Xu et al., 2014).
  - **Marginal per-capita return (MPCR):** Efficiency returns from punishment are greater when the baseline temptation to defect is high (Rosas, 2008).

- **Punishment sometimes reduces efficiency, especially in:**
  - Short or one-shot games, high-cost punishment, or when anti-social (misapplied) punishment is prevalent (Frey & Rusch, 2012; Rosas, 2008).
  - Settings where punishment is easily exploited, misdirected, or where design does not stop defectors from undermining cooperators.

**Empirical findings and meta-analyses confirm that punishment’s effect on actual group earnings or efficiency is generally positive when these moderating factors are favorable, but less so or even negative when not.** Behavioral outcomes (contributions, compliance) almost universally improve with punishment, but only a subset of studies quantifies the net efficiency effect.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For downstream prediction of average efficiency in PGG-like games with peer punishment enabled (given design dimensions and control efficiency):**

- **Punishment should be predicted to increase treatment efficiency over control if:**
  - The punishment institution is collective or well-aligned with the scale of the public good (Vasconcelos et al., 2022).
  - The game is repeated (many rounds), groups are stable, and learning is possible (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
  - The cost-effectiveness of punishment is high (i.e., per-unit punishment is impactful but not overly costly; see Rosas, 2008, Lim & Capraro, 2022).
  - Information and feedback on others’ behavior (showing summaries, learning opportunities) are provided.

- **Prediction should be more cautious, with the possibility of null or negative effects, if:**
  - The game is short or one-shot.
  - Punishment is expensive or not impactful (low punishment_tech, high punishment_cost).
  - Groups are randomly rematched (stranger design) and memory is short.
  - Design allows for anti-social punishment or misapplication.

**The most strongly informed dimensions for predicting the size of the efficiency increase are:** number of rounds, player count/group stability, punishment cost, punishment method/technology, information and learning features, and possibly MPCR. Where these factors signal favorable conditions, the literature provides strong reason to expect a positive effect of peer punishment on efficiency, typically moving group outcomes closer to social optimality.

**If control efficiency is already high (near 1), the marginal gain from punishment may be lower or even negative** due to the net cost of implementing punishment, especially in shorter games (Frey & Rusch, 2012).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count:** Modeled and manipulated in theory and experimental work; larger groups may require more effective/collective punishment institutions (Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Rosas, 2008; Vlerick, 2016).
- **num_rounds:** Strong evidence that longer time horizons increase the long-run payoff benefit of punishment (Frey & Rusch, 2012; Xu et al., 2014).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Central to several theoretical and simulation studies—both the direct cost to punishers and overall impact on defectors matter (Rosas, 2008; Lim & Capraro, 2022).
- **mpcr:** Addressed in both theory and experiment as a factor shaping the temptation to defect and the importance of punishment (Rosas, 2008; Vasconcelos et al., 2022).
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds:** Information and memory components are highlighted as important moderators of punishment’s efficiency, but rarely manipulated experimentally (Vasconcelos et al., 2022).

**Indirectly or Contextually Discussed:**
- **all_or_nothing, chat, default_contrib:** Occasionally referenced, with chat/communication generally improving cooperation independently of punishment (Brick & Visser, 2010), and step-level (all-or-nothing) designs discussed but not the focus.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Reward mechanisms are sometimes considered as alternatives or complements to punishment, but their interaction with punishment and efficiency is less systematically addressed (Montoya et al., 2015; Wang & Cui, 2022).
- **show_punishment_id:** Identity disclosure is sometimes relevant for reciprocity or anti-social punishment dynamics but receives little direct empirical or theoretical attention.

**Effectively Missing:**
- Many papers do not address default_contrib, reward_cost, reward_tech, or show_punishment_id directly.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical scarcity and outcome mismatch:** Only a minority of the literature reports **efficiency or payoff-based outcomes** directly, and even fewer normalize these outcomes in the way required for treatment-control prediction.
- **Predominance of theory over experiment:** Much of the core guidance relies on modeling and mechanism arguments that synthesize empirical patterns or meta-studies but do not supply new effect sizes or parameter estimates.
- **Limited coverage of some design dimensions:** Dimensions such as chat, reward_cost, reward_tech, default_contrib, and show_punishment_id receive little or no direct support, constraining prediction “coverage” for those variables.
- **Ambiguity and context dependence:** Even in well-covered dimensions (e.g., punishment_cost, num_rounds), the literature shows that effects are highly **context-dependent**; settings with short games, high costs, or mismatched punishment methods can yield no gains or even lower efficiency, but this is typically reported in qualitative rather than quantitative form.
- **Over-reliance on PGG structure:** Prediction in adjacent or highly modified environments (markets, large networks, real-world institutions) is only indirectly supported.

**In sum, the literature base provides strong conceptual and qualitative guidance on when and how punishment increases efficiency in PGG-like environments, with key moderating design dimensions well identified and empirically supported in a subset of the work. However, it lacks systematic, quantitative, empirical estimates of treatment-control efficiency differences across the full range of design dimensions required for highly precise prediction.**
