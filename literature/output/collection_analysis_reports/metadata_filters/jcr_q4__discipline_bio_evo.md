# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists entirely of theoretical modeling papers (13/13), with no empirical or experimental studies. The set is narrow in methodology (lacking data-driven effect size estimates or observed heterogeneity) but relatively broad in theory scope, encompassing canonical public goods games (PGG), variants such as the N-person prisoner's dilemma, shirker's dilemma, threshold games, and stylized collective action and resource management models. The models address both peer and institutional punishment, metanorms (rewarding punishers or punishing non-punishers), and some adjacent mechanisms such as generalized reciprocity and voluntary participation. Direct analyses of group efficiency or payoff are common, although several papers address only adjacent or weakly related paradigms. A minority of papers focus on conceptual critiques or context, with little quantitative relevance for prediction.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact:** 4 papers directly model PGGs or N-person prisoner's dilemmas with well-defined group structure, payoffs, and contribution rules (Deng et al., 2012; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Kurokawa et al., 2010; Bowles & Gintis, 2004).
- **Close:** 4 papers use highly similar paradigms (e.g., common-pool, resource sharing, or collective action with threshold provision) (Kendal et al., 2006; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Castro & Toro, 2008; Voelkl, 2015).
- **Adjacent:** 5 papers use related but distinct games (e.g., pairwise PD, stylized volunteering/threshold games, agent-based social simulations, or conceptual critiques) (Jaffe, 2004; Peña et al., 2024; Kurokawa, 2022; Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006; Hernández, 2021).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact:** 6 papers model explicit costly punishment (peer or institutional), with design parameters (Deng et al., 2012; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).
- **Adjacent:** 4 papers address punishment in an indirect or stylized way (e.g., via partner switching, withholding cooperation, or as part of a broader context) (Jaffe, 2004; Castro & Toro, 2008; Kurokawa et al., 2010; Kurokawa, 2022).
- **None/Weak:** 3 papers focus only on context or critique with no explicit punishment model (Peña et al., 2024; Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006; Hernández, 2021).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact/Close:** 10 papers model group efficiency or related aggregate payoff as a primary outcome, tying it explicitly to game dynamics and sanctioning (all except Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006; Hernández, 2021; Castro & Toro, 2008, for the treatment effect).
- **Adjacent:** 3 papers focus on behavioral outcomes or use qualitative argumentation without efficiency analysis.

**Summary:** The literature is strongest on theoretical mechanisms by which punishment (with carefully specified properties) affects efficiency in public-goods and closely related games. Task alignment is weakest for empirical effect sizes, precise calibration in real-world heterogeneity, and for non-PGG games or without explicit punishment.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**
- Efficiency (group payoff divided by full-cooperation payoff) is the central variable in most theoretical papers (e.g., Deng et al., 2012; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Jaffe, 2004; Voelkl, 2015).
- Related measures include group welfare, mean fitness, aggregate wealth, and surplus.
- Fixation probability and basin of attraction for efficient equilibria are often reported in evolutionary models.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- Some papers discuss the emergence or maintenance of cooperation (Kurokawa et al., 2010; Castro & Toro, 2008; Peña et al., 2024), norm compliance (Jaffe, 2004), or punishment frequency (Bowles & Gintis, 2004) without always linking directly to efficiency changes.
- Outcomes such as tolerance, generosity, and partner switching appear, often as mechanisms or correlates of efficiency, but are conceptually distinct.

**Distinction:** Most relevant papers explicitly report payoff-based outcomes, and they clearly separate non-payoff behavioral mechanisms unless directly mapped to aggregate group efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Effect of Punishment:**  
- Theoretical evidence strongly supports that enabling punishment—when well-designed—can transform low-efficiency, high-defection equilibria into high-efficiency, high-cooperation ones, especially in canonical PGGs and similar settings (Deng et al., 2012; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).
- Efficiency gains are most robust when punishment is not prohibitively costly, is sufficiently severe relative to the benefit from defection, and, in some models, is distributed (cost-shared) or incentivized (metanorms).

**Boundary Conditions and Moderators:**
- If punishment (costs or severity) is poorly tuned—too weak to deter defection, or too costly to punishers—efficiency does not improve, and may worsen (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Lee & Iwasa, 2014).
- Some models note that the initial conditions (frequency of punishers/cooperators) and group size interact with these thresholds (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Peña et al., 2024).
- Metanorms (rewarding punishers/punishing non-punishers) further expand efficiency regions, with reward-based norms being more robust (Kendal et al., 2006).

**Negative or Mixed Effects:**
- Jaffe (2004) finds that punishment increases norm compliance and generosity but, unless enforced norm-compliance creates synergistic surplus, punishment can reduce aggregate efficiency due to punishment costs outweighing cooperation gains.
- Peña et al. (2024) and related work support the baseline finding that—even without punishment—efficiency typically decreases with group size in threshold collective action games.

**Adjacent Mechanisms:**
- Voluntary participation (“loners”), partner choice, and tolerant/reciprocating sanctioning (rather than explicit costly punishment) can in some regimes improve efficiency or stabilize cooperation (Castro & Toro, 2008; Kurokawa et al., 2010; Kurokawa, 2022).

**Cautions:**
- Two conceptual papers caution that laboratory framing, cultural context, and hidden variables may moderate these results (Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006; Hernández, 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Overall Guidance:**  
The prevailing theoretical consensus is that, in standard PGG or PGG-like treatments, enabling peer (or institutional) punishment robustly *increases group efficiency* relative to the same game without punishment—*provided that* the punishment mechanism is effective (punishment magnitude > benefit from defection), not prohibitively costly, and not so severe as to introduce additional inefficiency.

**Prediction Implication:**
- *If control efficiency is high already,* the incremental gain from punishment may be small (ceiling effect); if control efficiency is low, and punishment effectiveness/cost is favorable, the efficiency gain may be large (potential to shift equilibrium).
- *If punishment is poorly designed* (very high cost, low effectiveness, or highly asymmetrical cost/benefit ratios), efficiency may not improve, and can even fall, because sanctioning costs subtract from group payoffs (Jaffe, 2004; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
- *Metanorms* (punish non-punishers or reward punishers) further improve prospects for efficiency gains, especially when initial frequencies of punishers are low (Kendal et al., 2006).

**Quantitative Calibration:**  
- Many core design dimensions (player count, MPCR, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech (severity), and presence of metanorms/rewards) are critical in shaping predicted outcomes.
- Game specifics (e.g., whether punishment costs are shared, severity of fines vs. cost, and mechanism for triggering punishment) empirically moderate effects.

**Empirical Gaps:**  
- All findings are theoretical—no empirical effect sizes or estimates of real-world variability are given.
- For games not closely matching PGG structure (pairwise PD, voluntary participation, explicit reward), only adjacent or weak guidance can be drawn.

**Non-payoff Mechanisms:**  
- Evidence distinguishes behavioral compliance or cooperation increases (likely with punishment) from direct increases in efficiency (which depend on sanction costs vs. generated surplus).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Modeled in most papers; group size affects thresholds, effectiveness, and cost of punishment (Deng et al., 2012; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Voelkl, 2015; Peña et al., 2024).
- `num_rounds`: Directly modeled in repeated games; longer games with repeated interaction can foster stable cooperation, especially with sanctioning (Kurokawa et al., 2010; Castro & Toro, 2008).
- `all_or_nothing`: Modeled as action type; binary vs. continuous contributions; affects stability and punishment regimes (Deng et al., 2012; Peña et al., 2024).
- `mpcr`: Central parameter; higher MPCR increases baseline cooperation and makes punishment more effective (Deng et al., 2012; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kurokawa et al., 2010).
- `punishment_cost` & `punishment_tech`: Key determinants of efficiency/impact (Deng et al., 2012; Voelkl, 2015; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Lee & Iwasa, 2014).
- `reward_exists` / `reward_cost`: Present in metanorm models (Kendal et al., 2006).
- `show_n_rounds`: Discussed in repeated game models (Kurokawa et al., 2010; Castro & Toro, 2008).

**Indirectly Informed:**
- `default_contrib`: Not modeled directly, but implication is that “opt-in” vs. “opt-out” could shape baseline cooperation (no explicit coverage).
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`, `chat`: Not systematically modeled; contextually discussed as potential moderators or framing effects (Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006).

**Effectively Missing:**
- No paper models or empirically studies `chat`; only framed as potentially influential in context (Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006).
- `show_other_summaries` and `show_punishment_id` appear in repeated PD or monitoring/partner-selection contexts, but are not systematically analyzed.
- Experimental framing or cognitive cue dimensions are contextually mentioned but not parameterized.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **No Empirical Data:** All results are based on mathematical or computational models. There are no empirical studies validating the magnitude, robustness, or noise in treatment-control efficiency comparisons with real subjects.
- **Parameter Calibration:** Key thresholds (punishment effectiveness, cost, severity, group size, MPCR) are theoretically mapped, but empirical parameter ranges and actual effect sizes are unaddressed.
- **Treatment Heterogeneity:** Most models assume well-mixed groups, no learning, and fixed strategies; real-world and experimental PGGs may show substantial individual and group heterogeneity, dynamic adaptation, or meta-strategic behavior.
- **Contextual and Framing Effects:** Non-modeled game design variables (communication, framing, salience, social/cultural context) can strongly affect observed outcomes but are not parameterized in the models (Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006).
- **Adjacent Paradigms:** Several papers address only pairwise, threshold, or opt-out paradigms rather than true PGG structure or do not include punishment directly.
- **Sparsity in Multi-dimensional Coverage:** Some design dimensions (e.g., `chat`, `show_punishment_id`, or detailed monitoring features) are not directly addressed, limiting guidance for prediction models containing these factors.
- **Ceiling/Floor Effects Unquantified:** Lack of empirical variation makes it hard to predict the effect when baseline (control) efficiency is already high, or to estimate how much punishment would increase efficiency in various starting states.
- **Mechanism Arguments Dominate:** Most findings carefully distinguish behavioral from payoff-based outcomes, but the translation from model mechanism to actual group-level payoffs in human settings remains untested. 

**Summary:** This paper set provides a robust theoretical foundation for the direction and moderators of punishment effects on group efficiency in PGG-like environments, especially concerning the relevant design dimensions. However, it lacks empirical validation, effect size calibration, and full coverage of some design/contextual features critical for downstream prediction tasks.
