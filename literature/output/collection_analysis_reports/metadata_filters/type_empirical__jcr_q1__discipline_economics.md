# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large (153 empirical papers, nearly all lab or field experiments) and highly focused on public goods games (PGG) and close variants. Most studies are empirical, with the overwhelming majority being experimental lab studies, and a substantial minority being field or framed field experiments. Nearly all treatments involve manipulations of punishment, sanctions, or institutional mechanisms affecting norm enforcement. The evidence base is, therefore, broad and deep for the downstream task of predicting efficiency effects of peer punishment in PGG-like settings.

Importantly, a significant subset of papers reports **payoff-based group outcomes** (efficiency, average earnings, welfare, surplus) across a range of punishment technologies, costs, group compositions, and institutional designs. Mechanistic/theoretical arguments are typically tested empirically, so evidence is weighted toward direct data rather than speculative models.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant**:  
- Relevance: **Exact to Close**. Most papers use canonical linear PGGs or close CPR variants. Some studies (especially field/CPR papers, trust games, contest games) are adjacent, but the main payoff and cooperation mechanisms are similar.

**Punishment or Sanctions**:  
- Relevance: **Exact to Close**. Most included papers directly manipulate the presence, form, or scope of peer punishment (costly, monetary, exclusion, central, democratic, informal, third-party). Others test reward, expulsion, or quasi-punishment mechanisms (minimum quotas, fines, milestones).

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**:  
- Relevance: **Exact to Mixed**. Many papers directly report group efficiency (payoff as a share of maximum possible), group earnings, or net welfare; others focus only on behavioral (contribution) outcomes or provide indirect payoff inferences (e.g., via extraction or contribution levels). Some otherwise highly relevant papers use contribution or cooperation rate as the primary metric, with group efficiency not always reported.

**Summary**: Overall, the literature is **highly relevant** for predicting the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, especially in lab settings. For field and CPR-like variants, relevance is close but with more contextual dependencies.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (central for prediction)**:
- **Efficiency**: Ratio of group earnings to social optimum; directly reported in many PGG studies (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Dickinson et al., 2015; Ertan et al., 2009).
- **Total group payoff/earnings/surplus/welfare**: Used interchangeably with efficiency (e.g., Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016; Masclet et al., 2003).
- **Net welfare/benefit (accounting for punishment/reward costs)**.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (cannot be equated with efficiency, but often accompany/warn of efficiency effects)**:
- **Contribution rate/level, cooperation rate**: Often reported as primary outcome; higher contributions typically map to higher efficiency, except when offset by punishment costs (e.g., Masclet et al., 2003; Bochet & Putterman, 2009).
- **Punishment frequency/assigned, anti/prosocial punishment rates**: Used to explain mechanisms for efficiency changes; high punishment may offset efficiency gains from increased cooperation.
- **Norm compliance, withdrawal, ostracism, retaliation, feuds**: Often analyzed for mechanistic understanding, occasionally key to efficiency outcomes in field or complex settings.

**Other**:
- **Institutional choice, selection, or support for enforcement**: Sometimes the ability to vote or select punishment schemes is an outcome itself; these influence downstream efficiency via mediation.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Empirical Patterns**:
- **Canonical PGGs (Lab, Small Groups, 10-30 Rounds, Continuous Contributions):**
  - **Enabling peer punishment increases efficiency relative to no-punishment baseline**, though typically the efficiency gain is *substantially smaller* than the gain in contributions because punishment is costly (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Dickinson et al., 2015; Masclet et al., 2003; Fudenberg & Pathak, 2010).
  - Magnitude: Typical gains are *modest but positive* (often 5–20% over control efficiency); initial earnings may be lower with punishment (due to early overuse), but as cooperation rises and punishment use declines, efficiency increases (Masclet et al., 2003; Fehr & Gächter, 2000).
  - **Effective targeting and absence of antisocial punishment are crucial:** Efficiency gains are highest when punishment is restricted/prosocial (targeting free riders) and lowest or negative when punishment is antisocial, indiscriminate, or dominated by feuds (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Ertan et al., 2009; Nikiforakis, 2008).
  - **Punishment costs matter**: High punishment costs reduce or erase efficiency gains (Masclet et al., 2003; Sutter et al., 2010).

- **Institutional and Design Moderators**:
  - **Endogenous/voted punishment schemes** (i.e., when groups choose punishment rules) consistently yield higher efficiency than exogenously imposed regimes (Putterman et al., 2011; Sutter et al., 2010; Ertan et al., 2009).
  - **Centralized punishment (delegated, leader-led) can outperform peer punishment** if it is cheap and well targeted (Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Markussen et al., 2014; Kube et al., 2015).
  - **Democratic (majority rule) punishment institutions** have higher efficiency due to lower antisocial punishment (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).

- **Contextual Moderators—Bounded Positive Effects**:
  - **Efficiency gains are context-dependent and not guaranteed**. In some settings, **punishment reduces or does not improve efficiency** due to:
    - High prevalence of antisocial punishment (e.g., Russia, rural India): efficiency falls below control (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Fehr et al., 2008).
    - Poor targeting (misdirected at cooperators): costs outweigh cooperation gains (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).
    - Normative conflict/asymmetry/heterogeneity in group composition: efficiency gains from punishment are absent or negative when returns are asymmetric or norms conflict (Nikiforakis et al., 2012; Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).
    - Imperfect or noisy monitoring: punishment is less effective or even harmful for efficiency if information about contributions is poor (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Nicklisch et al., 2016).
    - Retaliation/counter-punishment: efficiency falls or is not improved when counter-punishment is possible (Nikiforakis, 2008).

- **Field and CPR Variants**:
  - Externally imposed or reward-based institutions can yield *similar or higher efficiency* compared to peer punishment; in some communities, imposed sanctions crowd out cooperation and reduce efficiency relative to control (Vollan, 2008; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019).
  - The **effect of punishment on efficiency is shaped by community trust, support for intervention, and compatibility with local norms** (Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011; Vollan et al., 2013).

- **Reward, Communication, and Complementary Mechanisms**:
  - **Rewards**: Often produce larger or more immediate efficiency gains than punishment (Sutter et al., 2010; Dickinson et al., 2015), especially at high leverage.
  - **Communication (especially promises)**: Can substitute for punishment, amplifying or sometimes exceeding punishment’s efficiency effect (Bochet & Putterman, 2009).
  - **Combination effects**: Punishment plus reward, or punishment plus credible communication, can yield the highest efficiency (Andreoni et al., 2003; Bochet & Putterman, 2009).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**From this literature, the following principles should guide prediction of average efficiency with punishment enabled, conditional on control efficiency and design dimensions:**

- **In standard, small-group, lab PGGs with full information and continuous contributions**:  
  - **Treatment efficiency is likely to be higher than control efficiency** (with the gain often in the range of 5–20 percentage points or ratios), but the magnitude depends critically on punishment cost, its effectiveness, and targeting.
  - **Efficiency gains from punishment are almost always less than the gains in contribution rates**; overuse and cost of punishment erode some or all of the welfare benefit.
  - **If control efficiency is already high (e.g., >85%)**, enabling punishment typically yields *little or no additional gain*, and may reduce efficiency if cooperation is achieved at the cost of heavy punishment use (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).

- **Predicted efficiency gains shrink or reverse when:**
  - **Antisocial or misdirected punishment is common** (e.g., in contexts with low social trust, high conflict, or lack of shared norms).
  - **Monitoring is noisy, the punishment technology is weak/expensive, or punishment is easily retaliated**.
  - **Institutions are imposed without group support or are culturally misaligned**.

- **Game design dimensions most strongly associated with greater efficiency gains from punishment**:
  - Low punishment cost/high effectiveness (e.g., 1 to 3 or 4 impact per point spent).
  - Clear, observable feedback on individual contributions.
  - Ability to target punishments only at low contributors/free riders.
  - Endogenous (democratically chosen or voted) punishment regimes.

- **Control efficiency provides a strong baseline**, but additional prediction accuracy requires careful adjustment for the above dimensions. That is, for a new design, do not simply add a fixed bonus to control efficiency; consider the interaction with punishment cost/effectiveness, group composition, information conditions, and institution selection.

- **Field and CPR environments**:  
  - Greater heterogeneity and context dependence means uncertainty is higher.
  - External/civic authority or strong local support for punishment increases efficiency; externally imposed sanctions in high-trust communities can crowd out cooperation and reduce efficiency.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions**:
- `player_count` (group size): Consistently reported; efficiency effects of punishment are robust in small groups, and mitigation or reversal in larger or more heterogeneous groups (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Page et al., 2005).
- `num_rounds`: Well studied; effects of punishment on efficiency can change over repeated rounds (declining overuse, delayed gains).
- `mpcr`: Strongly informed; lower MPCR tends to increase the value/effect of punishment, higher MPCR can reduce or eliminate need for punishment (Kosfeld et al., 2009).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Central to all main estimates of efficiency.
- `all_or_nothing`: Both binary and continuous settings studied; all-or-nothing (binary) can constrain or amplify punishment effects.
- `chat` (communication): Directly tested; can substitute for or enhance punishment effects.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Multiple head-to-head studies comparing reward and punishment, with direct efficiency outcomes.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Feedback structure and visibility of peer contributions/outcomes are regularly manipulated or reported.

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed Dimensions**:
- `default_contrib`: Opt-in/opt-out rarely a focus, but some mention in contextual framing (Messer & Zarghamee, 2007).
- `show_punishment_id`: Anonymity of punishment is varied/occasionally studied (e.g., impact on antisocial punishment is sometimes discussed).
- `punishment_tech` beyond basic cost/effectiveness (i.e., specific targeting, leverage, retaliatory potential): sometimes only discussed contextually.
  
**Dimensions sparsely or rarely informed**:
- The **interaction** between default contribution framing and punishment/reward regimes is rare.
- The impact of feedback on the identity of punishers (`show_punishment_id`) is less frequently tested/quantified.
- Some parameterizations (e.g., all 14-game dimensions combined) are rare in single studies; most studies report on a subset.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes**: Many studies use contributions as their main outcome and do not always report efficiency or group payoff. Care is needed in translating behavioral findings to efficiency predictions, especially if punishment is used indiscriminately.
- **Lab vs. Field and Contextual Transferability**: Lab-based PGG findings (e.g., moderate group size, fixed rounds, anonymous matching) generalize less well to field, CPR, or complex institutional environments where local trust, baseline norms, and cultural attitudes to punishment can dominate (Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015).
- **Heterogeneity in social structure and culture**: Predictive power is reduced where there is high antisocial punishment, norm conflict, or lack of group support for punishment. Effect direction may be reversed.
- **Design gaps**: Some relevant design features (e.g., the interplay between punishment, identity, and feedback; opt-in/opt-out contribution framing) are underexplored.
- **Reporting gaps**: Even when efficiency is affected, studies often report only contributions, making quantitative mapping less precise.
- **Edge-case findings**: In some settings, enabling punishment moves efficiency downward (e.g., where feuds/counter-punishment dominate, or in one-shot contexts plagued by antisocial punishment).
- **Parameter Dependency**: Efficiency gains from punishment depend on achieving sufficiently high cooperation to offset punishment costs; if this fails, punishment can lower welfare.

---

**In summary**: For standard lab PGGs, the addition of peer punishment generally increases efficiency over the no-punishment baseline, conditional on effective targeting and moderate costs, but the gain is often less than is suggested by contribution rates alone. Numerous design dimensions—including punishment cost/effectiveness, group size/composition, monitoring fidelity, and institutional choice—modulate both the likelihood and size of efficiency improvements. These lessons transfer with diminishing precision to more heterogeneous, field-like, or CPR settings, where crowding out, antisocial punishment, and cultural factors can erase or reverse the efficiency gains. For prediction, control-group efficiency is a critical anchor, but fine-tuning must attend to the detailed game design and context, as indicated by the high-quality empirical evidence synthesized above.
