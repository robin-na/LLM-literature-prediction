# 1) Evidence Base

The set comprises 35 papers, spanning both empirical (laboratory/experimental) and theoretical studies. Empirical contributions, particularly lab experiments, are well-represented for public goods games (PGGs) and close variants, often reporting on efficiency or payoff outcomes (e.g., Kamei, 2019; DeAngelo & Gee, 2020; Abbink et al., 2020). Theoretical papers frequently present formal models or simulations mapping design dimensions to efficiency payoffs under various punishment mechanisms (e.g., Dutta et al., 2021; Wang & Lv, 2019; Fang et al., 2020). 

The paper set is broad in conceptual coverage, including: 
- canonical PGGs and several variants (centralized punishment, corrupt enforcers, networked settings, etc.),
- punishment and sanctioning implemented in diverse ways (peer, centralized, exclusion, monetary, reputation-based, etc.),
- alternative cooperation-promoting mechanisms (reward, role models, minimum contributions, information diffusion).

However, direct empirical evidence mapping treatment efficiency outcomes to all specified game design dimensions is sparser. Several key contributions (including heavily-cited canonical lab experiments and well-calibrated theory) directly address the efficiency impact of peer punishment in PGGs, but coverage thins markedly for some design dimensions (such as chat, visibility parameters, and mixed reward-punishment systems).

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**  
- `exact`: Many studies (e.g., Kamei, 2019; Dutta et al., 2021; Wang & Lv, 2019; DeAngelo & Gee, 2020) implement canonical linear or step-level PGGs, providing outcomes directly relevant to standard PGG design.
- `close`: A significant minority focus on CPR games, exclusion models, or structurally similar social dilemmas (Abbink et al., 2020; Brent et al., 2020; Shreedhar et al., 2020).
- `adjacent/weak`: Some papers analyze division of labor, Prisoner's Dilemma, reward- or reputation-based mechanisms, or formation/secession in cooperative networks rather than PGGs (Nakamaru et al., 2018; Raub et al., 2019).

**Punishment or Sanctions:**  
- `exact`: Many studies explicitly manipulate punishment existence, cost, magnitude, or structure—both experimentally and theoretically (Kamei, 2019; Dutta et al., 2021; Fang et al., 2020; DeAngelo & Gee, 2020). Both peer and centralized forms recur frequently.
- `adjacent`: Others study reward mechanisms, behavioral responses to unfairness, or only discuss punishment as theoretical context (e.g., reward-focused or minimum-contribution studies).

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**  
- `exact/close`: Core PGG and CPR experiments and models often report group payoff, welfare, or normalized efficiency as primary outcomes. Some importantly only infer efficiency indirectly from public good provision or cooperation rates (Chang et al., 2018; DeAngelo & Gee, 2020).
- `adjacent/weak`: Several studies focus exclusively on non-payoff outcomes—contribution/cooperation rates or punishment frequency—though some imply effects on payoff (Fraser & Nettle, 2020; Akdeniz & van Veelen, 2021).

**Summary:**  
- The evidence base is strongest for canonical PGGs, explicit punishment interventions, and efficiency/payoff outcomes, though with some gaps for edge-case dimensions and mixed-in studies not reporting efficiency directly.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Based Outcomes (Directly relevant):**
- *Efficiency*: Group earnings normalized to the cooperative optimum (Kamei, 2019; Dutta et al., 2021; Fang et al., 2020; Wang & Lv, 2019).
- *Total group payoff/welfare*: Raw or normalized earnings by group (Abbink et al., 2020; Brent et al., 2020; Shreedhar et al., 2020).
- *Surplus, net welfare*: Especially in studies with costly punishment or redistribution (Brent et al., 2020).
- *Average individual payoffs*: Sometimes aggregated or compared by treatment (Chang et al., 2018; Szekely et al., 2020).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Less directly relevant):**
- *Contribution rate, public good provision, cooperation rate*: Used frequently as proxies for group welfare but do not account for costs incurred via punishment (Fraser & Nettle, 2020; Chen et al., 2018; Akdeniz & van Veelen, 2021).
- *Frequency/amount of punishment assigned*: Interpreted as behavioral response but not itself efficiency (Chang et al., 2018; Dorrough et al., 2021).
- *Punishment/counter-punishment cycles*: Theoretical mechanisms or drivers of outcome variation (Barrett, 2020).

**Distinction:**  
Many core studies directly report or model efficiency or group payoff; some, however, only report contribution rates or frequencies, which can diverge from actual efficiency (e.g., costly punishment may raise contribution but lower net payoff).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Aggregate Effects:**
- **Canonical finding:** Enabling peer punishment in standard PGGs increases efficiency compared to the no-punishment control (Kamei, 2019; Dutta et al., 2021; Wang & Lv, 2019). The effect is dependent on punishment being sufficiently effective (punished loss > punisher's cost).
- **Structural moderation:** The net efficiency gain may be negated—or reversed—if punishment is excessively costly, mis-targeted (e.g., corrupt enforcers), or global monitoring is imperfect (Fang et al., 2020; Shreedhar et al., 2020; Abbink et al., 2020).
- **Non-monotonicity and structure:** Punishment that is too weak produces little effect; too strong can spawn counter-punishment, aggression, or excessive punishment spending, reducing net efficiency (Szekely et al., 2020; Chang & Zhang, 2021).

**Design Dimension Moderators (empirical/theoretical):**
- **Player count:** Main studies primarily use small groups (2-4 players); efficiency gains from punishment are best established in these ranges (Kamei, 2019; Dutta et al., 2021; Wang & Lv, 2019).
- **Punishment cost and tech:** Lower cost or higher impact per cost robustly predicts greater efficiency gains (Wang & Lv, 2019; Fang et al., 2020; Barrett, 2020).
- **Network structure:** Sparse/incomplete punishment networks may paradoxically increase efficiency by limiting wasted punishment (Shreedhar et al., 2020).
- **Monitoring regime:** Exogenous (automatic) monitoring sustains high efficiency with punishment; endogenous (voluntary consensus) monitoring may result in ineffective or underutilized punishment, erasing supposed efficiency gains (DeAngelo & Gee, 2020).
- **Corruption and incentives of punishers:** When punishers can extract bribes or are themselves the main beneficiaries, efficiency gains shrink or reverse (Abbink et al., 2020; Fang et al., 2020).
- **Communication (chat):** Enables coordination and joint decision-making that can substitute for or complement punishment in sustaining efficiency (Kamei, 2019).
- **Matching protocol (partner vs. stranger):** Punishment and efficiency are more robust under fixed pairs/partner matching (Kamei, 2019).

**Reward mechanisms:** Evidence for rewards or hybrid systems is sparse in this set, but reward can also raise efficiency, and effects may interact with punishment design (Ohdaira, 2021; Yang et al., 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Canonical PGGs:** For typical PGGs (2–4 players, 10–20 rounds, no chat, moderate MPCR, punishment of 1.5–3x per unit cost), enabling peer punishment predicts a substantial, robust increase in efficiency, often bringing efficiency close to full cooperation levels—conditional on punishment not being prohibitively costly (Kamei, 2019; Dutta et al., 2021).
- **Quantitative anchors:** Theory calibrated to classic experiments (Fehr & Gächter, 2000) finds average efficiency jumps from sub-optimal levels to near cooperative maximum with punishment enabled (Dutta et al., 2021), but only after a few rounds for learning.
- **Critical moderators:** The efficiency gain from punishment is dampened or reversed if:
    - Punishment cost is high, impact is low, or punishment is mis-targeted (Wang & Lv, 2019; Fang et al., 2020; Szekely et al., 2020).
    - The monitoring regime renders punishment unenforced or uncoordinated (DeAngelo & Gee, 2020).
    - Punishment is centralized and open to corruption/bribery; in such cases, group efficiency gains are present but mostly offset by redistributive losses (Abbink et al., 2020).
    - All-or-nothing, limited-information, or non-anonymous setups can mediate or moderate the efficiency effect, though most studies use continuous actions and anonymity as the baseline.
- **Indirect/weak evidence:** The efficiency effect is often inferred from increased cooperation/contribution, but where costly punishment is frequent but unproductive, net payoff may fall even as cooperation rises (Shreedhar et al., 2020).
- **When evidence is sparse or ambiguous:** For games employing dimensions less covered in the literature (e.g., large groups, reward plus punishment, minimum contribution levels, extensive chat, transparency features), confidence in the predicted efficiency effect from punishment should be moderated and uncertainty noted.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions (substantial empirical or theoretical evidence):**
- `player_count` (mostly 2–4): Directly manipulated in PGG lab and theory.
- `num_rounds`: Lab studies and theory (10–20 rounds common).
- `mpcr`: Widely varied across studies.
- `punishment_cost, punishment_tech` (magnitude): Both key foci, especially as moderators of efficiency effect.
- `all_or_nothing`: Present in both theoretical and experimental work.
- `chat`: Sometimes manipulated, with strong consensus on communication’s positive impact (Kamei, 2019).
- `reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech`: Only a handful of theory papers and indirect evidence (Ohdaira, 2021); relatively sparse for efficiency outcomes.

**Indirectly Informed Dimensions:**
- `matching protocol` (partner vs. stranger): Central in Kamei (2019); partner matching more robust for efficiency.
- `monitoring regime/structure`: Group vs. peer vs. centralized; exogenous vs. endogenous (DeAngelo & Gee, 2020; Shreedhar et al., 2020; Chang et al., 2018).
- `corruption, bribe availability`: Addressed in theoretical and lab contexts (Abbink et al., 2020; Fang et al., 2020).

**Contextually Discussed or Sparse:**
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Seldom directly manipulated; only a few models (Barrett, 2020; Deb et al., 2019) and adjacent experiments mention information/visibility parameters.
- `default_contrib`: Framing (opt-in vs. opt-out) occasionally mentioned but not systematically studied for efficiency with punishment.
- `reward-related dimensions` (reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech): Reward as a mechanism is primarily addressed in context or in hybrid models, not empirically in PGGs for efficiency.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency Not Always Reported:** Several studies substitute contribution/cooperation rates or public good provision for efficiency—even though high contribution under costly punishment can reduce net group payoff (Fraser & Nettle, 2020; Szekely et al., 2020).
- **Parameter Range and Design Gaps:** Most direct evidence is for small groups (2–4), moderate rounds (10–20), and typical punishment parameters. Little data are available for large groups, longer or highly variable rounds, or unusual parameter combinations on predicted efficiency under punishment.
- **Sparse Evidence for Information and Transparency Dimensions:** Few studies manipulate or report on game features like punisher/rewarder transparency, full feedback, or dynamic displays of round number.
- **Centralized and Corrupt Institutions:** Peer punishment is well-studied; centralized, especially corrupt, institutions demonstrate sharply reduced or even negative net efficiency gains from punishment compared to the classic peer punishment effect.
- **Contextual/Mechanism-General Evidence:** Many theoretical results generalize mechanism effectiveness (punishment, reward, reputation, institutional formation) but are only transferable by analogy to specific PGG designs with punishment enabled.
- **Ambiguity on Long-Term/Repeated Interactions:** Some simulation and theoretical studies find potential for negative short-term efficiency effects but positive effects in much longer or infinite-horizon repetitions—a pattern that may not translate to typical lab study durations.
- **Heterogeneity and Population Structure:** Empirical studies commonly use homogeneous groups and well-mixed (non-network) settings. Network structure, asymmetry, or spatial/prior relationship effects on efficiency under punishment are underexplored.
- **Reward Mechanisms:** Few studies directly pit punishment versus reward or hybrid regimes for efficiency outcomes.
- **No Coverage of Some Dimensions:** For several prediction dimensions (default contribution framing, show_punishment_id, reward-related parameters), either only contextual discussion or no evidence is provided regarding their effect on efficiency.

---

**Conclusion**:  
The literature base provides robust direct and theoretical evidence that, in standard PGGs and close variants, enabling peer punishment often produces a substantial and predictable improvement in efficiency, provided the punishment is not overly costly and is well-targeted. Critical moderating dimensions—especially punishment cost/magnitude, group size, monitoring regime, and presence of corruption—are well-supported by both empirical and theoretical studies. However, prediction confidence decreases outside the most-studied parameter regimes, and for several design dimensions, evidence is sparse or only indirectly available. Outcomes measured sometimes focus on non-payoff behaviors, so care must be taken to avoid over-interpreting proxy measures as direct indicators of efficiency.
