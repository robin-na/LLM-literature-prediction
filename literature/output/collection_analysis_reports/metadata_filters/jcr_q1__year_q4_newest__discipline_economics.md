# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists predominantly of empirical (mainly lab and field experiment) studies (about 30) supplemented by a few theory and simulation/modeling papers. The empirical papers primarily focus on variants of public goods games (PGGs) or adjacent environments such as common-pool resource (CPR) games, trust games, repeated prisoners’ dilemmas, and hybrid designs. A minority are observational or survey-based. The scope is moderate to broad in terms of experimental manipulations, but narrower with respect to directly reporting the downstream prediction target: namely, the average efficiency (total group payoff as a proportion of the social optimum) as affected by enabling peer punishment.

Most papers providing exact outcome relevance do so in the context of PGG or close variants, with payoff outcomes (efficiency, group earnings, welfare), but a significant portion only analyzes non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates, cooperation, punishment frequency). Several theory papers elaborate on mechanism design, thresholds, and comparative statics but without new empirical data.

Collectively, the evidence base is robust for understanding the qualitative effect of punishment in PGG-like environments. However, direct, quantitative efficiency comparisons (control vs. punishment) across the full range of 14 prediction task design dimensions are rare. Detailed mapping of dimension-specific effects is strong for punishment cost/effectiveness, group size, and enforcement credibility, and weaker for reward mechanisms, chat, default contribution, or summary information dimensions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- Relevance is high: Most papers use standard linear PGGs or close CPR analogs (including with continuous or all-or-nothing contributions), group sizes from 3–5, and repeated rounds.
- Some studies extend to multilevel PGGs, trust games, and hybrid designs. A handful are more distant (repeated PDs, discrete allocation, or real-world analogs).
- Label: *exact* for most; *close* for CPR/trust games; *adjacent*–*none* for reference/editorial or non-group tasks.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- Many studies directly manipulate costly peer or centralized punishment, occasionally with variants (leader punishment, third-party sanctions, rank-based penalties).
- Some examine only punishment-like or reputational mechanisms, social pressure, or completely lack punishment.
- Label: *exact* or *close* for most; *adjacent* or *none* for others.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- A subset measure *efficiency* or *group payoff* directly, but many analyze only contributions/cooperation, or report earnings only incidentally.
- About half provide direct efficiency data or very clear mapping (e.g., extraction/welfare in CPR).
- Label: *exact* where efficiency or total group payoff is reported; *adjacent* where implied (via contribution, extraction, or trust game returns); *weak* where only behavioral outcomes are available.

*In sum*: The set is highly relevant on pgg_or_variant and punishment; somewhat less so on direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes—the precise target for prediction.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (relevant to prediction):**
- Group efficiency: total payoff as a ratio of the group optimum (reported or can be calculated in several papers).
- Group earnings, total coins generated, average payoff per player (often available in PGGs and CPR analogs).
- Welfare or surplus (especially in CPR variants with extraction).

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (informative, but not directly usable for prediction):**
- Contribution/cooperation rates: how much individuals contribute to the group account.
- Punishment frequency/intensity: amount and pattern of sanctions assigned.
- Compliance rates, norm conformity, and restoration/settlement frequency in related economic games.

*Notably*, several papers report only behavioral outcomes, even when group payoff could in principle be reconstructed. Explicit caution is warranted: positive effects of punishment on contributions do not always translate to higher efficiency due to costs associated with punishment itself (e.g., Nhim et al., 2023; Praxmarer et al., 2024).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical findings:**

- **Punishment tends to increase efficiency, but only under specific conditions**:
  - In standard PGGs, enabling peer or centralized punishment usually increases group efficiency/payoff (Jiang & Wang, 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Kamei et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2022).
  - The efficiency gain from punishment depends on parameters: low punishment cost and high effectiveness amplify gains; if punishment is too costly or misdirected, efficiency may stagnate or decline (Nhim et al., 2023; Praxmarer et al., 2024).

- **Institutional details strongly moderate the effect**:
  - Leader punishment increases efficiency more when leaders are female, role-framed as responsible, and selected by voting (Jiang & Wang, 2024).
  - Third-party or hierarchical institutions (Fourth Party, Competition models) can outperform unrestricted peer punishment by reducing unnecessary or antisocial punishment (Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Zong et al., 2025).
  - Credible enforcement is essential: when the probability of punishment is low or its cost is high, gains in cooperation do **not** translate into efficiency (Alt et al., 2023; Nhim et al., 2023).

- **Punishment raises cooperation, but not always efficiency**:
  - Many studies show increases in contribution/cooperation, but if punishment is frequent, costly, or antisocial, group payoff/efficiency can remain flat or even be reduced (Nhim et al., 2023; Praxmarer et al., 2024; Angelsen & Naime, 2024).

- **Context and individual differences matter**:
  - Cultural background, group experience, order effects (e.g., single-group vs. multilevel), and even age/generation all moderate effects (Zhou et al., 2023; Otten et al., 2024; Praxmarer et al., 2024).

**Theoretical mechanisms:**

- Enabling punishment increases the enforceable set of efficient outcomes, but only if sanction strength overcomes incentive to defect (Libois, 2022; Marco & Goetz, 2024; Pei, 2024).
- Excessive sanctioning cost or institutional brittleness can dampen or reverse gains (Libois, 2022).

*Disagreements and ambiguities*:  
- Some environments (e.g., voluntary, opt-in punishment, or those allowing avoidance) show minimal or no efficiency gains from punishment (Del Ponte et al., 2025), suggesting that the mapping from cooperation gain to payoff gain is not automatic.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Punishment generally increases efficiency in standard PGGs—if punishment is credible, properly targeted, and not overly expensive.**
  - Use control-game efficiency as a strong lower bound: the treatment with enabled punishment is very likely to be more efficient, unless high punishment cost or antisocial punishment undermines gains (Jiang & Wang, 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Kamei et al., 2023).
  - Where possible, institutions moderating who may punish and how (competition, leader selection, third-party mechanisms) can further increase efficiency (Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Zong et al., 2025).
  - If punishment cost is high relative to benefit, or antisocial punishment is common, efficiency improvements are muted or reversed (Nhim et al., 2023; Praxmarer et al., 2024; Angelsen & Naime, 2024).
  - If punishment is voluntary and easily avoided, or if punishable actions can be opt-out, efficiency does **not** improve (Del Ponte et al., 2025).

- **Dimension-specific guidance**:
  - **Player count and group size**: Negative for larger groups unless punishment is institutionally reinforced (Libois, 2022).
  - **Punishment cost/tech**: Lower cost and higher impact increase efficacy (Jiang & Wang, 2024; Nhim et al., 2023; Libois, 2022).
  - **Information structure**: Allowing summary feedback or identification of punishers can moderate effect, as can enabling chat (Kamei et al., 2023; Krügel & Maaser, 2025).
  - **Order and context**: Prior game type experience can switch punishment's effect from positive to negligible/negative in multilevel variants (Otten et al., 2024).

- **If control efficiency is already high (due to strong norms or informal enforcement), incremental gains from enabling punishment may be limited**.

- **Use caution when importing effects from non-PGG or only contribution-focused studies**—many report only behavioral, not efficiency, effects.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (supported by strong efficiency or payoff evidence):**
- `player_count` (empirical and theory papers; Jiang & Wang, 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Libois, 2022)
- `num_rounds` (common 5–20; majority lab studies)
- `mpcr` (almost universal in lab designs)
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech` (many studies)
- `all_or_nothing` (both types present)
- `show_n_rounds` (some explicit)
- `show_other_summaries` (limited but present)
- `chat` (some studies vary this; Kamei et al., 2023, Muñoz-Herrera & Reuben, 2023)

**Indirectly informed:**
- `default_contrib` (examples with opt-in/out: Del Ponte et al., 2025; Boulu-Reshef & Schulhofer-Wohl, 2022)
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (considered in a few trust/third-party variants; Zong et al., 2025)
- `show_punishment_id` (only contextually discussed; Brouwer et al., 2023)

**Effectively missing or under-explored:**
- The specific impact of detailed framing of `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id`, and how showing other summaries or rounds is manipulated. Few studies systematically test their quantitative effect on efficiency under punishment.
- Complex interaction terms (e.g., reward + punishment, chat + punishment, or summary feedback nuances) are rarely jointly manipulated.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency as outcome is underreported**: Many studies, even those highly relevant on design, report only contribution or cooperation, leaving the net effect of punishment costs ambiguous.
- **Parameter coverage**: Coverage is strong for standard punishment dimensions (cost, player count, effectiveness), but sparse for many prediction dimensions (e.g., default contribution, reward schemes, detailed info feedback).
- **Generality and external validity**: Most studies are lab-based, with modest group sizes, student samples, and short durations. Real-world environments may differ, especially with respect to norm internalization and informal sanctions (Schippers & Soetevent, 2024).
- **Context and moderator effects**: Cultural, institutional, and demographic moderators can reverse the sign or size of effectiveness (Zhou et al., 2023; Angelsen & Naime, 2024; Praxmarer et al., 2024).
- **Punishment mechanism heterogeneity**: Effects differ among direct peer punishment, leader/authority-based, third-party, and automatic penalty schemes—these may not be interchangeable.
- **Potential for negative or zero gains**: Efficiency can decline or stagnate when punishment is antisocial, costly, voluntary but avoidable, or misaligned with group welfare (Praxmarer et al., 2024; Del Ponte et al., 2025).
- **Order and learning effects**: Exposure to specific game types or repeated play can dramatically moderate the impact of punishment (Otten et al., 2024).
- **Lack of systematic reward-punishment interaction studies**: Most focus on punishment alone or only mention reward schemes in passing.
