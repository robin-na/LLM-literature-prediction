# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set of 41 studies is broad in scope but contains a substantial concentration of empirical laboratory experiments directly manipulating punishment in linear public goods games (PGG) with continuous or all-or-nothing contributions. Most of the highly relevant studies use controlled, incentivized lab experiments comparing efficiency (total group payoff relative to full cooperation) between punishment-enabled and control (no-punishment) treatments, often systematically varying key design features (e.g., punishment cost, monitoring, information). The set also includes several adjacent or weakly related studies in trust games, inspection games, coalition formation, common pool resource (CPR) environments, and observational/cross-cultural contexts. Theoretical and mechanism-focused papers are sparse; almost all studies are empirical and experimental, with a minority using field or observational methods.

Overall, the evidence base is strongest for standard lab PGGs with peer punishment or sanctioning, moderately informative for certain close variants, and weaker (or lacking) for non-PGG environments and for indirect or reputational sanctioning. Many papers report efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes, but a notable fraction focus primarily on contribution/cooperation rates, behavioral responses, or perceptions without reporting group efficiency.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:
- **exact:** The core set of papers focuses directly on linear PGGs or very close variants (e.g., voluntary contribution mechanisms, star networks), meeting the exact relevance criterion (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Nikiforakis, 2010; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Tyran & Feld, 2006).
- **close/adjacent/weak:** Several papers use CPR, trust, coalition selection, inspection, or step-level games—strategically similar but not identical to PGG. These provide supporting, but less directly transferable, evidence (e.g., Akpalu & Martinsson, 2012; McEvoy, 2012; Engle-Warnick & Slonim, 2006; Bohnet & Baytelman, 2007).
- **none:** A small number of observational or theoretical studies are only contextually related or not relevant for specific PGGs.

**punishment_or_sanctions**:
- **exact:** A large subset of experiments explicitly introduce, vary, or compare costly peer punishment or formal sanctioning as an on/off treatment, targeting free-riding or norm violations (Kube & Traxler, 2011; Nikiforakis, 2010; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
- **close:** Papers modeling ostracism, coalition exclusion, inspection, partner switching, or indirect sanctions (reputation, feedback) often discuss punishment in a broader sense but with potential differences in implementation and psychological impact.
- **adjacent/weak/none:** A subset of papers discuss reputation or institutional structure without enabling explicit punishment.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
- **exact:** Most relevant PGG studies report group efficiency, total earnings, or welfare, often as a share of the socially optimal total (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
- **close:** Some studies report outcomes (total contribution, reduction in over-use) that are highly correlated with efficiency but do not directly provide payoff metrics, or only report behavioral change (e.g., cooperation rates).
- **adjacent/weak/none:** Several studies report only behavioral outcomes (punishment frequency, norm compliance, ostracism, partner choice), limiting their utility for efficiency prediction.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (reported in many core PGG studies):
    - **Group efficiency:** Often specified as mean group payoff divided by maximal possible payoff (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
    - **Welfare/total surplus:** In some cases directly compared across treatments.
    - **Total earnings/group payoff:** Sometimes presented as net of all costs (including punishment costs), key for efficiency assessment.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (prevalent in many studies, sometimes as proxies for efficiency):
    - **Contribution/cooperation rates:** Increase with punishment but can diverge from efficiency if punishment is costly.
    - **Punishment frequency/amount:** Used as an intermediate or mediating variable; important for understanding net efficiency impacts.
    - **Norm compliance, counterpunishment, retaliation, behavioral perceptions, trust/trustworthiness.**
    - **Voting/ostracism/coalition formation:** Influence behavior but usually not directly efficiency.
   
Several studies caution that higher contributions (with punishment present) do not always translate to higher efficiency due to the direct resource costs of imposing and receiving punishment (Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Colombier et al., 2011). Only studies measuring net payoffs or efficiency are suitable for primary inference.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical findings from highly relevant PGG experiments:**

- **Peer punishment often leads to higher contributions and efficiency,** but only when the cost of punishment is not so high as to offset gains from increased cooperation (Kube & Traxler, 2011; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Tyran & Feld, 2006).
- **Efficiency gains from punishment are *not* automatic:** When punishment is cheap, it is used more, which can lower net efficiency due to over-use (Anderson & Putterman, 2006). Costly or unconstrained punishment can erode efficiency gains.
- **Punishment effectiveness is highly sensitive to institutional/contextual design:**
    - **Information environment (feedback):** Showing contribution feedback sustains higher efficiency than showing earnings feedback or both. Feedback format is a critical moderator (Nikiforakis, 2010).
    - **Punishment structure:** Single-stage, anonymous punishment increases efficiency; multi-stage or counterpunishment-enabled punishment can drastically reduce efficiency—even below control (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
    - **Cost and magnitude:** High punishment cost reduces usage, sustaining efficiency; low cost encourages over-use, hurting efficiency (Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
    - **Mode of introduction:** Endogenous/voted punishment regimes can dramatically improve their effectiveness compared to exogenous imposition, especially for mild sanctions (Tyran & Feld, 2006).
    - **Network structure and status effects:** Efficiency gains from punishment depend on the status of actors in networked games (Eckel et al., 2010).
- **Punishment's effect can be negative in specific designs:**
    - In stochastic-loss PGG-like games or CPRs, or when costs of punishment outweigh gains from increased cooperation, group efficiency can fall with punishment relative to control (Colombier et al., 2011; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
    - Multi-round/counterpunishment enabled structures almost always lead to efficiency loss.
- **Reward mechanisms** (when studied in parallel) can outperform punishment in efficiency, producing higher payoffs for similar or greater increases in cooperation (Colombier et al., 2011).

**Supporting findings:**
- Increasing the minimum contribution *obligation* or introducing non-punitive social mechanisms (public attribution, communication) can increase contributions, sometimes comparable to mild punishment (Galbiati & Vertova, 2008; Denant-Boemont et al., 2011), but payoff outcomes often not reported.
- Evasion/multi-defection options, perceived procedural justice, or trust in authority can moderate effectiveness (Mulder et al., 2009; De Cremer et al., 2012).

**Adjunct findings (outside or adjacent to PGG):**
- Harsh or credible punishment in repeated trust and coalition games can sustain high efficiency, but only with sufficient horizon or reputational risk (Engle-Warnick & Slonim, 2006; McCabe et al., 1998).
- Sanctions/ostracism in CPRs or coalition selection—if implementable—can substantially improve efficiency, but often with costs or destabilizing effects if not properly structured (Akpalu & Martinsson, 2012; Ahn et al., 2008).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct evidence-based implications for prediction:**
- If *control efficiency* is high, enabling peer punishment will typically have a **less positive or possibly negative impact** on efficiency *if punishment is costly, unconstrained, or susceptible to counterpunishment or retaliation* (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Colombier et al., 2011; Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
- If *control efficiency* is low (due to low baseline cooperation), introduction of punishment can produce **large efficiency gains**—*but only if punishment is well-calibrated, single-stage, and costly enough to prevent excessive use* (Kube & Traxler, 2011; Tyran & Feld, 2006; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).
- The *punishment cost* and *punishment technology* dimensions are **critical**: cheap, highly effective punishment often increases contributions while lowering efficiency; moderate to high punishment costs tend to give net efficiency gains, especially when usage is limited/targeted (Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
- **Information structure** is **a key moderator**: Feedback limited to contributions supports the best efficiency; additional feedback about earnings or punishment amounts can undermine efficiency through increased punitive cycles (Nikiforakis, 2010).
- If *counterpunishment* or *multi-stage* punishment is possible within the design, **efficiency is likely to fall** compared to both control and standard single-stage punishment designs (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
- In settings where *punishment mechanisms are implemented endogenously (voted or opt-in)* rather than imposed exogenously, **positive efficiency effects** are much stronger, even for mild punishment (Tyran & Feld, 2006).
- **Network or group structure** (e.g., central leader status, monitoring fraction, group size): Effects of punishment on efficiency can depend on network position/status and the scope for mutual monitoring (Eckel et al., 2010; Carpenter, 2007).
- *Reward mechanisms,* if enabled, have consistently more positive effects on efficiency than punishment alone (Colombier et al., 2011).
- *Non-standard player matching,* group formation, or ostracism mechanisms should be treated cautiously; effects are design-contingent and cannot be assumed to generalize to normal peer-punishment PGG (Ahn et al., 2008).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions (with multiple high-relevance, empirical studies):**
- `player_count`: Addressed in almost all core studies; group size commonly varied (e.g., 3, 4, 5, 10).
- `num_rounds`: Both one-shot and repeated games well-represented; number of rounds directly moderates punishment effectiveness (dynamic effects, end-game unraveling).
- `mpcr`: Varied and reported; higher MPCRs often support more cooperation/efficiency (but with design- and context-specific interactions).
- `punishment_cost`: Strongly emphasized; cost per unit is a primary moderator of both usage and net efficiency.
- `punishment_tech`: Variants covered (single vs. multi-stage, anonymous vs. identified, severity, voting/ostracism).
- `all_or_nothing`: Addressed in many studies; most use continuous contributions but some use binary/all-or-nothing (not a major moderator except for step-level games).
- `chat`: Explicitly disabled in most; some mention effects of communication but systematic variation is rare.
- `show_other_summaries`: Feedback format with direct, strong empirical support for importance (Nikiforakis, 2010).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Reward mechanisms are occasionally enabled in contrast to punishment and found to be more effective for efficiency (Colombier et al., 2011).

**Indirectly informed or less commonly manipulated:**
- `default_contrib`: Framing (opt-in vs. opt-out) not widely varied or reported, except sometimes in instructions.
- `show_n_rounds`: Almost always specified; some papers test known vs. unknown horizon (limited impact unless indefinite horizon).
- `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes manipulated (anonymity/voting), with effects on retaliation and costs, but less systematically explored.

**Contextual/rarely discussed or missing:**
- Feedback on `show_other_summaries` (contents and degree of detail) is highlighted in a few key papers, but not all specify the exact format.
- `default_contrib`, except in design notes, is mostly missing.
- Dimensions such as player heterogeneity, network structure (beyond star networks), and external context (e.g., culture, trust) are sometimes mentioned but not tied empirically to efficiency effects of punishment.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Coverage Gaps for Some Design Dimensions:** A few design features (default contribution, precise framing, detailed reward tech, identity display) are sparsely covered or only contextually addressed; most core empirical evidence clusters on classic design variations (group size, punishment cost, round number, MPCR).
- **Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes:** Many studies report contribution/cooperation rates rather than net efficiency; translating behavioral outcomes to payoff impacts can be misleading when punishment costs are substantial.
- **Generalizability Beyond Lab Settings:** Most studies use university-student samples in artificial lab settings; effects may differ in field, high-stakes, or heterogeneous groups, or with real-world complexities.
- **Ambiguity and Mixed Results:** Efficiency effects are not universally positive; several high-quality studies show that punishment can reduce efficiency if mechanisms are unconstrained, multi-stage, or lead to excessive counterpunishment.
- **Multidimensional Moderators and Interactions:** Many critical moderators (information structure, endogenous vs. exogenous rules, network status, monitoring/fraction, procedural justice) can interact in complex ways not fully mapped by this literature.
- **Limited Evidence for Indirect/Alternative Sanctions:** Reputation, reputational damage, coalition selection, and ostracism mechanisms are explored, but their direct efficiency impacts are less documented than those for peer punishment (with some exceptions in CPRs).
- **Adjacent/Ecological Validity:** Adjacent studies (trust games, dyadic exchanges, indirect punishment) often confirm that punishment can increase compliance, but their mapping to group efficiency in multi-person PGGs is not always warranted.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature directly supports prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency in classic lab PGGs with peer punishment, especially regarding the moderating roles of punishment cost/structure, feedback/information, and the possibility of counterpunishment. Negative and ambiguous results highlight that enabling punishment does not universally boost efficiency—cost structure and institutional context matter greatly. Several design dimensions are well-informed (player count, cost/tech of punishment, rounds, MPCR, feedback), while information is sparse or only contextual for others. Extrapolation to unusual design combinations, real-world contexts, or indirect sanctions should be done with caution.
