# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is composed of a mix of theory-driven and empirical sources, with a marked emphasis on theoretical and simulation-based models (especially evolutionary game theory and agent-based modeling). Only two studies involve direct empirical laboratory experiments, and only one includes observational field evidence. The majority of papers are narrowly or contextually focused on features relevant to public goods games (PGGs) and their adjacent settings. While several theory papers are highly parameterized and directly model efficiency or payoff outcomes (notably in classic public goods game setups), much of the empirical and adjacent literature primarily reports on behavioral outcomes, strategic equilibria, or institutional mechanisms without direct efficiency measurement. Overall, the evidence base is moderately broad in disciplinary span (including economics, environmental governance, law, and public policy), but relatively narrow in the precision and directness of evidence for the downstream prediction task regarding treatment efficiency in PGGs with peer punishment.

# 2) Task Relevance

For the prediction task—estimating average efficiency in PGG-like games with punishment, based on game design and control efficiency—the literature shows varying degrees of relevance across three key dimensions:

**PGG or Variant**
- Most papers are either directly focused on standard PGGs or very close structural variants (e.g., trust games, common-pool resource dilemmas). About a third are *exact* matches for classic public goods games; the remainder are *close* or *adjacent*, often modeling multi-actor cooperation dilemmas or regulatory games sharing strategic features with PGGs.
  
**Punishment or Sanctions**
- About half of the papers model or analyze punishment mechanisms *exactly* (i.e., as implemented in PGG or similar social dilemmas), often with explicit cost and magnitude parameters. A few focus on *centralized* (rather than peer) punishment. Some studies are *adjacent*, considering broader sanctions (reward, monitoring, third-party punishment, or legal fines).

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**
- Direct measurement or modeling of efficiency (i.e., group payoff relative to maximum) is present in a minority of papers (*exact* in a few, *close* in others). In many papers, outcomes are reported as strategy frequencies, compliance rates, or stability of cooperative behavior, which are only *adjacent* or *indirect* proxies for efficiency.

In summary, while there is strong theoretical and indirect empirical support for the mechanisms of punishment in PGG-like games, the set is limited in precise, empirical, payoff-based outcomes corresponding *exactly* to the prediction outcome of interest.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Relevant for Prediction):**
- *Efficiency*, total group payoff, surplus, or welfare are reported or modeled explicitly in:  
  - Zhang & Cao (2020) (theoretical, exact efficiency in PGGs),  
  - Engel & Zhurakhovska (2017) (experimental, group profit/payoff; efficiency inferred),  
  - Park (2022), Baker & Choi (2018), Lim & Capraro (2022) (theoretical, efficiency in adjacent/modeled trust games or variants).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Only Indirectly Relevant):**
- Most adjacent literature, and some theory, measure:  
  - Contribution rates, cooperation or compliance rates, punishment frequency, norm adherence, or strategy probabilities.
  - Stability of cooperative equilibria, transition to compliance, and network spread of cooperative/punitive behavior.
  - Institutional or contextual determinants of the emergence of cooperation and punishment.

**Key Distinction:**  
- Only a few sources provide empirical (or simulated-theory) data directly mapping punishment to changes in efficiency or group payoff—the primary prediction outcome. The majority of the literature is limited to measuring or theorizing about non-payoff or hybrid outcomes and must be interpreted with that caveat.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### Empirical and Theoretical Evidence (Efficiency-Focused)

- **Punishment Can Increase Efficiency with Proper Parameterization:**  
  In standard PGG theory models (e.g., Zhang & Cao, 2020), efficiency increases with the introduction of *sufficiently strong* punishment: if the punishment's effectiveness (its magnitude and cost relative to group size and MPCR) clears certain thresholds, full cooperation (and thus maximal efficiency) is achieved. If not, punishment may have little or no effect, especially if alternative strategies (like insurance or opting out) are attractive (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Lim & Capraro, 2022).

- **Empirical Lab Evidence Confirms Efficiency Gains with (Centralized) Punishment:**  
  Laboratory experiments (e.g., Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017) show that introducing centralized punishment increases group payoffs above the Nash baseline (no punishment), with effects robust across role framing and variations in authority. However, efficiency is not always reported as a fraction of the full-cooperation optimum, so gains must sometimes be inferred.

- **Network and Dynamic Mechanisms Can Amplify or Modulate Punishment Effects:**  
  Theory papers on networked games (Lim & Capraro, 2022; Li et al., 2023) show that network structure can lower the threshold for punishment to induce high cooperation/efficiency. Punishment's efficacy is highest when targeted and not too costly, with diminishing returns or even negative effects at high cost or excessive magnitude. Dynamic, responsive punishment rules (vs. static ones) can also increase compliance and (by inference) efficiency (Wang & Cui, 2022; Jiang & Zheng, 2024).

- **Centralized vs. Peer Punishment:**  
  There are findings that centralized punishment (by authorities) may produce higher efficiency and is less likely to provoke counter-punishment or costly rivalry compared to peer punishment (Janssen, 2015; Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Baker & Choi, 2018). This is especially relevant in larger or more complex groups, although this may trade off with emergent trust and buy-in.

### Non-Efficiency Behavioral Outcomes

- **Punishment Predictably Increases Compliance/Cooperation:**  
  Even in adjacent or non-payoff literature, adding punishment mechanisms increases the probability of cooperation/compliance (Wang et al., 2022; Huo & Liu, 2024). However, translation from increased cooperation rate to increased efficiency can be ambiguous, especially if punishment is too costly or applied inappropriately (over-punishment or targeting errors).

- **Context and Moderators Matter:**  
  Effects of punishment are context-dependent—network structure, communication availability, group size, and social/cultural context moderate the realized efficiency gains (Janssen, 2015; Qirko, 2020; Armstrong et al., 2024).

- **Crowding Out and Diminishing Returns:**  
  Some sources note the risk of crowding out voluntary cooperation through overuse or misapplication of punishment, or where sanctioning becomes too routine or externally imposed, potentially reducing efficiency (Janssen, 2015).

### Limits and Mixed Results

- When punishment is too weak, infrequent, or costly, it may not shift outcomes away from defection; with high costs or targeting errors, efficiency gains can be offset or even reversed.
- In real-world or field settings, actual use of punishment is often more contingent and less frequent than in lab settings, reducing its effective impact on efficiency (Qirko, 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **General Guidance:**  
  Literature supports the hypothesis that enabling peer or centralized punishment in PGGs tends to *increase* group efficiency relative to games without punishment, provided the punishment is effective (not too costly, impact sufficient) and well-targeted. The magnitude of the increase depends on punishment parameters—especially cost, magnitude, and how punishment is deployed.

- **Marginal Returns and Thresholds:**  
  There is likely a *threshold* effect: if punishment is strong enough (high enough magnitude relative to cost, moderate group size, and high enough MPCR), it can induce nearly full cooperation (maximal efficiency), but gains plateau or even decline if punishment is excessive or poorly designed (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Lim & Capraro, 2022).

- **Control Efficiency Informs Treatment Effect:**  
  Where baseline (no-punishment) efficiency is already high (e.g., via communication, strong norms, or high MPCR), the *incremental effect* of enabling punishment may be smaller. Conversely, where baseline efficiency is low due to ample free riding, punishment's effect can be large if conditions for its success are met.

- **Dimension Interactions:**  
  The effect of punishment is moderated by player count (smaller groups may see more direct effects), number of rounds (more rounds generally reinforce higher efficiency under punishment), the ability to see others' actions (full information aids targeting), and network structure (structured/populated networks can amplify or reduce punishment's impact).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `all_or_nothing`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` — Frequently and explicitly modeled/theorized in core theory papers, with guidance on their interaction effects.
- `reward_exists` and `reward_cost` — Discussed where dynamic sanctioning includes both punishment and reward options (though direct efficiency consequences less consistently quantified).

**Indirectly or Contextually Discussed:**
- `chat`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id` — These are more contextually mentioned, especially regarding communication (Janssen, 2015) and the visibility of punishment/actions.
- `default_contrib` — Rarely discussed as a framing or default option; most studies assume neutral or opt-in baseline.

**Missing or Sparsely Addressed:**
- `show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries` — Only tangentially discussed in a small subset of papers (Qirko, 2020).
- Detailed `reward_tech` (technical/methodological aspects of reward) and the nuanced interplay with punishment are seldom explicitly parameterized.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical Gaps:**  
  The literature contains more theoretical than experimental payoff-based evidence; only a handful of sources report actual efficiency or group payoff outcomes empirically in standard PGGs with and without punishment.

- **Peer vs. Centralized Sanctions:**  
  Effects of peer punishment, as opposed to centralized punishment, are less empirically represented. Most experimental evidence is for centralized or authority-administered punishment, with limited evidence about peer-administered effects on group efficiency.

- **Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes:**  
  Many studies report only behavioral (compliance, cooperation, contribution) or strategy equilibrium outcomes. While these are often correlated with increased efficiency, the mapping is not always direct, especially where costly punishment can offset gains from higher contributions.

- **Generality and External Validity:**  
  Several adjacent or context-specific papers (field studies, agent-based models of real-world institutions, regulatory dilemmas) have limited external validity when extrapolated to lab-style PGGs.

- **Parameter Coverage and Sparsity:**  
  Some dimensions critical to prediction (e.g., default contribution framing, identity visibility, availability of chat) are sparsely parameterized or systematically manipulated. Effects of nuanced design choices, such as information visibility or the technological mode of punishment/reward, are not well quantified.

- **Lack of Quantitative Estimates:**  
  Many theoretical models provide qualitative or comparative statics but lack quantitative effect sizes or ready-to-use formulas for practical prediction in arbitrary parameter combinations—often requiring strong assumptions for extrapolation.

- **Ambiguity in Heterogeneous and Networked Settings:**  
  While network structure and dynamic punishment are identified as important moderators, their translation into practical predictive rules is only partially developed and often context-specific.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature base provides strong theoretical and some empirical support for the view that enabling punishment generally increases efficiency in PGG-like environments, especially when punishment is sufficiently strong and well-targeted. However, limitations in empirical measurement of efficiency, coverage of all design dimensions, and external validity of adjacent models mean that predictions for novel design configurations should be made cautiously and in light of context-specific moderators. Most robust guidance is available for punishment magnitude, cost, group size, and MPCR; other design parameters are less well covered.
