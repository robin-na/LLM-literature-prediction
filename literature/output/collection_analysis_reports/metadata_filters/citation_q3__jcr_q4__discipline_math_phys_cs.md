# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set consists of nine papers, spanning both experimental-empirical studies and formal theory/modeling papers. Of these, three are laboratory experiments that directly implement and analyze public goods games (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015), while the remaining six are theory papers or simulation studies that model repeated public goods, social dilemmas, or closely related environments (Deng et al., 2012; Thöni, 2014; Kurokawa et al., 2010; Kendal et al., 2006; Jaffe, 2004; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015).  

The evidence base is moderately broad in scope, covering a range of design dimensions, payoff structures, and behavioral mechanisms relevant to peer punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. However, direct empirical evidence is limited: only one experiment (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015) systematically varies punishment features and directly reports efficiency changes. Several theory papers offer strong mechanistic insight on how design features might translate into efficiency outcomes, but their assumptions and generality vary.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact:** 6 papers (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012; Thöni, 2014; Kurokawa et al., 2010; Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015) focus directly on PGG or standard variants.  
- **close/adjacent:** Blonski & Spagnolo (2015), Kendal et al. (2006), and Jaffe (2004) model closely related repeated social dilemmas, agent-based simulations, or meta-norm scenarios, but do not always instantiate classic PGGs.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact:** 7 papers (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012; Thöni, 2014; Kendal et al., 2006; Jaffe, 2004; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015) examine peer punishment or formal sanctioning mechanisms.  
- **none:** 2 empirical studies (Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015) contain no punishment and serve mainly as baseline or context.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact/close:** 6 papers directly model or report efficiency or total payoff/welfare as the primary outcome (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012; Kurokawa et al., 2010; Kendal et al., 2006; Jaffe, 2004).  
- **adjacent:** Thöni (2014) discusses efficiency only in relation to antisocial punishment, not reporting empirical payoff data. Drouvelis et al. (2015) and Martinsson et al. (2015) focus on contributions or conditional cooperation, not efficiency.

**Overall:** The literature is highly relevant to the PGG+punishment+efficiency frame, but direct empirical evidence on efficiency effects of enabling punishment—especially as a function of detailed design dimensions and control efficiency—is scarce. Most direct guidance comes from a small subset of the evidence base, supplemented by theoretical mechanisms and adjacent models.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (relevant for efficiency):**
  - **Explicitly measured:** Group payoff, welfare (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012; Kendal et al., 2006; Jaffe, 2004; Kurokawa et al., 2010)
  - **Implicitly modeled:** Population mean fitness or aggregate wealth (Kendal et al., 2006; Jaffe, 2004)
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - Contribution rate and cooperation rate (Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015)
  - Punishment frequency, retaliation, and antisocial punishment (Thöni, 2014; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015)
  - Strategy prevalence, fixation probability (Kurokawa et al., 2010; Deng et al., 2012)
- **Less relevant or not measured in context:** In many cases, efficiency is not reported empirically but inferred from models or through mechanisms (e.g., Thöni, 2014; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015).

**Distinction:** Non-payoff outcomes (e.g., contributions) are often higher with punishment, but this does not always translate into higher efficiency due to punishment costs (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Jaffe, 2004).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment Environment Structure Matters:**  
  Mechanisms such as the availability of multiple punishment stages, ability to retaliate, and the visibility of punishers' identities play a decisive role. A simple, anonymous, single-stage punishment (SP) enhances efficiency over control, while unconstrained punishment (RP) can cause efficiency to stagnate due to costly feuds and retaliation, despite increased cooperation (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

- **Punishment Cost and Severity:**  
  Theory suggests rare but severe, concerted punishment minimizes the total punishment cost and maximizes efficiency—especially in large groups and high-severity environments (Deng et al., 2012). If severe punishment is concerted and infrequent, efficiency is higher than with frequent or mild punishment.

- **Punishment Can Reduce Efficiency:**  
  Simulations show that costly punishment may increase norm compliance but reduce group efficiency unless it creates surplus value for the group (Jaffe, 2004). Thus, enabling punishment does not always improve payoff and can sometimes lower it.

- **Metanorms and Rewards:**  
  The reward of punishers or punishment of non-punishers (metanorms) are both theoretically effective in stabilizing high-efficiency equilibria, but explicit rewards for punishers are more robust and produce higher efficiency in populations starting with few norm enforcers (Kendal et al., 2006).

- **Antisocial Punishment as a Moderating Mechanism:**  
  When antisocial punishment is present, efficiency gains from punishment are undermined, particularly if the frequency and costliness of such behavior are high (Thöni, 2014). The design environment (e.g., fixed IDs, opportunity for retaliation) may exacerbate this risk (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

- **Control Efficiency as a Baseline:**  
  Where reported, baseline efficiency in control (no-punishment) is similar across treatments; the net effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on the factors above (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

- **Group Size/Player Count:**  
  Larger groups may get more efficiency benefit from well-designed (rare, severe, concerted) punishment (Deng et al., 2012), but also risk higher punishment costs or coordination problems if not carefully structured.

- **Behavioral Priming, Population Structure, and Communication:**  
  Studies on priming (Drouvelis et al., 2015) and social class (Martinsson et al., 2015) affect baseline cooperation but do not directly speak to prediction of efficiency with punishment enabled.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Principle:**  
The effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments depends not only on the existence of punishment but—critically—on the detailed design of the punishment mechanism and the possibility of retaliation or antisocial punishment.

**When predicting treatment efficiency:**
- If punishment is anonymous, constrained to a single stage per period, and retaliation is minimized, a significant positive effect on efficiency relative to control is likely, particularly in moderately sized groups and moderate punishment cost regimes (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- If the environment allows repeated or multi-stage punishment, fixed IDs, or full information facilitating retaliation and ongoing feuds, efficiency gains disappear, and there may be no net gain over control (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- The presence of metanorms—especially reward of punishers—improves the likelihood and speed of transitions to efficient states (Kendal et al., 2006).
- Theory strongly predicts higher efficiency with rare, severe, concerted punishment, especially as group size increases (Deng et al., 2012).
- Actual efficiency outcomes may be worse than predicted by contribution rates alone, since punishment costs can be substantial and offset group gains, especially with costly or antisocial punishment (Jaffe, 2004; Thöni, 2014).
- Baseline (control) efficiency is a necessary but not sufficient predictor; detailed mechanism design fundamentally affects the treatment–control difference.

**Under-determined prediction cases:**  
- If design details are unknown or many dimensions are unclear, no reliable effect of punishment on efficiency can be predicted from this literature alone.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Examined both empirically and theoretically; group size moderates punishment effectiveness and cost (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012; Kurokawa et al., 2010).
- `num_rounds`: Explored in repeated games (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Kurokawa et al., 2010; Thöni, 2014).
- `mpcr`: Used as a key parameter in most models for payoff structure.
- `all_or_nothing`: Some studies use binary actions, but effects are often robust to this dimension.
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`: Directly manipulated/analyzed (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Deng et al., 2012; Jaffe, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`: Present in the metanorm theory (Kendal et al., 2006).

**Indirectly or Sparsely Informed Dimensions:**  
- `chat`: Only present in studies not involving punishment (Martinsson et al., 2015; Drouvelis et al., 2015); not analyzed for influence on punishment efficacy.
- `default_contrib`: Discussed only contextually; baseline framing effects not connected to punishment efficiency effects.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Explored in select experiments (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Kurokawa et al., 2010); identity revelation and information flow flagged as key for retaliation risks, but not systematically cross-examined.
- `reward_tech`, `reward_magnitude`: Only included in metanorm models (Kendal et al., 2006).
- `punishment_exists`: Treated as the central manipulation throughout.

**Effectively Missing Dimensions:**  
- No papers directly model or experimentally test the marginal effect of dimensions such as chat, default_contrib, or visibility variables on the punishment–efficiency relationship in a comprehensive way.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of Comprehensive Empirical Coverage:** Only Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) report direct, treatment-by-treatment efficiency outcomes in PGGs with controlled manipulation of punishment structure. Most other studies are theoretical or simulate adjacent settings.
- **Behavioral Outcomes Are Not Efficiency:** Many papers focus on contribution rates or norm compliance, which do not reliably predict efficiency due to omitted accounting of costly punishment (Jaffe, 2004; Thöni, 2014).
- **Sparse Evidence on Several Dimensions:** Crucial design features (e.g., chat availability, default contribution framing, repeated versus one-shot play, summary statistics shown) are underexplored or unaddressed in nearly all theory and empirical work.
- **Heterogeneity in Game Variants:** Some "public goods–like" models may not transfer directly to standard linear PGGs, limiting quantitative prediction.
- **Theory’s Assumptions:** Most theory papers assume idealized conditions (well-mixed populations, specific forms of cultural transmission, or stylized payoff functions), which may not hold in laboratory or field applications.
- **Antisocial Punishment Not Well Quantified:** The impact of antisocial punishment on efficiency is theorized as negative but rarely measured for net payoff effects (Thöni, 2014).
- **Ambiguity and Disagreement Remain:** Some models predict increased efficiency with punishment, others predict the contrary; the realized outcome is highly sensitive to design details not uniformly reported or analyzed.

**Summary:**  
While the literature provides valuable guidance on the mechanisms through which punishment may affect group efficiency in PGG-like settings, especially the critical involvement of specific game-design dimensions, it remains limited in comprehensive empirical testing of treatment efficiency as a function of the full set of prediction dimensions and baseline control efficiency. Most robust conclusions are conditional on careful specification of punishment structure and the avoidance of mechanisms that facilitate retaliation or antisocial punishment.
