# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set contains 68 papers, with a mix of **empirical (lab experimental)** and **theoretical/simulation** studies. The mix tilts strongly toward theory and simulation, with relatively few empirical studies directly measuring efficiency in public goods games (PGGs) with peer punishment enabled versus disabled. This evidence base is **broad** in disciplinary coverage (economics, social sciences, physics), but **narrow** for the specific prediction task: only a subset of studies provide direct, quantitative evidence on the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in PGG-like games compared to a no-punishment control. Most papers are theoretical or simulation-based, and many report only behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation/contribution rates) or outcomes in adjacent game structures (e.g., N-person snowdrift, resource management, coordination games).

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. pgg_or_variant:**
- **Exact relevance:** Several key papers investigate PGGs directly, including both repeated and threshold PGGs, voluntary and all-or-nothing variants (e.g., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Vasconcelos et al., 2015; Dercole et al., 2013; Archetti & Scheuring, 2013).
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Many others address close variants (e.g., N-person snowdrift, CPR/commons dilemmas, group-forced dilemmas), or focus on mechanisms such as exclusion, reputation, or partner switching (Xu et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2015, 2017; Yuan et al., 2016).

**b. punishment_or_sanctions:**
- **Exact relevance:** There is a strong set of both empirical and theoretical studies on peer punishment and its design details (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Zhang et al., 2013; Dercole et al., 2013).
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Some model alternative mechanisms (enforced exclusion, delegable punishment, third-party enforcement); others study punishment-like mechanisms with different payoff structures (Lee et al., 2015/2017; Okada et al., 2015; Ohdaira, 2017).

**c. efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
- **Exact relevance:** Some papers report group efficiency or group payoff directly as their main outcome (e.g., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Dercole et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2013).
- **Adjacent/indirect relevance:** A sizeable body only reports on cooperation/contribution rates or behavioral strategies, not efficiency (e.g., many simulation studies).
- **None:** Several focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes without mapping to efficiency (e.g., strategy prevalence, cooperation rates).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff/efficiency outcomes:**
- *Directly reported efficiency or total group payoff* in empirical PGGs and in some theoretical models (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Vasconcelos et al., 2015; Dercole et al., 2013; Archetti & Scheuring, 2013; Griffin & Belmonte, 2017; Lee et al., 2015).
- *Proxy outcomes* (e.g., relative group payoff, population growth) in close variants.
- *Occasional adjacent measures:* group profit, resource level, total coins (Yuan et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2017).

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- The majority of theoretical and simulation papers focus on *cooperation/contribution rates*, *strategy frequencies*, or *phase prevalence*.
- Some address *prevalence of antisocial punishment* or *norm compliance*, with only qualitative discussion of efficiency effects.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Peer punishment increases efficiency in standard PGGs, but only under certain conditions**:
    - When punishment is well-calibrated, single-stage, anonymous, and peer-based, enabling punishment increases group efficiency (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Dercole et al., 2013).
    - Efficiency gains can be nullified if punishment is multi-stage, allows retaliation, is unconstrained, or if punishment costs are high (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Zhang et al., 2013; Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Griffin & Belmonte, 2017).

- **Structure of the punishment environment is critical**:
    - Efficiency is higher when punishment is *coordinated, group-based,* or *shared-cost*; uncoordinated or retaliatory punishment can reduce net benefits or create costly feuds (Villatoro et al., 2014; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

- **Population structure and context matter**:
    - Efficiency gains are more robust in spatial or structured populations and with local punishment, but are fragile or negative in well-mixed populations or under high punishment cost (Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Vasconcelos et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2015; Vukov et al., 2013).
    - Group size interacts with punishment effectiveness: large groups may require stronger or more efficient punishment (Dercole et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2015).

- **Nonlinear benefit functions moderate the effect**:
    - In linear PGGs, punishment can drive full cooperation and maximal efficiency; in nonlinear/threshold games, high efficiency may be possible without punishment, and additional punishment may add little (Archetti & Scheuring, 2013).

- **Antisocial punishment and implementation quality can undermine efficiency gains**:
    - The presence of antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators/prosocials) reduces or reverses efficiency gains (Thöni, 2014).
    - Effectiveness and honesty of enforcement mechanisms are crucial (Lee et al., 2015, 2017).

- **Other mechanisms (reputation, exclusion, third-party enforcement, meta-incentives) are relevant moderators**:
    - Reward, second-order incentives, and transparency can enhance or substitute for punishment, with important effects on efficiency (Okada et al., 2015; Yuan et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2015).

- **Calibration matters**:
    - The effectiveness of punishment is sensitive to punishment cost relative to its impact (Zhang et al., 2013; Xu et al., 2015; Vukov et al., 2013).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct implications:**
- **If the game is a standard, repeated, linear PGG with moderate group size, moderate MPCR, and peer punishment using a single, anonymous stage, enabling punishment is expected to increase efficiency compared to the no-punishment control, especially if baseline efficiency is low** (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Dercole et al., 2013; Archetti & Scheuring, 2013).
- **If the punishment scheme allows for retaliation, has high cost, or is unconstrained (multiple rounds, identified punishers), the efficiency effect can be null, negligible, or even negative due to wasted resources on punitive feuds** (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017).
- **In larger groups or less efficient control environments, efficiency gains from punishment may require harsher or more coordinated punishment and may be less reliable** (# group size effects: Dercole et al., 2013).
- **Nonlinear (threshold or sigmoid) benefit functions reduce or eliminate the efficiency benefit from adding punishment, because high efficiency can be attained via endogenous incentives** (Archetti & Scheuring, 2013).
- **Population structure, coordination of punishment, transparency, and calibration of punishment parameters are all crucial moderators** (Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Villatoro et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2015, 2017).

**Caveats:**
- Most direct payoff-based findings are theoretical or from simulations; empirical (especially lab experimental) evidence on varying punishment designs and large parameter sweeps is limited.

**Quantitative prediction:** Not typically available; guidance is mainly qualitative or comparative.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency not always reported**: Many studies report only behavioral outcomes (cooperation/contribution rates), forcing indirect inference about efficiency.
- **Limited parameter sweeps in empirical studies**: Most lab experiments test only a few points (e.g., with/without punishment), and rarely explore retaliation/coordination structure variations or very large/small group sizes.
- **Theory often assumes idealized models or parameter regions**: Unrealistic assumptions (unlimited rationality, agent homogeneity, perfect information) may not generalize to empirical settings.
- **Varied measurement and outcome definitions**: Efficiency is sometimes operationalized differently or inferred; some 'efficiency' outcomes are only loosely related to the PGG prediction task.
- **Antisocial punishment, retaliation, and heterogeneous populations are underexplored empirically**: Although shown theoretically to undermine efficiency, empirical evidence is limited on when and how these occur.
- **Confounding from other mechanisms**: Alternative paths to high efficiency (reputation, exclusion, reward, meta-incentives) may interact with or substitute for punishment, but are not always distinguished.
- **Adjacent games and models**: A nontrivial share of the literature analyzes close but non-identical games (e.g., snowdrift, stochastic resource dilemmas), increasing uncertainty in translating results to standard PGGs.
- **Lack of fine-grained quantitative effect sizes**: Most findings are comparative or qualitative, making precise prediction of treatment efficiency difficult.
- **Contextual factors (e.g., network structure, cultural factors, subject pool) are inconsistently addressed**.

---

*In sum*: While the literature directly addresses the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in PGGs under several relevant design dimensions, empirical payoff-based evidence is limited and often highly contingent on specific design and context factors. For prediction, qualitative direction and major moderators are well-supported, but fine-grained or universal quantitative prediction is not currently warranted given the evidence base.
