# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set (n=38) is heavily weighted toward **empirical, laboratory experimental research**, primarily focused on public goods games (PGGs) and related social dilemma designs. A smaller number of theory and agent-based modeling papers are present, mainly to propose or elucidate mechanisms rather than to provide empirical parameter estimates. Several papers use adjacent games (dictator, ultimatum, threshold PGGs, or field/lab-in-the-field interventions), and a minority address policy, neural, or organizational interpretations.

The evidence base is **broad in terms of behavioral contexts** (from laboratory PGGs to real-world collective action), but **narrower for direct, quantitative efficiency outcomes** under peer punishment. Several key studies have exact or close relevance for payoff outcomes under experimental manipulation of punishment in PGGs, but many report only contribution rates or norm compliance (behavioral outcomes). Some papers directly measure efficiency or group payoff under different game designs (notably for standard linear PGGs and threshold PGGs), but many treatment-control contrasts lack direct efficiency results. **The theory papers support mechanism arguments** but generally lack empirical validation.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Relevance is assessed separately by dimension:**

- **pgg_or_variant**
  - Several studies are of **exact PGG designs** or recognized variants (threshold PGGs, linear PGGs with peer or centralized punishment, hierarchies). Some are **close** (threshold games, resource extraction, manager-worker PGGs, etc.). A substantial number are **adjacent** (dictator/ultimatum games, trust games, CPR games, field context).
  - Overall, the evidence set is **strong (exact/close)** for PGGs and variants, but the mapping from adjacent designs is less certain.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**
  - Many papers are **exactly about peer punishment or institutional sanctions** (punishment stage, costly deduction, external or centralized punishment). Some probe third-party punishment, rule-based or probabilistic punishment, or compare reward and punishment.
  - A number are only **adjacent or weak**, discussing gossip, monitoring, reward, or norm enforcement broadly.
  - In sum, **there is good coverage of peer punishment institutions in economic games**, but with some studies diverging to adjacent or weaker forms.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**
  - Fewer papers are of **exact relevance** (i.e., directly measure group efficiency, group/individual payoff, or surplus as a primary outcome). More frequently, **adjacent or non-payoff behavioral** outcomes (contribution rates, cooperation rates, norm compliance, punishment frequency) are reported, which do not always translate cleanly to efficiency due to costs of punishment.
  - Some modeling papers report **mean/total payoff** estimates under parameterized punishment institutions.
  - In many cases, even high contributions do **not necessarily correspond to increased efficiency** if punishment costs are high (as in antisocial punishment).
  - **Exact payoff-based evidence is robust for standard linear PGGs but is sparse elsewhere.**

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**
  - **Group efficiency, total earnings, mean payoff, surplus, welfare:**  
    - **Directly measured** in major experimental PGG papers (e.g., Benard & Barclay, 2020; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Schlösser et al., 2018; Vollan et al., 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
    - **Efficiency is calculated** as the group’s actual payoff as a proportion of the social optimum.
  - **Modeled efficiency:**  
    - Some theory papers provide modeled or simulated payoff outcomes for different punishment regimes or organizational structures.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - **Contribution rate, cooperation rate, compliance, norm enforcement, punishment frequency, gossip frequency, fairness, intention/attitudes:**  
    - Most other papers report these, which are informative for mechanisms but do not in themselves define efficiency due to possible punishment costs or collateral dynamics.
- **Quality of measurement:**
  - **Studies that do not report efficiency explicitly** sometimes allow **inferential assessment of efficiency** when contribution rates and punishment costs are specified and MPCR is known.
  - **Some studies provide only weak or indirect inference** to efficiency, especially in adjacent game types or when only norm compliance or intentions are measured.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical Results:
- **Enabling peer punishment usually increases efficiency in standard linear PGGs,** provided that (a) punishment targets free riding effectively, and (b) punishment costs do not outweigh cooperation gains *(Benard & Barclay, 2020; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Schlösser et al., 2018)*.
  - *Waichman & Stenzel (2019):* Efficiency increases with peer punishment if feedback links punishment to prior behavior. No increase (and potential decrease) occurs if feedback is absent.
  - *Schlösser et al. (2018):* Contribution rate nearly doubles with punishment (from ~46% to ~92% of maximum). In standard linear PGGs, this conversion equates to large efficiency gains, matching classic literature.
  - *Benard & Barclay (2020):* Democratic selection of punishers/leaders enhances group efficiency further versus resource-based privilege.
- **Punishment can backfire (reduce efficiency) if costs are high or if antisocial punishment prevails,** as in some threshold/extraction games or when ethnic/status factors promote vengeful punishment *(Vollan et al., 2019)*.
- **Efficiency improvements depend on design details:**
  - *Endogenous, low-cost, shared punishment* (Kol'veková et al., 2021) delivers high mean payoffs as costs drop with stabilization.
  - *Hierarchical/managerial discretion* can promote or undermine efficiency depending on managers’ incentive alignment *(Billinger & Rosenbaum, 2019)*.
- **Effect sizes:**
  - With standardized, peer-based punishment in small groups (3–5 players, 10–21 rounds, moderate MPCR), **treatment efficiency with punishment tends to be much higher than control efficiency**, except when excessive or antisocial punishment is triggered.
- **Design moderators:**
  - *Punishment delay*: Little efficiency loss if feedback is preserved *(Waichman & Stenzel, 2019)*.
  - *Democratic/leader election*: Higher efficiency than resource-based appointment *(Benard & Barclay, 2020)*.
  - *Centralized versus peer punishment*: Peer punishment more targeted but can be vengeful/antisocial *(Vollan et al., 2019)*.

## Mechanisms and Modeling:
- **Theory and agent-based models support empirical results**: Moderate, flexible punishment (costs that are not excessive, punishment shared rather than monopolized) improves efficiency and maintains provision *(Kol'veková et al., 2021; Brandt & Svendsen, 2019)*.
- **Threshold effects and “tipping points”**: If the proportion of active punishers/support falls below a threshold, efficiency collapses *(Brandt & Svendsen, 2019)*.
- **Institutional structure matters**: Organizations with more levels, smaller units, or peer-based punishment better sustain higher contribution rates (and likely higher net efficiency if punishment is not socially wasteful) *(Moreno & Gutierrez-Garcia, 2018)*.

## Points of Ambiguity:
- **In threshold and field/lab-in-the-field variants, treatment efficiency may decrease with punishment** if costs and antisocial punishment outweigh cooperation gains *(Vollan et al., 2019)*.
- **When outcomes are contribution-based, efficiency improvements are often inferred but not always explicitly shown,** leading to uncertainty about net treatment gains after accounting for punishment costs.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Where the literature is strongest**: For prediction tasks involving **standard linear public goods games** (3–5 players, 10–21 rounds, moderate MPCR, explicit peer punishment), **the enabling of peer punishment is very likely to increase average efficiency**—sometimes nearly doubling it compared to control. This assumes punishment is used in a prosocial, cost-minimizing way and feedback links punishment to deviance.
- **Key moderators**:
  - If **feedback does not link punishment to deviant behavior**, or if the punishment method allows for **antisocial, vengeful, or excessive punishment**, efficiency gains are greatly reduced or can be negative *(Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Vollan et al., 2019)*.
  - **Democratic appointment of punishers** or **shared/endogenous punishment cost setting** increases efficiency beyond basic resource-based structures *(Benard & Barclay, 2020; Kol'veková et al., 2021)*.
  - Extreme **punishment cost**, group **status/ethnic heterogeneity**, or structures that promote **antisocial punishment** should lead to more conservative/uncertain predictions or even anticipating efficiency loss.
- **Control efficiency is a strong baseline**:  
  - If a game design’s control efficiency is low (as typical in repeated PGGs without punishment), enabling well-designed peer punishment should raise efficiency by substantial margins (approximate effects: ~+30 to +50 percentage points)—unless costs or social factors intervene.
- **Sparse or absent evidence**:  
  - For designs with **large groups, many rounds, or with rewards, chat, or third-party punishment only**, predictive uncertainty increases. Adjacent behavioral outcomes (contribution, compliance) do not translate straightforwardly into efficiency.
- **Agent-based and theory models**:  
  - Use these for **parameter sweeps**: for instance, low-cost, flexible, endogenous peer punishment mechanisms predict higher efficiency if learning mechanisms and social adaptation are included *(Kol'veková et al., 2021; Brandt & Svendsen, 2019)*.
- **Treatment-control efficiency differentials should be interpreted with caution** when only non-payoff behavioral outcomes are available: high contributions do not always compensate for potentially large punishment costs.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

Among the 14 specified design dimensions:

- **Directly and well informed:**
  - `player_count`: **Widely manipulated and reported** (core focus of many lab studies)
  - `num_rounds`: **Widely reported** (standard: 10–21; some high or one-shot studies)
  - `mpcr`: **Core parameter** in nearly all PGG studies
  - `punishment_cost`: **Parametric variation is common**
  - `all_or_nothing`: **Manipulated in some studies** (binary vs. continuous contribution)
  - `chat`: **Included in some studies**, shown to amplify or interact with punishment or rulemaking
  - `punishment_tech` (who can punish, voting for severity, etc.): **Directly addressed** in leader appointment, endogenous punishment models, etc.

- **Indirectly or partially informed:**
  - `default_contrib`: **Infrequently manipulated**; sometimes inferred from framing (opt-in/opt-out), but few results on this
  - `show_n_rounds`: **Described in methods**, but not often a focus of findings
  - `show_other_summaries`: **Sometimes included** (information/feedback on other players), directly shown to matter (esp. for targeting and feedback)
  - `show_punishment_id`: **Sometimes manipulated** (anonymity vs. transparency), esp. when feedback affects antisocial punishment
  - `punishment_tech`: **Form and accessibility of punishment** (peer, centralized, democratic selection) is a focus in select papers

- **Sparse or contextually discussed:**
  - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: **Rewards are examined in a minority of papers**, often as comparator or adjacent mechanism; little direct mapping to efficiency effects when both punishment and reward are present
  - Many **adjacent studies focus on reward or third-party punishment** (not peer punishment), with weaker links to target prediction.

- **Effectively missing or only contextually noted:**
  - Some **highly specific dimensions** (e.g., default contribution framing, opt-in/opt-out, meta-rewarding rates, etc.) are **rarely manipulated explicitly** for efficiency outcomes.
  - **Interaction effects or higher-order moderators** (e.g., group composition, ethnic heterogeneity, leadership quality) are alluded to but rarely directly varied across more than one study.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Gaps in efficiency measurement:**  
  - Many studies report only contributions, not efficiency or net payoff, making it **difficult to predict how punishment costs offset cooperation gains**.
- **Antisocial punishment not always modeled:**  
  - Effects of status, ethnicity, or group dynamics on the likelihood of inefficiency due to antisocial punishment are underexplored.
- **Sparse coverage for large, diverse groups:**  
  - Most studies use small groups (≤5 players); extrapolation to larger N is uncertain even though some evidence suggests weak dependence on group size for cooperation rates.
- **Reward and hybrid sanctioning**:  
  - Games with both punishment and reward are **underrepresented** in the efficiency literature.
- **Variations in feedback and information**:  
  - Whether participants receive clear feedback on who punished whom and why can swing efficiency effects; **few studies systematically manipulate all related dimensions.**
- **Theoretical ambiguities/inconsistencies:**  
  - Some models predict cyclical or threshold effects (where efficiency can collapse or recover depending on group composition or support for punishment), but applicability to empirical settings is less certain.
- **Predictive generalization is risky**:  
  - Empirical regularities are well supported for *standard linear PGGs with moderate group size*, but less so in variants (threshold/tipping, hierarchies, real-world field/lab-in-the-field).
- **Sparse evidence for highly contextual or institutional design elements**:  
  - E.g., degree of transparency, the nature of chat or communication, and the structure of summary and feedback are **not consistently reported or measured**.
- **Control efficiency baseline sometimes underreported**:  
  - This can hinder calibrating predictions for new designs where only treatment outcomes are available.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature supports a strong, positive impact of peer punishment on efficiency in **well-specified, standard linear PGGs** with adequate feedback and moderate costs, but warns of **efficiency loss under antisocial or untargeted punishment**—especially in threshold or field contexts. Prediction should **weight direct efficiency evidence most heavily, adjust for known moderators, and heed design gaps or ambiguities where evidence is lacking, particularly around the costs and social targeting of punishment.**
