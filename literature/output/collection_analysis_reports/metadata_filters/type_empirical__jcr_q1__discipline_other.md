# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base consists of 10 papers, predominantly experimental lab studies (7/10) with a focus on economic games involving public goods, sanctions, and cooperation. Most are empirical; two are observational field studies and one is a field experiment. The majority explicitly address punishment (peer or institutional) as an intervention. Several use canonical or slightly modified repeated linear Public Goods Games (PGGs), while others examine adjacent settings (e.g., principal-agent, market entry, collaborative policy, common-pool resources, ultimatum game analogs). 

Outcomes measured span efficiency, payoff, cooperation rates, norm compliance, externalities, and behavioral responses to (threats of) sanctions or rewards. However, only a subset centers on efficiency or closely related, group-level payoff outcomes—several papers focus primarily on behavioral rather than welfare or efficiency effects. Thus, while the paper set provides a narrow but strong empirical base on PGG variants and punishment, the directness of evidence for the core prediction task is mixed.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Four papers use canonical or close PGGs (Grechenig et al., 2010; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014; Engel, 2019; Bell et al., 2016).
- **Close/Adjacent relevance:** Several (Brick & Visser, 2010; Wei et al., 2025) use adjacent cooperation dilemmas or institutionally similar games; others (Rommel, 2015; Brevers et al., 2013; Montoya et al., 2015; Albergaria & Saes, 2018) are further removed.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact:** Grechenig et al. (2010), Eisenberg & Engel (2014), Engel (2019), Rommel (2015), Brick & Visser (2010), Bell et al. (2016).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Wei et al. (2025) (job rotation as punishment), others (Brevers et al., 2013; Montoya et al., 2015; Albergaria & Saes, 2018) discuss related sanctioning mechanisms.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact:** Grechenig et al. (2010), Wei et al. (2025).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Eisenberg & Engel (2014) (group payoff, earnings), Engel (2019) (total earnings, but not analyzed as efficiency), Brick & Visser (2010) (compliance with target, descriptive on earnings).
- **Weak or None:** Most other papers report only non-payoff behavioral measures.

Overall, only a small subset of the literature is **exactly** relevant to all three dimensions (canonical PGG, explicit punishment enable/disable, and direct efficiency-related outcomes). Most studies with high relevance to games and sanctions do not analyze efficiency per se.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**  
    - **Efficiency/group payoff/total earnings:** Directly measured in Grechenig et al. (2010), Eisenberg & Engel (2014), and Wei et al. (2025); described but not analyzed in Engel (2019) and Brick & Visser (2010).
    - **Compliance with targets:** Used by Brick & Visser (2010) as a behavior/payoff proxy.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
    - **Contribution/cooperation rates:** Most studies (Engel, 2019; Bell et al., 2016; Brick & Visser, 2010) stress participant contributions or norm compliance rather than group earnings.
    - **Punishment behavior (frequency, targeting):** Often reported (Grechenig et al., 2010; Bell et al., 2016).
    - **Market entry, norm violation, fairness:** Rommel (2015), Brevers et al. (2013), and others report effects on behavioral compliance or fairness measures, not group efficiency.

**Distinction:** The majority of the literature relies on behavioral metrics, while only a minority quantifies group efficiency, welfare gains, or total surplus.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical findings:**
- **Punishment boosts efficiency where monitoring is accurate:**  
    - When contributions can be reliably observed, enabling punishment in repeated PGGs (especially peer or institutional sanctions) substantially reverses efficiency decay (Grechenig et al., 2010; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014).
- **Noise/uncertainty in monitoring reverses the effect:**  
    - If information about contributions is noisy or unreliable, punishment can lower efficiency below the baseline, due to mis-targeted or excessive punitive behavior (Grechenig et al., 2010).
- **Severity and likelihood of punishment matter:**  
    - Stronger and more likely sanctions (e.g., treble damages, group-harm-based punishment) lead to larger, more persistent efficiency gains. Mild or uncertain sanctions are weak deterrents (Eisenberg & Engel, 2014).
- **Centralization and transparency may backfire:**  
    - High transparency (e.g., individual-level feedback on punishment and contributions) can actually decrease cooperation and, by implication, efficiency. Information about punishment can crowd out voluntary contributions (Engel, 2019).
- **Institutional/automatic sanctions outperform peer punishment where voluntary cooperation is weak:**  
    - Tax-based, rule-driven sanctions in emission target games achieve exceptionally high compliance and likely high efficiency, but may crowd out voluntary excess contributions (Brick & Visser, 2010).
- **Punishment sensitivity and social context:**  
    - The presence of punishment increases norm compliance but also introduces antisocial punishment and free-riding under some conditions (Bell et al., 2016; Grechenig et al., 2010).

**Theoretical arguments:**  
- Sustained cooperation in complex, real-world settings depends on the presence of both punishment and supporting structures (Montoya et al., 2015), but this remains qualitative.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

The literature strongly supports the general prediction that **enabling punishment in repeated, accurate-information PGGs typically raises group efficiency relative to control (no-punishment) games**. However, the effect's direction and magnitude **depends crucially on several moderators**:

- **Information accuracy:**  
    - When contribution monitoring is accurate and public, enabling punishment increases efficiency; when information is noisy or ambiguous, punishment can be harmful (Grechenig et al., 2010).
- **Punishment severity/likelihood:**  
    - Stronger, more reliably enacted sanctions produce larger efficiency gains (Eisenberg & Engel, 2014).
- **Feedback/transparency structure:**  
    - Too much transparency—especially highlighting individual punishment actions—may reduce cooperation and efficiency (Engel, 2019).
- **Sanction institution:**  
    - Institutional/automatic sanctions (Brick & Visser, 2010) may be more effective at achieving target outcomes than peer punishment.
- **Other behavioral outcomes (contribution, norm compliance):**  
    - While increases in cooperation generally signal higher efficiency, this translation is not always direct; in some designs, punishment shifts the cooperation norm or increases strategic complexity without clear gains (Bell et al., 2016).

Given these findings, **prediction models should adjust expected efficiency gains from enabling punishment according to the game’s monitoring, punishment parameters, and visibility/feedback features**. Control efficiency provides a baseline: if efficiency is already high, punishment may have limited or even negative marginal effect, especially under noisy monitoring or excessive transparency.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**
- **player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr:**  
    - Explored or controlled in nearly all experimental studies.
- **punishment_cost:**  
    - Explicitly manipulated in multiple studies (Grechenig et al., 2010; Eisenberg & Engel, 2014; Brick & Visser, 2010); costliness of punishment is key in shaping its effect.
- **punishment_tech:**  
    - Institutional/peer distinction addressed (Brick & Visser, 2010; Rommel, 2015).

**Indirectly/contextually discussed:**
- **chat:**  
    - Allowed in some studies as a communication channel (Brick & Visser, 2010; Bell et al., 2016), but its direct payoff effect is less explored.
- **show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:**  
    - Feedback/transparency design is central (Engel, 2019; Grechenig et al., 2010).
- **reward_exists, default_contrib:**  
    - Only tangentially addressed (Montoya et al., 2015 mention rewards in real-world context; default contribution framing is rarely the experimental focus).

**Dimensions sparsely/missing:**
- **reward_cost, reward_tech, reward_magnitude:**  
    - Rarely if ever empirically manipulated.
- **show_n_rounds:**  
    - Occasionally (Brevers et al., 2013), but no strong evidence on its payoff effect.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Direct efficiency evidence is limited:**  
    - Only two studies (Grechenig et al., 2010; Wei et al., 2025) measure efficiency outcomes in canonical or closely related games; others report only behavioral responses or approximate payoffs.
- **Generalizability is uncertain:**  
    - Many results, especially those with striking positive or negative effects, are confined to specific parameterizations (e.g., perfect vs. noisy monitoring, punishment severity regimes).
- **Incomplete coverage of design space:**  
    - Key prediction dimensions (e.g., reward mechanisms, framing effects, specific feedback display options) are untested or underexplored.
- **Behavior vs. payoff ambiguity:**  
    - Many papers treat contribution rate or compliance as proxies for efficiency; but payoff and efficiency gains are not always correlated, especially when punishment is misapplied or costly.
- **Institutional settings differ from PGGs:**  
    - Field and policy case studies (Montoya et al., 2015; Albergaria & Saes, 2018) offer minimal guidance for quantitative predictions in lab-based PGGs.
- **Idiosyncratic, contextually specific sanctions:**  
    - Some forms of punishment (e.g., job rotation, tax-based fines) may not translate directly to standard peer-sanctioned PGG environments.

**Conclusion:**  
The literature supports cautious optimism that punishment can improve efficiency relative to baseline but cautions prediction models to pay close attention to monitoring accuracy, sanction severity, and feedback transparency. The empirical foundation for fine-grained, multi-dimensional efficiency prediction is limited, with gaps in several key design dimensions and ambiguity as to when punishment is counterproductive. Quantitative prediction should be attenuated by these uncertainties.
