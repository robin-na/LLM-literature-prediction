# 1) Evidence Base

The provided paper set is moderately broad in scope but offers a core cluster of highly relevant empirical and theoretical studies directly targeting the effect of **punishment on efficiency in public-goods games (PGGs)** or their close variants. The set includes:

- **Empirical experimental papers**: Several high-quality, lab-based PGG studies (e.g., Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Gross et al., 2022; Harrell & Wolff, 2023) that directly manipulate punishment and report group efficiency outcomes.
- **Meta-syntheses and reviews**: Syntheses/meta-analyses (e.g., Eichenseer, 2023) that aggregate across dozens of experimental findings, explicitly relating punishment, design dimensions, and efficiency.
- **Theory/modeling papers**: Formal models generalize the conditions for punishment to enhance efficiency (e.g., Libois, 2022; Melkonyan et al., 2022), and some provide comparative statics or qualitative predictions transferable to PGGs.
- **Adjacent and indirect papers**: Many studies examine related games, behavioral outcomes, or discuss punishment in a PGG context without measuring efficiency (e.g., Espín et al., 2022; Bogdan et al., 2023; Adams et al., 2022).
- **Observational and field studies**: A small number link laboratory findings to real-world environments (e.g., Hergueux et al., 2023), but these less often address both punishment and payoff outcomes simultaneously.

In summary: The evidence base is **strong and direct on the core question of punishment's effect on PGG efficiency**, but is **more sparse or adjacent when examining the full range of the 14 game design dimensions** or when exploring contextual moderators (e.g., social identity, cultural effects).

# 2) Task Relevance

## a) pgg_or_variant

- **exact**: Multiple papers directly use repeated linear PGGs or close institutional variants (e.g., Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Gross et al., 2022; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
- **close**: Some papers use clearly related environments (e.g., CPR games, repeated social dilemmas, tax evasion games).
- **adjacent/weak**: Several studies are based on bargaining games, PD, Ultimatum, or non-PGG dilemmas, and thus are only indirectly relevant.
- **none**: A minority are theoretical/conceptual with no PGG or close variant.

## b) punishment_or_sanctions

- **exact**: Core cluster manipulate or measure peer (or institutional) punishment as a game design variable (e.g., Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
- **close**: Others model sanctions or reporting mechanisms with strong conceptual overlap (e.g., Libois, 2022; Romaniuc et al., 2022).
- **adjacent/weak/none**: Many address norm enforcement, reputation, or trust, or focus on reward, commitment, or gossip rather than direct punishment.

## c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome

- **exact**: Several papers measure group efficiency, total payoff, or monetary welfare as primary outcomes (e.g., Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Eichenseer, 2023; Melkonyan et al., 2022).
- **close/adjacent**: Many focus on proxy or behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation rate, compliance) or only report efficiency as a secondary/mixed outcome.
- **weak/none**: A significant portion address only social evaluations, reputational effects, or punishment assignment—without payoff outcomes.

**Overall**, the **task-relevant nucleus comprises empirical and meta-analytic studies that use PGGs, manipulate punishment, and report efficiency-based outcomes**, often with some core design dimensions specified. Surrounding this nucleus is a broad ring of adjacent or contextual literature that informs mechanisms or moderator effects but typically lacks efficiency outcome data.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**:
  - **Group efficiency**, **average profits/earnings**, **welfare/surplus**, **success scores** in team tasks, or **tax compliance** as a proxy for group payoff (e.g., Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Gross et al., 2022; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Romaniuc et al., 2022; Eichenseer, 2023).
  - **Theoretical efficiency** (ratio of achieved to optimal payoff) in models (e.g., Libois, 2022; Melkonyan et al., 2022).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
  - **Contribution and cooperation rates** (e.g., Espín et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022), **punishment frequency**, **norm compliance**.
  - **Social evaluations** (reputation, trust, perceived trustworthiness), **punisher/receiver perceptions**, **group selection choices**.
  - **Neural and cognitive measures** (e.g., inter-brain synchronization; Li et al., 2022).

**Key distinction**: Only a fraction of papers directly measure **efficiency or payoff**, while many report on **cooperation rates** or psychological/behavioral outcomes. The core prediction task requires focusing on payoff-based results.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical and Meta-analytic Results

1. **Classic PGGs: Enabling peer punishment increases efficiency—conditional on design**. In linear PGGs, introducing peer punishment robustly increases group profits/efficiency, especially after initial periods where the cost of punishment may reduce earnings (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
2. **Magnitude of efficiency gains depends on treatment details**:
   - Meta-analysis (Eichenseer, 2023): Peer punishment increases efficiency substantially (estimate: up to +0.47 on a 0-1 normalized scale over baseline), with stronger effects than centralized or leader-based punishment. Effects of reward are smaller (+0.11), and rotating leadership reduces efficiency.
   - Gains accrue mostly after a few rounds, as cooperation stabilizes.

3. **Effect of punishment is context-dependent**:
   - After group conflict or with salient outgroup/in-group identities, punishment can become less targeted to free-riders and less effective, sometimes reducing overall efficiency even as cooperation rates are unchanged (Gross et al., 2022). Hiding conflict histories recovers the positive efficiency effect.
   - Network structure matters: Dense information and large group size amplify the positive effect of peer punishment on efficiency (Harrell & Wolff, 2023).

4. **Social, psychological, and cultural moderators** can influence the impact of punishment, sometimes by moderating punishing behavior itself. However, many studies reporting on these moderators **do not measure efficiency directly**. Where cooperation is measured instead of payoff, the translation to efficiency is less certain and sometimes ambiguous (Zhang et al., 2022).

## Theoretical and Mechanism Insights

1. **Efficiency gains require the cost-effectiveness of punishment**:
   - If punishment is too costly relative to its deterrent effect, efficiency gains disappear, or net efficiency can even fall below control (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Libois, 2022).
   - Sufficiently strong, credible, or institutionalized punishments are necessary for first-best (full efficiency) outcomes (Libois, 2022; Melkonyan et al., 2022).
   - In continuous-contribution games, punishment alone may not create stable high-cooperation equilibria (Yan et al., 2023).

2. **Interaction with reward and commitment**:
   - Reward alone typically has weaker effects on efficiency than punishment, unless very large (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Eichenseer, 2023).
   - Voluntary, low-cost commitment devices can also create efficient, cooperative groups, sometimes more reliably than punishment (Lang et al., 2024).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct empirical and meta-analytic studies support increasing predicted efficiency when peer punishment is enabled, relative to control efficiency—provided game design is similar to classic repeated linear PGGs** (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Eichenseer, 2023).
- **The magnitude of this increase is sensitive to key game design parameters**:
   - Most robust for moderate-to-large group sizes, intermediate-to-high MPCR, and when punishment cost/tech allows non-trivial deterrent at manageable cost.
   - In large, dense groups or settings with strong information flow, the effect of punishment is especially strong (Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
- **If punishment cost is too high or targeting is poor (e.g., after conflict, with ambiguous norms), increases in efficiency may be lower or even negative** (Gross et al., 2022; Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- **When only contribution rates are reported in the literature, exercise caution: not all increases in cooperation translate into higher efficiency, as punishment itself is costly**. Empirical findings confirming group payoff increases are more predictive than ones reporting only behavioral change.

- **In parameter regions not represented in the empirical studies** (e.g., very different group sizes, reward-only treatments, non-linear payoff structures), extrapolate with caution, leaning on theoretical mechanism arguments where available.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

## Directly informed
- **player_count (group size)** — Directly manipulated and found important for punishment effects on efficiency (Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Eichenseer, 2023).
- **num_rounds** — Repeated games, with time course of efficiency effects described (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Gross et al., 2022).
- **mpcr (marginal per-capita return)** — Included in design and meta-analyses as moderator (Eichenseer, 2023).
- **punishment_cost & punishment_tech** — Cost-effectiveness of punishment is a central focus (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Zhang & Pei, 2022; Libois, 2022).
- **all_or_nothing** — Both continuous and binary-choice designs used in various studies.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech** — Some meta-analyses compare punishment to reward (Eichenseer, 2023); indirect in others.
- **chat** — Role of communication as a moderator of cooperation is well discussed and experimentally manipulated (Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Adams et al., 2022).
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries** — Information conditions sometimes manipulated; less central for punishment effects but present.

## Indirectly informed/contextual
- **default_contrib** — Mentioned in some framing/behavioral studies but rarely manipulated as a treatment in payoff-based outcomes.
- **show_punishment_id** — Sometimes addressed in studies about punishment reputation or targeting (Dhaliwal et al., 2022), but generally not as a focus of efficiency outcomes.

## Missing or very limited
- No studies directly manipulate or systematically explore **default_contrib** or **show_punishment_id** in relation to efficiency outcomes in PGGs with punishment.
- Some design dimensions lack adequate variation within controlled studies (e.g., rarely is entire reward structure systematically crossed with punishment presence).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency as an outcome is measured or reported in only a subset of studies**; many only address cooperation rate, so caution is needed when extrapolating from behavior to payoff.
- **Empirical coverage is densest for classic repeated linear PGGs with moderate group size and regular MPCR values**; evidence is sparser for variants (e.g., very large or small groups, extreme punishment costs, nonlinear payoffs, or rare, complex settings).
- **Most meta-analytic and comparative studies do not fully cross all 14 design dimensions**, so predictions outside tested parameter spaces are more speculative, relying on transfer from mechanism models.
- **Many theoretically or contextually interesting moderators (e.g., culture, identity, communication style, observed reputation) are not systematically built into controlled efficiency studies**, so their quantitative predictive value is weak.
- **Interaction effects and nonlinearities (e.g., threshold punishment required to boost efficiency, synergy/antagonism with rewards) are highlighted in some models but underexplored empirically**.
- **Observation of negative or null effects**: In some contexts (e.g., after intergroup conflict, with poor targeting, or very high punishment cost), enabling punishment can fail to raise or can even reduce group efficiency.
- **Studies often hold critical game design parameters constant**; generalizability across settings is constrained.

---

**In summary**:  
The empirical core of this literature robustly supports that **enabling peer punishment in repeated linear PGGs typically increases group efficiency over no-punishment control, especially when punishment is cost-effective and groups are moderately large**. The effect generalizes to settings with similar design dimensions but should be cautiously adjusted or qualified outside that parameter space. Key dimensions (group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are well informed. Many moderating factors, and some design dimensions (reward tech, default contributions), are only weakly or contextually addressed. Carefully distinguish literature reporting only cooperation from that measuring efficiency, and be alert to contexts (conflict history, identity) where punishment effects are null or negative. Mechanism models and meta-analyses inform qualitative and, sometimes, quantitative prediction, but there remain empirical gaps—especially for combinations of less-studied design features.
