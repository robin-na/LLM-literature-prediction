# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set consists entirely of theory/modeling papers (no empirical or laboratory data), nearly all offering analytical or simulation-based findings. The set is broad in its coverage of models—ranging from exact PGGs to adjacent social dilemmas—but nearly all results pertain to theoretical outcomes rather than observed behavior in real player groups. Most models consider a variety of game design dimensions and often focus on parametric or regime changes. Notably, there are some papers providing direct (exact or close) evidence for efficiency- or payoff-based outcomes in punishment-enabled PGGs, while a substantial share address only behavioral or cooperation-rate outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**  
- _Exact relevance:_ Most of the core papers address PGGs or optionally structured or spatial PGGs.
- _Close/adjacent relevance:_ A sizable number explore closely related settings (collective-risk dilemmas, innovation races, trust or coordination games), which are informative for general mechanisms but not an exact match for prediction.
- _Weak/none:_ A minority of papers are further removed, focusing on other paradigms or only contextualizing PGG concepts.

**Punishment or Sanctions:**  
- _Exact relevance:_ Many papers center on the impact of peer (or institutional) punishment, with detailed models of cost, magnitude, technology, and sometimes antisocial punishment.
- _Close/adjacent relevance:_ Others focus on related interventions—reward, social exclusion, ostracism, or indirect reputational sanctions, which may operate similarly but are not always interchangeable with classic peer punishment.
- _None:_ Some theory works consider no treatment/control comparisons or discuss only baseline cooperation mechanisms.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**  
- _Exact/close relevance:_ A moderate number provide direct or primary focus on efficiency (group payoff relative to cooperative optimum), total payoff, or welfare; these are most informative for the prediction task.
- _Adjacent/weak relevance:_ Many others report only cooperation rates, prevalence of strategies, or related behavioral measures, which are not equivalent to efficiency and must not be conflated.
- _None:_ A subset focus on conceptual mechanisms, neural/psychological pathways, or non-payoff outcomes, and offer little toward payoff-based prediction.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes:**  
  - Several highly relevant papers (e.g., Dong et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2020; Salahshour, 2021; Gao et al., 2020; García & Traulsen, 2019; Huang et al., 2018; Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2020; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021) yield efficiency, total group payoff, or closely equivalent welfare as their primary outcomes. Some provide explicit formulas or phase diagrams relating these outcomes to design parameters.
  - A few offer adjacent or indirect payoff outcomes (e.g., resource levels, principal payoffs, group welfare under reputation or social exclusion).

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  - A large fraction focus on cooperation/contribution rates, norm compliance, or strategy frequencies (e.g., prevalence of cooperators, punishers, or defectors), usually without reporting on efficiency or group payoff. While increases in cooperation are sometimes assumed to boost efficiency, the cost of punishment can offset any gains—so results about cooperation must be distinguished from efficiency claims.
  - Some report on ancillary behavioral outcomes (e.g., evolution of social norms, impact of gossip, leader status, or network rewiring), which may help explain mechanisms but do not themselves reflect efficiency.

- **Hybrid or Mixed Outcomes:**  
  - Where papers provide both, explicit caution is warranted to interpret which results genuinely bear on payoff-based efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the most directly relevant theoretical models (with PGG structure, explicit punishment manipulation, and group efficiency outcomes):

- **Punishment Can Increase Efficiency—If Appropriately Designed:**  
  - Enabling peer or institutional punishment often increases group efficiency in PGGs, particularly when punishment is effective (high impact per unit cost), neither too cheap (which can allow antisocial punishment or wasteful overuse) nor too expensive (which deters its use). Effectiveness regimes are parameterized by the ratio of fine to cost (Gao et al., 2020; Salahshour, 2021; Zhang & Cao, 2020; García & Traulsen, 2019; Dong et al., 2019).
  - Consensual or probabilistic punishment (Jiao et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2020) and designs that allow coordination or observation (García & Traulsen, 2019) tend to be more robust, reducing costs and increasing mean payoffs.
  - Institutional reward mechanisms often match or exceed punishment effects on efficiency, sometimes being strictly more robust, especially under decision errors or bounded rationality (Dong et al., 2019).

- **Punishment Can Reduce Efficiency—Under Certain Conditions:**  
  - Where punishment is badly calibrated (too weak, too cheap, or too expensive), where antisocial punishment is possible, or where institutions can be corrupted, enabling punishment may decrease efficiency, either by failing to deter defection (wasting resources, e.g. Salahshour, 2021; Wang et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2019) or by introducing counterproductive enforcement (e.g., antisocial punishment, corruption cycles).
  - The presence and detection of institution/conditional strategies (García & Traulsen, 2019), or corruption, are powerful moderators of the sign and size of punishment effects.

- **Interaction With Other Mechanisms:**  
  - The benefit of punishment may be strongly contingent on parallel mechanisms: the presence of reputation systems, exclusion as a sanction, leader/follower structures, or reward treatments (Podder et al., 2021; Kosfeld, 2020; Wang et al., 2021).
  - Reward and reputation mechanisms can substitute for or enhance punishment in promoting efficiency, with some evidence for hybrid strategies being optimal.

- **Parameter Regimes Matter:**  
  - Key prediction moderators include marginal per capita return (mpcr), group size (player_count), punishment cost and magnitude, observability, presence of antisocial punishment, structure of punishment (peer vs. institutional), and—critically—baseline (control) efficiency.
  - If control efficiency is already high (i.e., groups already cooperate), enabling punishment yields minimal benefit and can be negative if costly. If control efficiency is low, well-calibrated punishment or reward is more likely to improve efficiency.

- **Behavioral Outcomes ≠ Efficiency:**  
  - Many papers showing increases in cooperation/contribution with punishment do not assess efficiency, and it cannot be assumed that higher cooperation always translates to higher group payoff due to the cost of punishment itself.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Directly Informed Predictions:**  
  - When predicting efficiency with punishment enabled, use findings that specify regime boundaries (in terms of mpcr, group size, punishment cost/effectiveness) to inform expectations: e.g., if the control game is inefficient due to high defection and group parameters fall within the “punishment effective” regime of these models, expect efficiency to increase with punishment (Salahshour, 2021; Zhang & Cao, 2020; Dong et al., 2019; Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2020).

- **Dependence On Design Dimensions:**  
  - Efficiency gains from punishment are maximized when the punitive action is not too costly, can be well-targeted (avoiding antisocial use), and is observable in a way that enables credible threats (García & Traulsen, 2019; Gao et al., 2020). Designs must avoid overuse (e.g., very low punishment cost can lead to costly antisocial punishment) or underuse (if too costly or ineffective).
  - Reward and hybrid incentive designs are often as good or better, particularly in environments with decision errors or bounded rationality (Dong et al., 2019; Wang, S. X. et al., 2021).

- **Qualitative Moderators:**  
  - If underlying models allow for corruption, antisocial punishment, or poor information, the positive effect on efficiency is reduced or reversed (Lee et al., 2019; García & Traulsen, 2019).
  - The presence of communication or observability, as well as structured populations with leaders, reputation, or exclusion mechanisms, generally enhances the efficiency gains when punishment is enabled (Kosfeld, 2020; Podder et al., 2021).

- **When Evidence Is Indirect:**  
  - If only cooperation rate/contribution is reported, do not treat these as equivalent to efficiency. Use such results only as circumstantial support, and explicitly acknowledge the lack of efficiency data.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- `player_count`: Explicitly modeled (group size) in all core theory papers; effects predicted for small/large N.
- `num_rounds`: Modeled in repeated games; infinite-horizon/number of rounds is often assumed.
- `mpcr`: Directly parameterized/formalized as return/multiplier; important moderator.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Typically key parameters; both cost per unit and mechanism (peer/institutional/probabilistic/conditional) are deeply analyzed.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Enabled in many models, especially those comparing to reward or hybrid treatments.
- `all_or_nothing`: Some models allow for binary/continuous contributions and directly compare outcomes.

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `chat`, `show_punishment_id`: Observability and communication are sometimes discussed as moderators (García & Traulsen, 2019; Kosfeld, 2020; Song et al., 2020), but generally not the principal treatment variable.
- `default_contrib`: Framing/defaults are not analyzed as a main treatment but may be contextually mentioned in framing.
- `show_punishment_id`: Identity revelation is a moderator in some models, especially for supporting conditional strategies.

**Missing:**
- No direct models or analysis of the effect of `default_contrib` or most observability/display dimensions as principal drivers of efficiency.
- Limited consideration of experimental interface details (framing, interface choices, etc.).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Theory-Only Evidence:**  
  - The entire evidence base is theoretical; generalizability to laboratory or field outcomes is not empirically established. All conclusions rest on the accuracy and inclusiveness of model assumptions.

- **Scarcity Of Direct Efficiency Data:**  
  - Many studies focus on behavioral/strategy outcomes, not efficiency. Efficiency conclusions often require indirect inference, especially where only cooperation rates are reported.

- **Potential Overstatement Of Punishment Efficacy:**  
  - Some models assume perfect targeting of punishment, no antisocial punishment, no error, or no corruption. Realistic conditions may undermine predicted gains.

- **Sparse Coverage Of Certain Design Dimensions:**  
  - Several input dimensions relevant for experimental design (chat, interface, display, default framing) are rarely manipulated or analyzed as primary moderators of efficiency outcomes.

- **Parameter Sensitivity:**  
  - The sign and size of punishment’s effect on efficiency is highly sensitive to cost structure, group size, and return rate. There is no “one-size-fits-all” effect: models flag critical thresholds and non-monotonic responses.

- **Ambiguity Where Mechanisms Interact Or Conflict:**  
  - Synergies and conflicts between reward, punishment, reputation, and network structure sometimes yield ambiguous or regime-dependent results (e.g., Podder et al., 2021).

- **Limited Relevance Of Adjacent Paradigm Papers:**  
  - Many papers contextually discuss or model similar but not identical games; their findings must be cautiously transferred to core PGG efficiency prediction.

---

**In summary:**  
This literature base, though consistently theoretical and broad in the variant games and mechanisms considered, offers robust guidance for predicting the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments—*provided parameter regimes, design features, and the presence of control game inefficiency are carefully considered, and non-payoff outcomes are not conflated with efficiency*. Key design dimensions (punishment cost, magnitude, mpcr, group size, observability) are well-informed, but interface/framing dimensions remain largely unaddressed. The main limitations are the lack of empirical testing, sensitivity to parameter regimes and model assumptions, and sparse coverage of several practical design variables.
