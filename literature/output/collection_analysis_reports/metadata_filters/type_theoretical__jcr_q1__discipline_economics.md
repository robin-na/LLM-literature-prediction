# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set (N = 37) is heavily theory-dominated, with the great majority of contributions offering formal models and mechanism arguments about public goods, common-pool resources, trust, and related repeated games. There is a strong overrepresentation of theoretical analyses focused on various punishment and sanctioning technologies as mechanisms for sustaining cooperation, with extensive comparative statics and explicit predictions regarding group efficiency. Empirical experimental evidence is rarely presented directly; most findings are deductive, not data-driven, although some papers draw on the stylized results of laboratory experiments in their motivation or interpretation.

Breadth: The literature base is broad in its coverage of repeated cooperation dilemmas and the spectrum of punishment mechanisms, including both peer and institutional forms, reputation, expulsion, ostracism, and reward. However, the set is narrow and deep regarding direct experimental evidence on efficiency effects in standard laboratory public goods games (PGGs) with peer punishment—direct empirical estimation of treatment efficiency is scarce.

Thus, the paper set enables a relatively deep mechanistic/theoretical synthesis, but empirical calibration is limited.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Many core theory papers analyze repeated public goods games or very close variants (e.g., Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2021; Wolitzky, 2013; van der Weele, 2012; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999), providing direct predictions relevant to standard PGG designs.
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Numerous papers address related social dilemmas, common pool resources, trust, and partnership games. While these are not always canonical PGGs, their insights are generally transferable, especially when outcomes, institutional arrangements, or player structures are similar.
- **Weak/none:** A minority provide only distant or conceptual coverage (e.g., evolutionary frameworks, behavioral surveys)—these are not directly useful for the prediction task.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Most theory work centers on punishment or sanctioning, at various levels: peer, institutional, reputational, or via ostracism. Some also examine alternatives like reward or commitment mechanisms (e.g., self-sanction via deposit).
- **Close:** Some papers treat reputation or ostracism as special cases of sanctions, or focus on the subtleties of community vs. peer vs. institutional punishment structures.
- **Adjacent/weak:** A few address mainly non-punitive mechanisms or only conceptualize punishment within broader norm enforcement frameworks.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact/close relevance:** Several papers provide explicit model outcomes in terms of efficiency, group payoff, or surplus (matching the prediction target). 
- **Adjacent:** Some focus primarily on behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation, norm compliance), or only reference efficiency conceptually.
- **Weak/none:** Pure behavioral or evolutionary synthesis contributions do not report payoff-based outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Based Outcomes (Directly Matching Efficiency):**
- Many theory contributions report group efficiency, total payoff, or surplus as primary or model-dominant outcomes (e.g., Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2021; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Wolitzky, 2013; Libois, 2022).
- Some papers present conditions or explicit formulas for maximum attainable efficiency depending on punishment and design parameters.
- Reputable adjacent studies focus on welfare, average earnings, or sustainable yield (e.g., Tarui et al., 2008; Harbaugh & To, 2014; Heintzelman et al., 2009).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- Several papers measure or discuss cooperation rates, pattern of contributions, norm compliance, or frequency/type of punishment (e.g., Ostrom, 2000; Reuben & Tyran, 2010).
- These outcomes often correlate with efficiency but are not strictly equivalent and may not predict group payoff due to punishment costs ("wasted" resources or second-order counter-punishment).

**Distinction Maintained:**
- The evidence base is strong in distinguishing efficiency (welfare, payoff) from contribution or behavioral outcomes, consistently noting when efficiency gains are offset by costly punishment or retaliation (e.g., Anderies et al., 2011; Noussair & van Soest, 2014).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Direction of Punishment Effects:**
- **Punishment can enable high efficiency**, often shifting equilibria from near-total defection (low efficiency) to full cooperation (high efficiency), provided that punishment is credible, not overly costly to the punisher, and applied broadly or equitably (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Wolitzky, 2013; Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2021; Heller & Sieberg, 2010).
- **The marginal effect of punishment on efficiency is strongly context-dependent:**
    - **Positive if:** the baseline (control) game is inefficient, punishment is not prohibitively costly, monitoring is informative, and group composition allows for at least sporadic enforcement (even by a minority).
    - **Weak or negative if:** baseline efficiency is already high (norms suffice), punishment is easily misapplied/abused, or retaliation/extortion is possible (Barron & Guo, 2021; Noussair & van Soest, 2014).
- **Critical Moderators:**
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness**: Lower cost and higher impact are favorable for efficiency improvements (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2021; Heller & Sieberg, 2010).
    - **Player count and group size**: Increasing size can make enforcement harder; efficiency is generally easier to sustain in smaller, more connected groups, though some models show non-monotonicity (Tarui et al., 2008).
    - **Information structure and monitoring**: More informative signals about others’ behavior allow more effective punishment and higher efficiency (ABREU et al., 1991; Kandori, 1992; Bhaskar & Thomas, 2019).
    - **Institutional structure/technology**: Peer vs. centralized punishment, whether punishment can be targeted or misused, who can punish whom, and punishment/reward availability all shape results (Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2021; Saak, 2012; Barron & Guo, 2021).
    - **Forgiveness/re-entry**: Temporary ostracism often yields better efficiency than permanent exclusion (Ali & Miller, 2016).
    - **Communication and chat**: Communication robustly increases cooperation and can enhance the effect of punishment—sometimes more important than punishment alone (Ostrom, 2000; Noussair & van Soest, 2014).
    - **Baseline efficiency**: Punishment delivers greatest marginal efficiency gain where control efficiency is low due to defectors; if conditional cooperators are dominant, adding punishment may crowd out trust and reduce welfare (van der Weele, 2012).
    - **Norms and social preferences**: A minority of enforcers may suffice for efficiency (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999), but social preference heterogeneity introduces fragility (Richter et al., 2013).

**Conflicts & Ambiguity:**
- Occasional negative or mixed findings arise in cases of:
    - **Crowding out** of intrinsic motivation (van der Weele, 2012; Festré, 2010).
    - **Extortion or misused punishment** reducing or eliminating group surplus (Barron & Guo, 2021).
    - **Costly punishment** that outweighs deterrence benefits (Anderies et al., 2011; Noussair & van Soest, 2014).
    - **Retaliation** or second-order punishment cycles undermining efficiency (reviewed qualitatively by Noussair & van Soest, 2014; Anderies et al., 2011).
- Some papers model only non-payoff behaviors (cooperation rate, norm observance); others provide explicit efficiency formulas with specified design moderators.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Main implication:**  
  Given game design dimensions and the average efficiency of the control (no-punishment) game, prediction of efficiency when peer punishment is enabled should be strongly moderated by:  
  - **Current (control) efficiency:** If baseline efficiency is low (typical for PGGs without punishment), enabling punishment often yields a large positive shift in efficiency—often approaching the social optimum. If efficiency is already high, the gain may be small, zero, or negative (van der Weele, 2012).
  - **Punishment parameters:** Higher punishment impact, lower cost, broader and more equitable application, and easier monitoring predict larger efficiency gains (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2021; Libois, 2022).
  - **Design dimensions:** Group size, number of rounds, available information (e.g., who can observe others' actions—'punishment_tech', 'show_other_summaries'), presence of communication ('chat'), and presence of reward or alternative sanctioning options ('reward_exists') are all key moderators (Wolitzky, 2013; Saak, 2012).
  - **Critical thresholds:** Some models predict non-linearities—efficiency jumps to maximal if punishment is credible and crosses a key parameter threshold; below that, little or no gain (Evans & Thomas, 2001; Libois, 2022).
  - **Fragility:** Gains are less robust where punishment can be misused, retaliated, or crowd out trust. Presence of communication or strong baseline norms may reduce marginal value of punishment or even make it negative.
- **Quantitative guidance:** Theory papers often provide explicit formulas mapping design dimensions to maximum sustainable efficiency, but few empirical calibrations. Formulas are generally valid for the conditions of the model: repeated interactions, no/patience limits, full or partial observability, and at least some players willing to enforce.
- **Non-payoff outcomes:** Where only cooperation rate or norm compliance data is present, these may suggest, but not guarantee, efficiency improvements—especially if punishment is costly.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions (addressed with clear impact on efficiency/prediction):**
- `player_count` (group size): repeatedly shown as a central parameter moderating sustainable efficiency and punishment efficacy (many theory papers).
- `num_rounds`: relevant wherever repeated game logic is explicit; longer/indefinite games favor sustainability of cooperation via threat of punishment.
- `mpcr`: Marginal per-capita return, central in nearly all models for determining the cost-benefit calculus behind cooperation and effectiveness of enforcement.
- `punishment_cost` and, where specified, `punishment_tech/punishment_magnitude`: Key determinants of whether punishment can deter defection without imposing prohibitive cost.
- `punishment_tech` (who can punish whom): critical for evaluating targeting, coverage, and risk of misuse.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`, and related information dimensions: Strongly highlighted for their moderating impact on observability and thus sustainability of efficient punishment.
- `all_or_nothing`: Appears as a relevant variant (binary vs. continuous contributions).
- `reward_exists`: Sometimes evaluated as an alternative or complement to punishment.

**Indirectly or Contextually Discussed Dimensions:**
- `chat`: Identified as robustly increasing efficiency, often moderating or interacting synergistically with punishment.
- `show_n_rounds`: Appears in models with explicit time horizons, patience, or information timing (ABREU et al., 1991; Ali & Miller, 2016).
- `default_contrib`: Sometimes varied in alternate framings (giving vs. taking).

**Sparse or Effectively Missing:**
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, and related reward dimensions receive only high-level treatment outside of certain reviews; where available, the effect is context-dependent and much less studied.
- Dimension-level empirical (experimentally validated) effects are mostly missing—design-to-efficiency mappings are nearly always theoretical.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical Scarcity:** The set is overwhelmingly theoretical; true experimental evidence on treatment efficiency (with real peer punishment interventions in PGGs) is limited, meaning effect sizes and boundary conditions are grounded in models, not direct data.
- **Parameter Calibration:** Many explicit results only provide comparative statics or qualitative thresholds, not quantitative mappings between all design features and observed efficiency.
- **Behavioral Outcomes ≠ Efficiency:** Non-payoff or behavioral outcome papers (focusing on cooperation rate/norm compliance) may mislead if directly mapped to efficiency, as these do not account for punishment costs or second-order dynamics.
- **Generalizability/Transfer:** Several studies analyze adjacent dilemmas (trust, common property, reputation systems); findings mostly transfer, but there are contextual caveats (e.g., effectiveness of ostracism vs. peer monetary punishment).
- **Ambiguity/Context Sensitivity:** Effects of punishment on efficiency are context-dependent; studies note possible negative effects (crowding out, extortion, costly retaliation) that may reduce or reverse efficiency gains.
- **Design Gaps:** Key prediction dimensions (such as the full spectrum of reward technologies, detailed monitoring/communication richness, and diverse sanctioning mechanisms) are treated unevenly or not at all empirically. Some notable modifiers (e.g., cultural/field context, learning/information constraints, social preferences) remain only qualitatively mapped to the prediction task.
- **Limited Dynamic/Temporal Modeling:** While repeated games are dominant, many models assume infinite patience, homogeneous agents, and ignore learning or population dynamics that may occur in real lab or field experiments.

---

**In summary:**  
This literature set delivers a robust theory-backed account: enabling peer punishment in public-goods-like games nearly always supports higher efficiency, often dramatically so, under standard conditions and provided certain design parameters (especially cost, monitoring, information, baseline efficiency) are favorable. However, exceptions, ambiguity, and context-dependence are persistent themes, and the absence of direct experimental measurement of efficiency given full variation on all 14 prediction dimensions is a major limiting factor for quantitative prediction tasks.
