# 1) Evidence Base

The provided literature base comprises 32 papers, with a mix of empirical (mainly laboratory experimental) and theoretical modeling studies. The set is moderately broad, spanning standard public goods games (PGGs), structural variants (mutual aid, team production, contest games), and adjacent social dilemma designs. The experimental papers typically use repeated, small-group, lab-controlled designs with clear manipulations of sanction mechanisms and game structure. The theoretical papers vary from formal game-theoretic proofs to simulation/mathematical models.

Many papers investigate the effects of punishment (peer, third-party, or centralized), with several studying related mechanisms (ostracism, approval, reputation systems, compensation). Direct measurement of efficiency (group payoff relative to the cooperation benchmark) is less common than measurement of cooperation rates or other behavioral metrics, but several studies do report efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes. The set includes a number of theoretical and empirical works with exact, close, or adjacent relevance for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:
- *Exact*: About one-third of the papers analyze standard PGGs or close laboratory analogs with explicit public goods structures, matching the target task (e.g., Hou et al., 2019; Lippert & Tremewan, 2021; Micheli et al., 2021; Molenmaker et al., 2019).
- *Close/Adjacent*: Several papers use structurally similar repeated social dilemmas (e.g., repeated prisoner's dilemma, mutual-aid games, contest designs), providing contextually relevant insights but with caveats on structural transferability.
- *Weak/None*: A handful are conceptual, narrative/thematic, or address only economic/psychological concerns without modeling or empirical analysis of PGGs.

**punishment_or_sanctions**:
- *Exact*: Many papers directly manipulate punishment (peer, third-party, centralized), with detailed parameterizations (cost, magnitude, mechanism).
- *Close/Adjacent*: Some focus on alternative or auxiliary sanction mechanisms (ostracism, rating, approval, compensation)—important adjacent interventions but different from direct costly punishment.
- *Weak/None*: A subset deal only with other incentive structures (e.g., reputation, framing, voluntary behavior) or do not manipulate sanctions.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
- *Exact or Close*: Several studies report group earnings, total coins, welfare, or efficiency as primary or secondary outcomes (e.g., Lippert & Tremewan, 2021; Hou et al., 2019; Faillo et al., 2020; Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020).
- *Adjacent*: Many others focus primarily on cooperation/contribution rates, use these as proxies for efficiency, or provide only qualitative or theoretical arguments about efficiency (e.g., Greenwood et al., 2018; Klempt & Pull, 2018).
- *Weak/None*: A few do not address payoff-based outcomes at all.

**Summary**: The literature is quite relevant to the target prediction task, especially for standard lab PGGs with or without punishment. Direct efficiency data is present in a subset, with the remainder providing theoretically or behaviorally mediated insights. Evidence is more sparse or purely conceptual for atypical mechanisms or real-world translation.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes** (primary interest for prediction): 
    - *Group payoff / total earnings*: Directly measured in several lab and theory papers (e.g., Hou et al., 2019; Lippert & Tremewan, 2021; Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020; Mihm & Toth, 2020; Faillo et al., 2020).
    - *Efficiency*: Defined as realized group payoff divided by maximum cooperative payoff; sometimes reported directly, other times inferred.
    - *Related constructs*: Welfare, surplus, coins generated—similarly meaningful for prediction.
- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**:
    - *Contribution rate / cooperation rate*: Most common in experimental papers, often a proxy for efficiency but not always.
    - *Punishment frequency*: Directly measured as number or cost of punishment acts.
    - *Norm compliance, approval ratings, ostracism events*: Used in studies focusing on institutional or reputational interventions.
    - *Choice of sanctioning action (punishment vs. compensation)*: Occasionally, as in Ferguson (2021).
    - *Attitudinal/psychological/neural outcomes*: e.g., beliefs about norms, brain activation patterns; more common in adjacent or neuroscience papers.

**Distinction**: While contribution/cooperation rates are informative and typically correlated with efficiency, only payoff-based outcomes can be directly used for efficiency prediction as required by the task.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment generally increases efficiency compared to no-sanction baselines in standard PGGs**, provided that the cost and magnitude of punishment are within effective ranges and sufficient players are willing to punish (Hou et al., 2019; Lippert & Tremewan, 2021; Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020; Greenwood et al., 2018).
    - *Empirical*: Robust group payoff/efficiency gains are observed when explicit costly punishment or community enforcement is enabled.
    - *Theory*: Analytical thresholds exist—punishment improves efficiency only if enough punishers and appropriate punishment parameters exist (Greenwood et al., 2018).

- **The effect size of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on several design dimensions**, particularly:
    - *Punishment cost and magnitude*: Lower costs/higher impact yield more frequent (and more effective) punishment, thus higher efficiency (Nikias & Sy, 2021; Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020).
    - *Monitoring structure & information visibility*: Efficient outcomes depend on whether contributions and punishments are observable (Mihm & Toth, 2020; Jindani, 2020).
    - *Group size and matching*: Effects may diminish or become more complex as groups get large or more heterogeneous (Toelstede, 2020; Hou et al., 2019).

- **Interface with other mechanisms**:
    - *Reward or approval alone is less effective than punishment or mixed mechanisms for efficiency gains* (Hou et al., 2019; Faillo et al., 2020).
    - *Costless approval/rating can increase efficiency but is highly sensitive to the cost of sanctioning* (Faillo et al., 2020).
    - *Ostracism (costless group-level punishment) and central enforcement both increase payoffs more than reputation-only mechanisms* (Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020; Ramalingam et al., 2019).
    - *Motivation crowding/framing may attenuate or reverse anticipated efficiency gains from punishment if the signal is interpreted negatively (crowding out intrinsic motivation)* (Klempt & Pull, 2018; Gold, 2020).

- **Not all punishment increases efficiency**: If punishment is too expensive, infrequent, or normatively undermined (by lack of punishers, presence of 'bad apples'), the efficiency gains may disappear or become negative (Greenwood et al., 2018; Lois & Wessa, 2019).

- **Control efficiency is an important moderator for potential treatment gains**: When baseline (no-punishment) efficiency is already high (e.g., repeated, small group with high MPCR or strong identity/reputation cues), the marginal value of enabling punishment may be smaller (see relevant context papers).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct prediction of treatment efficiency is best supported in standard laboratory PGGs** with well-specified design dimensions and measured control efficiency.
    - If the control game (no punishment) yields moderate or low efficiency, enabling peer punishment (with moderate cost and substantial impact) will, in most lab settings, *increase average efficiency by a meaningful margin* (e.g., up to 10-20% of the full cooperation benchmark in several studies: Hou et al., 2019; Lippert & Tremewan, 2021).
    - The gains are largest when the punishment institution is salient, punishment is not prohibitively costly, and information about contributions/punishments is fully (or at least partially) transparent.

- **Design dimensions to emphasize for prediction**:
    - *Punishment cost/punishment tech*: Lower costs and higher deduction magnitudes drive more effective sanctions and higher efficiency (Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020; Nikias & Sy, 2021).
    - *Player count / group size*: The positive effect of punishment on efficiency is attenuated in larger groups (Toelstede, 2020), unless community punishment is feasible (Jindani, 2020).
    - *Monitoring/information (show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id)*: Finer information supports robust punishment and higher efficiency (Mihm & Toth, 2020).
    - *Reward existence and cost*: Reward or approval systems increase efficiency only if costless; monetary rewards or approval as substitutes for punishment are typically less effective (Hou et al., 2019; Faillo et al., 2020).

- **When only behavioral (not payoff) outcomes are available**:
    - Contribution rate improvements from punishment *usually* translate to higher efficiency, but not always (e.g., if punishment is overused, or high costs offset gains). Caution is warranted in using behavioral proxies alone for prediction.

- **Limitations to prediction**:
    - When control efficiency is already close to the cooperative maximum, adding punishment may yield small or non-significant gains.
    - Contextual and psychological factors (e.g., perception of distrust, crowding out, the number/frequency of punishers, the signaling effect of sanctions) may qualitatively moderate quantitative predictions, sometimes reducing or in rare cases, reversing efficiency gains (Klempt & Pull, 2018; Gold, 2020).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions** (backed by multiple payoff-relevant findings):
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`: Heavily studied in lab designs; effects of group size, period number, and return multiplier on efficiency and cooperation are well documented.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Explicitly varied and analyzed for their effect on both punishment frequency and group efficiency (Nikias & Sy, 2021; Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020).
- `all_or_nothing`: Manipulated in several studies, though less often reported as a major moderator of punishment effects.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` (information/transparency): Theoretical and empirical work emphasizes visibility as crucial for effective punishment (Mihm & Toth, 2020; Jindani, 2020).

**Indirectly Informed Dimensions**:
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Studied less frequently than punishment, but evidence suggests reward alone is less effective; combinatory effects are possible (Hou et al., 2019).
- `default_contrib`: Occasionally present; framing (opt-in/opt-out) not a primary moderator in most results but may interact with other mechanisms.
- `chat`: Some studies report chat as an ancillary feature; its direct effect on efficiency when paired with punishment is not distinctly separated.
- `show_n_rounds`: Mostly included for control; some evidence that knowledge of horizon moderates long-term sustainability.
- `show_other_summaries`: Clear from networked and transparency-focused studies (Mihm & Toth, 2020).

**Only Contextually Discussed / Effectively Missing**:
- `punishment_magnitude` (distinct from cost): Some studies conflate cost and magnitude; explicit parametric variation is rare.
- Framing variables (`default_contrib`, specific chat protocols, nuanced reward mechanisms): Mentioned but not systematically varied or connected to payoff results in most papers.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Measurement Gaps**: Efficiency is less often measured/reported than cooperation or behavioral outcomes, and sometimes only inferred from related outcomes. Several key studies provide indirect rather than direct evidence.
- **Design Coverage**: Many studies use stylized, small-N lab PGGs with limited variation in player count, group composition, or sanctioning mechanisms. Results for larger, field-like, or more heterogeneous settings may be less reliable, especially regarding information structure and punishment capacity.
- **Sanction Institution Nuance**: Results from third-party or centralized punishment, approval, or ostracism mechanisms are often not directly transferable to pure peer punishment as specified in the prediction task.
- **Contextual Moderators**: Motivation crowding, social framing, and population heterogeneity (propensity to punish, thresholds for effective sanctioning) are highlighted as critical, but not systematically modeled across the evidence set, introducing ambiguity in prediction.
- **Scope Limits**: Some papers on variants (e.g., mutual-aid games, contest games, reputation mechanisms, sequential interaction) provide only adjacent or indirect evidence, not directly usable for quantitative prediction of efficiency with punishment in standard PGGs.
- **Conflicting Evidence**: There is some disagreement or ambiguity in conditions where punishment may crowd out intrinsic motivation or where approval/social rewards alone have large or small effects on efficiency.
- **Missing Data/Dimensions**: Some prediction-relevant variables (reward cost/impact, chat, detailed framing, identity visibility, etc.) are sparsely examined or conflated with other factors, limiting fine-grained, dimension-wise inference.

**Summary**: While the literature offers substantial empirical and theoretical support for predicting the positive effect of peer punishment on efficiency in standard PGG settings (contingent on punishment parameters and group structure), prediction in atypical environments or on underexplored dimensions should be made with appropriate caution and acknowledgment of these limitations.
