# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises **27 papers** with a mix of empirical experimental (lab and field) and observational studies, as well as a few theoretical or large-scale cross-cultural analyses. The set is **moderately broad for the core prediction task** (estimating the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments), with a subset of highly relevant experimental PGG-with-punishment studies (Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020), some closely related but structurally distinct designs (threshold PGGs, network games, trust games with reputation, etc.), and a considerable number of adjacent or context-focused studies (norms, reputation, culture, non-payoff behavioral outcomes).
- **Empirical-experimental PGGs with punishment and payoff outcomes:** Several core studies (Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018).
- **Adjacent games/mechanisms:** Trust games with gossip (Fonseca & Peters, 2018), PDs with network structure (Li et al., 2018), partner choice or competition (Pleasant & Barclay, 2018), reward alternatives (Yang et al., 2018).
- **Contextual and observational studies:** Cross-cultural preference reports, real-world norm enforcement, and reputational dynamics.
- **Theory/synthesis:** Some mechanism arguments (norm enforcement, reputation, metanorms) and meta-analyses.

Overall, the set provides **strong experimental evidence for standard repeated PGGs with punishment**, moderate evidence for some variants, and a wide range of context for understanding boundary conditions, but **direct empirical payoff evidence is sparse outside canonical PGGs**.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Relevance by dimension:**
- `pgg_or_variant`:
  - **exact:** Core experimental PGGs (Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Pleasant & Barclay, 2018; Lindström et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2018).
  - **close:** Games with threshold effects, private solutions (Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Lohse & Waichman, 2020).
  - **adjacent/weak:** Network PDs (Li et al., 2018), trust games, observational studies, real-world enforcement.
- `punishment_or_sanctions`:
  - **exact:** Many papers directly manipulate or observe peer punishment in PGGs or very close analogs.
  - **adjacent:** Some focus on reward, gossip, third-party punishment, or institutional design.
  - **weak/none:** Several papers lack explicit punishment or only mention it contextually.
- `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`:
  - **exact/close:** Payoff efficiency is a primary outcome in a handful of PGGs/threshold games and select adjacent games.
  - **adjacent:** Behavioral measures (contribution rate, norm enforcement) dominate many others.
  - **weak/none:** Large portion of papers do not report group payoff/efficiency, limiting direct utility for payoff prediction.

**Summary:**  
The literature **speaks directly and with high relevance to PGGs with peer punishment and efficiency outcomes in a subset**, and more weakly, contextually, or adjacently in the remaining majority. The strongest predictive leverage comes from the canonical repeated PGG punishment studies and synthetic reviews.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**
  - **Efficiency** (group payoff/maximum possible): Explicitly measured in core experimental PGG studies (Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019).
  - **Group payoff, total earnings, surplus:** Sometimes used as equivalents to efficiency.
  - **Trust game efficiency:** In adjacent trust/gossip studies (Fonseca & Peters, 2018).
  - **Not consistently measured:** In studies on punishment perception, reputation, or observational norm enforcement.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - **Contribution rates, cooperation rates:** Frequently measured as proxies or mechanisms (Arechar et al., 2018; Pleasant & Barclay, 2018; Yang et al., 2018).
  - **Punishment/reward frequency, assignment patterns:** Used to analyze mechanism drivers, not outcomes.
  - **Moral and reputational judgments, norm compliance, sanctions assigned:** The primary focus in many observational and psychological studies.

**Distinction is clear:** Payoff-based outcomes are **central but only present in a minority of papers**; behavioral metrics dominate the remainder and cannot be directly translated into efficiency predictions without additional assumptions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of core results:**

- **Standard PGG with peer punishment:**
  - **Peer punishment reliably increases contributions and efficiency.** With moderate-cost, effective peer punishment, **average efficiency and payoff increase** compared to no-punishment control. The effect is robust in lab and online samples, across moderate variation in design (Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020).
  - **Magnitude modulated by punishment cost and effectiveness:** If punishment is too costly or cost-ineffective, efficiency gains shrink or disappear (Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020).
  - **Behavioral mechanisms:** Punishment mostly targets defectors (prosocial punishment), supporting both higher contributions and payoffs (Arechar et al., 2018).
  - **Endogenous institution choice:** Allowing participants to vote for or adopt punishment institutions increases efficiency most reliably when the institution is adopted and cost-effective; mere opportunity is insufficient if the cost/benefit is unattractive (Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020).

- **Structural/contextual moderation:**
  - **Antisocial punishment and partner competition:** When environments make antisocial punishment salient (e.g., when players compete to be chosen), punishment can be directed at high contributors, undermining or even reversing efficiency gains (Pleasant & Barclay, 2018).
  - **Threshold/collective solutions, multigenerational games:** Punishment can increase the probability of group success, but the cost of mutual punishment **can offset efficiency gains**, resulting in little average net gain in group payoff (Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019).
  - **Network and multi-neighbor games:** In static networks, **punishment fails to improve efficiency and can erase benefits of network reciprocity**, sometimes even reducing average payoffs below non-punishment conditions (Li et al., 2018).

- **Reward vs. punishment:** 
  - **Reward mechanisms can raise both contributions and efficiency, often outperforming punishment** (because punishment, even if it raises cooperation, also destroys resources via cost) (Yang et al., 2018).

- **Reputation, gossip, and observability:** 
  - Indirect social sanctions (e.g., gossip, observability) **can boost efficiency in trust/cooperation games** (Fonseca & Peters, 2018; Bradley et al., 2018), though these are not punishment in the strict sense and evidence is largely adjacent.

**Ambiguity and limitations:**
- Efficiency gains from punishment are not universal: They **depend on punishment being mainly prosocial, not antisocial**, and being cost-effective.
- In some institutional/plural solution settings, **punishment increases costly feuding or mutual sanctioning**, **reducing efficiency** (Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018).
- **No consistent evidence** for efficiency improvements from punishment outside standard PGGs.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**If predicting average efficiency of a repeated PGG when moving from no-punishment to peer punishment (with canonical design):**

- **Expect an increase in efficiency** when punishment is:
  - Sufficiently cost-effective (typical laboratory cost/impact ratios, e.g., cost 1, impact 3 as in Arechar et al., 2018).
  - Prosocial punishment is dominant; institution is either imposed or reliably adopted.
  - Game lacks strong partner competition or individual solution alternatives.

- **Magnitude of increase**:
  - Direct experimental evidence shows per-period payoffs can rise ~15–20% of the gap to full cooperation, but efficiency gains can erode with higher punishment costs or ineffective norm enforcement.
  - Use control efficiency as a baseline, predicting a higher treatment efficiency if above conditions hold.

- **Caveats:** 
  - **In the presence of partner competition or antisocial punishment, or when punishment costs are high, efficiency gains may be nullified or reversed** (Pleasant & Barclay, 2018; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018).
  - **Threshold/collective solution games:** Gains in group survival probability may not translate into higher aggregate efficiency if punishment is costly.
  - **Non-canonical games (e.g., with individual solution alternatives, large networks, or different punishment structures):** Do **not expect robust efficiency gains**; punishment may have no effect or may even lower efficiency.

- **Reward vs. punishment:** Where applicable, reward mechanisms may increase efficiency more reliably and by a larger margin than punishment, especially if reward is budget-balanced and low-cost (Yang et al., 2018).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**
- **player_count:** Directly reported in multiple PGG and adjacent experimental studies.
- **num_rounds:** Core treatment in repeated games; many studies use 10–20 rounds; round effects observed (Arechar et al., 2018).
- **chat:** Presence/absence considered in several studies; can affect cooperation but less often focal in payoff results.
- **all_or_nothing:** Often specified; some evidence for effect differences vs. continuous contribution.
- **mpcr:** Explicitly reported in core experiments as marginal per-capita return; affects baseline and treatment efficiency.
- **punishment_cost:** Detailed and shown critical for efficacy of punishment as institution (Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020; Arechar et al., 2018; Li et al., 2018; Gross & De Dreu, 2019).
- **punishment_tech:** Some studies manipulate how punishment is implemented (cost-to-impact, timing).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Directly examined in a few papers focused on reward, not punishment (Yang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2018).
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries:** Occasionally manipulated or reported; limited evidence of direct payoff impact.
- **show_punishment_id:** Examined only in the context of peer vs. anonymous punishment (rare).

**Indirect/contextual evidence:**
- **default_contrib:** Usually not a primary variable; framing rarely focal but occasionally described.

**Sparse or missing:**
- **Some dimensions, such as detailed elaboration of feedback structures or identity-revealing designs, are only rarely or contextually discussed.**
- **Aggregate adoption rate in endogenous institution choice is discussed, but not often cross-analyzed with all design dimensions.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Design specificity:** Strongest efficiency findings are for canonical, repeated, small-group PGGs with traditionally-parameterized, cost-effective peer punishment. **Evidence is much weaker or ambiguous outside this domain**.
- **Structural moderation:** In environments with partner competition, multiple solution pathways, high likelihood of antisocial punishment, or significant minorities, the effect of enabling punishment is unpredictable and can even be negative (Pleasant & Barclay, 2018; Gross & De Dreu, 2019).
- **Payoff-focused evidence is limited in number:** **Most studies focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes**, which do not map consistently to efficiency.
- **Reward mechanisms and alternative sanctions:** Comparisons to punishment are sometimes made using between-study inferences rather than within-study payoff contrasts, so **replacement or interaction effects are not precisely quantified**.
- **Sparse coverage of some design dimensions:** Some practical game parameters (e.g., visibility of punishment/reward, feedback details, institution choice mechanisms) are covered only in specific studies, leaving **gaps in the cross-dimensional evidence**.
- **External validity/real-world generalizability:** Observational and contextual studies highlight variance in how punishment is perceived and used cross-culturally or in daily life, but this is not mapped directly to efficiency effects in games.
- **Quantitative uncertainty:** There is **a lack of meta-analytic quantitative estimates** of treatment–control efficiency deltas as a function of the full list of design dimensions.

---

## Overall summary

**The strongest available evidence supports the claim that enabling peer punishment in canonical repeated PGGs increases average efficiency relative to control, with this effect strongly moderated by punishment cost and institutional effectiveness** (Arechar et al., 2018; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020). However, **this effect is not robust to changes in structural context** (such as competition, available private solutions, or network structure): in such settings, efficiency gains may disappear or reverse due to antisocial punishment, costly sanctioning, or mutual feuds (Pleasant & Barclay, 2018; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018). Many studies measure only behavioral outcomes, not efficiency. Evidence directly tying most of the 14 design dimensions to efficiency outcomes is concentrated in a small subset of robust PGG-with-punishment experiments; most other evidence is only contextually or adjacently informative.

**Prediction of efficiency under punishment should lean heavily on core laboratory and review findings, using design dimensions (most directly, player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost, and effectiveness) to set expectations and caveats, and should annotate predictions for settings structurally divergent from canonical lab PGGs**.
