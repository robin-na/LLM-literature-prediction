# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large (102 sources) and covers a broad cross-section of literature, including lab experiments, field experiments, observational studies, and theoretical models. The majority of highly relevant papers are experimental lab studies using standard public goods games (PGGs), with a subset using close variants (e.g., common pool resources, n-person PDGs, or other social dilemmas). There is a strong presence of theory and simulation papers modeling punishment and cooperation dynamics. While the literature broadly spans mechanism design, psychological foundations, and real-world institutional analysis, the evidence most directly pertinent to the prediction task (treatment efficiency in PGGs with/without punishment) is empirical and concentrated in standard or near-standard lab PGGs with explicit measurement of group efficiency or related payoffs.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant:** The literature’s core is highly relevant (`exact` relevance) to PGG or very close variants, especially within experimental studies (e.g., Fehr et al., 2002; Gintis et al., 2003; Barclay, 2004; Decker et al., 2003; Masclet, 2003). Some papers use adjacent paradigms (e.g., dictator, ultimatum, trust, or resource dilemmas), offering weaker or indirect relevance.

- **punishment_or_sanctions:** The dominant focus is on peer punishment or related sanctioning in PGGs (`exact`). Several papers address collective, centralized, or alternative sanctioning structures (`close`), while others discuss reward or ostracism as alternative enforcement mechanisms. A sizeable fraction of papers are adjacent or context-only (discussion of punishment as a social concept or via observation cues).

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** A substantial number of key empirical and theory papers address group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff outcomes (`exact` or `close`). However, a noteworthy minority report only cooperation or contribution rates, not direct efficiency measures (thus `adjacent` or `weak` on efficiency). A nontrivial portion of studies are limited to psychological, attitudinal, or reputational outcomes and lack any payoff-based measures (`none` for efficiency).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes:** 
  - Many core empirical studies provide direct measurement of group efficiency (e.g., Fehr et al., 2002; Gintis et al., 2003; Barclay, 2004). Here, efficiency is explicitly reported as the ratio or level of total group earnings relative to the cooperative maximum.
  - Several other key works (e.g., Decker et al., 2003; Masclet, 2003; Ostrom et al., 1992; Brown et al., 2004) report group earnings, welfare, surplus, or surplus net of punishment costs, qualifying as direct or very close proxies.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
  - Numerous studies (e.g., Hilbig et al., 2012; Balliet et al., 2011; Mulder et al., 2006; Hilbig & Zettler, 2009) focus on contribution rate, cooperation, norm compliance, punishment frequency, or individual behavioral change, but do not report efficiency or net group payoff. 
  - Some research provides only psychological (e.g., trust, fairness, intentions) or reputational outcomes, which are not suitable substitutes for efficiency measurement in the prediction task.

- **Mixed/Indirect Outcomes:**
  - Some literature provides both behavioral and (simulated) efficiency estimates (e.g., Hilbig et al., 2012) or infers efficiency from contributions/punishments without explicitly reporting it.

- **Theoretical/Simulation Outcomes:**
  - Numerous theory papers model equilibrium payoffs or evolutionary stability (e.g., Boyd & Richerson, 1992; Gintis, 2003; Macy, 1993).
  - Some theory or ethnographic work focuses instead on mechanism stability, psychological traits, or norm dynamics without explicit payoff computation.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment often increases efficiency but only under specific conditions:** 
  - In standard, homogeneous PGGs, the introduction of costly peer punishment robustly increases cooperation and average group efficiency, often by 30-40% (Fehr et al., 2002; Gintis et al., 2003; Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Masclet, 2003; Barclay, 2004).
  - The efficiency boost from punishment is strongest when punishment is targeted at defectors, group structure is stable, punishment is effective (high impact-to-cost), and the baseline (control) game has low/no efficiency due to free riding.

- **Punishment effectiveness is highly moderated by group composition, heterogeneity, and cultural context:**
  - Cultural or group composition greatly affects the efficiency effects of punishment: some groups see efficient, positive returns, others see negative or no effect (Barclay, 2004; Reuben & Riedl, 2009).
  - In groups with privileged members or ethnic heterogeneity, punishment may increase inequality or be less effective, leading to smaller or negligible gains in efficiency or even efficiency losses due to misdirected or retaliatory punishment (Reuben & Riedl, 2009; Guala, 2012).

- **Punishment can be efficiency-destroying, especially if costs are high or misuse is prevalent:**
  - Where punishment is uncoordinated, severe, or motivated by emotion (rather than norm enforcement), its cost can outweigh cooperative gains, thereby reducing efficiency (Decker et al., 2003; Guala, 2012; Balliet et al., 2011).
  - Punishment tends to reduce efficiency when imposed alone (without communication or endogenous institution-building), or when control-game efficiency is already high.

- **Combination with other mechanisms (communication, endogenous choice) is often required for maximal efficiency:**
  - Communication or endogenous selection of punishment norms (e.g., voting on rules, group choice) amplifies the efficiency benefit of punishment (Ostrom et al., 1992).
  - In some designs, communication alone can produce efficiency gains as great or nearly as great as punishment.

- **Efficiency gains may take time to materialize:**
  - Introduction of punishment in repeated games can initially reduce efficiency due to the cost of punishment, with efficiency rising over time as cooperation stabilizes and the need for punishment fades (Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Balliet et al., 2011).

- **Alternative sanctioning mechanisms (ostracism, exclusion, centralized sanctioning):**
  - Exclusion/ostracism can function similarly to monetary punishment and produce significant efficiency gains, especially in small groups (Masclet, 2003; Kerr et al., 2009).
  - Centralized punishment (e.g., elected monitors) can be as or more effective than peer punishment, mainly when legitimacy is high (Grossman & Baldassarri, 2012; Hamman et al., 2011), but efficacy depends greatly on institutional trust and design.

- **Important moderators and caveats:**
  - Group size: Efficiency gains from punishment may be attenuated in larger groups (Boyd & Richerson, 1992; Kerr et al., 2009).
  - Punishment cost and effectiveness: There is a threshold on the punishment cost/impact ratio—if punishment is too costly or weak, it cannot sustain high efficiency (Cai & Kock, 2009; Fehr & Gintis, 2007).
  - Monitoring technology and information timing: Effective punishment and thus efficiency gains depend on timely, accurate monitoring (Abreu et al., 1991).
  - Reward and reputation: In some settings, reputation or reward can substitute for punishment as an efficiency-enhancer (Semmann et al., 2004; Kiyonari & Barclay, 2008), but are not the focus for the punishment prediction task.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct, empirical effect prediction is most justified in standard, homogeneous, repeated PGGs with individual, observable punishment of defectors, and moderate punishment costs.** If prediction conditions mimic these, enabling punishment can be expected to substantially increase efficiency, especially where control efficiency is low (Fehr et al., 2002; Gintis et al., 2003; Barclay, 2004; Fehr & Gintis, 2007).

- **Efficiency increases shrink or disappear in settings with:**
  - High baseline (control) efficiency,
  - Heterogeneous group composition (privileged groups, cultural outgroups),
  - Severe or emotional punishment (leading to high cost and inefficiency),
  - Large group size (diluted punishment impact),
  - Uncoordinated or unobservable punishment.

- **Game design dimensions that must be accounted for:**
  - Control game efficiency is a strong predictor; large positive effects occur from low controls, but incremental or even negative effects when controls are high.
  - Player_count, punishment_cost, mpcr, num_rounds, and punishment_tech all reliably moderate punishment effects and must be included.
  - Chat/communication, endogenous institutional choice, and visible information about others (i.e., monitoring, transparency) further moderate the effects; communication generally enables higher efficiency when combined with punishment.
  - Reward mechanisms or alternative sanctions (exclusion) should be flagged, as they can interact with or substitute for punishment in producing efficiency gains.

- **Scenarios with only behavioral (not payoff) outcomes:**
  - If only data on contribution/cooperation rates are available, efficiency predictions are less certain, as increased contributions can be offset or outweighed by the costliness or misuse of punishment (Decker et al., 2003; Balliet et al., 2011).

- **Where game details differ from standard PGGs (e.g., one-shot, adjacent paradigms), direct prediction is not warranted, and only qualitative expectations are justified.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` – consistently manipulated and analyzed in core experimental and theory literature (e.g., Fehr et al., 2002; Decker et al., 2003; Boyd & Richerson, 1992).
- `all_or_nothing` – continuous vs. binary contribution is a reported moderator.
- `chat` – presence/absence analyzed for its effects on efficiency, especially in combination with punishment (Ostrom et al., 1992; Hamman et al., 2011).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` – discussed as alternative or complementary mechanisms (Semmann et al., 2004; Kiyonari & Barclay, 2008).

**Indirectly Informed:**
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` – several experiments allude to the effect of information and visibility, but these dimensions are less frequently the object of direct manipulation or separate analysis.
- `default_contrib` – rare explicit analysis, but some framing and initial propensity insights are present.

**Only Contextually Discussed or Effectively Missing:**
- `show_punishment_id` – While punishment anonymity is mentioned, few directly test identification versus anonymity as a systematic treatment dimension.
- Framing dimensions (`default_contrib`) and fine-grained informational displays are only contextual inferences or reported as invariant.
- In many adjacent literature pieces (e.g., trust/dictator games, reputation cues), public goods structure and direct mapping to 14 design dimensions are missing.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Payoff outcomes often not reported:** A significant portion of the literature reports only on contributions/cooperation, with efficiency only inferred, not measured. This can lead to overestimation of punishment's value for efficiency, as higher contributions often coincide with higher punishment costs (Balliet et al., 2011; Decker et al., 2003).

- **Cultural, group-composition, and context dependencies underexplored:** Many experiments use relatively homogeneous, Western student populations; where group heterogeneity or real-world variation is examined, efficiency effects of punishment often attenuate, reverse, or become highly group-specific (Barclay, 2004; Reuben & Riedl, 2009).

- **Design dimension coverage uneven:** Some critical moderators (e.g., chat, information display, endogenous institution-building) are studied in only a minority of papers, limiting precise predictions for designs with these features altered.

- **Temporal, contingent, and institutional dynamics:** Efficiency effects may change over time (e.g., initial punishment costs high, later gains as cooperation stabilizes), and may be sensitive to the possibility of removing or adding punishment mid-game—a scenario not always studied.

- **External validity and generalizability:** Some scholars (e.g., Guala, 2012) caution that lab-based PGG findings do not always generalize to field contexts, where sanctions are often low cost, coordinated, or non-monetary; real-world institutions for sustaining efficiency often differ substantially from lab settings.

- **Mechanism distinction is sometimes blurred:** Not all 'punishment' in the literature is strictly comparable (e.g., monetary deduction, ostracism, exclusion, centralized sanctions), making direct effect size inference hazardous for some design variants.

---

**Summary:**  
Predictions of treatment efficiency (punishment enabled) from control efficiency and game design are well supported for standard, repeated, homogeneous PGGs with clearly specified punishment regimes, moderate group sizes, and well-designed information structures. Prediction accuracy drops in the face of group heterogeneity, high baseline efficiency, uncoordinated or severe punishment, and poorly specified or under-studied design dimensions. Caution is warranted in generalizing outside direct empirical tests—particularly to field settings or to designs with substantial use of communication, endogeneity, or alternative sanctioning/reward mechanisms.
