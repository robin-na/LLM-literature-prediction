# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is relatively broad, with 40 papers spanning laboratory experiments, analytic theory, and simulations. It includes a substantial number of directly relevant empirical studies on public goods games (PGGs) and close variants, as well as several theoretical models focused on punishment and payoff outcomes. There is a mix of standard linear PGGs, threshold PGGs, and a handful of adjacent social dilemmas (e.g., Prisoner’s Dilemma, trust games, networked and exclusion games). Theories and mechanism discussions complement the empirical literature by providing formulas and qualitative predictions about when and how punishment can impact efficiency. Both empirical and theoretical contributions directly address the prediction task, though the breadth means that contextual and boundary cases are discussed (e.g., peer versus centralized punishment, network structures, reward versus punishment, and social norms/reciprocity). While the evidence is strong for standard peer-punishment PGGs, empirical evidence is less dense for specialized variants (e.g., optional PGGs, spatial/networked PGGs, group size extremes, and games combining multiple incentives).

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact:** Many papers (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Gao et al., 2020; Botta et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2019; Greenwood et al., 2018; Kanitsar, 2021) analyze standard or canonical PGGs.
- **close:** Several theory and empirical studies work with threshold PGGs, optional PGGs, or structurally similar games such as exclusion-and-group-reformation or surplus allocation (Liao et al., 2021; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Rauwolf & Bryson, 2018).
- **adjacent/weak:** Some papers examine adjacent domains—e.g., the Prisoner’s Dilemma or trust games—discussing mechanisms generalizable to public-good-like interaction (Zhao et al., 2018; Nakamura, 2019; Matsuzawa & Tanimoto, 2018).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact:** Numerous studies focus directly on peer punishment, its cost, effectiveness, and mechanism details (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Gao et al., 2020; Botta et al., 2021; Greenwood et al., 2018; Kanitsar, 2021).
- **close/adjacent:** Other works address centralized or institutional punishment, exclusion, or refusal to cooperate (Grund et al., 2020; Di Guida et al., 2021), or costly signaling frameworks not always mapping directly onto peer punishment (Niu et al., 2020).
- **weak/none:** A minority of studies focus on games with no or only reputational, non-costly sanctions.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact:** Many key papers report efficiency, group earnings, welfare, or surplus allocation directly (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Gao et al., 2020; Botta et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Di Guida et al., 2021).
- **close/adjacent:** Some rely on proxy outcomes (sum of contributions, investment rates, average final wealth), or focus heavily on behavioral outcomes while providing some payoff-related discussion (Yang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020; Grund et al., 2020).
- **non-payoff:** Several report only cooperation/contribution rates, strategy frequencies, or other non-payoff behaviors, constraining direct support for efficiency predictions.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes:**  
  - **Group Efficiency (ratio of actual to maximal possible group payoffs):** Directly measured in a majority of highly relevant empirical and theoretical papers (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Gao et al., 2020; Botta et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Rauwolf & Bryson, 2018; Di Guida et al., 2021).
  - **Total Group Earnings, Welfare, Surplus Allocation, Average Final Wealth:** Also commonly reported as exact or close proxies for efficiency, sometimes under alternative labels.
  - **Group Investment or Success Rate (in threshold or exclusion games):** Used in some close-variant studies (Liao et al., 2021).

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  - **Contribution or Cooperation Rates:** Very widely measured; used frequently as the primary outcome in studies focused on behavioral mechanisms (e.g., Greenwood et al., 2018; Nakamura et al., 2019; Matsuzawa & Tanimoto, 2018; Niu et al., 2020).
  - **Punishment or Reward Frequency, Participation Rates, Norm Compliance:** Often recorded but are only meaningful to the prediction task insofar as they are proximate causes of payoff outcomes.
  - **Strategy Adoption Frequencies:** Used in evolutionary or agent-based models, but do not translate directly to group efficiency.

Importantly, the distinction is upheld in the literature: studies reporting non-payoff outcomes often do not make claims about efficiency, and caution is needed in interpreting them for payoff-based predictions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical and Theoretical Synthesis

- **General Effect of Peer Punishment:**  
  - Consistent empirical and theoretical evidence shows that, in standard public goods games, enabling peer punishment usually increases group efficiency (earnings relative to the full-cooperation benchmark)—but the effect is moderate on average and variable across contexts (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Gao et al., 2020; Botta et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021).
  - The positive effect is contingent on the willingness of players to punish free riders (Greenwood et al., 2018; Gao et al., 2020), the cost-effectiveness of the punishment mechanism (Kanitsar, 2021; Gao et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2019), and the absence of widespread anti-social or unproductive punishment (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).

- **Moderators of Punishment Efficiency Impact:**
  - **Punishment Cost-Effectiveness:** Lower punishment costs (higher impact per unit cost) reliably yield higher efficiency gains (Kanitsar, 2021; Zhang et al., 2019).
  - **Network/Group Structure:** In denser public good networks (everyone can punish everyone), efficiency effects are greater; in sparse or generalized exchange networks, punishment often fails to improve efficiency (Kanitsar, 2021).
  - **Cultural/Social Norms:** The presence of strong reciprocators (who reward norm adherence) enhances efficiency via punishment, while 'norm-keepers' (who punish both high and low contributors) diminish gains (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
  - **Consensus and Mechanism Design:** Collective/consensual punishment with low consensus thresholds is more effective than mechanisms requiring broad agreement or those that operate automatically but without flexibility (Gao et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2019; Botta et al., 2021).
  - **Baseline Efficiency:** Games with very high control efficiency sometimes gain little or even lose from added punishment due to overpunishment costs or crowding out; those with moderate or low baseline efficiency are more likely to gain (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Kanitsar, 2021).

- **Insufficient or Non-Positive Effects:**
  - **Weak or Automatic Punishment:** Punishment regimes that are weak, automatic, or centralized (rather than peer-driven) can reduce efficiency or have non-significant effects (Yang et al., 2020).
  - **High-Cost or Poorly Targeted Punishment:** If punishment is too costly, or is directed at non-defectors (antisocial punishment), efficiency gains are eliminated or reversed (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Matsuzawa & Tanimoto, 2018).
  - **Thresholds and Population Composition:** Sufficient willingness to punish is required; if the proportion of punishers is below threshold, punishment fails to improve efficiency (Greenwood et al., 2018).

- **Boundary Conditions and Complexity:**
  - **Optional/Loner Participation:** In optional PGGs, effective punishment can stabilize high efficiency, but only above certain parameter thresholds (Botta et al., 2021).
  - **Resource/Awareness Effects:** When resource limitation, wealth inequality, or visibility interacts with punishment, these can amplify or mitigate the efficiency effects (Zhang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020).
  - **Nonlinear or Threshold PGGs:** In games with collective thresholds or nonlinearity in returns, punishment's effectiveness is shaped by the group structure and the nature of the public good (Gao & Liang, 2020; Liao et al., 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Baseline:**  
  The efficiency of the control condition (no punishment) is a critical predictor; punishment will commonly increase efficiency above this baseline, especially when the initial efficiency is not already close to optimal.

- **Parameter Moderation:**  
  The magnitude of the efficiency gain when enabling peer punishment is strongly moderated by:
    - **punishment_cost** and **punishment_tech:** Lower cost/higher effectiveness amplifies efficiency gains.
    - **player_count:** Standard PGGs (n=4–6) show the typical results; larger groups or complex groupings may moderate or diminish the effect.
    - **num_rounds:** More rounds generally provide more opportunity for punishment to affect group behavior, but the effect may saturate or vary with endgame effects.
    - **mpcr:** Higher MPCR increases the returns to mutual cooperation and can facilitate greater efficiency gains via punishment, but very high MPCR can make punishment redundant.
    - **network/punishment structure:** Peer punishment in dense networks is effective; sparse/generalized networks show weaker or no gains.
    - **reward_exists:** The presence of a competing reward mechanism may substitute for, or interact with, punishment.

- **Threshold and Nonlinear Effects:**  
  There are critical thresholds in parameters (punisher prevalence, consensus threshold, punishment intensity) below which no improvement in efficiency occurs, and above which the boosts can be substantial.

- **Evidence Gaps:**  
  For games with chat/communication, default contribution framing, optional/voluntary participation mechanisms, and rich show_punishment_id/show_other_summaries manipulations, evidence in terms of efficiency effects is sparser.

- **Mechanism:**  
  Most efficiency improvement arises not from direct deterrence or reduction in free-riding, but from higher contributions by those already willing to cooperate spurred by the threat or anticipation of punishment.

**Summary Practical Guidance:**  
- For a typical PGG, enabling peer punishment increases predicted group efficiency relative to baseline/control, with the increase moderated up or down by punishment cost-effectiveness, network structure, and the mix of punishment strategies in the group. Do not expect a large or even positive effect if punishment cost is high, if the mechanism is automatic/centralized rather than peer-driven, or if norms support indiscriminate or antisocial punishment. The presence of strong reciprocators, low punishment costs, and dense interaction networks all boost the likely efficiency gain.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

## Directly Informed Dimensions:
- **player_count:** Well studied, especially in standard n=4–6 PGGs; larger group effects are mentioned but fewer data points.
- **num_rounds:** Commonly varied in both experiments and simulations; evidence supports round-number as a robustness or opportunity moderator.
- **mpcr:** Extensively manipulated; theoretical and empirical support for MPCR as a moderator.
- **punishment_cost / punishment_tech:** Central variables in many mechanism and effect-size analyses; critical moderators for predicted efficiency impacts.
- **all_or_nothing:** Studied both in binary and continuous PGGs; effect of punishment appears robust but parameter-dependent.
- **reward_exists:** Some comparative studies; reward can be a substitute or complement to punishment.
- **chat:** Manipulated in several lab studies, but efficiency consequences specifically (versus behavioral outcomes) are less direct.

## Indirectly Informed or Contextual Dimensions:
- **default_contrib:** Rarely addressed as a controlled design manipulation.
- **show_n_rounds / show_other_summaries / show_punishment_id:** Discussed in a few theory and experiment papers regarding information availability, tracking, and identity, but little efficiency-focused evidence.
- **punishment_tech (e.g., who can punish whom, centralized vs. peer):** Clear evidence that structure matters, especially network density and peer-vs-central distinction.
- **reward_cost, reward_tech:** Covered in studies comparing reward to punishment, but less central for settings where only punishment is enabled.

## Effectively Missing or Sparse:
- **Punishment/reward identity revelation (show_punishment_id):** Little/no direct efficiency evidence.
- **Default contribution framing:** Not directly evaluated in efficiency/punishment context.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limits of Generalizability:**  
  Most core results are on standard, linear PGGs (n=4–6, no or minimal chat, fixed MPCR, peer punishment). Generalization to highly nonlinear, spatial, threshold, or networked variants, or those with rich information/communication features, relies on adjacent or mechanistic evidence.

- **Behavioral vs. Efficiency Outcomes:**  
  Many studies report only contribution rates or behavioral outcomes; claims about efficiency must be limited to those directly measuring payoff-based metrics.

- **Empirical vs. Theory Gaps:**  
  Some of the strongest structural and moderator insights come from theory/simulation; empirical support may be limited for less standard design or population variants.

- **Cultural and Group Composition Effects:**  
  Papers indicate substantial between-group and between-population heterogeneity in punishment strategies and their effects on efficiency, with little ability to predict which types of groups an arbitrary lab sample (or field sample) will resemble. This introduces uncertainty into predicted effect sizes when group composition or broader cultural norms are not known (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).

- **Sparse Evidence on Certain Dimensions:**  
  Several design parameters relevant to the prediction task (chat, identity revelation, contribution framing, information feedback) are underexplored with respect to efficiency outcomes in punishment-enabled designs.

- **Edge-Case Game Structures:**  
  Settings with optional participation, loner options, nonlinear returns, and generalized exchange/sparse networks can show null or negative punishment effects, underscoring limits on simple additive expectations of efficiency gain.

- **Ambiguity and Variance:**  
  Some evidence points to moderation by details such as consensus threshold, punisher prevalence, and population structure, highlighting that effect direction/magnitude is not always uniform and can vary across plausible experimental or field settings.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature strongly supports that introducing effective peer punishment in canonical public goods games (and close analogs with standard parameters) generally increases efficiency relative to controls, but the exact magnitude and reliability of this effect is sensitive to the punishment mechanism’s cost-effectiveness, group structure, baseline efficiency, and population composition. Most evidence for design-dimension moderation is empirical or theoretical, with several dimensions (information structure, identity revelation, communication/chat, and contribution defaults) still lacking robust links to efficiency outcomes in the context of punishment. Predictions should thus integrate known moderators but caveat settings with design or population factors outside the main evidence base.
