# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 59 sources, with a significant proportion being laboratory experimental studies directly on public goods games (PGGs) and a substantial number of theoretical (mechanism, evolutionary, and game-theoretic) papers. There is also a mix of field experiments and studies on closely related but non-PGG environments such as trust games, bargaining games, coordination (minimum effort) games, and repeated Prisoner’s Dilemmas. The literature base is broad with varied environments, but payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group earnings) are systematically reported and analyzed in only a subset of the experimental PGG studies and select theory papers.

Empirical results dominate for standard PGG environments, especially laboratory designs, and these frequently test the effects of enabling or disabling punishment (and sometimes reward) while measuring both contribution rates and efficiency or group payoffs. Several theory papers provide nuanced predictions, mechanism analyses, and equilibrium conditions relevant to PGGs with and without punishment.

The evidence is strongest where experimental studies report both control (no-punishment) and treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency, and directly vary the 14 prediction-relevant design dimensions. However, some design dimensions are less frequently or systematically manipulated (e.g., chat, default contribution, summary feedback options), and field or applied studies often only report behavioral outcomes without direct efficiency measures.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact:** Most of the experimental and theoretical evidence is for standard or near-standard PGGs (e.g., Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Carpenter et al., 2012; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Castillo et al., 2021).
- **Close/Adjacent:** A secondary group of papers addresses adjacent environments—minimum effort games, repeated Prisoner's Dilemmas, trust or bargaining games, team investment or surplus allocation—which are structurally related and often illuminate mechanisms but may not precisely map onto PGG design dimensions.
- **Weak/None:** Some theoretical or field experimental papers lack direct PGG relevance, either due to different interaction structures (e.g., two-player games, partnership formation).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact:** A core set of laboratory studies and theory papers directly manipulate the presence/absence and form of punishment or sanctioning, mapping closely to the key design dimensions (e.g., punishment cost, effectiveness, technology).
- **Close/Adjacent:** A significant number address related forms (centralized punishment, third-party sanctions, exclusion, voluntary refusal to cooperate, non-monetary/approval-based sanctioning, or reputational feedback).
- **Weak/None:** Some deal exclusively with reward, institutional choice, or other forms of incentive, or have no sanction mechanism.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact:** Many laboratory PGG studies and several theoretical works report group efficiency (mean group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum), total earnings, welfare, or surplus.
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some studies only report contribution rates but indicate implications for efficiency, or focus on adjacent outcomes like minimum group member payoff (minimum effort games), productivity per work time, or bargaining surplus.
- **Weak/None:** A significant minority restricts reporting to non-payoff behavioral outcomes—contribution rates, punishment frequency, norm compliance, or feedback usage.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Directly Informing Efficiency):**
- **Group Efficiency:** Ratio of actual group earnings to maximum possible (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Carpenter et al., 2012; Kanitsar, 2021; Peng, 2022).
- **Group Payoff/Earnings:** Directly measured as average or total group earnings in both control and punishment treatments.
- **Surplus/Net Profits/Welfare:** Used in some adjacent games or theoretical models (minimum effort games, surplus allocation, donation/prisoner’s dilemma with repetitive or large-N structure).
- **Related Payoff Measures:** Minimum group earnings (minimum effort), share of surplus allocated, total coins generated.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Do Not Directly Measure Efficiency):**
- **Contribution/Cooperation Rates:** Widely reported; sometimes used as proxies when payoff data is absent.
- **Punishment Frequency/Intensity:** How often and how much players punish (or reward).
- **Norm Compliance, Rejection/Approval, Exclusion, Reputation Indices:** Used particularly in adjacent or theory-focused papers.
- **Belief Elicitation, Trustworthiness, Social Norm Perceptions:** Address behavioral underpinnings but not efficiency.

**Distinct Separation:** Many papers note an explicit distinction that higher contributions or cooperation do not automatically imply higher efficiency due to the direct costs of punishment.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Evidence (Direct Efficiency/Payoff Results):**
- **Punishment often increases cooperation/contribution but does not always increase efficiency**: The cost of punishment frequently outweighs gains from increased cooperation, leading to unchanged or reduced efficiency compared to controls (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Calabuig et al., 2024; Abbink et al., 2004).
- **Reward tends to increase both cooperation and efficiency** (when present) and is consistently preferred when compared to punishment (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Peng, 2022).
- **Positive efficiency effects of punishment are conditional**: Efficiency gains from punishment are observed in some settings, particularly when punishment is costless, highly effective, or rarely needed (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Carpenter et al., 2012; Kanitsar, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Annen, 2011; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).
- **Network structure and institutional form are major moderators**: Efficiency effects of punishment depend critically on network topology (who can punish whom), centralization (manager/third-party control), and group openness/migration (Carpenter et al., 2012; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Antisocial punishment and feuding can erase efficiency gains**: When punishment can be retaliated or is assigned based on ambiguous or antisocial norms, efficiency drops or remains unchanged vs. control (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Thöni, 2014).
- **Behavioral context (cultural/subject pool, expectations, prior corruption exposure, norm composition) matters**: The mix of strong reciprocators (prosocial punishers) and norm-keepers (punish regardless of other's behavior) predicts the direction/magnitude of the efficiency effect (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016).
- **Centralized punishment often has more positive efficiency effects** than peer punishment, especially when well designed and managed by prosocial actors (Castillo et al., 2021; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010).
- **Specific design features can easily tip the balance**: Features such as multiple punishment stages (retaliation possible), identity visibility, cost/impact ratio, and exclusion options all critically shape the efficiency outcome.

**Theoretical Mechanism Findings:**
- **Efficiency gains are possible if punishment is strong, targeted, and credible**—full cooperation can be sustained, and efficiency approaches the social optimum if defectors are deterred with high enough probability/severity and the cost/benefit structure permits (Botta et al., 2021; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).
- **Optimal punishment is mild, rare, and used only as threat** in high-trust/high-entitlement groups—minimizing actual costs and maximizing efficiency (Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Annen, 2011; Jones, 1999).
- **Group and institution selection effects**: Baseline efficiency and group composition affect whether punishment can push outcomes toward full cooperation (Carpenter & Matthews, 2010).

**Cross-paper Disagreement/Ambiguity:**
- Some studies find punishment reliably reduces efficiency (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Calabuig et al., 2024); others find modest positive effects depending on punishment mode, cost, and structure (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021).
- The sign and magnitude of the efficiency change upon enabling punishment are not uniform and are frequently contingent on both design and group-level behavioral context.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given a specific set of design dimensions and control efficiency:
- **Punishment often raises contributions but may lower or only modestly raise efficiency.** The direct cost of punishment can quickly offset any group benefit unless punishment is highly effective, rare, and mostly motivational rather than actively used (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Direct mapping from control to punishment-enabled efficiency is possible when design dimensions are very similar to the benchmark experiments.** If the underlying control efficiency is already low (typical PGG decay), mild or well-controlled punishment is more likely to increase efficiency, provided antisocial punishment and feuding are minimized (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021).
- **Key moderators for the size and sign of the effect:**
    - **Punishment Cost and Impact:** Low cost and/or high impact (fine per cost) support efficiency gains; high cost, low impact do not.
    - **Punishment Network/Topology:** Complete networks or centralized (manager/third-party) punishment are more likely to support efficiency than sparse or directed punishment networks.
    - **Information and Identity:** Visible, full assignment and multiple punishment stages can backfire via retaliation/feuding (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - **Cultural/Group Context:** More strong reciprocators and fewer norm-keepers in a group predict greater efficiency gains from punishment (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
    - **Institutional Design:** Migration/open group structures benefit more from formal (centralized) punishment (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022). Voting on institutions per se is not necessarily efficiency-enhancing; it depends on outcomes chosen (Kocher & Matzat, 2016).
- **Controls with higher efficiency (baseline cooperation) can sometimes see diminished or negative returns from enabling punishment, especially if punishment is predominantly antisocial or inefficiently targeted.**
- **Parameters untested in direct efficiency outcome studies, eg. chat or opt-in/opt-out framing, provide little systematic guidance and must be scored as unknown or contextually inferred from adjacent designs.**
- **In summary:** Prediction should weigh design proximity to the main benchmark studies, check for presence of risky features (e.g., multiple punishment stages, retaliation risk, high punishment cost), and allow for positive, zero, or negative changes in efficiency based on these moderators. Absolute efficiency improvement cannot be reliably assumed.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed (repeatedly manipulated and essential for effects):**
- **player_count**: Most studies specify and examine 4–5 player groups; some extend to larger groups, but efficiency effects of punishment are empirically studied mainly for 3–5 player designs.
- **num_rounds**: Strong manipulation; repeated games of 6–30 rounds frequently analyzed, and time path of efficiency is often critical (decay, learning, retaliation).
- **mpcr**: Explicitly varied; higher MPCR (benefit of cooperation) generally increases overall contributions, but efficiency effects of punishment remain mixed.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Central; both cost and effectiveness/fine per cost are varied and shown to be key drivers of efficiency impact.
- **all_or_nothing**: Both continuous and all-or-nothing designs are included; effect on efficiency of punishment is directly analyzed.
- **centralization**: Peer punishment versus central manager/third-party or formal punishment explicitly compared.
- **network structure**: Who can punish whom and how richly connected networks are (e.g., Carpenter et al., 2012; Kanitsar, 2021).

**Indirectly Informed/Contextual (sometimes manipulated, sometimes just reported):**
- **chat**: Very rarely directly manipulated in conjunction with punishment in payoff-focused studies; generally absent, but known in adjacent games to facilitate higher efficiency (Brosig et al., 2004).
- **default_contrib**: Contribution framing (opt-in vs. opt-out) is occasionally reported/framed but not a main focus.
- **reward_exists, reward_tech, reward_cost**: Select studies include these; reward is generally found to increase both contributions and efficiency more consistently than punishment (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Peng, 2022).
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Public/private feedback and summary information are occasionally manipulated and show sometimes counterintuitive effects (public monitoring can decrease efficiency when not paired with sanctions—Becchetti et al., 2015).

**Effectively Missing/Sparse:**
- No systematic evidence on the isolated effects of **chat**, **default_contrib**, or complex feedback structures apart from the presence/absence of punishment itself.
- Some dimensions (e.g., visibility of punishment/reward identity, granularity of summary information, opt-in framing) may be discussed anecdotally or in adjacent games but lack empirical studies reporting efficiency transitions due to those features.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Non-uniformity of Efficiency Effects:** Punishment’s effect on efficiency is not consistent across studies—even holding design parameters constant. Minor contextual features or group composition can flip the result.
- **Sparse Manipulation of Some Dimensions:** Critical design features such as chat, opt-in framing, and feedback/reporting mechanisms are rarely isolated for study in conjunction with efficiency outcomes, leaving large areas of the design space uncovered.
- **Behavioral Outcomes ≠ Efficiency:** Many studies only report on contribution/punishment rates, but do not measure net payoffs or efficiency, making it risky to infer efficiency effects from cooperation data alone.
- **Limited Field Generalizability:** Most evidence derives from laboratory PGGs with monetary stakes and student samples; field settings and real-world institutional characteristics are underexplored or yield contrary results (Noussair et al., 2015).
- **Adjacency and Interpretation Risks:** Several informative theoretical models and experimental results concern structures adjacent to PGGs (trust/bargaining games, minimum effort, prisoner's dilemma, surplus allocation), which may not transfer quantitatively to PGGs without adjustment.
- **Group-Specific Effects:** Cultural or demographic factors (proportion of strong reciprocators, presence of norm-keepers, social capital, past exposure to corruption) can override or moderate predicted efficiency effects from design dimensions alone.
- **Lack of Data on Rarely Used Complex Designs:** Features like multiple punishment stages, identity visibility, and endogenous institution selection are underreported regarding direct efficiency outcomes, contributing to prediction uncertainty.

In summary, while the literature provides substantial experimental and theoretical support for how design dimensions and control efficiency moderate the effect of punishment on group efficiency in PGG-like environments, there remains significant contextual and parameter-specific ambiguity, considerable gaps for certain dimensions, and a critical need to distinguish between increased cooperation and increased efficiency. Direct prediction is best grounded in close design matches to the core PGG studies with explicit net payoff measurement, with only cautious extrapolation to wider design or population contexts.
