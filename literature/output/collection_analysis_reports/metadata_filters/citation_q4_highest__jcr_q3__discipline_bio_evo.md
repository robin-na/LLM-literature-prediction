# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is **theory-dominated**: the vast majority of the 47 included sources are formal theoretical or simulation models (with some literature reviews), and there are only a few genuine empirical (lab or field experiment) papers directly relevant to the downstream prediction task. The set is **broad** in coverage of social dilemma theory, spanning public goods games (PGG), indirect reciprocity, reward, punishment, migration, spatial structure, and evolutionary mechanisms for cooperation, but **narrow** when it comes to direct empirical evidence from actual PGG experiments measuring treatment effects on efficiency under varied design dimensions.

Most papers focus on **fundamental mechanisms**—such as punishment, reward, social norms, group size, and network structure—using formal models to map conditions for cooperation and efficiency. Several review or discuss laboratory findings, but few report parameterized, empirical outcomes mapped to all the game design dimensions. A minority of papers are **empirical studies** (e.g., Ashlock et al., 1996; Rilling et al., 2008) but, even there, most do not report payoff-based efficiency outcomes.

**Summary:** This is a *theory-rich*, empirically thin evidence base, offering deep mechanistic insight but with limited direct quantitative or experimental mapping to the full set of prediction-relevant game design parameters.

# 2) Task Relevance

Three `target-relevance` axes are specified: PGG or variant, punishment or sanctions, and efficiency or related payoff outcome.

- **pgg_or_variant:**
  - **Exact:** Numerous papers develop theory for the canonical PGG or immediate extensions—especially Cressman et al. (2012), Gintis (2000), Rand et al. (2010), Henrich & Boyd (2001), Hilbe et al. (2015).
  - **Close:** Some focus on related \(n\)-player games with similar structure, but not standard PGGs (e.g., group-structured indirect reciprocity).
  - **Adjacent/Weak:** Several are about dyadic games, the PD, or Ultimatum, or provide high-level social dilemma models.

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**
  - **Exact:** Many include explicit models of peer or institutional punishment as a central mechanism (e.g., Cressman et al., 2012; Gintis, 2000; Rand et al., 2010).
  - **Close/Adjacent:** Others treat punishment analogously (exit, partner choice, refusal) or focus on reward, reputation, or norms, rather than explicit costly punishment.
  - **None:** Several key theory papers on efficiency or cooperation exclude punishment.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
  - **Exact:** Some directly analyze group payoff as a fraction of maximum, group earnings, or welfare (e.g., Cressman et al., 2012; Henrich & Boyd, 2001; Gintis, 2000; Gintis et al., 2001; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).
  - **Close/Adjacent:** Many focus primarily on cooperation rates or frequencies of strategies, with efficiency only indirectly inferred or discussed.
  - **Weak/None:** A considerable fraction focus on behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates, retaliation likelihood), reputational perceptions, or neural correlates, not payoff.

**Summary:** For the downstream task of predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions **in PGG-like environments with/without peer punishment**, this evidence base is rich in directly relevant theory, but sparse in parameter-matched empirical payoff results. Task relevance is often **exact** or **close** on PGG and punishment, but less consistent on direct efficiency measurement.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**:  
  - **Group efficiency** (payoff as a proportion of the maximum possible), **total earnings**, **group welfare**, and **mean fitness** are primary outcomes in theory papers examining evolutionary stability, replicator dynamics, and equilibrium payoffs (e.g., Cressman et al., 2012; Gintis, 2000; Gintis et al., 2001; Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Ohtsuki & Iwasa, 2004, 2006).
  - **Empirical measurement of efficiency** is rare: most laboratory studies focus on **contribution rates** or observe behavior rather than directly reporting group efficiency.

- **Non-payoff (behavioral) outcomes**:  
  - **Contribution/cooperation rates, frequency of punishment, norm compliance, strategy frequency** dominate the evolutionary and lab literature.
  - Several papers focus on **reputational perception**, **emotional/neural responses**, or **partner choice**, which are only indirectly related to efficiency.

- **Key distinctions**:  
  - Several theoretical analyses equate **higher cooperation rates** with **higher expected efficiency**, but this is only true for some games/designs; in some scenarios, punishment can reduce earnings through overuse or antisocial punishment.
  - Some simulation studies report **cost-effectiveness** of punishment (cooperation gain per unit punishment cost), distinct from net efficiency.

**Summary:** While many papers support the assumption that enabling (pro-social) punishment boosts both cooperation and efficiency, most direct efficiency analyses are theoretical or computational. Where empirical data exists, it is usually about behavior, not realized group welfare.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**SYNTHESIS ACROSS PAPERS, focusing on when enabling punishment increases efficiency (“treatment effect”), and the moderating role of game design dimensions:**

- **General consensus:** Enabling (pro-social, sufficiently effective, not too costly) punishment in standard PGGs almost always increases group efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline, by stabilizing higher contributions and deterring free-riding (Cressman et al., 2012; Gintis, 2000; Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Henrich & Boyd, 2001). This is robust across group sizes, MPCRs, and enforcement types, *given pro-social punishment and minimal antisocial retaliation.*

- **Crucial caveats/moderators:**
  - **Anti-social punishment:** If game design allows defectors to punish cooperators (“anti-social punishment”), efficiency gains can disappear or reverse—enabling punishment may do nothing or even lower payoffs relative to control (Rand et al., 2010; Hauser et al., 2014).
  - **Retaliation/visibility:** If punished players can identify and retaliate against punishers, especially in larger groups or with cheap retaliation, the efficiency benefit of punishment is greatly reduced (Janssen & Bushman, 2008).
  - **Cost/benefit structure:** Punishment must not be prohibitively costly, and its effectiveness (magnitude relative to cost) is critical. High punishment cost or low impact can lead to a net loss of efficiency (Gintis, 2000; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
  - **Institutional vs. Peer Sanctioning:** Institutional punishment (especially with cost-sharing) is predicted to be more efficient than peer punishment (Cressman et al., 2012; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).
  - **Reward vs. Punishment:** Rewards alone can sustain some cooperation, but do not reliably produce full efficiency or stability; the combination of reward and punishment appears optimal in theory (Cressman et al., 2012).
  - **Conditional vs. Unconditional Punishment:** Conditional (group-based) punishment is predicted to achieve higher cooperation per punishing cost, particularly when punishment is expensive (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).

- **Group size effects:** Increasing group size generally reduces baseline (no-punishment) efficiency and cooperation; punishment can close this gap, but the required strength and cost-benefit levels become more demanding as groups grow larger (Hilbe et al., 2015; Gintis, 2000; Suzuki & Akiyama, 2007).

- **Spatial/network structure:** The effectiveness of punishment may depend on the population/network structure, sometimes making peer punishment more sustainable (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013), but the evidence is mainly theoretical.

- **Norms, reputation, and indirect enforcement:** Reputation-based (costless) 'punishment' (e.g., losing standing, justified refusal) can also sustain high efficiency, sometimes more robustly than costly punishment (Ohtsuki & Iwasa, 2004, 2006; Brandt & Sigmund, 2004; Chalub et al., 2006)—this blurs with standard PGG only when such mechanisms are directly implemented.

- **Mechanism arguments vs. evidence:** Most findings are from formal models and simulations, not empirical measurement of realized efficiency in matched experimental settings.

**Summary:** The effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is **strongly positive** when punishment is pro-social and effective but is **modulated or muted** by the potential for anti-social punishment, retaliation, high cost, or design features that undermine targeting of defectors.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How the literature should inform prediction of treatment efficiency (punishment enabled) given control efficiency and game design:**

- **If the game implements peer punishment that is targeted, not prohibitively costly, and does not allow anti-social punishment or easy retaliation**, theoretical and simulation models suggest a **substantial increase in average efficiency** relative to control, with the absolute change modulated by group size, MPCR, and punishment parameters.

- **If anti-social punishment is permitted**, or if the platform makes retaliation easy and cheap, **do not expect a positive effect**; efficiency may remain unchanged or even decrease (Rand et al., 2010; Hauser et al., 2014; Janssen & Bushman, 2008).

- **Efficiency gains are maximized** when:
  - **Punishment cost is low and impact high** (Gintis, 2000).
  - **Punisher anonymity is maintained**, limiting retaliation (Janssen & Bushman, 2008).
  - **The baseline (control) efficiency is low** (large groups, low MPCR)—punishment delivers a larger marginal gain.
  - **Punishment is designed as conditional or institutionally pooled** (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).

- **Reward mechanisms** alone can slightly increase efficiency but not reliably stabilize it; the **combination** of reward and punishment is optimal according to theory (Cressman et al., 2012).

- **Reputation-based refusals** (costless 'punishment') can sometimes achieve similar or higher efficiency, but only if all observers share coherent social norms (Ohtsuki & Iwasa, 2004, 2006; Chalub et al., 2006).

- **For prediction, map as follows:**  
  - Start from measured control efficiency (no punishment).
  - Assess whether the design **allows only pro-social punishment** (vs. anti-social), and whether **punisher anonymity/retaliation is possible**.
  - Adjust for **punishment cost** and **technology**: lower cost and higher impact imply larger efficiency gains.
  - **Group size**, **rounds**, and **MPCR**: Larger groups and more rounds tend to depress baseline efficiency but allow larger possible gains.
  - If the environment permits **retaliation**, **anti-social punishment**, or **punisher identification**, expect smaller or no efficiency gain.

**Key caveat**: Nearly all guidance is based on **theoretical predictions**, not direct empirical effect sizes.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

Among the **14 design dimensions**, the following are best and most directly informed:

- **player_count**: Directly modeled; group size is a critical parameter in nearly all theory papers, consistently showing larger groups have lower baseline cooperation/efficiency.
- **num_rounds**: Common in modeling repeated vs. single-shot games; more rounds can increase scope for cooperation and punishment.
- **mpcr**: Core parameter (often "multiplication factor" or "enhancement factor"); higher MPCR makes cooperation more profitable and generally boosts both baseline and potential treatment efficiency.
- **punishment_cost** and **punishment_tech**: Key moderators of punishment effectiveness and efficiency gains; lower cost and higher impact are associated with larger benefits (Gintis, 2000; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
- **all_or_nothing**: Present in some models (threshold PGGs) but not consistently analyzed for its effect on efficiency change with punishment.
- **reward_exists/cost/tech**: Discussed in contrast to punishment and in combined incentive models (Cressman et al., 2012); presence of reward can influence the effect size of punishment.
- **show_punishment_id**: Discussed indirectly (Janssen & Bushman, 2008) as a determinant of retaliation risk, in terms of punisher anonymity's impact on punishment's effectiveness.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries**: Sometimes captured in parameters involving information structure, but rarely explicitly.
- **chat, default_contrib**: Seldom addressed directly—empirical lab work sometimes includes communication but this is not central in most theory models.

**Design dimensions that are only** *contextually discussed* **or effectively missing**:
- **chat**: Rarely modeled.
- **default_contrib**: Not addressed.
- **show_other_summaries**, **show_n_rounds**: Information about group or round structure is discussed broadly but not specified as a tested dimension.

**Key dimensions where evidence from this set is most actionable**:  
- **player_count**, **num_rounds**, **mpcr**, **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech**, **reward_exists**, **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**, **show_punishment_id** (via mechanisms affecting retaliation).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of direct, parameter-matched empirical evidence:** The literature is dominated by theoretical modeling, with few direct experiments reporting treatment-control efficiency changes across varied parameter sets.
- **Indirect operationalization of key variables:** Many studies equate cooperation rates with efficiency but do not provide clear mappings to group payoff/efficiency, especially in the presence of costly punishment.
- **Sparse empirical variation across less-modeled dimensions:** Chat, framing (default contribution), and real-world information treatments are rarely modeled, limiting external validity.
- **Possible overstatement of punishment benefits:** Most theory assumes punishment is pro-social and well-targeted; in real-world or poorly controlled settings, anti-social punishment, retaliation, or mis-targeting can erode or reverse efficiency gains—highlighted in a subset of models, but often ignored in predictions.
- **Norm- and institution-dependence:** Many positive results for efficiency from punishment require strong assumptions about social norms, institution design, and the non-existence of second-order free-riding or anti-social behavior, which may not generalize to all experimental or field contexts.
- **Ambiguity and disagreement remain:** Where anti-social punishment or retaliation is possible, the predicted effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is contentious; some models predict gains, others losses, depending on minor differences in mechanism.
- **No quantification of effect size:** While directionality is (theoretically) clear under standard pro-social punishment, actual effect magnitudes and their parameter sensitivity are almost entirely untested empirically within this paper set.

---

**In sum**: This literature base provides *mechanistically detailed, theory-based guidance* about when and how enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like settings should increase group efficiency given knowledge of the game design and baseline (control) efficiency. However, most findings are theoretical, not empirical; empirical payoff effects are rarely measured; and core moderators (anti-social punishment, retaliation, cost/benefit ratios) are critical in determining the sign and size of the effect. Prediction should be cautious and attentive to these moderators, and should not interpolate beyond the design dimensions, populations, and outcome definitions for which at least indirect evidence is available in the synthesized literature.
