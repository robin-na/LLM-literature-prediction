# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of a broad collection of 59 studies, primarily empirical lab experiments (dominant), supported by theory-based simulation/modeling papers and a small number of field/observational and adjacent-topic works. The empirical coverage ranges from classic linear public goods games (PGGs) and close variants (e.g., threshold PGGs, CPR games, hierarchical or centralized punishment designs), to games with adjacent incentive, information, or institutional structures. A subset (~10-12) of papers provides **exact** empirical evidence on the core prediction task: the efficiency impact of adding punishment to a PGG, measured as group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum. Theory/simulation papers add complementary predictions for similar design spaces. However, many papers—especially those focused on behavioral, neural, or institutional mechanisms—address non-payoff outcomes or adjacent dilemmas, which, while informative for mechanism or context, do not directly support the primary prediction task.

# 2) Task Relevance

**By Target-Relevance Dimension:**

- **pgg_or_variant:**
    - **exact**: Substantial core set—multiple papers use standard repeated PGGs with and without punishment (e.g., Gürdal et al., 2021; Ramalingam et al., 2019; Glöckner et al., 2018).
    - **close**: Covered via threshold PGGs, CPR games, and hierarchical or centralized intervention variants (Chugunova et al., 2020; Liao et al., 2021; Robbett, 2019).
    - **adjacent/weak/none**: Several studies use related social dilemmas (e.g., trust, dictator, or ultimatum games), providing only mechanism or context insights.

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**
    - **exact**: Many directly manipulate costly, peer, or centralized punishment, including cost and technology specifics (e.g., Gürdal et al., 2021; Glöckner et al., 2018; Ramalingam et al., 2019).
    - **close/adjacent/weak**: Some use endogenous or automatic sanctions, enforcement, or symbolic punishment; others discuss adjacent sanctioning (e.g., exclusion, information disclosure).
    - **none**: A sizable minority focus on rewards only, unrestricted communication, or no sanction context.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
    - **exact**: Several report efficiency as the primary/group outcome (Gürdal et al., 2021; Glöckner et al., 2018; Ramalingam et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019).
    - **close/adjacent**: Others report related group payoffs, welfare, surplus, or total earnings.
    - **weak/none**: Many papers focus solely on contribution rates, cooperation rates, or punishment frequency, without mapping these to efficiency.

> **Summary:** **Exact, directly predictive evidence exists, but only for a subset of papers. Many others are only closely or adjacent-relevant, often lacking payoff-based outcomes or precise PGG mapping.**

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related (Efficiency, Group Payoff, Surplus, Welfare):**
    - **Direct (exact/close):** Several studies measure total group payoff or efficiency, crucial for the prediction task (e.g., Gürdal et al., 2021; Glöckner et al., 2018; Ramalingam et al., 2019; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Robbett, 2019).
    - **Indirect:** Some use proxy measures—probability of achieving threshold (Liao et al., 2021), group surplus (Kusterer & Schmitz, 2020), or group earnings (Billinger & Rosenbaum, 2019).
    - **Absent/Mixed:** Many focus on adjacent outcomes—e.g., extraction rates (in CPRs), compliance, or taxation—requiring inference about efficiency.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (Not equivalent to efficiency):**
    - **Predominant in many papers:** Contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency or assignment, compliance, reported willingness to punish, or norm adherence (e.g., Krawczyk & Szczygielski, 2019; Yang et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2020).
    - **Important moderator/mechanism:** Increases often tracked as signals of norm enforcement or institutional functioning, but not always linked to improved efficiency (cost of punishment can offset gains).

- **Other outcomes:** Neural activation, self-reported attitudes, or beliefs, used in some studies, do not map to efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical synthesis for standard/static PGGs:**

- **Punishment frequently increases efficiency (group payoff relative to optimum),** particularly in repeated, small-group PGGs with peer or centralized punishment, positive MPCR, no chat, and well-calibrated punishment cost/benefit ratio. Effects are robust to culture (Gürdal et al., 2021; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Zhang et al., 2019 [theory+simulation]).
- **Magnitude of the effect varies:**
    - **Strongly positive:** When peer punishment is enabled under typical parameters (Gürdal et al., 2021).
    - **Mixed/Neutral:** In 'Give' frame games, efficiency gains are often offset by punishment costs—higher contributions do not always convert to higher group payoffs (Ramalingam et al., 2019; Glöckner et al., 2018).
    - **Strongly context-dependent:** Enabling both observed and unobserved punishment channels can unlock positive efficiency effects that do not appear when only one is allowed (Glöckner et al., 2018).

**Critical moderators identified:**

- **Punishment cost/technology:** Lower punishment cost and/or more effective punishment targeting increase efficiency gains (Zhang et al., 2019).
- **Framing:** 'Take' frames (acts of commission) yield greater efficiency gains from punishment than 'Give' frames, due to more effective targeting (Ramalingam et al., 2019).
- **Clarity of norm violation:** If defectors are hard to identify (e.g., due to private information), punishment is less used, mis-targeted, and less effective (Robbett, 2019).
- **Enforcement frequency or modality:** Even rare or symbolic punishment threats can sustain high cooperation and efficiency (Glöckner et al., 2018; Nakamura, 2019 [theory]).
- **Design specifics:** Democratic selection of punishers/leaders enhances efficiency compared to resource-based assignment (Benard & Barclay, 2020). Endogenous institution choice increases the impact (Gürdal et al., 2021).
- **Institutional architecture:** In competition between reward and punishment regimes, efficiency gains in punishment communities depend on migration/opting-out and can be lower than for rewards alone (Chugunova et al., 2020).

**Boundary conditions/negative results:**

- Weak, automatic, or non-peer punishment does not increase efficiency, and may be strictly welfare-reducing (Yang et al., 2020; Robbett, 2019).
- In games with private/ambiguous signals about defectors, enabling punishment may decrease efficiency (Robbett, 2019).
- The cost of punishment can outweigh the cooperation benefits, especially if anti-social punishment (punishment of contributors) is significant (though this is rare under some designs—Gürdal et al., 2021).

**Theory/modeling findings:**

- Models consistently predict increased group utility or stable cooperation under low punishment cost and/or strong fairness norm weighting (Zhang et al., 2019; Gao & Liang, 2020).
- The structure and consensus mechanism for deploying punishment (e.g., collective consent) strongly moderate effectiveness (Gao & Liang, 2020).
- Existence of payoff-increasing punishment is robust, but sensitive to population preferences and resource levels.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**What does the literature suggest for predicting treatment efficiency from design plus control efficiency?**

- When given a control (punishment-disabled) efficiency and a set of clearly specified PGG-type design parameters (especially player count, MPCR, punishment cost/technology, observability, contribution method), **the enabling of peer punishment almost always increases efficiency,** with the following caveats:

    - **The magnitude of the efficiency gain depends on:**  
        - Punishment cost: Lower cost (to punisher) yields higher efficiency gains (Zhang et al., 2019).
        - Targeting clarity: If defectors are clear/easily identified, punishment is more effective and efficient (Ramalingam et al., 2019; Gürdal et al., 2021).
        - Frame/design: Take-frame (commission) games yield stronger efficiency gains (Ramalingam et al., 2019). Democratic mechanisms or joint observed/unobserved punishment increase gains (Benard & Barclay, 2020; Glöckner et al., 2018).
        - Institution choice: Allowing migration or endogenous institution selection may further amplify efficiency improvements (Gürdal et al., 2021; Chugunova et al., 2020).
        - Punishment channel(s): Multiple punishment modes (simultaneously observed and delayed/unobserved) outperform single-mode punishment in terms of efficiency (Glöckner et al., 2018).
    - **Limited or negative effects:** If punishment is weak, automatic, not peer-administered, or anti-social punishment is common, efficiency may not improve or can worsen (Yang et al., 2020; Robbett, 2019; Glöckner et al., 2018).

- **Control group efficiency** is often a useful baseline—the scope for efficiency gain via punishment is linked to how far the group is from the cooperative optimum under control. Where baseline efficiency is already high (e.g., due to other cooperation-promoting features), the potential improvement is smaller.

- **For closely related but not exact settings (e.g., CPR, threshold, or hierarchical games):** Effects are similarly positive but modulated by institutional specifics (Selles et al., 2020; Billinger & Rosenbaum, 2019).

- **If only non-payoff behavioral outcomes are available for similar designs, forecast uncertainty should be increased and default to conservative (null) or mechanism-based moderation.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Design dimensions directly informed (multiple empirical/theory studies):**

- **player_count**: Almost all relevant empirical papers specify and vary group size; findings generalize best to small-n settings (3–4 players typical).
- **num_rounds**: Strong evidence for repeated game dynamics (10+ rounds common).
- **mpcr**: Often varied or explicitly reported. Higher MPCRs facilitate both cooperation and efficiency; efficiency effects of punishment are generally robust across typical mpcr ranges.
- **punishment_cost**: Extensively manipulated; consistently identified as a strong moderator (lower cost → higher efficiency gain).
- **punishment_tech**: Several papers contrast peer vs. automatic/centralized punishment, observed vs. unobserved or delayed application.

**Indirectly informed or sometimes present:**

- **chat**: Included as a moderator in some studies (Benard & Barclay, 2020), typically disabled in strictest PGG studies.
- **all_or_nothing**: Both continuous and discrete (all-or-nothing) contribution settings are analyzed.
- **default_contrib**: Rarely the focus, but occasionally discussed (Chakraborti et al., 2018).
- **reward_exists**: Reward-only and mixed reward/punishment designs are studied (Chugunova et al., 2020), often contrasted with punishment.

**Contextually discussed or sparse:**

- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries**: Common as background in experiment specs, but rarely manipulated explicitly or discussed as key treatment.
- **show_punishment_id**: Sometimes manipulated; anonymity/pseudonymity is often present, real identity rarely so.
- **reward_cost, reward_tech**: For studies with reward as a main mechanism, these are well documented but less so in punishment-focused designs.

**Effectively missing:**

- **Rich manipulation or systematic analysis of several secondary dimensions** (default_contrib, summary feedback, identity revelation, specific communication content) is rare.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical coverage is uneven:** While several papers provide strong, direct evidence, many others only report behavioral outcomes without mapping to efficiency or focus on settings adjacent—not exact—to the public goods prediction task.
- **Generalizability:** Most studies are small-n, lab-based, homogeneous in subject pool (often students), limiting direct extrapolation to field or larger-scale environments.
- **Limited coverage of certain dimensions:** Few studies systematically vary dimensions such as **reward_exists/cost**, **show_punishment_id**, or **default_contrib**; most focus on a standard core set (player count, rounds, mpcr, punishment cost/tech).
- **Complex, real-world institutional features (e.g., migration, endogenous institution selection, external enforcement, or symbolic reputation mechanisms)** are infrequently manipulated in isolation and may not be independently estimable for prediction.
- **Potential publication bias:** Predominance of positive or significant effects may mask null or negative results in non-published studies.
- **Ambiguity where papers disagree:** Some studies (e.g., Ramalingam et al., 2019; Robbett, 2019; Glöckner et al., 2018) explicitly show that enabling punishment does *not* increase efficiency, especially if costs are high, anti-social punishment is common, or targeting norms are ambiguous.
- **Lack of effect size comparability:** Direct quantitative mapping from design dimensions to efficiency effect sizes is rare; effect direction and qualitative moderators are better supported than precise effect prediction.

---

**Conclusion:**  
The evidence base provides good, though not exhaustively systematic, coverage for predicting the impact of enabling punishment on efficiency in well-specified PGG environments, with clear moderators and contingencies. Direct empirical and theoretical findings suggest a robust positive effect, but with substantial heterogeneity driven by punishment cost, targeting clarity, procedural design, and baseline efficiency. Many design dimensions are well informed; some are only contextually or sparsely addressed. For missing or non-payoff outcome contexts, extrapolation should be cautious and explicit about uncertainty.
