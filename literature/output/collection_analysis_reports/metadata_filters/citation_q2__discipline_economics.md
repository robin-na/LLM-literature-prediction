# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large (184 sources) and broad, but a considerable core consists of empirical (primarily laboratory) studies on public goods games (PGGs) and close variants, complemented by a sizable number of theoretical/conceptual analyses. The core evidence for the downstream prediction task—predicting efficiency changes when peer punishment is enabled in PGG-like environments—comes predominantly from empirical lab experiments, many of which manipulate or compare game design dimensions relevant for prediction. The theoretical papers formalize mechanisms and offer parameter boundaries or necessary conditions for efficiency gains via punishment.

While there is extensive empirical coverage of canonical PGGs with/without punishment, the set also includes studies of variants (common-pool resource games, trust games, etc.) and field/lab-in-the-field experiments, often in context-rich or naturalistic settings. Many studies report on payoff-based group outcomes (efficiency, total group payoff, welfare, etc.), but a significant fraction only analyze behavioral outcomes such as contributions, cooperation rates, or punishment behavior, which are not equivalent to efficiency.

Overall, the evidence base is wide and deep for canonical linear PGGs and well-represented for key design variations. Nonetheless, for some prediction dimensions (e.g., `chat`, `show_punishment_id`, large group sizes, exogenous/endogenous monitoring regimes), evidence is sparser or more indirect. There is also notable diversity in institutional features (centralized vs decentral, reward mechanisms, endogenous institution choice), allowing for nuanced cross-study synthesis.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**: The majority of core studies have `exact` relevance, specifically implementing standard or closely related linear PGGs (as per the canonical Fehr & Gächter, 2000/2002). Many additional sources are `close`—using CPR games or threshold PGGs with payoff structures matching group contribution dilemmas.
- **punishment_or_sanctions**: Most relevant papers (`exact`, `close`) manipulate the presence/absence or design of punishment mechanisms, including peer-to-peer punishment, centralized/manager punishment, voting on punishment institutions, or other sanctioning regimes. Adjacent evidence covers exogenous fines, ostracism, or indirect sanctions (e.g., allocation rules).
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: Approximately half report `exact` measures of efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of cooperative maximum), surplus, or total earnings; others (`close`, `adjacent`) use closely related group welfare measures or can be mapped to efficiency with minor assumptions. However, a substantial minority study only behavioral proxies (non-payoff outcomes), which, while valuable, cannot be directly equated to efficiency.

The set thus provides a strong empirical and theoretical basis for the downstream prediction task, especially for canonical PGGs and many common design variations. However, evidence is less direct or robust for some rare variants or alternative institutional details.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**: Group efficiency (total payoff/social optimum), average group earnings, surplus/welfare, or similar. These are central for prediction of treatment efficiency and most directly informative.
- **Behavioral outcomes**: Contribution rates, cooperation rates, punishment assignments, norm compliance, trust, prosocial reciprocation, exclusion votes, strategy use, and so forth. These are widely analyzed and often correlated with efficiency, but must be distinguished (as increased contributions do not always imply higher efficiency, especially if offset by costs from punishment).
- Numerous studies detail both, revealing (a) cases where punishment boosts both cooperation and efficiency, and (b) cases where the gains from increased contribution are eroded by punishment costs, resulting in neutral or negative efficiency effects despite higher cooperation.

The literature is generally explicit in distinguishing these outcome domains, and the best-informative papers provide both sets, allowing direct mapping from design to efficiency effects.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesized empirical findings:**

*General pattern:*
- In canonical linear PGGs, enabling peer punishment almost always increases average contributions; however, its effect on efficiency is highly variable and sensitive to game design dimensions and context.

*Efficiency Gains:*
- *Punishment increases efficiency* only when: punishment is not too costly, is sharply targeted at defectors, is supported by sufficient monitoring/legitimacy, and is not heavily used antisocially (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Wang & Qin, 2015; Angelovski et al., 2018; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Kamei, 2019; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Dutta et al., 2021; Hwang, 2017; Kranz, 2010; Kol'veková et al., 2021).

*Efficiency Losses or Null Effects:*
- *Punishment reduces or does not increase efficiency* when: punishment is frequently misused (antisocial), is too costly relative to its deterring effect, the legitimacy of punishment is low, group composition is diverse or subject to in-group favoritism (e.g., ethnic mix), information for norm targeting is incomplete, or the design allows easy retaliation/extortion (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Mantilla et al., 2021; Drouvelis et al., 2021; Vollan et al., 2019; Robbett, 2016; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Colombier et al., 2011).

*Moderators and Contextual Findings:*
- Efficiency gains from punishment are observed in small, symmetric groups, especially with perfect monitoring and clear, legitimate norms (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).
- Group size: Larger groups may actually benefit more from coordinated punishment (Hwang, 2017; Buchholz et al., 2014), but only if coordination is achieved—otherwise, free-riding in enforcement can undermine deterrence (Kritikos & Bolle, 2004; Cordes et al., 2021).
- Centralized vs decentralized punishment: Centralized mechanisms can yield efficiency gains, but only if monitoring costs are moderate; otherwise, gains are eroded (Kingsley & Brown, 2016; Castillo et al., 2021).
- Institutional details: Democratic or otherwise legitimized punishment increases both effectiveness and efficiency (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Gatiso & Vollan, 2017).
- Monitoring and information: Incomplete or noisy monitoring reduces the efficiency benefit of punishment (De Geest & Kingsley, 2019; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Gallo et al., 2022).
- Cultural and emotional context: Efficiency impacts are modulated by cultural predispositions toward antisocial punishment (Weber et al., 2023), emotional induction (Lee & Min, 2021), and perceived fairness.
- Matching structure: Fixed partner or high viscosity environments support higher efficiency gains from punishment (Grimm & Mengel, 2011; Kamei, 2019).
- Endogenous institution formation (voting): When groups can choose punishment, efficiency increases, particularly if initial control efficiency is low (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Buchholz et al., 2014).
- Punishment cost and technology: Efficiency gains from punishment are attenuated when costs are high or impact is low (Kranz, 2010; Kingsley & Brown, 2016; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).
- Heterogeneity and observability: Heterogeneous endowments or returns reduce the efficiency benefit of punishment unless observation enables targeting norms proportionally (De Geest & Kingsley, 2019; Waichman, 2020).
- Feedback and targeting: Feedback mechanisms linking punishment to past deviation are crucial for positive efficiency impacts (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Khadjavi et al., 2017).
- Combination with reward: Reward (alone or with punishment) often produces at least as high or higher efficiency, with less waste from cost (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Page et al., 2013; Colombier et al., 2011; Stoop et al., 2018).

*Theoretical mechanisms:*
- Models unanimously predict that, under certain conditions (not too costly, sufficiently effective, sufficient compliers), enabling punishment shifts equilibria towards higher efficiency, but also highlight various failure modes (Kranz, 2010; Hwang, 2017; Dutta et al., 2021; Congleton & Vanberg, 2001; Libois, 2022; Brandt & Svendsen, 2019).
- However, if too much antisocial punishment is possible, if monitoring is incomplete, or if defectors can retaliate/extort (Barron & Guo, 2021), punishment may actually reduce welfare/efficiency.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For predicting the average efficiency of PGG-like games with punishment enabled, given design dimensions and control efficiency:**

- **Direction of Effect**: Enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency when:  
  - Baseline efficiency is low (from high free-riding in control),
  - Punishment is sufficiently effective (moderate/high impact relative to cost),
  - Norms are clear and can be targeted due to observability/legitimacy,
  - Group is homogeneous or information is sufficient to adjust norms for heterogeneity,
  - Antisocial punishment is rare or minimized by institution design (democratic selection, visible punishment identity, group consensus),
  - Monitoring is cheap/automatic or peer-based in small groups.

  In contrast, punishment may have *no effect or reduce* efficiency if:
  - Baseline efficiency is already high (punishment adds cost without increasing cooperation),
  - Punishment is misused (antisocial, retaliatory, extortion), especially in large/in-group diverse settings with weak norms,
  - Information is insufficient to target defectors or norm violations,
  - Punishment cost is high, monitoring is costly, or only a few can punish and/or do so indiscriminately,
  - Exogenous, centralized, or group punishment mechanisms crowd out voluntary cooperation or generate rent-seeking,
  - There is persistent emotional or group-based cleavage leading to misapplication of punishment.

- **Magnitude of Effect**:  
  - The net efficiency gain from punishment (treatment-minus-control) is highest when punishment is sharply targeted, moderately costly, and the punishment institution is robust (democratic, legitimate, or supported by agreement/communication).
  - In many canonical lab settings (4–5 players, 10–20 rounds, MPCR 0.4–0.6, peer punishment at 1:3 or similar cost:impact), the *increase in efficiency* is robust and substantial—**if** antisocial punishment and mis-targeting are minimal and monitoring is sufficient.
  - If punishment is made redistributive rather than destructive, efficiency gains are markedly larger (Page et al., 2013).
  - The effect can be negative (punishment reduces efficiency below control) in contexts with mixed or fragmented groups and/or where antisocial punishment is pervasive (Mantilla et al., 2021; Vollan et al., 2019; Drouvelis et al., 2021).

- **Role of Control Efficiency**:  
  - Control efficiency is a strong moderator—punishment yields large gains for low baseline efficiency. If control efficiency is >80% of the social optimum, punishment often *reduces* efficiency by adding unnecessary costs (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Kocher & Matzat, 2016).

- **Advice for Modelers**:
  - When specifying a predictive model, rely on control efficiency as a key input.
  - Use dimension-level knowledge to adjust the expected impact of punishment (positive, null, negative) according to the contextual fit, especially on variables where there is strong evidence of moderation (group size, punishment cost/impact, monitoring/observability, group heterogeneity, legitimacy, institution choice, matching structure, network, communication).
  - Be wary of predicting monotonic positive treatment effects for punishment; acknowledge zones of negative or null effect per above moderators.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions**:
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`—Explicitly manipulated or reported in nearly all direct PGG studies and models. Effects on efficiency are well-documented and often explicitly parameterized.
- `all_or_nothing`: Most experiments use continuous contribution, but all-or-nothing (binary) variants are present; the mechanism sometimes differs, but effect directionality is consistent.
- `punishment_exists`: Central dimension across almost all papers.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Many studies compare reward and punishment, though overall direct evidence for reward is less common.
- `show_n_rounds`: Commonly manipulated and often tied to end-game effects.
- `chat`: Communication/cheap talk is directly studied in a significant subset; its interaction with punishment is shown to be a strong moderator (e.g., communication amplifies or sometimes substitutes for punishment effects).
- `show_other_summaries` and `show_punishment_id`: Some studies explicitly manipulate these, particularly in the context of legitimacy or efficacy of punishment enforcement.
  
**Indirectly informed or contextually discussed**:
- `default_contrib`: Framing (opt-in vs opt-out) less commonly explored but discussed as a possible moderator for control efficiency and potentially for punishment impacts.
- `show_punishment_id`: Manipulated in legitimacy/retaliation studies (Zheng & Nie, 2013); impacts on antisocial punishment and efficiency in larger groups.
- `show_other_summaries`: Tied to information/monitoring structures, crucial for norm targeting.

**Effectively missing or sparse**:
- Fine structure of `punishment_tech` beyond cost:impact ratio, e.g., nuances like limits per round, or probabilistic vs deterministic impact assignments are less consistently discussed.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: While some compare reward vs punishment, direct exploration is sparser.
- Effects of combining punishment and reward, or of institution switching, are only systematically explored in a few studies.
- High-frequency and large group-size environments, or natural field contexts, remain less studied.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Measurement alignment**: Not all studies report efficiency or payoff outcomes; reliance on contribution rates or behavioral proxies can misstate true effects on group welfare.
- **Ecological validity**: Lab designs may not capture the complexity, heterogeneity, and noise of real-world settings; field/lab-in-the-field results are sometimes less positive for punishment.
- **Design coverage**: Some parameter spaces (e.g., very large groups, dynamic monitoring, endogenous partner selection under punishment, hybrid reward/punishment) are underexplored.
- **Downstream prediction challenge**: Many prediction-critical moderators (e.g., group composition, antisocial punishment propensity, baseline cooperativeness) are only sometimes measured or are exogenous to design dimensions.
- **Contextual specificity**: Effects of punishment are highly conditional, not universal. There exist real negative and null results for punishment, especially in context-rich or fragmented social settings.
- **Interaction effects**: The effect of punishment is often nonlinear in combination with other dimensions (chat, communication, endowment heterogeneity, etc.), so simple additive prediction models risk misestimation.
- **Aggregation of mechanism and empirical work**: While theoretical models provide key intuition and necessary conditions, empirical studies are sometimes inconsistent with predictions (e.g., observed antisocial punishment undermining theoretical gains).
- **Cultural and emotional moderators**: Some evidence suggests culture, emotion, or group identity may strongly modulate punishment’s effectiveness, but such moderators are rarely parameterized for direct use in prediction.

## Conclusion

The literature base offers substantial, multi-method empirical and theoretical insight to support prediction of treatment efficiency in PGG-like environments with punishment enabled. However, the effect size and even sign are highly sensitive to specific design dimensions and initial conditions. To inform downstream prediction, control efficiency plus detailed design parameters—especially around punishment cost/impact, information structure, group composition, and institutional legitimacy—are essential. Where evidence is behavioral rather than payoff-based, or where dimensions are mismatched, predictions should be made cautiously. The strongest, most robust effect is that enabling punishment in low-efficiency, well-monitored, symmetric, and legitimate environments can yield sizable efficiency gains, but elsewhere effects may be small, negligible, or negative.
