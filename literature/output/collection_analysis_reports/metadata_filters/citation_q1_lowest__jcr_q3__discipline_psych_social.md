# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set comprises 32 papers, representing a balanced mix of empirical (lab and some field experiments) and theory papers, with a moderate breadth regarding game-theoretic collective action environments and sanctioning mechanisms. Several empirical studies involve standard or close variants of the public goods game (PGG) with explicit manipulation of punishment (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2019; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Nikias & Sy, 2021). Others are more theoretical, developing formal models of punishment, cooperation, and efficiency in repeated or networked dilemmas (e.g., Prétôt et al., 2024; Corriveau, 2012; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).

Adjacent papers focus on conceptually related social dilemmas (e.g., CPR, contest, or dictator games; Ziegler, 1997; Klempt & Pull, 2018) or on moderators such as reputation or framing, without measuring efficiency directly. Several studies address only behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates, willingness to punish), and a subset of papers provide no empirical measurement at all, focusing on mechanisms or high-level theory.

Overall, the evidence base is reasonably strong for standard PGGs with and without punishment. The base is narrower for less common design variants (e.g., institutionally organized punishment, very large groups, or dynamic/field settings), and direct mappings to all specific prediction dimensions are uneven.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Papers were assessed on three dimensions:**

- **pgg_or_variant:**  
  - Several papers are *exactly* relevant (e.g., direct experimental manipulation in PGGs or linear public goods), while others use close variants (e.g., CPR, team production, or contest games). Yet, some are merely adjacent—e.g., focused on trust or dictator games, or on reputation mechanisms.
- **punishment_or_sanctions:**  
  - There is strong relevance, with numerous papers manipulating or modeling explicit peer and third-party punishment or institutional sanctioning. Still, some focus on indirect punishment (reputation damage) or conceptual discussions without implementation.
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
  - Direct efficiency-related outcomes are measured in about a third of the papers. Some only report non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation or contribution rates, punishment rates), which cannot be uncritically equated to efficiency (even though they may covary).

**Summary of coverage:**
- There is *exact* or *close* relevance for PGG/variant and punishment/sanctions in a majority of papers, and for efficiency/payoff in about half. Indirect or adjacent relevance is common for behavioral or mechanism-focused papers.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**
- Directly measured: group payoff, group earnings, welfare, surplus, or total coins generated appear in a subset of experiments (Sparks et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2019; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Nikias & Sy, 2021). Efficiency—defined as the ratio of realized to maximal possible group payoff—is sometimes explicitly reported, sometimes can be inferred from group earnings, but is not always the stated outcome.
- Theoretical efficiency is modeled in several formal papers (Prétôt et al., 2024; Corriveau, 2012; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- Most remaining studies measure contribution/cooperation rates, willingness to punish, punishment frequency, or other norms-related behaviors (e.g., Molenmaker et al., 2019; Kido & Takezawa, 2024; Lois & Wessa, 2019).
- Some focus on beliefs, attitudes, or perceived trust, which are at best indirect proxies for payoff/economic efficiency.

**Distinction maintained:**
- Several papers demonstrate gains in contribution rate without reporting corresponding efficiency (e.g., Loukopoulos et al., 2006), or vice versa.
- Some report negative or absent efficiency effects despite positive behavioral changes (e.g., Myers, 2016, where increased costly participation does not increase group welfare).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis across the most informative papers:**

- **Direct empirical evidence:**  
  - *Costly peer or third-party punishment* in repeated PGGs generally leads to higher average group earnings and efficiency, with effects that become more positive over time as cooperation stabilizes (Sparks et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2019). Over enough rounds, initial reductions in payoff from punishment costs are offset by increased cooperative contributions.
  - *Third-party reward* alone is much less effective. Reward combined with punishment may further increase efficiency, but not always beyond punishment alone (Hou et al., 2019).

- **Impact of punishment structure:**  
  - *Graduated (escalating) punishment* is less effective than strict punishment in the short run but can match or exceed strict punishment in long-term CPR/PWG-style games (van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024). *Endogenously chosen* (voted) punishment institutions further improve efficiency, though marginally.
  - *Lower punishment costs* increase the use of punishment, which in turn increases efficiency (Nikias & Sy, 2021). However, when punishment is institutionally funded (rather than unilateral), the theory predicts more robust increases in efficiency (Prétôt et al., 2024).

- **Potential for negative or null effects:**  
  - The presence or structure of punishment does not universally improve efficiency; crowding out effects (Klempt & Pull, 2018; Gold, 2020) or targeting of non-defector behaviors can make punishment counterproductive (Antoci & Zarri, 2015).
  - Efficiency gains depend on who is punished: targeting defectors promotes efficiency, but punishment of cooperators or indiscriminate punishment can undermine it (Antoci & Zarri, 2015).

- **Role of expectations and norms:**  
  - Experiments without punishment underline the central role of expectations and social feedback in sustaining cooperative contributions and efficiency (English, 2012), while descriptive norms shape the actual deployment of punishment (Lois & Wessa, 2019).
  - Decisional timing and signaling also moderate the use of punishment, potentially affecting downstream efficiency (Molenmaker et al., 2019; Klempt & Pull, 2018), though these studies mostly track behavior rather than payoff.

**Contextual factors:**
- Effects of punishment on efficiency are typically more robust in small, transparent, repeated-game laboratory settings; in large groups, field contexts, or 'real world' settings, delays, heterogeneity, and lack of credible punishment can attenuate or reverse these effects (Toelstede, 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Most supported prediction:**  
  - For standard repeated PGGs (4–6 players, 10–40 rounds, continuous or all-or-nothing contributions, no chat, moderate MPCR, explicit costly peer or third-party punishment, no reward), enabling punishment is expected to increase efficiency relative to the same game without punishment, especially as the game progresses (Sparks et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2019; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).

- **Magnitude and conditions:**  
  - The magnitude of the increase depends on the cost (punishment_cost), effectiveness (punishment_tech), and type (peer/institutional/third-party) of punishment, the tendency toward second-order free riding, and group size (Prétôt et al., 2024; Nikias & Sy, 2021; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).
  - Controls for potential negative efficiency effects should consider whether the punishment allows targeting of cooperators or generates psychological or motivational crowding out (Antoci & Zarri, 2015; Gold, 2020).

- **Relevance to prediction task:**  
  - Where prior control-game efficiency is known, including key game dimension settings, the literature supports modeling a positive incremental effect for treatment efficiency when enabling punishment for the majority of (but not all) lab-style PGG designs (see section 6 below on dimension coverage).

- **Boundary conditions and caveats:**  
  - When games introduce additional complexity (large groups, endogenously chosen institutions, or real-world uncertainty), the direction and magnitude of expected efficiency boosts become more uncertain.
  - Effects of punishment are typically measured in clean, fixed-lab environments; field, loosely controlled, or one-shot environments could yield smaller or null effects.

- **Behavioral versus efficiency outcomes:**  
  - Many papers only measure behavioral change; predictions about efficiency should be based on those directly reporting payoffs, not only on increased cooperation rates.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Dimensions directly informed with respect to efficiency effects:**
- *player_count:* Explicitly varied in several studies; smaller groups (4, 6) are the context for most direct evidence of positive punishment effects.
- *num_rounds:* Both short (2–10) and extended (40) round designs are studied; longer games show more pronounced efficiency gains from punishment (Sparks et al., 2024; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).
- *mpcr:* Variation in marginal per-capita return is well represented, with balanced-moderate values used in direct evidence papers.
- *punishment_cost & punishment_tech (effectiveness):* Both cost and magnitude are shown to moderate the use of punishment and its impact on efficiency (Nikias & Sy, 2021; Prétôt et al., 2024).
- *all_or_nothing:* Both continuous and all-or-nothing formulations are represented.
- *reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:* When reward is included, it is typically less effective than punishment for boosting efficiency, except in combined regimes (Hou et al., 2019).

**Dimensions indirectly or contextually discussed:**
- *chat:* Most direct evidence studies do *not* include chat (communication), but contextual impact (e.g., expectation management via information) is discussed in some papers (English, 2012).
- *show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:* These dimensions are less systematically manipulated but sometimes noted as affecting transparency and efficiency effects.
- *default_contrib:* The influence of opt-in/out frames is more common in theoretical or adjacent studies, but the primary literature does not report substantial direct evidence on this for efficiency outcomes.

**Dimensions effectively missing or very sparse:**
- Institutional forms (other than peer or basic third-party), very large group settings, natural field conditions, and effects of chat are underrepresented for direct efficiency effects.
- No empirical paper systematically manipulates *show_punishment_id* or reputation-linked punishment alongside measured efficiency changes.
- Variability in initial control efficiency is often not a point of focus; how base efficiency modulates the effects of punishment is theorized, less often empirically measured.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Scant direct measurement of efficiency:**  
  - Despite many studies on behavioral outcomes, only a minority report group earnings, total payoff, or formal efficiency, limiting the generalizability of the prediction to all game features.

- **Generalization gaps:**  
  - The bulk of empirical evidence is from small group, repeated lab experiments; field evidence or large group dynamics are rare and more speculative.
  - Many contextual dimensions (e.g., chat, framing, reputation visibility) are insufficiently varied in efficiency-measuring studies to support fine-grained prediction.

- **Ambiguity and boundary cases:**  
  - Several theory papers (Antoci & Zarri, 2015; Corriveau, 2012) and adjacent findings highlight that punishment can be maladaptive or ineffective, especially under crowding out, misapplied sanctions, or when the sanctioning structure allows perverse incentives.
  - There are documented cases where increased punishment does not yield increased efficiency (e.g., when strategic composition of the population or signaling undermines cooperative equilibria).

- **Behavioral ≠ payoff:**  
  - Many findings report increased cooperation/contribution rates without direct evidence of improved efficiency (and sometimes with evidence that efficiency does not improve, e.g., increased costly participation without payoff gains).

- **Sparse mapping to all design dimensions:**  
  - Several potentially relevant prediction dimensions (visibility settings, communication, dynamic institutional change) are inconsistently covered, reducing confidence in out-of-sample or edge-case prediction.

---

**In summary:**  
This literature set robustly supports the inference that, in standard, small-group, repeated PGGs, enabling explicit, costly punishment generally increases group efficiency above control when holding other major design features constant and for similar baseline efficiencies. The effect is moderated by punishment cost/effectiveness, time horizon, and institution structure, and may not generalize to all environments or forms of punishment. Direct support for prediction is strongest for cases with laboratory settings, observed group payoff or earnings, and clear peer or third-party punishment mechanics. Gaps and ambiguities remain, especially regarding nuanced institutional forms, non-lab settings, and for underrepresented design dimensions.
