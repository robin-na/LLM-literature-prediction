# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set analyzed here comprises 27 papers, including both empirical laboratory experiments and a diverse set of theoretical models. Of these, about a third are direct empirical laboratory tests in public goods or closely related environments; the remainder are theory works or empirical studies in adjacent, but not exact, institutional settings (e.g., trust games, Cournot duopoly, helping games, ultimatum games, and coordination games).

Empirical papers mostly focus on behavioral outcomes (such as compliance, cooperation rates, use of punishment) rather than direct efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, but a few do have efficiency or total earnings as primary measures. The theoretical contributions are split between mechanism design, evolutionary dynamics, and repeated game theory, generally aimed at identifying under what strategic or institutional configurations punishment/sanctioning mechanisms can support (or undermine) efficiency.

As a whole, this evidence base is broad in terms of mechanism coverage, but is relatively narrow and fragmented with respect to our downstream prediction task: predicting **average efficiency** in PGG or direct variants as a function of game design dimensions when enabling/disabling peer punishment. Many empirical estimates are context-specific or limited in generalizability, and direct mappings to the 14 design dimensions are often only partial. Quantitative estimates for efficiency shifts are rare; qualitative mechanism-oriented and theoretical results are more common.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant**  
- **exact:** Several key papers focus on standard public goods games (PGGs) or very close variants with only minor institutional modifications (e.g., expropriation-as-punishment, two-harbor resource games) — (Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Ogaki & Tanaka, 2017; Otto & Bolle, 2016).
- **close:** Some papers examine settings that are structurally similar to PGGs, such as tax compliance experiments, resource harvesting, or repeated games with centralized authority, but are not classic PGGs (Hsu, 2013; Becchetti et al., 2015; Gaudeul et al., 2017).
- **adjacent/weak/none:** Several studies analyze adjacent games (e.g., trust games, helping games, ultimatum games, Cournot duopoly) or settings lacking repeated multi-person interaction or public good structure (Pin & Rogers, 2015; Leppänen & Hämäläinen, 2017; Song & Zhong, 2015).

**Punishment or Sanctions**  
- **exact:** A core cluster of papers empirically test or theoretically analyze explicit peer punishment (as designed in PGG laboratory experiments) — (Ogaki & Tanaka, 2017; Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015).
- **close:** Others focus on sanction-like mechanisms (e.g., expropriation, audit, exclusion/exit, hierarchical allocation as substitute for punishment) — (Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Hsu, 2013; Otto & Bolle, 2016).
- **adjacent/weak/none:** Many adjacent works address implicit, bilateral, group-level, or endogenous punishment, or only discuss the concept theoretically (Harbaugh & To, 2014; Golman, 2016).

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**  
- **exact:** A limited number of empirical papers report efficiency, group payoff, or group welfare as main outcome — (Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Otto & Bolle, 2016; Gaudeul et al., 2017).
- **close/adjacent:** Most provide adjacent evidence: report average earnings, welfares, or make inferences from cooperation/contribution rates (Ogaki & Tanaka, 2017; Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017).
- **weak/none:** Many papers focus only on behavioral outcomes, such as compliance, contributions, or emotional/motivational measures, without group payoff implications.

Overall, direct evidence for the specific downstream prediction task (PGG, with peer punishment, predicting efficiency shift) is **sparse**, but mechanism-based and indirect evidence is substantial.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based outcomes (relevant for efficiency prediction):**
  - Group payoff, net group earnings, group surplus, and experimental efficiency (e.g., group payoff relative to theoretical maximum).
    - Measured directly in select papers (Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Otto & Bolle, 2016; Gaudeul et al., 2017; Becchetti et al., 2015).
    - Inferred or modeled in theoretical works (Olcina & Calabuig, 2015; Camera & Gioffré, 2017).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (not direct efficiency but may indicate mechanisms):**
  - Contribution/cooperation rates (Ogaki & Tanaka, 2017; Hsu, 2013), compliance frequency, punishment frequency/targeting, exit/entry rates (Gaudeul et al., 2017).
  - Decisions to punish, reward, or exert peer pressure (Spitzer, 2016; Johnson, 2016).
  - Norm compliance and its sustainability (Teraji, 2016).
  - Emotional responses and psychological measures (Leppänen & Hämäläinen, 2017; Golman, 2016).
- **Notably, several key works document that increases in cooperation rates due to punishment do not always translate to increases in group efficiency, due to punishment costs, inefficient targeting, or perverse incentives (Hsu, 2013; Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017).**

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Regularities:**

- **Enabling punishment generally increases cooperation rates** in public goods games (Ogaki & Tanaka, 2017; Teraji, 2016), and often (but not always) raises group efficiency. However, the efficiency gains are highly sensitive to the design of the punishment mechanism and the balance between the deterrence effect versus costs of punishment itself (Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013).
- **Well-coordinated, targeted punishment** (e.g., requiring several group members to jointly punish; high minimum group size) is more likely to increase efficiency. If punishment is too easy to trigger or is poorly targeted, it may actually reduce group payoff via excessive or misapplied sanctions (Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017).
- **Communication and information sharing** can have larger positive effects on efficiency than punishment per se, though combined punishment and communication generally yield the highest efficiency (Zhosan & Gardner, 2013).
- **Exit or exclusion options, if easily exercised as a sanction, often lower efficiency** due to pessimistic beliefs and excessive exit (Gaudeul et al., 2017).
- **Institutional authority and hierarchy** can substitute for peer punishment; direct allocation rights or centralized control can yield efficiency gains as large as, or greater than, punishment (Otto & Bolle, 2016).
- **Heterogeneity and social context:** Targeting, minority status, prior history, and group structure shape both the effectiveness and the fairness of punishment, with consequences for net efficiency and equity (Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017; Harbaugh & To, 2014).

**Theoretical Mechanisms:**

- **Thresholds for effectiveness:** The efficiency benefit from enabling punishment depends on punishment parameters—sufficient severity, low enough cost, and strong coordination or peer pressure (Olcina & Calabuig, 2015; Camera & Gioffré, 2017; Laclau & Tomala, 2017).
- **Monitoring structure:** The degree and form of monitoring moderates whether punishment leads to high efficiency. If free-riding/deviations are not attributable, punishment cannot efficiently sustain cooperation (Laclau & Tomala, 2017; Harbaugh & To, 2014).
- **Limits of punishment:** In some contexts, excessive reliance on punishment can undermine the evolution of intrinsic cooperation and lower long-term welfare, even if short-run efficiency is higher (Povey, 2014; Ognedal, 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

The literature provides the following guidance for predicting average treatment efficiency in PGG-like settings, using design dimensions and control efficiency as input:

- **Qualitative Directionality:** Enabling peer punishment typically increases group efficiency relative to no-punishment baselines, but not always; the effect is moderated by punishment parameters, targeting quality, and the costliness and structure of sanctions (Ogaki & Tanaka, 2017; Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017).
- **Magnitude and Modulating Factors:**
  - If **punishment is costly and poorly targeted** (low coordination requirements, easy to misuse), efficiency gains are reduced or can even become negative due to the direct cost of punishment and misapplication (Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017).
  - **High punishment effectiveness**, strong peer pressure, or coordinated group punishment (e.g., requiring multiple punishers) increase the chance of achieving high efficiency (Olcina & Calabuig, 2015; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013).
  - **Communication** further boosts efficiency, often more so than punishment alone, and the combination is especially effective (Zhosan & Gardner, 2013).
  - Structural features—**hierarchical control** or authority mechanisms—can achieve efficiency on par with or exceeding punishment (Otto & Bolle, 2016).
  - **Heterogeneity** and minority status require careful attention: the net efficiency effect may be diminished if punishment is misdirected or if subgroups’ responses to punishment differ (Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017; Harbaugh & To, 2014).
- **Payoff Cost Caveat:** Empirical evidence shows that **higher compliance or cooperation rates due to punishment do not equivalently map onto efficiency**—punishment consumes resources and can reduce the group’s net payoff if overused or not tightly controlled (Hsu, 2013; Gaudeul et al., 2017).
- **Information Structure:** Public information on individual actions, in the absence of punishment, does not substitute for sanctions and can even undermine efficiency in low-capital settings (Becchetti et al., 2015).

Given these points, **prediction of efficiency when enabling punishment should not assume a universally positive effect**: the benefit depends critically on the punishment mechanism's targeting, cost, ease of coordination, and the presence of supporting features like communication.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr` (group/round structure; explicitly varied or specified in most experiments/theory).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (both critical in theoretical and empirical studies: costliness, coordination requirements, effect magnitude, targeting quality).
- `chat` (directly manipulated in communication studies; strong effects on efficiency).
- `all_or_nothing`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries` (varied in some studies; implications for information and structure).
- `reward_exists` (present or absent in some theoretical constructions).

**Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
- `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id` (some discussion in context of signaling/social distance, but not rigorously tested or shown as main effect moderators).
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (addressed in theoretical papers, but minor for most empirical results).

**Effectively Missing:**  
- Direct mappings for several design dimensions (`default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id`, explicit empirical manipulation of `reward_cost`/`reward_tech`) are largely absent.
- No studies implement all 14 dimensions in one design; cross-paper synthesis infers their importance.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Gaps in direct empirical efficiency measurement:** Many laboratory experiments focus on behavioral compliance or contribution rates, not on efficiency or total group payoff relative to an optimal benchmark; this limits the precision of outcome prediction.
- **Sparse multi-dimensional mapping:** No single empirical paper covers the full set of 14 design dimensions; interactions and higher-order conjunctions of these dimensions are inferred, not demonstrated.
- **Limited quantification and external validity:** Most evidence is qualitative or relative (increases/decreases), with few statistical estimates of effect size. Quantitative prediction of efficiency shifts under new combinations of dimensions remains extrapolative.
- **Ambiguity under conflicting institutional features:** Certain design choices (e.g., making exit easy, adding information disclosure without sanctions) produce contradictory effects in adjacent settings, making generalization risky.
- **Payoff vs. behavior disconnect:** High rates of punishment or compliance do not always equate to greater efficiency, due to costly or misapplied punishment lowering net payoff.
- **Time horizon and evolutionary concerns:** Some theoretical work suggests that while punishment can raise short-run efficiency, it may undermine long-term or evolutionary outcomes (Povey, 2014), a dynamic not captured in typical lab experiments or most theory papers.
- **Generalizability issues:** Some findings from adjacent games (e.g., Cournot competition, trust games) may not fully generalize to PGGs; caution is needed in interpreting these results as direct evidence.
- **Absence of certain mechanisms:** Purely institutional/centralized punishment, decentralized community-based punishment, and authority-based allocation are not all represented together in the same studies.

---

**In summary:**  
This literature set provides robust evidence that the effect of enabling peer punishment on PGG efficiency is **strongly dependent on the specific game design**, especially the structure and cost of punishment, the quality of targeting, and supporting information/communication features. While it is reasonable to predict efficiency gains in well-structured punishment environments, the size, direction, and reliability of these gains are **not universal**, and model-based predictions must account for institutional details, not just baseline control efficiency.
