# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 21 papers, including both empirical and theoretical work, but is moderately broad in terms of focus. About one-third of the papers present empirical evidence from laboratory or field experiments using linear public goods games (PGGs) or very close variants (e.g., Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015). Several theory papers provide insights into punishment effects on efficiency in repeated games, social dilemmas, and common-pool resource environments (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015; Forges et al., 2016). A significant number of papers are only adjacent, addressing closely related dilemmas, hypothetical scenarios, or conceptual models without actual payoff-based outcomes or explicit implementation of punishment (e.g., Roberts & Lea, 2016; Beraldo & Sugden, 2016). Overall, the evidence base is strong in standard PGG lab settings for some design dimensions, but patchy or indirect for many real-world or nuanced experimental configurations.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact:** Several studies use canonical or very close repeated linear PGGs (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016).  
- **close:** Some field settings and theoretical models involve common-pool/collective action games structurally similar to PGGs but not strictly matching (Noussair et al., 2015; Voelkl, 2015; Lee & Iwasa, 2014).  
- **adjacent/weak:** Many others study related cooperation dilemmas or two-player games (Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014; Liu & Riyanto, 2017), or focus on generalized signaling/reputation topics (Golman, 2016).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact:** Multiple empirical and theoretical papers directly manipulate or model peer punishment or sanctions (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Lee & Iwasa, 2014).  
- **close/adjacent:** A few papers examine only leader/centralized punishment (Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016), indirect punishment forms, or only discuss punishment as a mechanism.  
- **none/weak:** Roughly half include no punishment, instead focusing on reward-only, transparency, or social signaling.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact:** Direct efficiency or group payoff is reported in only a small subset of core PGG studies and some theoretical papers (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).  
- **adjacent:** Many studies report only cooperation/contribution rates or proxy behavioral outcomes, not total group payoff or efficiency.  
- **none:** Several papers operate exclusively at the perception, attitude, or preference level.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Efficiency, Group Payoff):**
- Reported directly in a minority of empirical papers (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- Modeled or discussed in theoretical contributions (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015; Forges et al., 2016).
- Frequently missing or only indirectly referenced through related behavioral measures.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- Most commonly measured: individual and group contribution rates, cooperation rates, norm compliance, frequency of punishment, and attitudes toward punishment.
- Frequently reported in both laboratory and field studies, as well as hypothetical or scenario-based research (Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016; Roberts et al., 2013; Lopez, 2017).
- These outcomes are often used to infer potential payoff implications, but do not substitute for direct efficiency analysis.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment Effects on Contributions vs. Efficiency:**  
  Multiple studies find that enabling peer punishment reliably increases contribution rates above baseline (no-punishment) controls (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015). However, the efficiency impact is ambiguous: punishment often decreases total group efficiency because its costs outweigh the gains from increased cooperation, unless punishment is carefully constrained or used sparingly.
  
- **Moderation by Punishment Mechanism Design:**  
  The efficiency effect of punishment is highly sensitive to design details. In repeated linear PGGs, one-shot, anonymous, non-retaliatory punishment stages (SP) can yield efficiency gains, while rich, multi-stage, or retaliatory, identity-identified punishment regimes (RP) often eliminate or reverse efficiency gains due to cycles of punishment/retaliation (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
  
- **Role of Antisocial Punishment:**  
  Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators or as revenge) undermines efficiency, and its prevalence depends on subject pool and game structure. Game designs that foster opportunities for antisocial punishment will tend to see reduced or even negative net efficiency effects when punishment is enabled (Thöni, 2014).

- **Partner vs. Stranger Matching:**  
  Some evidence suggests efficiency outcomes are robust across partner and stranger matching in canonical linear PGGs (Kocher & Matzat, 2016), but this is not broadly generalized in the literature.

- **Reward Mechanisms:**  
  Where compared, reward institutions typically yield higher group efficiency than punishment or standard control (Kocher & Matzat, 2016).

- **Theoretical Models:**  
  Models generally predict that sufficiently strong or graduated punishment stabilizes cooperation and increases efficiency if costs do not dominate (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015; Forges et al., 2016), but effects depend on player heterogeneity, error, information, and punishment redistribution details.

- **Non-Lab/Field Environments:**  
  Field settings and common-pool variations show more muted or absent effects of peer punishment on behavioral outcomes, and little evidence of efficiency gains (Noussair et al., 2015; Becchetti et al., 2015).

- **Transparency Without Punishment:**  
  Public feedback / monitoring without punishment can decrease efficiency by promoting harmful conformity (Becchetti et al., 2015).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Control Game Efficiency as Baseline:**  
  Empirical evidence supports using control (no-punishment) efficiency as an anchor: efficiency changes in treatment depend largely on the implementation and constraints of the punishment mechanism.

- **Punishment Likely Reduces Efficiency in Canonical PGGs:**  
  In classic laboratory linear PGGs with peer punishment enabled (single-stage, anonymous, no retaliation), significant increases in contributions do not always translate into higher efficiency. Instead, punishment costs often lead to lower average efficiency than the no-punishment control, unless the design limits punishment costs or enhances its effectiveness (Kocher & Matzat, 2016).

- **Design Features Most Critical:**  
  Prediction of efficiency effects should be most sensitive to:
    - **punishment_cost:** Lower costs permit more punishment, potentially reducing efficiency. Higher costs deter use but can also make punishment ineffective at increasing cooperation.
    - **punishment_tech:** More effective punishment (higher magnitude) increases both deterrence and potential excess punishment, again potentially offsetting efficiency gains if misuse or revenge-punishment is common.
    - **punishment_stage_design:** Multi-stage, reputational, or identity-revealing punishment environments tend to reduce or nullify efficiency effects due to retaliation/feuds (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - **reward_exists/cost/tech:** When compared, reward mechanisms typically increase both contributions and efficiency relative to punishment.

- **Limited Evidence for Other Dimensions:**  
  Dimensions such as chat, default contribution framing, or showing the number of rounds have limited direct evidence regarding their moderation of punishment's efficiency effect within this set.

- **Context and Generalizability:**  
  Effects observed in lab settings with undergraduate subject pools do not necessarily generalize to field or culturally diverse populations.

- **Theory vs. Empirical Contrast:**  
  Theoretical models generally predict efficiency gains from implementing punishment, but empirical results highlight that in real experimental games, those gains are only realized under specific, well-designed conditions that control for excessive or antisocial use.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- `player_count`: Frequently specified; some empirical and all theoretical models address group size.
- `num_rounds`: Explicit in lab studies and theory for repeated environments.
- `mpcr`: Marginal per capita return is central in most experimental designs.
- `all_or_nothing`: Distinction between all-or-nothing vs. continuous contribution designs is sometimes analyzed.
- `punishment_cost` & `punishment_tech`/`punishment_magnitude`: Central to both theoretical and empirical investigations; key lever for efficiency prediction.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Directly examined in several studies with side-by-side institution comparisons.
- `show_punishment_id`: Studied in designs testing anonymity vs. fixed-identity regimes.

**Indirectly Informed/Contextual:**  
- `chat`: Sometimes mentioned, few direct manipulations with respect to punishment's effect.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Occasionally specified, rare as treatment variables.
- `default_contrib`: Sometimes a framing dimension, but little direct evidence on its interaction with punishment and efficiency.

**Effectively Missing:**  
- Little or no specific evidence about design features such as default contribution setting, timing or salience of round numbers, or granularity of feedback, unless embedded within broader experimental context.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse Direct Efficiency Data:**  
  Although group efficiency is central to the prediction task, only a few papers provide direct comparative efficiency data for control vs. punishment-enabled PGGs. Many papers report only on contribution rates or other behavioral markers.
  
- **Limited Dimensional Coverage:**  
  Not all 14 supplied design dimensions are systematically manipulated or analyzed in relation to efficiency under punishment; evidence is especially sparse for chat, feedback structure, and default framing.
  
- **Design-Dependent Outcomes:**  
  Strong evidence of context dependency: outcomes hinge critically on details of punishment implementation (stage structure, anonymity, ability to retaliate), limiting generalizability and transferability of specific findings.
  
- **Theory-Empirical Gap:**  
  While theoretical models almost uniformly predict that appropriately designed punishment will raise efficiency, real-world and experimental outcomes show that punishment often decreases efficiency due to costs, misuse, and antisocial applications.
  
- **External Validity:**  
  Most direct empirical evidence comes from standard undergraduate laboratory subjects; effects are less clear or even reversed in field experiments and nonstudent samples.
  
- **Scope of Non-Payoff Measures:**  
  Many papers report non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution, cooperation, punishment frequency) and use these as proxies for efficiency, which is not always valid.
  
- **Reward Mechanisms Not Always Incorporated:**  
  Conclusions about the moderation of punishment effects by the presence of reward are available in only a few studies, even though these may strongly affect efficiency outcomes.

- **Ambiguity**: Inconsistencies remain about whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency, as seen in direct empirical data versus theoretical predictions and across different punishment structures.

---

This synthesis, grounded strictly in the provided digest, shows that while some dimensions and main mechanisms are well understood in laboratory PGG settings, the literature base only partially supports robust prediction of the effect of peer punishment on efficiency across the full set of game design dimensions. Disaggregation by punishment design, baseline efficiency, and context is essential.
