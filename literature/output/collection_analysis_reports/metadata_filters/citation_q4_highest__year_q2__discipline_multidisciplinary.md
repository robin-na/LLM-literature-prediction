# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set (62 items) is broad in its coverage of cooperation, social dilemmas, and norm enforcement, but only a subset of papers address the effect of punishment on efficiency in **public goods games (PGGs)** or their close variants. Both **empirical** (lab and field experiments) and **theoretical** (analytical/simulation-based) studies are well-represented, encompassing direct manipulations of punishment, comparative mechanism designs (e.g., reward vs. punishment), and evolutionary/game-theoretic models. Some papers only discuss PGGs tangentially, or use other social dilemma paradigms (e.g., Prisoner’s Dilemma, Ultimatum Game), which are useful mainly for context or mechanism insight rather than direct prediction of treatment efficiency. The set includes both exact matches to the prediction scenario (repeated multi-player PGG with and without punishment, measuring efficiency or total payoff) and studies with adjacent relevance.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Several experimental and theoretical papers (e.g., Andrighetto et al., 2013; Gächter et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016; Gross et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2015; Sasaki et al., 2015; Barrett, 2016; Liu et al., 2017) analyze standard or repeated PGGs where the key game dimensions are specified and align with those in the prediction task.
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Some studies use adjacent games (e.g., Prisoner’s Dilemma, contest games, or mechanism variants) that share relevant structures but differ in player configuration or payoff calculation.
- **Weak/none:** A significant fraction covers only adjacent cooperation paradigms or does not include PGG settings (e.g., Ultimatum Game, Dictator Game).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Many empirical and theoretical studies explicitly manipulate the presence and design of costly punishment (peer or pooled), as in the prediction task.
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Some feature exclusion, reward, social ostracism, or institutional variations.
- **Weak/none:** Several studies do not implement or analyze sanctions/punishment at all.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact/close relevance:** A subset directly reports **efficiency**, group welfare, total earnings, or surplus changes with and without punishment (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; Andrighetto et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2016; Gross et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2017; Abbink et al., 2017).
- **Adjacent/weak relevance:** Many measure only contribution or cooperation rates (not equivalent to efficiency); some infer efficiency from behavioral proxies without direct reporting.
- **None:** Several papers focus only on behavioral, motivational, or psychological variables.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (directly relevant):**
- **Efficiency (group payoff relative to social optimum):** Multiple PGG studies report average payoffs/efficiency across treatments (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; Andrighetto et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2016; Gross et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014; Abbink et al., 2017; Sasaki et al., 2015).
- **Average group earnings, welfare, total surplus:** Sometimes used as alternative payoff measures (e.g., Gross et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014; Chen et al., 2015).
- **Material payoffs in contest or competitive settings** (relevant for contest/framing moderators): e.g., De Dreu et al., 2016; Gavrilets & Richerson, 2017.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (secondary or indirect relevance):**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates:** Common outcomes reported in many papers as indicators of ‘success’ or prosociality; only loosely related to efficiency when punishment is costly.
- **Frequency and magnitude of punishment assigned**
- **Norm compliance, partner choice, exclusion, and similar mechanisms**
- **Behavioral expectations, beliefs, conditional cooperation**
- **Reputation effects and psychological constructs**

Many papers explicitly note that increased cooperation rates do **not always translate to higher efficiency**, especially if punishment is expensive, counter-punishment occurs, or cooperation is socially harmful (Abbink et al., 2017).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical findings (payoff/efficiency):**
- **Punishment increases efficiency in standard PGGs** **when the cost-to-impact ratio is favorable.**
    - E.g., Gächter et al., 2017: Adding peer punishment boosts efficiency to near-social-optimal levels in both provision and maintenance PGGs (cost-to-impact 1:3, 27 rounds, n=4).
- **Punishment can reduce efficiency if costs are too high, retaliation occurs, or cooperation is inefficient.**
    - Wu et al., 2016: Moderate-cost punishment (1:3) reduces efficiency; gossip (costless) increases it.
    - Abbink et al., 2017: In PGGs with negative externalities (MPCR < 1/n), punishment enforces destructive norms, reducing efficiency.
    - De Dreu et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2017: In contest or competitive settings, or pairwise games, punishment does not increase and may decrease efficiency.
- **Key design moderators of punishment’s effect on efficiency include:**
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness (cost-to-impact/fine ratio)**
    - **Informational structure (e.g., onymity, norm-signaling, communication: Andrighetto et al., 2013)**
    - **Presence of corruption/bribery opportunities (Muthukrishna et al., 2017)**
    - **Institutional structure (e.g., voluntary centralization: Gross et al., 2016; second-order punishment: Hilbe et al., 2014)**
    - **Combination with other mechanisms:** Rewards or adaptive hybrid incentives can outperform punishment or reward alone (Chen et al., 2015; Sasaki et al., 2015).
- **Contextual nuances:**
    - **Punishment increases efficiency in "us-vs-nature" (standard PGG) contexts but may destroy efficiency in "us-vs-them" (contest) frames (Gavrilets & Richerson, 2017).**
    - **Punishment effectiveness often hinges on the absence of opportunities for corruption, proper norm cues, and group consensus on both the norm and the appropriate punishment mechanism.**
    - **Psychological variables (e.g., perceived fairness, similarity, or expectations) can mediate punishment's efficacy via their effect on norm salience and compliance (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Mussweiler & Ockenfels, 2013).**

**Theoretical insights (mechanism and evolutionary models):**
- **The cost-to-impact ratio and second-order free rider problem are central.** Punishment only increases efficiency if sufficiently strong and/or subsidized, or if combined with rewards (Barrett, 2016; Chen et al., 2015; Sasaki et al., 2015).
- **Mechanisms like exclusion, prior commitment, and hybrid policies can outperform pure punishment in efficiency (Liu et al., 2017; Han et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2015).**
- **Repeated interaction and voluntary institution choice can further enhance efficiency if punishment institutions are well-calibrated and consensus-based (Hilbe et al., 2014).**

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Key guidance, conditional on design dimensions and control efficiency:**
- **Enabling peer punishment in a standard, repeated PGG with moderate or low punishment cost/strong impact, and without reward or bribery/corruption opportunities, generally increases efficiency relative to control**, often restoring it to near-optimal levels **if the MPCR is above the social dilemmas threshold** (i.e., MPCR > 1/n); (Gächter et al., 2017; Hilbe et al., 2014; Andrighetto et al., 2013).
- **The magnitude of the efficiency gain depends heavily on:**
    - **Punishment cost-to-impact/fine ratio**: Lower cost and higher impact favor positive efficiency changes; high-cost, low-impact punishment often fails or reduces efficiency (Wu et al., 2016; Barrett, 2016).
    - **Presence of supporting/competing mechanisms**: Rewards, norm-signaling, voluntary power centralization, or second-order punishment can amplify effects (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2015; Gross et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014).
    - **Structure of feedback and observability**: Allowing communication or norm-signaling (chat) tends to increase efficiency effects (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2016).
    - **Game structure and context**: If MPCR < 1/n, or if cooperation generates negative externalities, punishment can *decrease* efficiency (Abbink et al., 2017). In competitive or corruptible institutional environments, efficiency may fall (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
- **Control group efficiency (with punishment disabled) serves as a lower bound in typical designs without strong anti-cooperation cues.**
    - **If control efficiency is already high, introducing punishment may yield only modest improvements or even reduce efficiency if punishment usage is high or misdirected.**
    - **If control efficiency is low, correctly calibrated peer punishment can drive up to optimal efficiency, unless contextual factors (norm misalignment, corruption, high punishment cost) intervene.**
- **Structural details not directly manipulated in the literature (e.g., show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id, default_contrib) are only weakly or contextually discussed, and their predictive power is less certain from this evidence base.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Dimensions directly informed by multiple papers (with empirical or theoretical payoff consequences):**
- `player_count`: Systematically explored. Effects can vary with group size: some theory suggests intermediate sizes can hinder punishment’s efficacy (Chen et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2017)
- `num_rounds`: Repeated games yield stronger punishment effects; less evidence for one-shot settings unless punishment is rare but anticipated (Lergetporer et al., 2014)
- `mpcr`: Outcomes are sensitive to MPCR; low values risk negative efficiency effects with punishment (Abbink et al., 2017; Barrett, 2016)
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech` (`magnitude`): **Central to almost all predictions:** lower cost and higher impact favor positive efficiency effects (Wu et al., 2016; Gächter et al., 2017)
- `chat`, `all_or_nothing`: Some coverage; chat/norm-signaling increases gains from punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2013)
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Explored in models comparing combined reward/punishment vs. either alone—joint mechanisms often yield higher efficiency (Chen et al., 2015; Sasaki et al., 2015)
- `show_other_summaries` (peer information/feedback): Contextually important in several studies; feedback may moderate punishment’s effectiveness

**Dimensions with indirect or contextual coverage:**
- `default_contrib`: Only rarely specified; framing effects are indirectly discussed (Cone & Rand, 2014)
- `show_n_rounds`: Occasionally varied; little evidence that it materially affects efficiency unless horizon is strategically relevant (Hilbe et al., 2014)
- `show_punishment_id`: Rarely manipulated; onymity and identity manipulation mainly studied in adjacent designs (Wang et al., 2017)
- `reward_magnitude`: Not often reported separately from `reward_cost`/`reward_exists`

**Dimensions rarely or never covered:**
- Interactions between visualization or summary conditions (`show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`) and efficacy of punishment are largely unmeasured.
- Specific implementation/UX details (e.g., interface layout, coin denomination) not discussed.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Not all studies report efficiency or group payoff directly:** Many measure only cooperation/contribution, which may not track efficiency in punishment-enabled conditions where sanctions are costly.
- **Most evidence comes from small groups of 2–5 players and moderate number of rounds:** It is unclear how results generalize to very large or very small groups or one-shot/very short games.
- **Some designs are lab-based and not field-validated; ecological validity may thus be limited.**
- **Context dependence of punishment effects:** Efficiency gains are often moderated, sometimes reversed, by context—particularly when punishment supports inefficient norms (Abbink et al., 2017), is subject to corruption (Muthukrishna et al., 2017), or payoff structure penalizes prosocial action.
- **Few studies systematically vary less-obvious dimensions:** e.g., default contribution framing, visibility of punishment assigners, or precise feedback architecture.
- **Theory-model results may assume rational updating, error-free execution, or institutional forms not directly analogous to lab peer punishment.**
- **Ambiguity and disagreement in the literature:** Some studies demonstrate robust positive effects (Gächter et al., 2017; Hilbe et al., 2014), others find negative or null effects (Wu et al., 2016; Abbink et al., 2017; Barrett, 2016; Wang et al., 2017), depending on nuanced and sometimes unmeasured moderators.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature most directly informs prediction in standard, repeated PGGs with explicit, well-calibrated peer punishment, especially when group size (player count), rounds, MPCR, and punishment cost/impact are specified. Reward systems and feedback structure are also important moderators. However, positive efficiency effects of punishment **cannot be assumed universally**: They depend critically on the cost and purpose of punishment, game context, and potential for norm misalignment, retaliation, or corruption. Several prediction-relevant design dimensions are directly covered (player count, MPCR, punishment parameters), while others (feedback visibility, default contribution, etc.) are less empirically anchored. There are strong limitations due to context dependence, lack of direct efficiency reporting in many studies, and varying underlying mechanisms/measures.
