# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of 17 papers with a mix of empirical (laboratory experiments) and theoretical works. Empirical studies dominate, with the majority using laboratory or field experiments, often with well-specified game parameters. Theoretical contributions provide mechanism-level predictions mainly for adjacent game forms. The breadth of the set is moderate, covering classic and adjacent game types (e.g., public goods, trust, coordination, bargaining) and a diverse set of sanctioning and reward institutions, but direct tests of standard public-goods-game (PGG) efficiency under peer punishment are outnumbered by studies on adjacent or hybrid environments. A few papers report or enable inferences about efficiency directly; others focus more on contribution behavior or mechanisms.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *exact:* Several papers test standard linear PGGs (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Pancotto et al., 2023; Peng, 2022).
- *close/adjacent:* The majority study adjacent games (minimum effort, trust, coordination, bargaining, team investment, donation, cost-sharing), which share some institutional or behavioral features with PGGs but may differ in strategic incentives or role symmetry (e.g., Herne et al., 2022; Lec et al., 2023; Gueth & Otsubo, 2023; Calabuig et al., 2024).
- *none:* A few papers are farther afield (e.g., Bellucci, 2022 – dyadic trust games without sanctions).
  
**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *exact:* Many papers implement or analyze explicit punishment opportunities (peer or third party), with payoffs parametrized by cost and effectiveness (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Pancotto et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023).
- *adjacent:* Others include only weak or symbolic “punishment” (Kamei & Tabero, 2025) or treat reward/partnership as alternatives (Peng, 2022; Buso et al., 2025).
- *none:* Several lack any punishment or sanctioning mechanism (Bellucci, 2022).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *exact:* Roughly half of the papers report group-level efficiency or explicit payoff data that maps to the efficiency definition (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Peng, 2022; Lec et al., 2023; Herne et al., 2022).
- *adjacent/close/weak:* Several report payoff-adjacent aggregate outcomes or require inference from contributions (Buso et al., 2025; Kamei & Tabero, 2025).
- *none:* Some papers (e.g., Wiederhold & Bühren, 2025; Dato & Friehe, 2025) focus primarily on individual punishment behavior or norms, not group-level payoff.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**  
- Direct group efficiency, total group payoff, or explicit welfare metrics: Cobo-Reyes et al. (2022), Peng (2022), Li et al. (2023), Herne et al. (2022), Lec et al. (2023), Pevnitskaya & Ryvkin (2022), Gueth & Otsubo (2023), Calabuig et al. (2024).
- Inferred payoffs/group welfare (from average contributions, etc.): Pancotto et al. (2023), Buso et al. (2025), Kamei & Tabero (2025).
- Theoretical models with explicit equilibrium payoff analysis: Gioffré & Tampieri (2025), Uchida et al. (2024), Lütz et al. (2023).

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
- Contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency, norm compliance, trust/investment rates, punishment intensity, and communication content: Pancotto et al. (2023), Buso et al. (2025), Li et al. (2023), Wiederhold & Bühren (2025), Dato & Friehe (2025), Kamei & Tabero (2025), Bellucci (2022).

Many studies report only behavioral or norm-based outcomes; these do not always map directly to group efficiency, and sometimes payoff data is missing or must be inferred.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### Empirical Evidence (PGGs and Closest Variants)
- **Punishment increases efficiency:** Enabling (especially formal/centralized) punishment increases average efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum), with evidence strongest for open (migration-allowed) societies (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022). Effect sizes depend on group openness.
- **Contribution increases imply likely efficiency increases:** Where only contribution or cooperation rates are reported, punishment robustly increases cooperation and implies higher group payoffs, especially in compulsory play (Pancotto et al., 2023), but this requires inferring efficiency gains.
- **Reward versus punishment:** Reward mechanisms (particularly coordinated/majority-vote rewards) can also increase efficiency, especially under MPCR homogeneity, and may substitute for punishment in some settings (Peng, 2022).
- **Negative or null effects:** In adjacent trust, team investment, and some coordination games, punishment can decrease or fail to increase efficiency, as costly punishment may outweigh its deterrent benefits—especially if punishment is frequent or coordination is poor (Herne et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024). There is evidence that punishment does not always overcome the efficiency cost barrier.
- **Coordination games:** Minimum effort and stag-hunt-type games see net efficiency gains from enabling punishment, but sometimes only after an initial dip as groups transition to higher effort (Lec et al., 2023; Gueth & Otsubo, 2023).

### Theoretical Mechanism Results
- **Sufficient punishment sustains efficiency:** Grim-trigger or strong-threat punishments theoretically support cooperation and efficiency at a wide range of parameters, provided the cost/benefit ratio is within specified bounds (Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Uchida et al., 2024). Psychological factors (e.g., prospect theory) can make weaker punishment more effective than expected under standard models (Uchida et al., 2024).

### Moderators and Context
- *Type and visibility of punishment:* Observable, preannounced, or centralized punishment mechanisms lead to more robust efficiency gains (Li et al., 2023; Gueth & Otsubo, 2023; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
- *Punishment cost-to-impact ratio:* Lower-cost, higher-magnitude punishment is associated with net efficiency gains; high-cost, low-impact punishment may be inefficient (Herne et al., 2022; Pancotto et al., 2023).
- *Punishment usage rates and coordination:* High rates of punishment (especially uncoordinated or anti-social punishment) can reduce group efficiency (Calabuig et al., 2024).
- *Reward and partnership:* Institutional alternatives (reward, cost-sharing) can achieve similar or higher efficiency without punishment (Peng, 2022; Buso et al., 2025).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

The literature provides moderate direct support for predicting treatment efficiency in PGGs with peer punishment from design dimensions and control (no-punishment) efficiency, but most clearly in settings with:
- Well-parameterized PGGs.
- Specified punishment/reward technology.
- Group closure/openness, as this strongly moderates punishment effectiveness on efficiency.

Where effects are positive, enabling punishment typically increases efficiency over control, especially under:
- Open group structures.
- Well-coordinated, observable, or formal (centralized) punishment mechanisms.
- Lower punishment costs and higher punishment effectiveness.

However, negative or null effects are possible in adjacent team, trust, or investment games, especially when punishment is frequent, costly, or poorly coordinated.

For actual prediction:
- If control efficiency is known and matches classic PGG lab settings (well-defined MPCR, punishment cost and magnitude, fixed or random matching), empirical results (especially Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022) suggest enabling peer (or especially formal/centralized) punishment will tend to increase efficiency—sometimes substantially, particularly in open groups.
- In adjacent or asymmetric games, the effect of punishment is less reliably positive and should be adjusted downward or made conditional on the potential for punishment misuse, inefficiency due to high sanctioning rates, and the prevalence of anti-social punishment.

Where papers only report contribution or behavior rates, use caution: these tend to move in the same direction as efficiency but are not proxies, especially when sanctions are costly.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**  
- `player_count`: Most experiments report this; effects of group openness and size are tested (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Lec et al., 2023).
- `num_rounds`: Repeated games, round structure, and known horizon are common.
- `all_or_nothing`: Binary versus continuous contributions are specified in most papers.
- `mpcr`: Reported in classic PGG and variants; central to efficiency estimation.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Cost and impact of punishment are directly manipulated in many studies.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_tech`, `reward_cost`: Studies on reward or alternatives provide data for these dimensions (Peng, 2022).
- `chat`: Communication is reported; its effects (vs punishment) are discussed.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Round-count display and feedback are sometimes specified, but less systematically tested.

**Indirectly/Contextually Informed:**  
- `default_contrib`: Some studies specify endowment framing but do not systematically vary defaults or analyze their effect.
- `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes discussed (e.g., whether punishment is anonymous or visible; Li et al., 2023), showing that visibility or observability of punishment can moderate effects.

**Effectively Missing:**  
- No systematic empirical evidence across the set for the effect of `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id`, or many nuanced variations of feedback/on-screen summaries or identity cues.
- Some design features (e.g., real-effort versus abstract tokens) are only incidentally discussed.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Exact PGG-with-punishment evidence is limited:** Few studies measure group efficiency in canonical linear PGGs with peer punishment and report all necessary variation. Most studies examine adjacent or variant designs.
- **Control efficiency not always reported:** Several studies lack direct control (no-punishment) efficiency data, requiring inference from contribution rates or indirect comparisons.
- **Behavioral (not payoff) outcomes dominate some papers:** Many only show contribution/cooperation/punishment rate effects, not group payoffs/net efficiency.
- **Generality and heterogeneity:** Effects are highly sensitive to context—group size, matching, magnitude and cost of punishment, openness of groups, and the presence of reward or partnership mechanisms.
- **Ambiguity in adjacent games:** Results from trust games, minimum effort games, and team investment games may not transfer directly to PGGs, as equilibrium selection, role structure, and sanctions interact differently.
- **Rare coverage of all prediction dimensions:** Most papers address only a subset of the 14 game design dimensions; prediction outside those empirically tested regimes requires cautious extrapolation.
- **Direction and magnitude of efficiency effects can disagree:** Some environments see efficiency gains from punishment (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Lec et al., 2023), while others see losses (Herne et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024); this disagreement is not always resolved by the literature.

**Summary:**  
The current literature supports a conditional prediction: in standard PGGs, introducing peer or formal punishment typically increases efficiency, especially in open groups and with well-designed sanctions, but the effect varies strongly by game context, mechanism details, and behavioral patterns. Prediction for new designs is best grounded in papers with the most similar dimensions, and efficiency improvements cannot be assumed when extending results from adjacent or hybrid games. Payoff-based outcome data should be prioritized over behavioral proxies.
