# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set constitutes a broad literature collection with 68 unique sources, predominantly experimental lab studies in economics and social psychology, complemented by a smaller set of theory and simulation papers. There is a clear core of high-relevance empirical studies that use repeated linear public goods games (PGGs) to investigate punishment effects on cooperation and payoffs, alongside a sizable proportion of papers in adjacent or related domains (resource dilemmas, dictator/trust/ultimatum games, reputation and norm-enforcement contexts, and field studies), many of which address punishment behaviorally but not efficiency outcomes directly.

The evidence base is most robust for laboratory PGG environments with repeated interaction, observable contributions, and standard punishment institutions (peer vs. centralized, costly vs. costless). Several recent studies provide direct, payoff-relevant evidence on treatment versus control efficiency when punishment is enabled. However, numerous papers—especially those in adjacent domains, field, or theory—focus on contribution rates, norm compliance, or the psychological/evolutionary mechanisms of punishment rather than on payoff-based efficiency. Evidence on how the full set of design dimensions moderates the efficiency impact of punishment is unevenly distributed.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant**
- **exact**: The central finding set comes from repeated, standard linear PGG lab experiments (`exact`). A minority of studies are close variants (common-pool games, team production, etc.).
- **close/adjacent/weak**: Many relevant insights come from games with similar structure (e.g., common-pool resource, trust/dictator/ultimatum games), but these are labelled as `close` or `adjacent`. Some field and observational studies offer `weak` relevance due to setting or outcome measures.
- **none/irrelevant**: A nontrivial proportion of the literature is fully outside the repeated, multi-player, contribution-punishment format (`none` for PGG).

**Punishment or Sanctions**
- **exact**: The direct evidence base is strong for endogenous, costly, peer punishment (and sometimes centralized punishment) in PGGs (`exact`).
- **adjacent/weak**: Theory and field studies often treat punishment more abstractly, or include only exogenous, third-party, group, or reward-based sanctions (`adjacent`).
- **none**: Some studies explore other forms of norm enforcement or cooperation enhancement without punishment.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**
- **exact**: A key subset of lab experiments reports efficiency (group or total earnings, group welfare) under both no-punishment and punishment-enabled conditions (`exact`).
- **close/adjacent**: Some report behavioral proxies (contribution/cooperation rate), requiring inference regarding efficiency (`close/adjacent`).
- **weak/none**: Many papers measure only behavioral, neural, or attitudinal outcomes, not payoff/efficiency.

**Summary**:  
The literature includes a solid core of `exact` relevance along all three axes, surrounded by more diffuse evidence that often requires careful interpretation or discounting for the prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes** (used directly for prediction):
    - Efficiency (total group payoff as a proportion of max possible) is explicitly reported in several empirical PGG lab experiments (e.g., Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Gürerk, 2013; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Qin & Wang, 2013; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Aakre et al., 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2013).
    - Group earnings/welfare/surplus, total coins generated, average payoffs are also commonly reported and convertible to efficiency.

- **Non-Payoff (Behavioral) Outcomes** (not directly usable, must be distinguished):
    - Contribution rate, cooperation rate, norm compliance, punishment frequency or target choice, trust/reciprocity, emotional responses, social approval/legitimacy, neural or psychological correlates.
    - Many studies (especially theory, field, or cognitive/psychological) report only behavioral or attitudinal outcomes without linking to group payoffs.

- **Mixed Outcomes/Transitional Cases**:
    - Studies using behavioral proxies as evidence for likely efficiency impact (e.g., increased cooperation inferred to increase efficiency in standard PGG), but must acknowledge the lack of direct measurement.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis across key experimental PGG studies:**

- **Punishment can increase efficiency relative to control** when designed and implemented under the right conditions, with several papers showing clear positive treatment-control differences in payoff-based efficiency (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Gürerk, 2013; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Qin & Wang, 2013; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Aakre et al., 2016; Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013; Cason & Gangadharan, 2013).

- **The design of the punishment institution is a major moderator:**
    - **Peer vs. centralized**: Centralized (especially prosocial leader) punishment tends to yield higher efficiency and lower over-punishment than peer punishment (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017).
    - **Costly vs. costless punishment**: Costless punishment by peers leads to excessive, often inefficient punishment and can reduce efficiency (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - **Retaliation risk, multiple punishment stages, information**: Rich punishment environments (multiple stages, known IDs, full information) generate feuds, retaliation, and can nullify efficiency gains (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - **Size and targeting**: Restricting punishment to norm violators (restricted/prosocial) outperforms unrestricted/untargeted punishment (Qin & Wang, 2013; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Xiao & Kunreuther, 2016).
    - **Punishment parameters**: The efficiency effect of punishment is non-monotonically related to probability/severity (Qin & Wang, 2013), and high punishment costs can erode gains.
    - **Context, legitimacy, and social learning**: The legitimacy and perceived fairness of the punisher (including social learning/historical feedback) increase both acceptance and efficiency of punishment institutions (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Gürerk, 2013).

- **Key moderators from adjacent/variant game studies:**
    - **Communication**: Communication and mediation synergize with punishment to improve efficiency, sometimes more than either alone (Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013).
    - **Reward alongside punishment**: Enabling both tends to favor higher cooperation, but reward impacts efficiency differently and may lead to unequal payoff distributions (Drouvelis et al., 2017).
    - **Action space (all-or-nothing, negative contributions)**: Designs allowing negative contributions or undoing others' contributions can lead to much lower control efficiency and may interact problematically with punishment effects (van Soest et al., 2016).

- **Control Payoff Baselines**:  
    - Control (no-punishment) efficiency varies strongly with baseline cooperation, readability of the game, group size, and game environment. High baseline efficiency can make further gains with punishment marginal; low baseline efficiency provides greater scope for punishment to improve payoffs.

- **Cases where punishment reduces or fails to increase efficiency:**
    - **Antisocial punishment and feuds**, especially in rich/retaliatory environments or heterogeneous groups.
    - **Local competition**: When group-level competition is present, punishment can be used to harm in-group rivals, reducing total group efficiency (Barker & Barclay, 2016).
    - **Unconstrained or poorly targeted punishment**, or when only some types of free-riding are sanctionable (Aakre et al., 2016).

- **Robustness/Generalizability**:  
    - Many experimental effects are robust across small to moderate group sizes (3–6), moderate MPCRs (0.3–0.5), and over 6–30 rounds. Evidence is sparser for larger groups, extreme MPCR or cost regimes, or when additional design features (chat, display of summaries, show punishment identities) are manipulated.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based on the direct evidence from experimental PGGs:

- **Punishment increases treatment efficiency over control** when:
    - Costs of punishment are moderate (neither costless nor prohibitively high);
    - The punishment institution minimizes retaliation/antisocial punishment (e.g., anonymous, single-stage peer, or prosocial-centralized);
    - The control (no-punishment) game has moderate to low baseline efficiency (providing room for improvement);
    - Peer information (social learning, summary feedback) supports norm clarity and compliance.

- **Enabling peer punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency:**
    - If punishment is costless, unconstrained, easily targeted for antisocial motives, or the environment supports feuds/retaliation, efficiency can fail to improve or even decline.
    - High punishment usage among peers is often offset by increased punishment costs, so even with higher cooperation, group payoffs may not rise.

- **Key prediction variables/dimensions that most strongly inform efficiency change:**
    - **punishment_cost**: Directly moderates effect; moderate costs work best.
    - **punishment_tech**: Centralized, peer, leader-based, restricted/unrestricted.
    - **player_count** and **num_rounds**: Effects observed consistently in small to moderate sizes/rounds; evidence sparser for other ranges.
    - **mpcr**: Standard values studied; impacts incentives, gains from cooperation.
    - **all_or_nothing**: Present in many studies; rarely varies within studies.
    - **chat/communication**: When present, can augment the positive effect (Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013).
    - **show_punishment_id** and **show_other_summaries**: Limited but suggestive evidence that observed punishment identities/full info can undermine efficiency via retaliation.

- **If only control efficiency and design variables are known**, use baseline efficiency to calibrate the likely maximum treatment efficiency with punishment, conditionally on the institutional features and the baseline level: improvement is most likely when control efficiency is low and punishment is moderate, targeted, and constrained.

- **Uncertainty should be acknowledged** when extending beyond the specific parameter ranges directly studied, or where the evidence relies solely on behavioral proxies rather than efficiency outcomes.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (experimental estimate on efficiency):**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `all_or_nothing`, and `punishment_tech` (esp. centralized vs. peer, restricted vs. unrestricted). Many studies directly manipulate or report these.

**Indirectly informed (behavioral or partial outcome):**
- `chat` (presence/absence or communication styles assessed in a few key studies), `show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries` (inference from retaliation/norm salience evidence), `show_n_rounds`.

**Only contextually discussed, not systematically tested:**
- `default_contrib` (framing), `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `reward_magnitude`. Few studies test reward alongside punishment, and usually as a separate treatment rather than a moderator.

**Effectively missing or insufficiently addressed:**
- The full interaction of all 14 dimensions is never tested in a single design.
- Dimensions like `default_contrib`, some forms of `information revelation` (beyond standard feedback), rare/field/large-group variants, are rarely examined with respect to efficiency in punishment-enabled PGGs.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Generalizability**: Most evidence is from WEIRD, student samples in laboratory conditions. Real-world contexts may differ (see field/observational papers for rarity/fragility of costly punishment in naturalistic settings).
- **Sparse assessment of design interactions**: No study systematically manipulates all 14 prediction dimensions across a wide efficiency range. Most focus on a subset (e.g., player count, punishment cost, or institution).
- **Limited large-N/group/round data**: Most data come from groups of 3–6 people over 6–30 rounds.
- **Limited examination of communication, chat, and identity revelation**: Effects inferred but not thoroughly mapped to efficiency outcomes.
- **Behavioral outcome reliance**: Many papers use cooperation/contribution rate to infer efficiency, which is valid for standard PGGs but risky for more complex or atypical environments.
- **Ambiguity and variation in adjacent domains**: When moving beyond standard PGGs (to resource dilemmas, money-burning, trust/dictator, field settings), punishment's impact on efficiency is often negative or ambiguous.
- **Degree of baseline dependence**: Gains from punishment are highly contingent on baseline (control) efficiency and participant expectations.
- **Relative neglect of reward/reward-punishment interaction**: The impact of enabling both punishment and reward together is less systematically studied regarding efficiency.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature provides robust, directly relevant evidence that enabling peer punishment in standard repeated PGGs often increases efficiency (group payoff), but this effect is highly dependent on the institution's design, the cost and targeting of punishment, and the risk of antisocial or retaliatory punishment. Several game design dimensions (most notably punishment cost, type, group size, rounds, and information structure) are well informed for making predictions regarding treatment efficiency, especially when combined with the control efficiency baseline. However, important limitations persist regarding untested design interactions, generalizability, and reliance on non-payoff outcomes in much of the evidence base. Prediction outside the experimentally-studied parameter space or in field/large-scale settings should be treated with caution.
