# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 35 sources, heavily empirical, primarily experimental studies (lab and field), with a few observational works and sparse theoretical commentary. A substantial minority focus exactly on laboratory public goods games (PGGs) manipulating punishment and measuring group payoffs or efficiency. However, a significant number of studies are only adjacent to the prediction task: they use other social dilemma games (e.g., trust games, ultimatum, dyadic mutualisms in animals), focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation rates, punishment rates), or examine mechanisms and moderators (e.g., culture, emotion, group identity) without providing efficiency-based outcomes. Thus, the evidence base is strong for basic PGGs in laboratory settings, but much weaker for contextually complex, real-world, or structurally varied public-goods-game-like (PGG-like) environments.

# 2) Task Relevance

Task relevance is assessed on three axes:

- **pgg_or_variant**:
  - **exact**: Multiple key studies use canonical laboratory PGG designs with standard parameters (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR), directly manipulating punishment (e.g., Sääksvuori et al., 2011; O'Gorman et al., 2009; Traulsen et al., 2012; Egas & Riedl, 2008).
  - **close/adjacent**: Some field experiments and animal studies use common-pool resource games or mutualisms with similar incentive structures but do not match the canonical PGG structure (e.g., Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011; Bshary & Grutter, 2005).
  - **weak/none**: Several studies use dyadic games (trust, dictator, ultimatum), reputation games, or observational ecological setups that do not map directly onto PGGs.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:
  - **exact**: Several studies directly manipulate peer or pool punishment in PGGs (e.g., Sääksvuori et al., 2011; O'Gorman et al., 2009; Traulsen et al., 2012; Egas & Riedl, 2008).
  - **adjacent/weak**: Many studies focus on reputation (reward or withdrawal), or punishment in other game structures, or discuss punishment at the mechanistic level but do not combine it with relevant payoff outcomes.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
  - **exact/close**: A subset of papers measure group efficiency or payoff explicitly (e.g., Sääksvuori et al., 2011; O'Gorman et al., 2009; Egas & Riedl, 2008; Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011).
  - **adjacent/weak**: Several studies report only behavioral or process outcomes (e.g., contribution or cooperation rates, punishment frequency) without translating these to efficiency.
  - **none**: Mechanism-focused or observational papers (e.g., neural, emotional, or cultural mechanism studies) often do not report any group payoff outcomes.

**Summary:** Task relevance is solid for laboratory PGGs with punishment and efficiency outcomes but drops quickly for field, animal, or contextually rich studies. Only a subset combine all three forms of “exact” relevance.

# 3) Outcomes Measured in the Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (*efficiency, group payoff, welfare, total earnings, surplus*): Directly reported in a key subset of laboratory and some field PGG studies. These are most consequential for the prediction task.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (*contribution rate, cooperation, punishment frequency, norm compliance, antisocial punishment, withdrawal, reputation, emotional response*): Reported in most papers, especially in those discussing mechanisms, cross-cultural variance, or animal/field behavior. These outcomes may correlate with efficiency but are not equivalent.
- **Mixed or ambiguous reporting**: Several papers report increases in cooperation or reductions in defection but do not clarify impacts on net group earnings due to the costs of punishment.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant to Prediction

## Synthesized Payoff-based Findings

- **Punishment increases cooperation, but its effect on efficiency depends on context and game design**:
    - In repeated lab PGGs, **peer punishment can sustain higher contributions** and often stabilizes or slightly increases efficiency versus cooperation’s decay in no-punishment controls; however, **the cost of punishment frequently cancels out or exceeds the benefits of increased cooperation**, leading to net zero or negative changes in efficiency (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Sääksvuori et al., 2011; Traulsen et al., 2012).
    - **Pool punishment** (especially with second-order punishment) often *reduces efficiency relative to control* due to the high costs involved, even as it achieves high cooperation (Traulsen et al., 2012).
    - **Single designated punishers** can achieve similar cooperation at lower cost and thus higher efficiency than diffuse (all can punish) setups (O'Gorman et al., 2009).

- **Intergroup competition is a highly effective moderator**:
    - **With between-group competition**, enabling punishment can dramatically increase group efficiency—sometimes to levels exceeding optimal control efficiency—by amplifying both cooperation and the value of success (Sääksvuori et al., 2011).
    - **Without group competition**, the efficiency effect of punishment is small or negative.

- **Cultural, trust, and contextual moderators matter greatly**:
    - The efficiency effect of punishment is *highly variable across cultures* (Gächter et al., 2010, 2009). In some cases, punishment increases efficiency (via higher cooperation), in others there is no benefit or even a reduction in earnings due to antisocial punishment.
    - **Group’s self-determination and social trust** moderate field outcomes: externally imposed punishment can crowd out cooperation in high-trust or unsupportive groups—reducing efficiency, not increasing it (Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011).

- **Design features interact with punishment effect**:
    - **Punishment cost-to-impact ratio** is a key parameter. Only cheap, effective punishment (high impact for low cost) can sometimes increase net efficiency; otherwise, costs usually outweigh the social gain (Egas & Riedl, 2008).
    - **Communication, rewards, and reputation incentives**: While not punishment per se, these can substitute for or override punishment mechanisms, often achieving higher efficiency (Semmann et al., 2004).
    - **Antisocial punishment, group size, and repeated interactions**: Larger groups can dilute incentives to punish; presence of antisocial punishment can undermine any beneficial efficiency effect; repeated, non-anonymous interactions can alter baseline cooperation and the marginal value of punishment (Gächter et al., 2010; Bshary & Grutter, 2005).

## Non-payoff behavioral findings (explicitly not efficiency)

- **Punishment robustly increases cooperation/contributions in many lab settings**—but does not guarantee increased efficiency. Some studies report cooperation, not earnings, as primary outcomes (Gächter et al., 2010; Gächter & Herrmann, 2009).
- **Severity, frequency, and targets of punishment** are shaped by emotion (anger), parochialism, and social context, not only game incentives.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based on this literature:

- **Enabling peer punishment in standard lab PGGs most often increases average cooperation and sometimes efficiency, but the efficiency effect is contingent**:
    - **If the control (no-punishment) game yields high efficiency (close to optimal)**, enabling punishment may *reduce* efficiency due to unnecessary punishment costs.
    - **If the control efficiency declines over rounds due to unraveling cooperation**, punishment can maintain higher group earnings and efficiency, *but often only matches or slightly improves upon control*—the net effect depends on whether punishment costs are offset by increased public good production (see O'Gorman et al., 2009 vs Egas & Riedl, 2008).
    - **Single-punisher regimes** and *low-cost, high-impact punishment technologies* tend to deliver the greatest efficiency; diffuse punishment or high-cost mechanisms risk efficiency losses (O'Gorman et al., 2009; Egas & Riedl, 2008).

- **Key moderators that must be considered (and may not be captured by design variables alone):**
    - **Between-group competition** massively amplifies the efficiency benefit of punishment (Sääksvuori et al., 2011).
    - **Culture, trust, legitimacy of institutions, and anti-social punishment**: These can nullify or reverse the net effect of punishment on efficiency, even when design features are held constant (Gächter et al., 2010; Gächter & Herrmann, 2009; Vollan, 2008).
    - **Self-determination (e.g., group voting on rules) and norm internalization**: Socially accepted or chosen rules work better than externally imposed sanctions (Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011).

- **When prediction is based solely on game design dimensions plus control efficiency**, literature suggests:
    - **Punishment introduction yields a positive efficiency effect only when it allows cooperation to be sustained at a higher level than the control with lower net punishment cost.**
    - **Otherwise, efficiency remains unchanged or declines. The magnitude and direction of change are sensitive to context—especially punishment cost/tech, group competition, and sociocultural factors** (directly or indirectly related to the design variables).

- **Reward mechanisms and reputation incentives, while outside the core punishment focus, often achieve efficiency gains without the net welfare loss seen with costly punishment**.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions**:
- **player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Frequently varied and explicitly discussed in lab PGGs, central to efficiency predictions (Sääksvuori et al., 2011; O'Gorman et al., 2009; Traulsen et al., 2012; Egas & Riedl, 2008).
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries**: Sometimes mentioned; rarely found to be pivotal for efficiency except as context.

**Indirectly/contextually informed**:
- **chat**: Direct communication is generally absent in key studies; when present, often leads to higher cooperation and potentially confounds the role of punishment.
- **default_contrib**: Framing (opt-in/opt-out) is rarely manipulated in PGG punishment studies.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Important in studies addressing reward/reputation in lieu of punishment (Semmann et al., 2004).
- **show_punishment_id**: Occasionally present in animal studies, rarely manipulated as a central variable in human PGGs.

**Effectively missing**:
- Comprehensive, systematic manipulation of **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, default_contrib, show_punishment_id, reward_cost, reward_tech** as core variables influencing efficiency is rare. When present, these features are usually held constant or not analyzed as main drivers of efficiency changes due to punishment.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Contextual moderators often omitted**: The reviewed laboratory experiments typically cannot account for the effects of sociocultural context, social trust, or legitimacy, which field evidence suggests are critical to real-world efficiency outcomes. Models that use only design dimensions will systematically miss these effects (Vollan, 2008; Gächter et al., 2010).
- **Payoff–behavior disconnect**: Many studies measure and report behavior (contributions, punishment, emotions) but not efficiency; thus, care must be taken not to conflate increases in cooperation with increased group payoff or efficiency.
- **Heterogeneity of punishment regime**: Peer punishment, pool punishment, designated punisher, and probabilistic or external sanctioning may have divergent effects; these distinctions are not always operationalized in prediction dimensions.
- **Insufficient parameter variation**: While some game dimensions (player_count, num_rounds, MPCR, punishment_cost) are varied, others are rarely manipulated, limiting extrapolation.
- **Scarcity of long-term, real-world studies**: Most direct efficiency evidence comes from short-term lab games; field studies introduce ecological and contextual moderators but often lack control conditions or have only "close" relevance.
- **Antisocial punishment and negative effects**: Instances where punishment reduces earnings (due to revenge, resentment, or targeting of cooperators) are context-dependent and under-reported or under-analyzed.
- **Reward and reputation mechanisms sometimes confounded**: Effects attributed to punishment may be due to or moderated by implicit or explicit reputation signals, not always distinguished in prediction frameworks.

---

**In conclusion:** For prediction of group efficiency in PGGs when punishment is enabled, this literature supports the following guidance:
- Strongest, most direct evidence is from lab PGGs with careful manipulation of punishment.
- Enabling peer punishment is not consistently efficiency-improving and may in some cases reduce efficiency, depending strongly on punishment costs, impact, group competition, and contextual moderators.
- Extrapolation should be conservative—especially when control efficiency is already high or when sociocultural/field context is important but uncaptured by design variables. Predictions that rely solely on design dimensions plus control efficiency, without accounting for group competition, culture, or trust, may be severely limited in real-world settings.
