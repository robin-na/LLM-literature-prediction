# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 43 sources with a strong core of laboratory experiments, several field experiments, and a few observational/theoretical contributions. Empirical evidence dominates, and a significant subset provides exact public goods game (PGG) designs, often with direct efficiency or payoff measurements. Many others are variants (e.g., common-pool resource or third-party punishment games) that are adjacent or at best close to the PGG+punishment+efficiency prediction task.

Most of the evidence is drawn from laboratory economics or behavioral experiments, with a moderate range of variation in game designs (player count, punishment regimes, MPCR, etc.). Some field and contextually-rich studies expand the base to include ecological and cultural moderators. Mechanism-focused and neuroscientific papers are numerous but only rarely report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

Overall, this is a relatively broad and robust literature set for understanding how punishment affects PGG outcomes, but the highest task relevance (i.e., directly addressing efficiency effects of peer punishment in PGGs as a function of design dimensions) is concentrated in a smaller subset of the papers.

---

# 2) Task Relevance

### By Key Dimensions

#### `pgg_or_variant`
- **Exact:** A substantial number of papers use standard linear PGGs with classic design features; several more use close variants (common-pool resource, institutional punishment in PGGs).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Many others examine adjacent games (third-party punishment, Prisoner’s Dilemma, trust/ultimatum games), providing context or mechanistic insight but less direct transferability.
- **Weak/None:** Some papers do not use PGGs or variants at all.

#### `punishment_or_sanctions`
- **Exact:** Many studies manipulate peer or institutional punishment directly, with known cost-to-impact structures.
- **Close/Adjacent:** Others address reward, reputation, or partner switching as substitutes for punishment, or focus on circumstances where punishment cannot meaningfully occur.
- **Weak/None:** Mechanistic or cognitive studies often discuss punishment, but do not manipulate it as an institutional feature of the experimental design.

#### `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
- **Exact:** Several papers report efficiency or group payoff (total earnings relative to the socially optimal outcome).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some report behavioral proxies (e.g., contributions, overharvest, defection rates), where effects on efficiency are inferred but not measured.
- **Weak/None:** A large cluster exclusively report behavioral, cognitive, reputational, or neural outcomes; many do not report payoffs or group efficiency.

### Overall Summary

There is strong coverage and several direct sources for the prediction task: studies with exact/close matches report both the relevant treatments (with/without punishment) and payoff efficiency. However, much of the remaining literature is only contextually or mechanistically relevant. There are multiple clear gaps—e.g., direct experimental evidence remains sparse for many context/intervention combinations (e.g., games with chat enabled, or dynamic reward/punishment choices).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes:**  
  - *Directly Measured*: Group efficiency/total payoff, average earnings, welfare, surplus, coins generated.  
    - Examples: Sääksvuori et al. (2011), O’Gorman et al. (2009), Gächter et al. (2017), Egas & Riedl (2008), Vollan (2008), Gelcich et al. (2013).
  - *Occasionally Inferred*: When group contributions or resource extraction is closely tied to group payoff but not directly reported as efficiency.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  - *Contribution or cooperation rate*: Very common, often as primary outcome.  
  - *Punishment frequency/amount*: How often or severely punishment is used.  
  - *Norm compliance*: Adherence to cooperative or extraction norms.  
  - *Reputational response, emotional/cognitive reactions, neural responses*: Many studies focus on these proximate mechanisms.
  - *Crowding-out, trust, or legitimacy*: Sometimes measured as behavioral moderators, not as payoffs.

**Distinction**:  
Only a subset of studies link behavioral outcomes to efficiency outcomes. Behavioral improvements (e.g., higher contributions) do not always translate into efficiency gains—frequent finding is that costs of punishment can fully offset or even outweigh the efficiency benefits of increased cooperation.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### Synthesis (Empirical Evidence with Efficiency/Payoff Outcomes)

- **Punishment often increases cooperation, but does not always increase efficiency:**  
  - In standard PGGs, introducing costly peer punishment typically raises contribution rates. However, the net effect on efficiency (group payoff relative to social optimum) depends on how much the costs of punishment eat into group earnings (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Sääksvuori et al., 2011).
  - Efficiency gains from punishment are clearest when:  
    - Punishment is cost-effective (high impact per cost) (O'Gorman et al., 2009; Egas & Riedl, 2008; Gächter et al., 2017);
    - There is intergroup competition—punishment reduces free-riding and boosts both contributions and payoffs (Sääksvuori et al., 2011).

- **Punishment can reduce efficiency under certain conditions:**  
  - If punishment costs are high, or if antisocial punishment (punishment of cooperators) is prevalent (often as a function of culture or context), then overall group welfare and efficiency can be reduced compared to the no-punishment baseline (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2009).
  - Pool punishment and especially second-order punishment (punishing non-punishers) can strongly suppress efficiency, even as cooperation is stabilized (Traulsen et al., 2012).

- **Institutional or leader-based punishment effects are context-dependent:**  
  - Institutional punishment can be undermined by corruption/bribery, flipping the sign of its effect on contributions/efficiency (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
  - External regulation (exogenous punishment) may crowd out cooperation in high-trust communities or when imposed without group support, sometimes reducing efficiency relative to baseline (Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011).

- **Reward, reputation, and communication as alternatives or complements:**  
  - Reward and explicit reputation can increase efficiency without the deadweight losses of punishment; these effects can be as large as or larger than those of punishment (Semmann et al., 2004; Fehr & Schneider, 2010).

- **Group composition, culture, and buy-in moderate effects:**  
  - The impact of punishment is strongly affected by baseline trust, group social capital, self-determination (institution selection by group), culture/norms, and prior group performance (Gächter et al., 2010; Vollan, 2008; Gelcich et al., 2013; Castillo et al., 2011).
  - Control efficiency is often predictive of whether punishment will have much room to improve overall performance.

### Synthesis (Behavioral/Mechanism/Context)

- Many studies elucidate the motives, neural correlates, or predictors of punishment allocation, but do not report efficiency:  
  - These tend to confirm that psychological and social context (fairness, expectation, outgroup/ingroup structure, observability, etc.) mediate when and how much punishment occurs, but not its net efficiency effect.

### Ambiguity and Disagreement

- **Positive Efficiency Effects:**  
  - Seen in repeated lab PGGs with effective, peer punishment and especially when punishment is focused (e.g., single designated punisher) and not redundant (O’Gorman et al., 2009; Gächter et al., 2017).

- **Null/Negative Efficiency Effects:**  
  - Found where punishment is cost-ineffective, antisocial, or pool/second-order punishment is prominent (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Traulsen et al., 2012); and in some field studies when context is unfavorable (Castillo et al., 2011).

- **Strong Context Dependence:**  
  - Efficiency effects of punishment can vary not just in magnitude but in sign, especially across cultures or between field/lab environments (Vollan, 2008; Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Gächter et al., 2010).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**From this literature, the following guidance emerges for the downstream prediction task (predicting efficiency change when enabling peer punishment, controlling for design and control efficiency):**

- **Magnitude and Direction:**  
  - *Most commonly*, enabling peer punishment in a standard lab PGG with moderate group size, moderate MPCR, no chat, and continuous contributions should increase or at least maintain efficiency relative to control *if* punishment is cost-effective, antisocial punishment is rare, and no other strong negative contextual moderators are present (O’Gorman et al., 2009; Gächter et al., 2017).
  - If punishment is expensive (high cost, low impact), efficiency can decline.
  - If pool/second-order punishment is present, or institutional/leader punishment is subject to corruption, efficiency losses are likely (Traulsen et al., 2012; Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
  - Field and culturally diverse settings introduce greater odds of punishment crowding out cooperation, or of efficiency losses, especially where the mechanism is top-down, not group-supported (Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011).

- **Key Design Dimensions for Prediction:**  
  - **Directly Informed:**  
    - Player count, number of rounds, MPCR, all-or-nothing vs. continuous, punishment cost (including cost-to-impact), punishment regime (peer vs. institutional, single vs. multiple punishers), presence/absence of chat, group competition/competition framing, and sometimes presence of explicit reward/reputation mechanisms.
  - **Indirectly Informed:**  
    - Default contribution framing, presence of group-vote/self-determination, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries.
  - **Contextually Discussed/Missing:**  
    - show_punishment_id (punisher anonymity vs. transparency), detailed reward structures (reward cost, reward tech, show_reward_id), and more nuanced description of chat/inter-player communication. Many papers do not manipulate or report these.

- **Use of Control Efficiency:**  
  - Very low control efficiency (floor effect) can sometimes mean little room for further decline, making negative effects of costly punishment less likely. Conversely, already high control efficiency may limit observable gains from enabling punishment (Gelcich et al., 2013).

- **Caveats:**  
  - Do not predict uniform positive effects of punishment across all dimensions. Moderators such as baseline trust, ability to coordinate punishment, cultural context, corruption, and even randomness in group composition can change the sign and size of the effect.

---

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed (Commonly Manipulated/Reported):**
  - `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `all_or_nothing`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (cost-to-impact).
- **Moderately Informed (Often Reported, Sometimes Manipulated):**
  - `chat` (usually absent), `show_n_rounds` (typically known to players), `show_other_summaries` (sometimes included for transparency).
- **Less Informed/Indirectly Relevant:**
  - `default_contrib`, `punishment_exists` (nearly always the treatment variable, not a parameter), `reward_exists` (in a minority of studies).
- **Sparse or Contextual:**
  - `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `show_punishment_id`, `show_reward_id`—rarely manipulated as independent variables.
- **Contextual Moderators Not in Dimensions List but Important:**
  - Corruption/bribery opportunities, presence of intergroup competition, degree of self-determination/voting for institutions, baseline social capital or trust, culture, leadership structure.

---

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse/Nonexistent for Some Dimensions:**  
  - Evidence is limited or missing for effects of chat, default contribution framing, show_punishment_id, detailed reward technologies, or the effect of making punishment identity known.

- **Behavioral ≠ Efficiency:**  
  - Most studies prioritize contribution/cooperation rates over efficiency; translating behavioral gains to group payoff is nontrivial, as punishment costs can offset contributions.

- **Strong Contextual Effects:**  
  - Cultural, ecological, or institutional context can dramatically alter both the effect and direction of punishment on efficiency. Context moderators like group voting, corruption, social capital, and trust are not specified as formal prediction dimensions here but are shown to be critical.

- **Lab-Field and Variant-Generalizability Gaps:**  
  - Robust lab effects do not always transfer to field; effects observed in classic PGGs may not translate to common-pool or institutional variants, especially with added real-world noise (legitimacy, trust in authorities, economic necessity).

- **Neglect of Dynamic or Complex Designs:**  
  - Few studies explore dynamic (e.g., changing punishment cost), endogenous institution selection, or hybrid mechanisms (punishment plus reward, or layered sanctioning structures).

- **Ambiguous or Inferred Payoff Outcomes:**  
  - Some studies imply, but do not measure, efficiency or group payoff.

- **Overlap in Predictors/Moderators:**  
  - Effects of design dimensions on efficiency sometimes interact (e.g., punishment cost and group size), but the independent and joint effects are not always disentangled.

**In summary:** While the evidence base is rich for standard laboratory PGGs with peer punishment and efficiency measured, the literature should be treated carefully in making predictions outside of these core settings or when considering under-examined design dimensions or strong contextual moderators. Predictive power may be limited in contexts with substantial institutional, cultural, or ecological complexity.
