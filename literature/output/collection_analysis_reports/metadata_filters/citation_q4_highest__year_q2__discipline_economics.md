# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set contains 28 sources with a strong preponderance of empirical, laboratory-based public goods game (PGG) experiments, supported by a few field experiments and theoretical contributions. Many papers provide direct evidence about the effects of enabling peer punishment in linear PGGs under various design dimensions. A substantial subset reports primary outcomes in efficiency or closely related group payoff measures, while another subset focuses on behavioral outcomes (such as contribution rate) without always linking them quantitatively to efficiency.

Coverage of the canonical experimental PGG framework is broad in terms of player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment parameters, and feedback. A minority of papers address adjacent or more specialized environments (dynamic endowments, partnership games, weakest-link, institutional sanctions, or exclusion). The set includes both direct empirical tests of punishment activation (enabled vs. disabled within otherwise identical games) and richer analyses of moderators—including heterogeneity, emotional context, group formation, and communication channels.

A smaller but important number of theory papers establish mechanism arguments and upper bounds on sustainable efficiency. The literature is richer for standard (static) PGGs than for dynamic or field variants, and evidence is more robust for peer punishment (informal, voluntary) than for exclusion, ostracism, or reward systems.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact**: Most empirical lab papers use canonical linear PGGs or voluntary contribution mechanisms (e.g., Drouvelis & Grosskopf 2016; Dickinson & Masclet 2015; Reuben & Riedl 2013; Markussen et al. 2014; Kölle 2015; Dal Bó & Dal Bó 2014; Choi & Ahn 2013; Joffily et al. 2014; Weng & Carlsson 2015).
- **close/adjacent**: A few studies use partnership games, dynamic PGGs, weakest-link games, or related field analogues (Kosfeld & Rustagi 2015; Gächter et al. 2017; Riedl et al. 2016; Ali & Miller 2016).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact**: Many studies use explicit peer-to-peer costly punishment as the primary treatment (e.g., Drouvelis & Grosskopf 2016; Reuben & Riedl 2013; Dickinson & Masclet 2015; Markussen et al. 2014; Dal Bó & Dal Bó 2014; Kölle 2015; Choi & Ahn 2013; Joffily et al. 2014; Weng & Carlsson 2015).
- **adjacent**: Some use other forms, such as exclusion, ostracism, or formal sanctions (Charness & Yang 2014; Ali & Miller 2016; Riedl et al. 2016).
- **none**: Several relevant control or contextual papers do not address sanctions (e.g., baseline studies, chat/disclosure manipulations).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact**: Several directly report efficiency (ratio of realized to maximum group payoff), group earnings, or welfare (Drouvelis & Grosskopf 2016; Reuben & Riedl 2013; Markussen et al. 2014; Kölle 2015; Dal Bó & Dal Bó 2014; Kosfeld & Rustagi 2015; Gächter et al. 2017; Wolitzky 2013).
- **close**: Others provide detailed group payoff or wealth data, which are convertible to efficiency (Choi & Ahn 2013; Charness & Yang 2014; some theory).
- **adjacent/weak**: A subset reports only contribution rates, punishment assigned, or norm behavioral outcomes.

**Summary:** The task relevance is high for standard PGGs with peer punishment and efficiency or group-payoff outcomes. For variants (dynamic games, ostracism), field or adjacent mechanisms, and non-payoff-only papers, relevance is reduced.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (direct relevance):**
- Efficiency (as the % of optimal group payoff): Drouvelis & Grosskopf 2016; Reuben & Riedl 2013; Markussen et al. 2014; Kölle 2015; Charness & Yang 2014; Wolitzky 2013 (theory); Dal Bó & Dal Bó 2014; Gächter et al. 2017 (group wealth).
- Group earnings, welfare, total payoff, or similar measures: Choi & Ahn 2013 (implied via contribution data); Kosfeld & Rustagi 2015 (field: trees/ha as a proxy); Dal Bó & Dal Bó 2014.

**Behavioral (non-payoff) outcomes:**
- Contribution or cooperation rates: Joffily et al. 2014; Weng & Carlsson 2015; Dickinson & Masclet 2015 (with payoffs as secondary), Choi & Ahn 2013, Joffily et al. 2014, Sylwester et al. 2013.
- Frequency and magnitude of punishment assigned: Dickinson & Masclet 2015; Choi & Ahn 2013; Joffily et al. 2014.
- Emotional reactions, norm compliance, punishment/reward choices: Joffily et al. 2014; Weng & Carlsson 2015.

**Distinction:** Not all increases in contribution rates translate into increased efficiency, due to costly punishment reducing net payoffs, antisocial punishment, or the destruction of resources by excessive sanctioning.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

1. **Peer Punishment Increases Efficiency in Standard PGGs:**  
   The majority finding for standard lab PGGs (fixed endowments, continuous contributions, small groups) is that enabling peer punishment reduces free-riding, increases contributions, and—crucially—increases group efficiency compared to the no-punishment control (Drouvelis & Grosskopf 2016; Reuben & Riedl 2013; Markussen et al. 2014; Wolitzky 2013; Dal Bó & Dal Bó 2014; Choi & Ahn 2013).

2. **Moderating Factors:**  
   - **Group Heterogeneity:** The effectiveness of punishment is moderated by the nature of heterogeneity—punishment increases efficiency most in capability-heterogeneous groups and less/none in valuation-heterogeneous groups (Kölle 2015).
   - **Punishment Cost and Effectiveness:** Punishment is most efficient when the cost-to-impact ratio is favorable; increases in cost reduce but do not eliminate the positive impact (Markussen et al. 2014; Weng & Carlsson 2015).
   - **Emotional Context and Venting:** Emotional states and opportunities for cooling-off modulate efficiency: too much or too little venting can reduce the efficiency gains from punishment (Drouvelis & Grosskopf 2016; Dickinson & Masclet 2015).
   - **Antisocial Punishment and Inequality:** When punishment is antisocial or inequality is high, enabling punishment can have null or negative efficiency effects due to resource waste (Gächter et al. 2017; Sylwester et al. 2013; Kosfeld & Rustagi 2015).
   - **Length and Structure of Interaction:** In dynamic or long games, or games with endogenous endowments, the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is diminished or reversed (Gächter et al. 2017).
   - **Communication and Voting:** Endogenous selection of punishment regimes and communication about punishment enhance efficiency (Markussen et al. 2014; Cooper & Kühn 2014; some evidence from Charness & Yang 2014 about exclusion).

3. **Mechanism Arguments:**  
   Theory confirms that punishment can sustain high-efficiency equilibria, particularly in repeated settings given good monitoring and favorable discount factors (Wolitzky 2013; Hilbe et al. 2015). However, the upper bound depends on (i) the ability to detect defection; (ii) costs of punishment; (iii) group size and structure.

4. **Exceptions and Cautions:**
   - In field or dynamic settings, or with high inequality and prevalence of antisocial punishment, punishment may not increase and may reduce efficiency (Gächter et al. 2017; Kosfeld & Rustagi 2015).
   - The design and implementation of the punishment system (e.g., permanent ostracism vs. temporary with forgiveness) critically affect whether efficiency is realized (Ali & Miller 2016; Markussen et al. 2014).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Baseline Expectation:** For standard lab PGGs, controlling for the observed efficiency of the control (no-punishment) game, enabling peer punishment will generally produce a significant and robust increase in average efficiency. The predicted efficiency with punishment should rarely fall below the control efficiency, and is often near or at the maximum feasible (Reuben & Riedl 2013; Markussen et al. 2014; Wolitzky 2013).

- **Adjustments:**  
  The magnitude of the efficiency boost depends on:
    - **Group heterogeneity:** For capability-heterogeneous groups, expect especially large gains; for valuation heterogeneity, little or no gain (Kölle 2015).
    - **Punishment cost and technology:** As punishment becomes more expensive (or less effective per unit), the efficiency gain shrinks but does not typically vanish unless extreme (Markussen et al. 2014; Weng & Carlsson 2015).
    - **Emotional context:** The effect of punishment may diminish under anger or excessive venting, as punishment costs rise without further increasing cooperation (Drouvelis & Grosskopf 2016; Dickinson & Masclet 2015).
    - **Possibility of antisocial punishment:** If antisocial punishment is frequent, or group inequality is high, the efficiency gain can even reverse (Gächter et al. 2017; Sylwester et al. 2013).
    - **Repeated/dynamic games:** In dynamic public goods games or contexts where wealth accumulates and inequality emerges, the efficiency benefit can be negated or reversed (Gächter et al. 2017).

- **Key Moderators Lacking Data:**  
  For games with chat/communication, endogenous group formation, exclusion/forgiveness instead of direct costly punishment, reward mechanisms, or field settings, predictions should be cautious and possibly rely on adjacent findings.

- **Use of Non-Payoff Outcomes:**  
  Where only contribution rate or punishment data are available, infer efficiency only when the punishment cost is low relative to gains, and note possible overstatement if excessive punishment occurs.

- **Aggregate Prediction Heuristic:**  
  If game design closely matches canonical lab PGGs, upgrade efficiency by a substantial fixed increment unless moderators (noted above) indicate otherwise. Down-adjust or hold steady for settings with high antisocial punishment, dynamic wealth, or high initial inequality.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed:**  
  - `player_count` (varied across studies with evidence on scaling: Reuben & Riedl 2013; Markussen et al. 2014; Kölle 2015)
  - `num_rounds` (short vs. long games: Dickinson & Masclet 2015; Markussen et al. 2014; Gächter et al. 2017)
  - `mpcr` (explored within and across papers: Wolitzky 2013; Kölle 2015)
  - `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (multiple cost/impact settings: Markussen et al. 2014; Weng & Carlsson 2015)
  - `all_or_nothing` (mostly continuous, some discrete: Choi & Ahn 2013)
  - `show_other_summaries` (feedback almost always present)
- **Indirectly or Contextually Discussed:**  
  - `chat` (rare; only non-punishment papers focus here: Oprea et al. 2014)
  - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (occasionally, e.g., Choi & Ahn 2013 as comparison arms)
  - `show_n_rounds` (occasionally reported, uncertain relevance)
  - `show_punishment_id` (punishment usually anonymous; in some, discussed as a moderator, e.g., Markussen et al. 2014)
  - `default_contrib` (rarely manipulated)
- **Effectively Missing:**  
  - Little direct variant on `default_contrib`
  - Rare direct manipulation of `show_punishment_id` or identity disclosure
  - Very limited direct evidence on `chat` as an interaction with punishment

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavioral–Payoff Disconnect:** Many papers infer efficiency gains from increased contributions, but do not always quantify the net impact after punishment costs; in settings with excessive/antisocial punishment, contributions can rise without increasing or even while decreasing net efficiency.
- **Parameter Coverage:** While core PGG dimensions are well covered, design features such as communication (`chat`), identity disclosure, default framing, and explicit reward mechanisms are less systematically varied in PGGs with punishment.
- **Field and Policy Relevance:** Lab effects may not transfer cleanly to field, dynamic, or larger-scale environments—real-world complexity, culture, and heterogeneity (e.g., antisocial punishment in high-inequality settings) can alter the effect (Kosfeld & Rustagi 2015; Gächter et al. 2017).
- **Complex Moderation:** Effects are often conditional on unobserved moderators (e.g., emotional context, social norms, leader motives), which may not be part of prediction input data.
- **Lack of Long-term Data:** Most evidence concerns short to medium time frames; long-run dynamics, especially under repeated sanctioning, are underexplored.
- **Limited Evidence for Some Dimensions:** For several design variables (noted above), evidence is indirect or unavailable. Prediction for settings with those features is less certain.
- **Adjacency of Mechanisms:** Some high-efficiency mechanisms (exclusion, forgiveness, endogenous group choice) are not strictly costly peer punishment and may operate differently.

---

**In summary:**
This literature strongly supports the expectation that enabling peer punishment in standard public goods games will increase efficiency relative to the control game, but the effect is moderated by group structure, punishment cost, social and emotional conditions, and the presence of inequality or antisocial punishment. The prediction task is well informed for canonical lab PGGs, but for games with non-standard dimensions—especially chat, exclusion, dynamic/field settings, or varying incentive structures—the evidence is sparse and extrapolation must be cautious.
