# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 16 papers that blend **empirical lab experiments** and **theoretical models**. The evidence base is relatively **narrow with regards to the prediction task**, as about half the papers focus directly on standard public goods games (PGG) and explicit punishment mechanisms, while the rest examine adjacent or related social dilemmas (such as generalized exchange, threshold games, or intertemporal resource dilemmas), or discuss adjacent mechanisms like exclusion, reputation, or redistribution. Most empirically grounded papers use canonical or nearly canonical PGG setups and laboratory manipulations, but a significant subset theorize about sanctioning mechanisms in structurally similar—but not identical—settings.

There is a **good representation of both peer and centralized punishment** mechanisms, with some studies exploring variations such as exclusion, social feedback, and redistribution. The literature includes prominent experimental results focusing on group earnings/efficiency, and several theoretical papers that model the efficiency effects of punishment under different institutional and parameter regimes. However, direct quantitative comparisons of efficiency across a wide variety of the 14 game-design dimensions are rare.

# 2) Task Relevance

Evaluation by the three target-relevance dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant**
    - **exact:** ~7 papers (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Grund et al., 2020; Nax et al., 2018)
    - **close/adjacent:** Most others, engaging with structurally similar games (e.g., Kanitsar, 2021; Bolle, 2021; Di Guida et al., 2021; Becchetti et al., 2018; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020)
    - **weak/none:** Only a few model settings with little overlap (e.g., McCannon, 2018)

- **punishment_or_sanctions**
    - **exact:** 7-8 papers, addressing explicit costly punishment either in peer or centralized form (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Bolle, 2021)
    - **adjacent/close:** Studies on exclusion, social feedback, or redistribution mechanisms are adjacent (Di Guida et al., 2021; Grund et al., 2020; Becchetti et al., 2018; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020)
    - **none:** Several theory papers focus on alternative cooperation mechanisms (e.g., McCannon, 2018; Madeo & Mocenni, 2021)

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**
    - **exact:** Several key papers directly measure or model group payoff, total efficiency, or welfare (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Nax et al., 2018; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018)
    - **close/adjacent:** Others focus on contributions/cooperation (a proxy), but not payoff per se (e.g., Grund et al., 2020; Becchetti et al., 2018)
    - **non_payoff_behavior:** Several theoretical contributions and discussions center on cooperation, norms, or group structure with only adjacent or theoretical links to efficiency (e.g., Okada, 2020; Andersson, 2020; McCannon, 2018; Madeo & Mocenni, 2021)

**Summary:**  
The subset of papers with **high task relevance** (exact PGG, explicit punishment, explicit efficiency outcome) is limited; several others provide valuable adjacent context but must be interpreted cautiously for downstream prediction.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (directly relevant):**
    - Group earnings or net profits (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021)
    - Efficiency: ratio of realized group payoff to social optimum (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Nax et al., 2018; Di Guida et al., 2021)
    - Welfare (Friehe & Tabbach, 2018)

- **Closely related or indirectly relevant:**
    - Surplus allocation (Di Guida et al., 2021)
    - Efficiency in adjacent games (e.g., resource sustainability, Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020)

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (not payoff):**
    - Contribution rate, cooperation frequency (Grund et al., 2020; Becchetti et al., 2018; Okada, 2020)
    - Punishment frequency, norm compliance (Okada, 2020; Grund et al., 2020)
    - Emotional state, norm adherence (Andersson, 2020; McCannon, 2018)

**Explicit note:**  
Several studies carefully distinguish between changes in cooperation/contributions and changes in group efficiency or payoff. Some report only behavioral outcomes, with only theoretical or implied links to payoff.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical Evidence
- **Enabling punishment generally increases group efficiency or earnings** in canonical PGGs, compared to a control without punishment (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021), though **the effect is moderate and highly variable**.
- **Centralized punishment** (e.g., via a group “manager”) yields robust efficiency improvements versus control, and is effective across several variants of punishment cost/effectiveness (Castillo et al., 2021).
- **Peer punishment’s positive effect on efficiency is contingent**: The baseline culture (e.g., prevalence of strong reciprocators) and punishment styles (norm-keepers vs. strong reciprocators) strongly moderate the treatment effect (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Costless punishment yields efficiency gains**, but costly punishment often does not—efficiency improvements are not observed if the cost of sanctioning is high (Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Group structure matters:** In “dense” sanctioning networks, punishment may be effective; in “sparse” or generalized exchange setups, it is not (Kanitsar, 2021).
- In **adjacent institutional settings**, other mechanisms can also boost efficiency: contribution-based grouping (Nax et al., 2018), public social feedback (Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020), redistribution (Becchetti et al., 2018), exclusion or replacement (Grund et al., 2020).

## Theoretical Evidence
- **Punishment can stabilize full cooperation and thus maximize efficiency** if the punishment fraction or effectiveness exceeds critical thresholds, and is shaped by group size and MPCR (Botta et al., 2021; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021).
- **Action-based punishment (punishing based on actions, not just outcomes) more reliably increases efficiency** in threshold/volunteer games (Friehe & Tabbach, 2018).
- **Punishment may fail or even reduce efficiency** in generalized exchange, threshold PGGs, or where resistance is individually costly (Bolle, 2021).
- **Emotional or non-standard preferences (friendly/hostile states) can facilitate or hinder return to efficient cooperation** after punishment (Andersson, 2020).

## Nuanced Mechanism Points
- **Antisocial punishment (targeting high contributors)** is rare, but 'norm-keeper'-style punishment (punishing both high and low contributors) can reduce efficiency (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Punishment effectiveness, cost, and who holds punitive power** (peer vs. institutional, and in-government type) affect the magnitude of efficiency gains (Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- When **peer or centralized punishment is enabled in a standard PGG**, **efficiency typically increases** compared to a no-punishment control, **but the effect varies**:
    - **Magnitude depends** on punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and group-level social preferences (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
    - **Efficiency gain is large when:** punishment is costless or low-cost, effectiveness is high, and strong reciprocators dominate the group.
    - **Efficiency gain is limited/absent when:** punishment is costly, norm-keeper-style punishment is common, or the structure is sparse/generalized exchange.
- **Game design dimensions** that can be reliably used for prediction from this literature set include: `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, and the presence/form of `punishment_tech` (peer/centralized/fractional).
- **Control efficiency** can serve as a reference/baseline, but **the increase due to enabling punishment is not uniform**; it is context-dependent based on the above moderators.
- **Absent/poorly informed design dimensions** (e.g., `chat`, `show_other_summaries`, `default_contrib`, `reward_exists`, visibility variables) may introduce noise or uncertainty in the prediction.
- **Empirical and theoretical evidence converge** to recommend **cautious, upward adjustment of expected efficiency** when punishment is enabled, subject to:
    - Downscaling if the game involves high punishment costs or network sparsity.
    - Upscaling if centralized punishment or fractional random punishment with sufficient scope is used.
- **Adjacent mechanisms** (redistribution, social feedback, exclusion) may also elevate efficiency, but the degree to which these map onto explicit punishment-enabled designs is limited.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly and richly informed (both empirically and theoretically):**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `all_or_nothing`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (peer vs. centralized; fractional vs. full), and sometimes `punishment_magnitude`
- Control vs. treatment comparisons explicitly available for these variables

**Indirectly or sparsely informed:**
- `chat` (occasionally present as a manipulation, but not systematically linked to efficiency outcomes)
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` (feedback and information variables are contextually discussed; some evidence from social feedback studies suggests importance, but few direct ties to payoff)
- `default_contrib` (framing of default behavior is rarely isolated as a variable)
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (very few settings study the coexistence of reward and punishment; effect on efficiency is underexplored)
- `punishment_magnitude` (sometimes specified, but rarely systematically varied except in theory/simulation)
- `reward_magnitude` (almost never varied or discussed)

**Missing or only contextually discussed:**
- Direct manipulation or systematic study of all feedback/visibility dimensions (`show_xx`) is rare.
- No papers systematically compare design variants along more than a handful of the 14 dimensions simultaneously.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Feasibility of generalization is limited**: Most findings are in canonical lab PGGs with limited variation; prediction outside these settings (e.g., with chat, complex feedback, mixed incentive structures) is uncertain.
- **Punishment’s effect is context-dependent**: Group composition, culture, and network structure can strongly moderate the efficiency effect—features that are not always encoded in the 14 design variables (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Many papers report only non-payoff outcomes**: Contributions/cooperation rates are often surrogates for efficiency, but do not always map cleanly, especially when punishment costs are high.
- **Sparse evidence on several design features**: Little is known about how chat, feedback, default options, and especially combined punishment/reward systems (and their costs) affect efficiency.
- **Empirical heterogeneity is high**: Moderate treatment effects and broad variation across groups and societies warn against strong point predictions.
- **Adjacent results require careful mapping:** Results on exclusion, reputation, social feedback, and redistribution cannot be uncritically applied to explicit punishment predictions.
- **Theoretical models rely on strong assumptions** about rationality, observability, or homogeneity that may not hold in practical settings.
- **Absence of multifactorial manipulations:** Most studies change one or two variables at a time; joint effects of several design dimensions remain unclear.

---

**In summary**, this literature set provides solid but contextually bounded evidence that enabling peer or centralized punishment in PGGs increases group efficiency relative to baseline. The effect is meaningful but contingent—most reliably predicted as positive when punishment is not too costly and the group/network context is favorable. Many game-design dimensions essential for fine-grained prediction are only partially addressed; salient moderators (group composition, network structure, cultural factors) are difficult to code from the standard parameter set. Thus, while the literature supports a positive punishment effect on efficiency, uncertainty remains substantial, especially outside of simple, canonical designs.
