# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set provides a very broad and deep empirical foundation for understanding the effects of punishment in public-goods-game (PGG) and closely related environments. The vast majority of papers are experimental—overwhelmingly lab experiments, with some lab-in-the-field and framed field experiments, and a smaller number of observational studies. The studies range from highly canonical linear PGGs with standard parameters to more complex, endogenous-institution environments and context-specific CPR games.

Crucially, more than two dozen empirical papers are highly **directly relevant** to the prediction task: they experiment with enabling/disabling punishment in PGG or close variants, systematically measure payoff-related outcomes (especially group efficiency, group payoff, or total earnings), and report results as a function of game design dimensions. These include manipulations of group size, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost and technology, information structure, and more.

In addition to the main PGG literature, there is a sizable adjacent literature on related social dilemmas (CPRs, trust games, contests, markets, and bargaining), and on key behavioral moderators (e.g., antisocial punishment, norm diversity, group composition). A substantial secondary tier of studies focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes such as cooperation rates, punishment patterns, and norm enforcement, but without direct measurement of efficiency or group payoff, limiting their quantitative predictive value.

Theoretical, review, and meta-analytic work is present but secondary—most findings are derived from detailed empirical studies, providing strong support for empirical generalization and prediction.

**Summary:** The evidence base is extensive, recent, and methodologically rigorous for the target prediction task, especially for classic PGGs with and without punishment. However, some prediction dimensions (e.g., chat, reward, information structure) are more sparsely or indirectly covered.

# 2) Task Relevance

### pgg_or_variant:
- **Relevance**: *Exact* for the majority of studies; many papers use standard linear or binary PGGs, or tightly related variants (VCM, threshold public goods, CPR with linear payoffs).
- **Empirical coverage**: Broad and direct—most tested settings match the structure of the prediction task.

### punishment_or_sanctions:
- **Relevance**: *Exact* in key studies, *close* in CPRs and some institutional environments, *adjacent* in reputation/gossip/ostracism or trust games.
- **Empirical coverage**: The majority of experimental studies manipulate the presence, cost, technology, or process for enabling punishment (peer, centralized/authority, third-party, endogenous choice, exclusion, etc.). Effects of reward and combined punishment/reward are also studied, but less central for the task.

### efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:
- **Relevance**: *Exact* in a substantial core—group efficiency (total payoff/time, welfare as % of max, or average earnings) is a primary outcome in many studies.
- **Empirical coverage**: Some studies only report contribution rates, norm compliance, or other behavioral metrics (*adjacent*), while others report both behavioral and payoff outcomes. Where direct efficiency outcomes are not measured, contribution and punishment frequency are provided as context, with interpretation needed for payoff consequences.

**Summary:** Overall, the literature set is highly relevant and directly informative for prediction across all three dimensions, with efficiency outcomes being available and focal in the central PGG/punishment literature.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

### Payoff-related Outcomes
- **Efficiency, group payoff, total (group or average) earnings, welfare, surplus, coins generated**: Many key studies focus directly on these, reporting outcomes both as levels and as ratios to the cooperative optimum.
- **Incremental efficiency gain (treatment vs. control)**: Several studies explicitly report and contrast the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency, controlling for baseline (no-punishment) performance.

### Non-Payoff, Behavioral Outcomes
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**: Contribution per round, frequency of cooperation.
- **Punishment frequency, targeting (prosocial/antisocial), costs**: How often punishment is used, and against whom.
- **Compliance, norm enforcement, satisfaction, trust, fairness, belief dynamics**: Processes driving behavior, not always tied to efficiency outcomes.
- **Sanctioning and reward behavior and reputation effects**: How incentives are perceived and acted upon, effects of sanctions on reputation, etc.

**Distinction:** While high contribution rates are associated with high efficiency if punishment is costless, in most real-world (and experimental) PGGs, punishment is costly and may not translate into higher efficiency. Thus, studies that measure only contributions or punishment rates cannot be assumed to show increases in efficiency unless payoff consequences are also measured and reported.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### General Patterns (Empirical):
1. **Punishment often increases efficiency, but not always**:
    - In standard linear PGGs with peer or centralized punishment, enabling punishment often (but not universally) increases group efficiency relative to a no-punishment control, especially when baseline efficiency is low (e.g., (Arechar et al., 2018); (Gürdal et al., 2021)). However, efficiency gains depend on the costs and effectiveness of punishment, group composition, and institutional design.

2. **Key moderators affecting punishment’s efficiency effect**:
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness**: Efficiency gains are maximized when punishment is effective but not too costly (Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020).
    - **Antisocial punishment**: When antisocial punishment is prevalent—punishment targeted at high contributors—efficiency can be reduced or reversed relative to control (Bruhin et al., 2020; Mantilla et al., 2021).
    - **Information structure**: Full information about others’ contributions and endowments enables effective punishment and raises efficiency; incomplete or noisy information reduces punishment’s effectiveness and can cause mis-targeting, reducing or negating efficiency gains (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Nicklisch et al., 2021).
    - **Institutional design (centralized, democratic, authority, exogenous vs. endogenous)**: Centralized punishment, especially when assigned to a leader or by group vote, tends to be more efficient than dispersed peer punishment due to reduced coordination failures and lower punishment cost (Engl et al., 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Nockur et al., 2021).
    - **Cultural/group composition and heterogeneity**: Diversity (ethnic, normative), distribution of punishment types (presence of anti-social types) affect both the direction and magnitude of efficiency gains from punishment (Bruhin et al., 2020; Mantilla et al., 2021).
    - **Emotional/social environment and framing**: Emotional induction (anger vs. happiness), presence of communication, structure of feedback, and game framing (Give/Take, contest/prize, social dilemma/coordination) all moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency (Lee & Min, 2021; Koch et al., 2021; Fatas et al., 2020).

3. **Contexts where punishment reduces or does not enhance efficiency**:
    - **High baseline efficiency (cooperative groups)**: If the control (no-punishment) group is already efficient, enabling punishment may reduce efficiency due to punishment costs outweighing small behavioral benefits (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).
    - **High-frequency antisocial or mis-targeted punishment**: Reduces group welfare, can cause efficiency to decrease below control levels (Nockur et al., 2021; Bruhin et al., 2020; Vollan et al., 2019).
    - **Ineffective or too costly punishment, collective sanctions, improper targeting**: Default or automatic punishment with weak deterrence, collective sanctions (punishing whole groups), or very expensive punishment institutions may reduce or fail to increase efficiency (Yang et al., 2020; Chapkovski, 2021; Safarzynska, 2020).

4. **Reward mechanisms and comparison to punishment**:
    - Peer reward can sometimes generate higher efficiency gains than punishment (Kamijo et al., 2020; Gürerk et al., 2018), especially when cost/benefit ratio is favorable and reward is budget-balanced or targeted.
    - In environments where both are available, the presence of reward can complement punishment or substitute for it in sustaining cooperation and raising efficiency.

5. **Design-dimension-specific empirical signals**:
    - **Group size**: Efficiency effects of punishment can vary non-linearly with player count—benefits may be greatest in small to medium groups, but over-punishment and coordination problems in large groups can negate gains (Kamei, 2020).
    - **Number of rounds/time horizon**: Long games allow positive effects of punishment to accumulate and learning to take place, but end-game effects may still erode gains unless institutions co-evolve (Fatas et al., 2020; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
    - **Chat/communication**: The addition of chat or communication can, in some contexts, fully substitute for the efficiency benefits of punishment, but when paired with punishment can mitigate antisocial punishment and enhance efficiency further (Morgan et al., 2019; Koch et al., 2021).

### Theory/Mechanism arguments:
- **Deterrence**: Punishment’s main efficiency pathway is via deterrence of free riding/defection; when deterrence is weak, punishment is ineffective.
- **Targeting and coordination**: Institutions that enable precise targeting of punishment, prevent over-punishment, and coordinate sanctioning are more effective at improving efficiency.
- **Normative context**: Social norms around fairness, reciprocity, and appropriate use of punishment moderate how institutions are used and whether efficiency gains are realized.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based on the literature:

- **Baseline control efficiency is a strong but insufficient predictor of treatment (with-punishment) efficiency**. The effect size and sign of efficiency change when enabling punishment depend strongly on several interacting design dimensions:
    - **If punishment is low-cost, high-impact, and prosocial (mainly targets free riders)**: Expect a substantial positive effect on efficiency, especially if the control game is inefficient. [(Arechar et al., 2018); (Gürdal et al., 2021); (Gürerk et al., 2018)]
    - **If punishment is costly, mis-targeted, antisocial, or information about contributions/endowments is incomplete**: Efficiency gain may be zero or negative, even if control efficiency is low. [(Bruhin et al., 2020); (Mantilla et al., 2021)]
    - **Peer punishment vs. centralized/democratic/authority punishment**: Peer punishment often incurs higher costs, coordination failures, and antisocial punishment, reducing net efficiency relative to centralized or democratic punishment. [(Nockur et al., 2021); (Harrell, 2019); (Engl et al., 2021); (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019)]
    - **Game framing (Give/Take)**: In Give-frame environments, punishment increases contributions but often does not increase efficiency; in Take-frame or commission-based framing, punishment more reliably increases earnings and efficiency. [(Ramalingam et al., 2019)]
    - **Presence of strong reward mechanisms**: If reward is available (especially budget-balanced and well-targeted), it may substitute for or enhance punishment, leading to even higher efficiency. [(Kamijo et al., 2020); (Yang et al., 2018)]

**For predictive modeling:**
- Efficiency in punishment-enabled games = Control efficiency + Increment conditional on (punishment_exists, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, targeting, info structure, antisocial_punishment risk, group composition, and possible moderators such as chat).
- When only behavioral outcomes (contributions, cooperation rates) are reported, do **not** assume efficiency increases unless punishment is costless or reward-based mechanisms are present.
- **Crucial moderators to model as interaction terms include**: punishment_cost × antisocial_punishment, info quality × punishment_exists, authority structure × punishment_exists, endowment/distributional heterogeneity × punishment_exists.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

### Directly Informed Dimensions
- `player_count` – Strong evidence for effects of group size on efficiency impacts of punishment.
- `num_rounds` – Covered; time horizon and end-game dynamics are empirically mapped.
- `mpcr` – Central in the literature; efficiency and punishment effects vary with MPCR.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` – Extensively manipulated; cost/impact ratios are critical moderators.
- `all_or_nothing`, `default_contrib` – Both continuous and binary (all-or-nothing) PGGs are covered.
- `chat` – Multiple papers compare with/without communication.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` – Degree of information and identification is well-studied (especially the effects on targeting, antisocial punishment, and norm enforcement).

### Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` – Used in some studies to contrast with or supplement punishment.
- `show_punishment_id` – Sometimes manipulated (anonymous vs. identified punishment); effects on targeting and antisocial punishment observed.

### Effectively Missing or Very Sparse
- Certain variants: highly complex institutional environments, rare interaction types, or unusual framing.
- Some interaction terms, such as the effect of default contribution framing (`default_contrib`) in the presence of punishment, are less directly studied.
- Fine-grained manipulation of summary feedback (`show_other_summaries`) in interaction with punishment is less common, though feedback in general is addressed.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **External validity:** Most evidence is from lab settings, primarily university student samples; effects in field, cross-cultural, or naturally occurring institutions may differ (Bruhin et al., 2020; Pedersen et al., 2020).
- **Unobserved variation in control efficiency:** Some papers report only treatment outcomes; use with caution where control efficiency must be imputed.
- **Complex moderators and heterogeneity:** Antisocial punishment, group composition, information structure, and emotional context introduce substantial heterogeneity in effects; average effects may mask important minority or contextual failures.
- **Indirectness for some design dimensions:** Certain dimensions (reward, chat, summary feedback) are well-studied, but others (framing, timing, identification) are less so, or their interaction with punishment is not always explicitly modeled.
- **Payoff vs. behavior outcome confusion:** Many studies report only contribution rates or cooperation frequency; unless group payoff/efficiency is explicitly reported, these must be treated cautiously, especially if punishment is costly or mis-targeted.
- **Dynamic and endogenous institutions:** While studies of endogenous, evolving punishment institutions exist, their dynamics may not generalize to fixed, exogenous policy changes as in the prediction task.
- **Absence of explicit modeling for more complex payoff structures:** Many real-world public goods settings have nonlinear returns, threshold effects, or external risks that are not captured in canonical lab designs.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature provides strong, nuanced, and directly applicable empirical support for predicting how enabling punishment will affect efficiency in PGG-like environments, conditional on game design dimensions and baseline (control) efficiency. However, the predictive relationship is non-monotonic and context-dependent, and must account for critical moderators such as punishment cost/effectiveness, information structure, antisocial punishment, and group composition. Behavioral outcomes alone are insufficient to predict efficiency gains; payoffs must be directly observed or carefully inferred with consideration of the cost structure. Where design dimensions are only sparsely addressed, extrapolation should be done with caution.
