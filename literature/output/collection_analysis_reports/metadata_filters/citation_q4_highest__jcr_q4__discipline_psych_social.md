# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set consists of four papers: two theoretical reviews/models (Okada, 2020; KRAINES & KRAINES, 1993) and two empirical laboratory studies that do not use public goods games (Manesi et al., 2016; Bourrat et al., 2011). None of the papers presents direct empirical data from public-goods-game (PGG) experiments with peer punishment enabled/disabled, nor do they track group efficiency (total surplus relative to cooperation) as an outcome of experimental design variants. Thus, the evidence base is broad in conceptual coverage (theory of social dilemmas, reputation, punishment, and prosociality) but quite narrow and indirect regarding the downstream prediction task.

# 2) Task Relevance

Relevance is assessed for three core dimensions: PGG or variant, punishment or sanctions, and efficiency or related payoff outcome:

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - No paper studies standard public goods games.  
  - Okada (2020) and KRAINES & KRAINES (1993) are adjacent, drawing on iterated prisoner's dilemma (IPD) and indirect reciprocity settings, which share structural similarities with PGGs but lack certain group-level and funding features.  
  - Manesi et al. (2016) involves altruistic helping, which is adjacent in motive but not in formal game structure.  
  - Bourrat et al. (2011) does not feature economic games (none).
  
- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - Okada (2020) is close, as it focuses on theoretical sanctioning and reputation mechanisms, but not explicit peer punishment in PGGs.  
  - KRAINES & KRAINES (1993) is adjacent—no explicit punishment but adaptivity to negative payoffs could be interpreted loosely as endogenous punishment.  
  - Manesi et al. (2016) is weak, discussing prosociality under observation, not sanctioning.  
  - Bourrat et al. (2011) is adjacent, with a focus on moral condemnation induced by surveillance cues but no direct punishment manipulation.
  
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - KRAINES & KRAINES (1993) is exact, discussing efficiency and payoff in IPD, albeit not in a group context.  
  - Okada (2020) is adjacent; some discussion of payoff stability in theoretical reputation systems.  
  - Manesi et al. (2016) and Bourrat et al. (2011) are weak or none—focus is on behavioral and attitudinal outcomes, not group payoffs or efficiency.

There is **limited direct relevance** because none of the studies provides experimental or empirical data measuring the effect of enabling or disabling peer punishment on efficiency in PGGs.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, welfare, surplus):**
  - Only KRAINES & KRAINES (1993) explicitly measures and analyzes efficiency and average payoffs, but in 2-player IPD scenarios, not group PGGs.
  - Okada (2020) sometimes references stability of cooperation and cost-benefit ratios in theoretical terms but does not measure efficiency in PGGs.
  - Neither Manesi et al. (2016) nor Bourrat et al. (2011) report any payoff-based outcomes.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, prosocial acts, norm compliance, punishment frequency):**
  - Manesi et al. (2016): Prosocial behavior in a helping task—amount of work contributed, not mapped to payoffs.
  - Bourrat et al. (2011): Moral acceptability ratings following surveillance cues, not behavioral or payoff outcomes.
  - Okada (2020): Focus on stabilization of behavior (cooperation), norm compliance, and reputation effects.
  - KRAINES & KRAINES (1993): Behavioral strategy dynamics, mainly measured via payoff metrics.

**Distinguishing point:** Most measured outcomes are non-payoff behavioral; direct, quantitative efficiency data relevant to group public goods games with/without punishment is effectively missing.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesized across papers:**

- **Punishment and Cooperation**: Theory emphasizes that sanctioning mechanisms (punishment/reward/reputation) can stabilize cooperation in repeated social dilemmas under certain structural and informational conditions (Okada, 2020).
- **Efficiency in Dyads vs. Groups**: In dyadic settings (IPD), adaptive (Pavlovian) strategies can lead to high mutual efficiency and cope better with noise than simple reciprocal strategies (KRAINES & KRAINES, 1993), but this is not demonstrated in group PGGs with punishment. Explicit peer punishment and group outcomes are not tested.
- **Observation Effects**: Visual cues of being watched (e.g., eyes images) can increase prosocial behavior and norm endorsement even when no payoff or sanctioning mechanisms are present (Manesi et al., 2016; Bourrat et al., 2011). However, these effects relate to reputation and social context, not formal punishment or efficiency gains.
- **Dimension Interplay**: Okada (2020) and KRAINES & KRAINES (1993) both note the importance of context dimensions (e.g., number of players, rounds, benefit-to-cost ratio, error rates) in determining whether cooperation can be stable, but neither provides empirical mapping for group efficiency changes when punishment is enabled.

**Critical limitation:** All findings relating to punishment and prosociality are either theoretical, indirect (from dyadic games), or based on non-economic tasks; none provides direct group efficiency data from PGG designs.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Guidance is necessarily speculative and qualitative:**
- **Direct Evidence Deficit**: The literature does not supply empirical data to support quantitative prediction of efficiency effects when enabling punishment in group PGGs, especially as a function of design dimensions plus control efficiency.
- **Conceptual Implications**: Theory suggests punishment can stabilize cooperation (and thus, potentially raise efficiency), but real outcome depends on factors such as the structure of norms, reputation tracking, error rates, and possibly group size and payoff function (Okada, 2020).
- **Transferability Caveat**: Evidence from IPD and helping settings (KRAINES & KRAINES, 1993; Manesi et al., 2016) cannot be confidently transferred to predict group efficiency effects of punishment in PGGs.
- **Design Dimensions**: Theoretical work references relevant variables (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost) as important for cooperation stability, not for direct efficiency prediction.

**Bottom line:** Use general theoretical expectation (conditional on norm structure and error rates, punishment can support group cooperation and potentially improve efficiency) but recognize that empirical predictive power for treatment efficiency under peer punishment, as a function of design variables, is not supported by this paper set.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed Dimensions (theory only):**
   - player_count (Okada, 2020; KRAINES & KRAINES, 1993)
   - num_rounds (Okada, 2020; KRAINES & KRAINES, 1993)
   - all_or_nothing (Okada, 2020; KRAINES & KRAINES, 1993)
   - mpcr (Okada, 2020; KRAINES & KRAINES, 1993)
   - punishment_cost (Okada, 2020)
   - punishment_tech (Okada, 2020)
- **Indirectly or Contextually Discussed:**
   - chat (Manesi et al., 2016: social/observational context may be conceptually similar)
   - show_n_rounds (Manesi et al., 2016)
   - show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech, default_contrib: Not substantively addressed.
- **Effectively Missing:**
   - No studies systematically manipulate or empirically measure the impact of any dimension on efficiency in PGGs with and without punishment.
   - reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech, show_punishment_id: not discussed.

**Interpretation:** Most dimensions are discussed only in theory or referenced as important variables but lack data-link to group efficiency under punishment.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **No empirical PGGs studied**: None of the papers experimentally tests the impact of peer punishment on efficiency in public goods game environments.
- **Absence of treatment-control efficiency comparisons**: There is no quantification of efficiency gains/losses from enabling peer punishment within any set of game design parameters.
- **Outcome mismatch**: Most measured outcomes are behavioral/attitudinal (e.g., prosocial act frequency, norm assessment), not efficiency or group payoff.
- **Contextual disconnect**: Relevance to the prediction task is at best adjacent; inferential leaps are required, which are not warranted by evidence.
- **Missing dimensions**: Several key prediction variables are unaddressed or only discussed in passing. No systematic, multidimensional empirical data is present.

**Summary:**  
This literature set provides theoretical (not empirical) support for the idea that punishment and reputation systems can stabilize cooperation in repeated social dilemmas under certain structural conditions. However, for the specific prediction task—estimating the efficiency impact of peer punishment in public-goods-game-like settings as a function of design variables and control efficiency—this evidence base is indirect, incomplete, and does not provide operationalizable guidance.
