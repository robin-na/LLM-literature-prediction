# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises seven empirical experimental laboratory studies, mostly focused on variants of social dilemma games, including but not strictly limited to public goods games (PGG) with and without punishment or sanctions. The mix is empirically broad in terms of behavioral mechanisms discussed (punishment, reputation, feedback, partner selection, norm induction) but limited in its coverage of direct payoff-related outcomes (i.e., efficiency, surplus, total earnings) in standard repeated PGGs with peer punishment. Most papers focus on behavioral outcomes (who punishes, why, under what incentives or norms), reputational inferences, or efficiency in adjacent but structurally different environments (e.g., trust games with feedback, repeated labor markets with endogenous contract renewal). Only a minority directly measure efficiency, and none do so in a canonical repeated PGG with peer punishment.

The evidence base is thus predominantly empirical (all are experimental lab studies, no theory-only papers), but is somewhat indirect for the downstream task of predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency in PGGs with peer punishment. The paper set is moderate to broad for understanding mechanisms, but relatively narrow and indirect with respect to financial efficiency outcomes in PGGs with punishment.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant:**  
  - **Exact relevance:** 1 paper (Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016) studies PGGs with peer punishment, but does not report efficiency outcomes.
  - **Close/adjacent relevance:** Most (Goette et al., 2012; Falk et al., 2005; Li & Xiao, 2014; Brown et al., 2004; Schram & Charness, 2015; Ashlock et al., 1996) involve games structurally adjacent to PGGs, such as trust games with feedback, repeated dyadic social dilemmas, or market and allocation games.
  - **Summary:** The literature mostly addresses PGG variants or analogous games, with only limited direct evidence from canonical PGGs with efficiency measures.

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**  
  - **Exact relevance:** Several papers (Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016; Falk et al., 2005; Goette et al., 2012) cover peer punishment mechanisms or informal sanctions. Others (Brown et al., 2004) examine endogenous (relationship-based) punishment, and Li & Xiao (2014) address reward mechanisms as a feedback analog, not formal punishment.
  - **Adjacent/weak:** Some work (Schram & Charness, 2015) deals with social (not material) sanctions, and Ashlock et al. (1996) with reputational consequences of sanctioning.
  - **Summary:** The literature is generally strong on mechanisms of punishment and sanctions, but not always in classic PGG settings.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
  - **Exact relevance:** Only two studies (Li & Xiao, 2014; Brown et al., 2004) report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, and neither in canonical repeated PGG with peer punishment.
  - **Adjacent/weak:** Most others focus on behavioral metrics or reputational inferences (punishment frequency, norm compliance).
  - **Summary:** Literature has limited direct information on efficiency as required for prediction; strongest for adjacent markets, trust games, or reputation systems, weakest for standard PGG with peer punishment.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (efficiency, total or group payoff, surplus):**  
  - Measured directly in:  
    - Li & Xiao (2014): Efficiency in trust game with costly feedback and rebate intervention.
    - Brown et al. (2004): Efficiency and surplus in repeated labor-market contracts with informal punishment (relationship breakdown).
  - Not measured (or only discussed) in:  
    - Peysakhovich & Rand (2016); Falk et al. (2005); Goette et al. (2012); Ashlock et al. (1996); Schram & Charness (2015)
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
  - Includes contributions, frequency and targeting of punishment, withdrawal, norm compliance, reputation ratings, and inferences about partner suitability.
  - The majority of the papers focus on these outcomes.

**Distinction:**  
While all papers address behavioral change mechanisms—key for understanding *how* punishment could affect efficiency—only a minority actually measure or report group efficiency or total payoff. Most findings thus require careful interpretation when predicting the impact of punishment on actual efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical findings:**
- **Peer punishment is often used by cooperators to sanction defectors** in both PGGs and adjacent social dilemmas (Falk et al., 2005; Goette et al., 2012; Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016).
- **The cost-effectiveness of punishment influences its use** but punishment is still implemented even when less effective (Falk et al., 2005).
- **Antisocial punishment can emerge under intergroup competition,** leading to destructive outcomes (Goette et al., 2012). Authors argue (but do not measure) that such antisocial punishment likely reduces group efficiency.
- **Reputation and withdrawal as alternatives to punishment** affect partner selection, but only when the sanctioner retains a positive payoff, underscoring the potential costliness of punishment (Ashlock et al., 1996).
- **Reward/precommitment mechanisms increase efficiency** in trust/feedback games: introducing rebates (analogous to precommitment rewards) for costly feedback leads to higher efficiency (Li & Xiao, 2014).
- **Informal punishment through relationship termination** (not material peer punishment) produces much higher efficiency and surplus compared to random rematching or absence of enforcement in repeated markets (Brown et al., 2004).

**Theoretical/mechanism arguments:**
- **Punishment is a costly but persistent tool for enforcing cooperation,** justified by norm enforcement rather than pure payoff maximization (Falk et al., 2005; Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016).
- **Social norms and observability** can shift behavior toward prosociality without material sanctions but do not guarantee efficiency improvements in the absence of real incentives (Schram & Charness, 2015).

**Consensus/gaps:**
- Punishment can increase cooperation, but effects on efficiency depend strongly on context (cost, frequency, antisocial use).
- Only in adjacent settings (not canonical PGGs) do we see direct evidence that sanction/feedback/rewards mechanisms *increase* efficiency.
- Where antisocial punishment or negative reputational externalities occur, the impact on efficiency may be zero or negative but is not systematically quantified.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

The evidence base suggests that the prediction of treatment efficiency (efficiency with peer punishment enabled) using game design dimensions and control efficiency must be made with caution:

- **Efficiency gains from enabling punishment are not assured.**  
  Direct evidence that punishment improves efficiency is available only in adjacent settings—markets with endogenous relationships (Brown et al., 2004) and trust games with costly feedback (Li & Xiao, 2014)—not in canonical repeated PGGs with peer punishment.

- **Punishment's impact is highly sensitive to context,** including the possibility of antisocial punishment under group competition (Goette et al., 2012), the cost and impact factor of punishment (Falk et al., 2005), and participants’ behavioral norms (Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016).

- **When reward/precommitment mechanisms are present,** efficiency increases are robust, especially with high take-up of the mechanism (Li & Xiao, 2014). However, the analog to PGGs is limited, and this should not be directly mapped without caution.

- **Dimension-specific prediction should focus on:**
  - Conditions that support prosocial use of punishment (neutral vs. competitive framing, low antisocial tendencies).
  - Punishment cost and effectiveness, as these influence both the likelihood that punishment is used and the size of any efficiency gains/losses.
  - The structure of interaction—repeated vs. one-shot; opportunity for partner switching; observability of actions.

- **Control efficiency as predictor:**  
  When baseline (punishment-off) efficiency is low but there is little risk of antisocial punishment and costs are not prohibitive, enabling punishment might raise efficiency, particularly when social norms for cooperation and fair punishment are strong. If control efficiency is already high, gains may be marginal.

**Bottom line:**  
Predictions about efficiency gains from enabling peer punishment in PGG-like settings should be adjusted for risks of antisocial punishment, magnitude/cost of punishment, and social context; gains are most likely in repeated, neutral, noncompetitive group contexts with well-calibrated punishment costs.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed dimensions:**  
  - `player_count` (multiple papers): All studies specify group or dyad size.
  - `num_rounds`: Repeated or one-shot play is specified in all empirical games.
  - `punishment_cost`: Explicitly manipulated or reported in several papers (e.g., Falk et al., 2005; Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016; Ashlock et al., 1996), but effect on efficiency is not directly measured.
  - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`: Li & Xiao (2014) examines reward/precommitment rebate mechanisms as analogs to rewards.
  - `chat`, `all_or_nothing`, `mpcr`: Often specified, rarely analyzed for direct effect on efficiency.
  - `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Discussed directly in Brown et al. (2004), where observability of actions and outcomes is linked to informal punishment and efficiency outcomes.

- **Indirectly/contextually discussed:**  
  - `punishment_tech` and `punishment_magnitude`: May be present, but rarely the focus; some mention of timing, method, or magnitude in design.
  - `show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries`: Sometimes specified, important for social norm salience but not systematically linked to efficiency outcomes.
  - `default_contrib`: Mentioned in framing (Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016), but not systematically studied.

- **Missing or very sparse:**  
  - The precise effects of `chat`, `all_or_nothing`, `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id` on efficiency in the context of punishment-enabled PGGs are mostly missing.
  - No studies report systematic variation on these dimensions in direct relation to efficiency with punishment enabled.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of direct efficiency outcomes in PGGs with peer punishment.** Only adjacent games report efficiency effects; for actual repeated PGGs, all available data are behavioral, not payoff- or efficiency-based.
- **Scarcity of studies spanning the full design space.** Most design dimensions relevant for prediction are only partly discussed or present as controls rather than active interventions.
- **Insufficient evidence for cross-dimensional interactions.** No data on how multiple game design dimensions jointly affect efficiency outcomes with versus without punishment.
- **Risk of context confounds:**
  - Antisocial punishment is highlighted as a risk (Goette et al., 2012), but quantitative effects on efficiency are not reported.
  - Positive efficiency gains via partner choice or long-term contracting (Brown et al., 2004) may not generalize to PGGs without such relational structures.
  - Reward or precommitment mechanisms (Li & Xiao, 2014) are promising but structurally different from punishment in PGGs.
- **Behavioral outcome emphasis.** The literature largely reports changes in cooperation, punishment allocation, or norm compliance, and only infer possible efficiency effects.
- **Potential publication bias.** Most empirical evidence is positive about the mechanisms under study, but negative or null effects on efficiency may be underreported.

**In summary:**  
The paper set offers valuable empirical insight into *how* punishment is used, why, and when its use might or might not support cooperation. However, it provides only sparse and indirect evidence for the *actual* effect of peer punishment on efficiency in repeated PGGs. For downstream predictions, one must apply the insights about behavioral context, cost, and social structure with caution and be explicit about the inferential gaps that remain.
