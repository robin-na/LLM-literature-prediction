# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is **broad and diverse**, containing 189 papers spanning **empirical experimental studies** (mainly lab, with some field/observational), **theoretical models**, and **simulation analyses**. The set includes:
- **Direct empirical evidence** from standard linear Public Goods Games (PGG) and their close variants (e.g., variants with exclusion, reward).
- **Theory and simulations** covering repeated, networked, and evolutionary games.
- Work on **common-pool resource games, trust/dictator games, and related social dilemmas**, supplementing direct PGG evidence.
- A **rich mix of experimental manipulations**: peer vs centralized punishment, endogenous vs exogenous institution adoption, network and information structure, cost/effectiveness of punishment, and context (cultural, group composition, institutional framing, etc).
- A **substantial minority of papers** discuss only adjacent settings (e.g., Prisoner’s Dilemma, snowdrift, trust games) or report only on behavioral outcomes, not efficiency/payoff.

Overall, the **evidence base is strong and multi-faceted for the core PGG prediction task**, especially regarding lab experiments with well-specified game parameters; however, the literature becomes much thinner for certain design dimensions (e.g., chat, detailed network structure, non-standard payoff functions), for non-lab contexts, and for payoff-based outcomes in many adjacent or theoretical works.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) PGG or Variant**
- **Exact relevance**: The majority of high-quality empirical and theoretical papers use standard linear public goods games or explicit close variants (threshold/step PGG, excludability, common-pool resource, etc.) (e.g., Gürerk et al., 2018; Engl et al., 2021; Kingsley, 2016; Bruhin et al., 2020).
- **Close relevance**: A smaller subset uses structurally similar, but not identical, games (e.g., repeated resource dilemmas, trust/dictator games, team production).
- **Adjacent/weak relevance**: Many theoretical or mechanism-focused papers use two-player games (Prisoner's Dilemma, snowdrift) or unstructured settings.
- The **coverage of standard PGGs is strong**, but direct transferability is sometimes limited in adjacent domains.

**b) Punishment or Sanctions**
- **Exact relevance**: Many studies precisely manipulate peer or centralized punishment in classic PGGs, often including variants (exclusion, ostracism, higher-order punishment, reward, or costless sanctions).
- **Close/adjacent relevance**: Some papers manipulate only reward, exclusion, or partner choice (not standard costly punishment), or only allow for informally punitive feedback or reputation mechanisms.
- **Weak/none**: Some studies discuss punishment normatively, theoretically, or as a behavioral outcome but do not manipulate or measure it directly.

**c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcomes**
- **Exact relevance**: Key empirical/theoretical papers **directly measure and report group efficiency, total earnings, or well-defined payoff ratios** (e.g., Gürerk et al., 2018; Engl et al., 2021; Kingsley, 2016; Qin & Wang, 2013).
- **Close/adjacent**: Numerous studies only **infer efficiency from contribution rates**, report behavioral proxies, or discuss changes in group behavior but do not report explicit payoff-based efficiency. The translation from cooperation/contribution rates to efficiency requires strong assumptions about cost structures.
- **Weak/none**: Some papers focus exclusively on behavioral outcomes (punishment assigned, norm compliance, psychological motives, etc.) or theoretical mechanisms, not efficiency.

**Summary**: The set contains a **core group of highly relevant papers** for the prediction task—standard PGGs with explicit punishment manipulation and measured efficiency outcomes—while much of the remainder is supportive, mechanistic, or contextually adjacent but less directly informative.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related Outcomes (Relevant for Prediction):**
- **Group efficiency**, total group earnings, average payoffs, welfare/surplus, and similar ratio-based or aggregate monetary measures are frequently (but not universally) reported in high-quality PGG and close-variant experiments, especially when the prediction task requires mapping control efficiency to treatment efficiency.
- Many theory and simulation papers provide **explicit equilibrium utility or efficiency formulas** as functions of design parameters—valuable for mechanistic prediction.
- A sizable minority of empirical papers, especially those from behavioral economics or experimental psychology, **report only behavioral outcomes** (contribution/cooperation rates, frequency/type of punishment) or only infer likely efficiency impacts.

**Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes (NOT direct efficiency):**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates, norm adoption, punishment frequency/types, retaliation, expectation updating, social identity, psychological traits/motives**—these are common outcomes across the set but do NOT directly map to payoff-based efficiency, except under strong assumptions.
- Several papers report only the **presence, frequency, or conditional use of punishment** (e.g., antisocial vs pro-social), not its payoff impact.

**Distinctions Maintained:**  
Where only **behavioral outcomes** or **mechanisms** are reported, this is not a direct measure of efficiency, but may still inform necessary preconditions for efficiency changes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Patterns (PGG and Close Variants):**
- **Enabling peer or centralized costly punishment** generally increases both contributions and efficiency (group earnings/welfare) compared to control (no punishment), *when antisocial punishment is limited and the cost-to-impact ratio is favorable*. This result is especially robust in small lab groups with homogeneous endowments, no chat, continuous contributions, and standard punishment ratios (e.g., 1:3 or 1:4) (Gürerk et al., 2018; Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Qin & Wang, 2013; Marcin et al., 2019; Fu et al., 2017).
- **Efficiency gain from punishment depends critically on punishment design**:
    - High punishment cost or inefficient targeting (e.g., indiscriminate punishment, high antisocial punishment) can erase or even **reverse efficiency gains**, either by dissipating surplus through “punishment wars” or by deterring cooperation (Bruhin et al., 2020; Bortolotti et al., 2015; Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
    - **Incomplete or asymmetric punishment networks** (who can punish whom) tend to yield **lower or no efficiency gain**—incomplete monitoring increases the prevalence of defectors and anti-social punishment (Leibbrandt et al., 2015; Boosey & Isaac, 2016).
- **Cultural and group contexts matter**: The net effect of punishment depends strongly on group composition (prevalence of anti-social punishers, baseline norm compliance, endowment/cultural homogeneity), with **some societies or subject pools showing no efficiency gain or even a loss** due to antisocial punishment or norms of “leveling” (Bruhin et al., 2020; Bortolotti et al., 2015; Kocher et al., 2012).
- **Endogenous institutions & information:** Allowing groups to choose punishment institutions (endogenously) tends to **enhance the effectiveness and efficiency** of punishment (when supported by history/social learning), but the *mechanism of adoption* (voted/forced) is often less important than the presence and design of the punishment itself (Gürerk, 2013; Marcin et al., 2019).
- **Role of history, social learning, and information**: Providing detailed *history/social learning* makes punishment institutions both **more accepted and more efficient**; full transparency strengthens targeting and supports pro-social punishment (Gürerk, 2013; Kamei & Putterman, 2015).
- **Punishment vs Reward**: **Rewards tend to produce higher efficiency (net group earnings) than punishment** when their design allows for net-positive surplus, but punishment is better at increasing contributions when rewards are not as cost-effective or when the social dilemma is severe (Gürerk, Irlenbusch & Rockenbach, 2009; Gürerk et al., 2018).
- **Exclusion/Ostracism**: Costless exclusion mechanisms (ostracism) consistently increase both contributions and efficiency; *costly* exclusion can result in net lower earnings, despite higher contributions (Dannenberg et al., 2020).
- **Non-material or costless punishment (e.g., disapproval points, emoticons, reputation, etc.)**: These mechanisms can sometimes significantly increase cooperation and efficiency (if they strongly affect behavior), but are context-dependent and do not always transfer to efficiency gains (Dugar, 2013; Peeters & Vorsatz, 2013).

**Theory/Mechanism Insights:**
- **Effectiveness is highly sensitive to punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group structure, and network configuration** (Levine & Modica, 2016; Sigmund et al., 2011).
- **Population structure, monitoring, and heterogeneity** (in patience, social preferences, or information) are critical moderators: punishment stabilizes efficient cooperation in small, tightly clustered or well-monitored groups, but can fail or backfire in large, unstructured, or highly heterogeneous populations (Pollock, 1988; Haag & Lagunoff, 2006).
- **Antisocial (perverse) punishment, retaliation cycles, and revenge undermine efficiency**: When punishment is used against high contributors or devolves into spiteful tit-for-tat, net efficiency falls and may be well below control (Bruhin et al., 2020; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
- **Voluntary participation, conditional exit, and partner switching**: The ability to *exit* or *sort partners* can substitute for punishment in raising efficiency; punishment effects are attenuated when sorting is possible (Nosenzo & Tufano, 2017; Bernard et al., 2018).
- **Punishment vs Reward in long-term stability**: Rewards “jumpstart” cooperation but are vulnerable to second-order free-riding, whereas punishment (when well-targeted) is more stable for long-term cooperation (Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; Wang et al., 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Core Guidance for Predicting Treatment Efficiency:**
- **In environments structurally similar to lab PGGs** (fixed player count, repeated rounds, continuous/all-or-nothing contributions, peer or centralized punishment with cost-to-impact ratio >1, homogenous endowments), **expect enabling punishment to increase average efficiency above control**, conditional on antisocial punishment being low and punishment costs not being excessive (Gürerk et al., 2018; Engl et al., 2021).
- **MAP design dimensions to outcomes**:
    - *Lower punishment cost* and *higher punishment impact* (especially ratios of 1:3 or better) yield higher predicted efficiency increases.
    - *Symmetric, complete punishment networks* produce stronger efficiency gains than incomplete/asymmetric networks.
    - *Absence of strong antisocial punishment* is pivotal—where it's present, *do not* predict an efficiency gain (Bruhin et al., 2020; Bortolotti et al., 2015).
    - *Provision of detailed history/social learning* or *explicit, transparent punishment information* magnifies effectiveness and efficiency (Gürerk, 2013; Kamei & Putterman, 2015).
    - *Reward mechanisms* with net positive transfer (reward tech >1:1) generally outperform punishment for efficiency.
    - *Ostracism/exclusion* is highly effective and can sometimes substitute for or outperform direct costly punishment, but only if costless (Dannenberg et al., 2020).
- If the **control (no-punishment) efficiency is already high (close to cooperative optimum)**, **expect weaker marginal effect** from enabling punishment. Conversely, **when control efficiency is low, punishment's effect is typically stronger**, unless antisocial punishment dominates.
- **Game-specific features matter**: Binary/all-or-nothing vs continuous contributions, group size, round number, information structure (revealing contributions and IDs), and matching protocol (fixed vs random) can all moderate the magnitude of the effect.
- **Do not generalize efficiency gains** from punishment found in one-population (e.g., student samples) to more heterogeneous or "real world" populations without verifying the antisocial punishment rate and norm compatibility (Bortolotti et al., 2015; Kocher et al., 2012).
- **Control efficiency alone is not always a lower bound** for treatment efficiency—especially when punishment is likely to induce retaliation wars or is used antisocially.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

#### **Directly informed dimensions (strong, multi-paper evidence):**
- **player_count**: Extensively tested (typically n=3–6), robust finding that group size moderates punishment effectiveness, but diminishing returns or over-punishment may arise in much larger groups or poorly coordinated centralized punishment (Kamei, 2020; Levine & Modica, 2016; POLLOCK, 1988).
- **num_rounds**: Most studies use 10–30 rounds; temporal dynamics (early/later effect, path-dependence).
- **mpcr**: Central to all payoff-based analyses. Higher MPCR supports higher cooperation, but the marginal effect of punishment is stronger at lower baseline MPCR.
- **punishment_cost** and **punishment_tech** (effectiveness): Strongly modeled and empirically manipulated; cost-to-impact ratios and their implications for net group earnings are clearly established.
- **all_or_nothing** and **continuous** contributions: Both modeled; most lab PGGs use continuous.
- **show_other_summaries**: Many empirical studies provide summary info; theoretical papers note its importance for monitoring/punishment targeting.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Directly tested in several key papers; the structure of reward mechanisms is a critical comparator or moderator.

#### **Indirect, contextual, or variably informed:**
- **chat**: Some studies manipulate explicit communication; evidence suggests chat may substitute for or amplify the effect of punishment, but is less systemically varied with efficiency outcomes in the literature than core punishment dimensions.
- **default_contrib**: Rarely varied; some evidence from "opt-in" vs "opt-out" framing but no systematic synthesis.
- **show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id**: Occasionally manipulated; transparency/identifiability moderates punishment's deterrence and counter-punishment, but these variables are usually treated as secondary to network structure and cost-impact parameters.

#### **Missing or sparsely treated:**
- **Detailed network structure** (beyond symmetric/asymmetric networks): While some papers study incomplete or limited networks for punishment, many design variables (such as real social networks, dynamic or endogenous networks) are not systematically mapped to efficiency changes.
- **Contextual variables** (societal norms, cultural background, personality traits, heterogeneity): Discussed, but rarely as structured, manipulable dimensions.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Outcome measurement gap**: A large minority of studies report only behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, punishment frequency), not efficiency/payoff; inferring efficiency from these is valid only if punishment costs are modest and anti-social punishment is low.
- **Heterogeneity and context**: Effects are **not universal**—cultural background, group composition, baseline norm strength, and subject pool can cause direction and magnitude of effect to reverse (Bruhin et al., 2020; Bortolotti et al., 2015; Kocher et al., 2012; Kingsley, 2016).
- **Antisocial punishment**: Presence of antisocial punishers or "revenge wars" is a **well-documented failure mode** for punishment-based efficiency gains, sometimes leading to net efficiency loss relative to control.
- **Design interaction effects**: Punishment effectiveness interacts with **information structure, monitoring network, reward options, communication**, and institution adoption process; effects are often *conditional*, not additive.
- **Limited field/general population evidence**: Lab findings, especially with student samples and stylized settings, may not extrapolate to field or more heterogeneous real-world groups.
- **Sparse evidence on some dimensions**: Dimensions like chat, default contribution framing, identifiability, and initial conditions are less systematically tested as moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.
- **Adjacent/close variants**: Substantial evidence is from closely related games (Snowdrift, trust, or resource games), which are informative mechanistically, but their quantitative implication for standard PGGs must be interpreted with caution.
- **Unobserved moderators**: Outcomes may be shaped by unmeasured psychological, cultural, or institutional variables not present in the experiment’s parameterization.
- **Publication/reporting bias**: Studies with null effects or negative effects of punishment (especially in “real world” or field samples) may be less commonly published, biasing the expected sign in meta-synthesis.

---

**In summary: The paper set provides strong and direct evidence for predicting that, in standard lab PGG environments with homogeneous participants and well-designed, targeted punishment mechanisms, enabling punishment increases group efficiency compared to control, but this effect is strongly moderated and sometimes reversed by punishment cost, network/information structure, group/cultural composition, and antisocial punishment. The literature is rich on core design dimensions but sparse on some contextual and social moderators, and generalization outside the lab and to certain untested combinations of design variables is limited.**
