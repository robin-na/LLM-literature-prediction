# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set consists of 14 papers: a mix of empirical laboratory experiments and formal theoretical models. About half are tightly focused on standard linear public goods games (PGGs) with explicit peer or centralized punishment; several others cover adjacent game types or indirect sanctioning/reward mechanisms. Most empirical works use traditional lab-PGG paradigms, sometimes with variants involving institutional design or network structure. Nearly all theoretical papers provide mechanistic or equilibrium-based arguments in closely related social dilemmas (e.g., optional PGGs, threshold games, generalized exchange). Overall, the empirical contributions are strong and relatively broad for the prediction task, while the theoretical papers offer a range of mechanisms and outcomes, but with some deviation from the exact prediction scenario.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**  
- **Exact:** Several papers use standard linear PGGs or optional PGGs (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Grund et al., 2020; Nax et al., 2018).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Others extend to common pool resource (CPR) games, volunteer's dilemma, surplus allocation, or games on networks (Kanitsar, 2021; Bolle, 2021; Becchetti et al., 2018; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Okada, 2020; Di Guida et al., 2021; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020; Andersson, 2020; Madeo & Mocenni, 2021).
- **None/Weak:** No papers are entirely unrelated to PGG or its variants; even adjacent works are conceptually close.

**punishment_or_sanctions**  
- **Exact:** Multiple papers directly manipulate peer punishment, centralized punishment, or collective punishment (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Bolle, 2021).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Several examine related mechanisms (exclusion/replacement, redistribution/taxation, refusal to cooperate, social feedback, emotional states) that may serve similar functions to punishment (Grund et al., 2020; Becchetti et al., 2018; Di Guida et al., 2021; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020; Andersson, 2020; Okada, 2020; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018).
- **Weak:** Some theoretical papers discuss self-regulation or endogenous preferences without explicit sanctioning (Madeo & Mocenni, 2021).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**  
- **Exact:** Several experiments and models report directly on group efficiency, total payoff, or welfare (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Nax et al., 2018; Di Guida et al., 2021; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020; Andersson, 2020; Madeo & Mocenni, 2021).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some report contribution rates, cooperation, or group composition with indirect inferences about payoff (Grund et al., 2020; Becchetti et al., 2018; Okada, 2020; Bolle, 2021).
- **Weak/None:** A minority focus only on behavioral outcomes with insufficient or absent efficiency analysis.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**  
- Average group efficiency relative to the cooperative optimum (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Nax et al., 2018; Di Guida et al., 2021; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020; Andersson, 2020; Madeo & Mocenni, 2021).
- Group earnings, total coins, net profit, surplus or welfare (used as synonyms for efficiency).

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
- Contribution rate, cooperation rate, frequency or structure of punishment/reward, norm compliance, and group member choices (Grund et al., 2020; Becchetti et al., 2018; Okada, 2020).
- Some works measure only these behavioral outcomes and must be interpreted with caution when inferring about efficiency.

**Hybrid or indirect outcomes:**  
- Inferences about efficiency from increased cooperation or redistribution (Becchetti et al., 2018; Okada, 2020).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Across the relevant literature:

- **Enabling punishment in standard PGGs generally increases group efficiency and average payoffs relative to the no-punishment control.** This effect is robust in both centralized (institutional) and peer punishment settings (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
- **Magnitude and variability:** The efficiency boost from punishment is moderate on average but displays high group-level and cross-society variance, heavily influenced by the mix of punishment strategies (e.g., strong reciprocators vs. norm-keepers) (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Nature and cost of punishment:** Costless or low-cost punishment is more reliably associated with increased efficiency; costly punishment may not improve efficiency, particularly in peer settings (Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Antisocial punishment is rare, but norm enforcement by punishing both high and low contributors can reduce efficiency (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).**
- **Centralized punishment (institutional manager) improves efficiency regardless of manager selection mechanism; the prosociality of the manager is more important than institutional legitimacy (Castillo et al., 2021).**
- **Optional PGGs:** Theoretical work shows that partial or probabilistic punishment of defectors can stabilize high cooperation and full efficiency, provided a threshold level of punishment is reached (Botta et al., 2021).
- **Generalized exchange and network structure:** The efficiency-enhancing effect of punishment does not necessarily transfer to sparse network or generalized exchange games; peer punishment's impact depends on sanctioning network density (Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Related mechanisms:** Exclusion, redistribution, refusal to cooperate, and public social feedback (as non-monetary sanctions) can also boost efficiency, but with variable magnitude and sometimes only indirect or inferred improvement (Becchetti et al., 2018; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020; Di Guida et al., 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

#### General Principle  
**Enabling explicit peer (or centralized) punishment in standard PGGs, all else equal, reliably increases group efficiency compared to a no-punishment control.** The effect size is contextually moderated by punishment cost, network structure, and the distribution of strategy types (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).

#### Conditioning on Game Design Dimensions  
- **Player count, number of rounds, MPCR, and punishment cost** are directly evidenced as moderating the size and likelihood of efficiency gains (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
  - Lower punishment cost generally favors higher efficiency post-punishment (Kanitsar, 2021).
  - Sufficient punishment effectiveness is needed to surpass thresholds for stable cooperation (Botta et al., 2021).
- **All-or-nothing vs. continuous contributions, presence of chat, and knowledge of game length** are present in multiple papers but less clearly tied to the payoff effect of punishment (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Grund et al., 2020; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020).
- **Centralized punishment and social feedback mechanisms** (as opposed to purely peer punishment) offer similar or higher efficiency gains (Castillo et al., 2021; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020; Becchetti et al., 2018).
- **Network topology / sanctioning structure:** Dense networks (all-to-all in PGG) support efficiency gains from punishment; sparse (generalized exchange) do not (Kanitsar, 2021).

#### From Control Efficiency to Treatment Efficiency  
- Control efficiency provides a baseline; enabling punishment consistently produces higher efficiency, but the effect size varies widely depending on the above design factors and group composition (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- Predictive models should be attentive to contextual moderators: punishment cost, social context, possible presence of norm-keepers, and institutional form.

#### When Punishment May Fail or Backfire  
- In threshold public good games with strong non-cooperative incentives (e.g., protests), collective punishment may not increase efficiency and can even reduce it (Bolle, 2021).
- Costly peer punishment in sparse or non-standard network structures may be ineffective (Kanitsar, 2021).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- `player_count`: Explicitly manipulated in most empirical and theoretical models.
- `num_rounds`: Typically specified and sometimes experimentally varied.
- `mpcr`: Multiplier or per-capita return varied/recorded in multiple studies.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (peer vs. centralized): Central to several experiments/models.
- `all_or_nothing`: Present in both binary and continuous variants.
- `chat`: Sometimes manipulated or controlled.
- `reward_exists`: Occasional presence in experiments/theory as a comparison.

**Indirectly Informed:**
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Inferred relevance in feedback/public feedback contexts (Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020).
- `default_contrib`: Framing/choice structure is present but less formally analyzed.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Mentioned in theory or as part of combined mechanisms, but not always the focus.

**Contextually Discussed:**
- `show_punishment_id`: Some mention in the context of social feedback or public vs. private sanction, but not a central focus.
- `reward_magnitude`: Limited direct evidence.

**Effectively Missing:**
- Specific manipulation or systematic assessment for `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id`, and detailed technological distinctions for reward/punishment other than peer vs. centralized are sparse.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Heterogeneity in Outcomes and Generalizability:** Effect sizes for efficiency gains from punishment mechanisms are highly variable across groups, societies, and design specifics (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes:** Some papers report only on contribution/cooperation rates or mechanism use, which may not map directly to payoff/efficiency outcomes (Grund et al., 2020; Becchetti et al., 2018; Okada, 2020).
- **Adjacency and Structural Gaps:** Several theoretical models and experiments are in adjacent but not identical domains (e.g., common pool resource games, repeated PDs, generalized exchange, volunteer's dilemma), limiting precision of cross-mapping to standard PGG prediction scenarios.
- **Sparse Evidence for Some Dimensions:** Design features relating to feedback, identity revelation, and specific forms or costs of reward are under-explored, hindering prediction for those variants.
- **Network and Institutional Structure:** The effect of punishment mechanisms is strongly contingent on network topology and institutional form, which are not always matched to the prediction environment.
- **Societal/Cultural Moderators:** The composition of groups and cultural norms around punishment substantially moderate efficiency effects, but these are not always captured by game design dimensions.
- **Mechanism Overlap:** Distinctions among sanction types (exclusion, social feedback, reward vs. punishment) are sometimes blurred, complicating clear identification of peer punishment effects alone.

**In summary:** The literature base broadly supports the prediction that enabling (peer or centralized) punishment in a standard PGG increases group efficiency, but the magnitude and reliability of this effect are substantially moderated by punishment cost, network structure, and group composition—factors only partially captured by common design dimensions. Prediction in unfamiliar designs or social contexts should be conservative and attentive to these moderating features and reported ambiguities.
