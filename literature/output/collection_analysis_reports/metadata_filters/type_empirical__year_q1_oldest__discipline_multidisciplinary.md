# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is composed primarily of high-quality, experimental empirical papers (both laboratory and field), with a substantial portion directly targeting public goods games (PGGs) or close variants. Within the 64-paper set, there is a mix between:  
- **Direct empirical evidence** on standard PGGs with and without peer punishment (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gürerk et al., 2006; Rand et al., 2009; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012),  
- **Adjacent or indirect** studies (e.g., Prisoner's Dilemma, trust games, or real-world commons management), and  
- Some **mechanistic or context-focused papers** with either no direct manipulation or measurement of punishment or efficiency, or using non-payoff behavioral proxies.

The literature overall is relatively **broad** with respect to behavioral and psychological mechanisms for cooperation and sanctioning, but is **narrower and more focused** regarding direct, quantitative evidence about the impact of enabling punishment on **payoff-based efficiency** in canonical PGGs, which is the most relevant for the downstream prediction task.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. pgg_or_variant:**  
- *Exact relevance*—A substantial minority of papers use standard, multi-person, repeated public goods games, which map directly onto the prediction task (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gürerk et al., 2006; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Rand et al., 2009; Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006).
- *Close/adjacent*—Several studies use dyadic games (Prisoner's Dilemma, trust games), indirect reciprocity/generosity environments, or natural commons which are structurally similar but not fully equivalent.
- *Weak/none*—A number of studies either use modified Dictator or Ultimatum Games, animal mutualisms, or observational field studies and are essentially non-informative for core PGG predictions.

**b. punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *Exact relevance*—Numerous empirical experiments include a direct manipulation of the punishment institution (peer punishment or formal sanctions), with varied cost and technical implementations (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gürerk et al., 2006; Rand et al., 2009; Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006).
- *Close/adjacent*—Some papers employ sanctioning in adjacent settings or study non-monetary/social punishment, or punishment by centralized authorities, or use reputation as a substitute/moderator.
- *Weak/none*—Studies purely about reputation, partner choice, or unstructured field contexts often do not manipulate punishment directly.

**c. efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *Exact/close*—A handful of core experimental papers report and analyze group efficiency or total earnings directly (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gürerk et al., 2006; Rand et al., 2009), while others only infer efficiency from contribution rates or related measures.
- *Adjacent/weak*—Many report only behavioral measures (contributions, cooperation rate, punishment frequency). Very few adjacent papers report explicit efficiency or payoff data connected to punishment interventions.

**Summary:**  
The most **task-relevant papers** are those that use repeated multi-person PGGs with peer punishment manipulation and explicit reporting of group efficiency or total earnings (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Gürerk et al., 2006; Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006). The evidence pool is strong for exact PGGs with payoff measurement and weak for settings without these features.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**a. Payoff-related outcomes (direct):**
- *Efficiency* (i.e., group payoff vs. the cooperative optimum), *total group earnings*, *welfare*, *surplus produced*, and *average earnings per round*.
    - Directly reported in a subset of key PGG punishment papers (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006; Gürerk et al., 2006; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).
    - Sometimes inferred from contribution and deduction data where explicit ratios to optimum are provided.

**b. Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- *Contribution or cooperation rates*, frequency and targeting of punishment, anti-social punishment rates, norm compliance, retaliation (vendetta) incidence, emotion or psychological measure proxies.
    - These are much more widely reported throughout the literature.
    - Many findings about punishment refer to its effect on behavior, but do *not* translate directly to changes in group efficiency.

**c. Indirect or contextually related measures:**
- *Natural resource stocks*, *community-level success scores*, image/reputation-based outcomes, neural/psychological/affective responses to punishment.
- These serve as proxies or moderators and are often present in adjacent or weakly relevant papers.

*Distinction:*  
For the prediction task, only directly measured or clearly inferable efficiency or group payoff outcomes (not just increased cooperation or punishment activity) are valid evidence.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## a. Enabling punishment increases efficiency in canonical repeated PGGs (with significant caveats):

- **Substantial positive effect:**  
    - Enabling *peer punishment* in standard, repeated public goods games with moderate cost and sufficient frequency **substantially increases group efficiency and total payoff**, pushing outcomes much closer to the social optimum compared to no-punishment controls, especially when baseline cooperation is low (Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gürerk et al., 2006; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006).
    - The strength, severity, and automaticity of the punishment mechanism matter; strong, externally imposed punishment institutions are especially effective (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).

- **Punishment cost moderates effect:**  
    - If punishment is very costly relative to its impact, efficiency gains can be offset or even reversed by punishment expenditures, especially if vendettas or cycles of counter-punishment arise (Fehl et al., 2012; Rand et al., 2009).
    - *Vendettas* or anti-social punishment can reduce net efficiency, particularly where norms for punishment are weak or institutions are not robust.

- **Context dependence:**  
    - The impact of punishment is **not universal**; in some societies or group contexts, *anti-social punishment* (punishing cooperators) is common, diminishing or entirely reversing the efficiency benefit (Herrmann et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2009 for more culturally adjacent settings).
    - In repeated dyadic games (e.g., Prisoner's Dilemma), punishment often increases cooperation but **does not increase and may even reduce efficiency** due to its costs outweighing gains (Dreber et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2009).

- **Interaction with other mechanisms:**  
    - Reputation systems or reward options can substitute for or amplify the effect of punishment. The synergy between punishment and reputation mechanisms yields the highest efficiency, as punishment is used more sparingly and more effectively when reputation is salient (Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006).
    - Communication alone can sometimes equal or exceed the effect of punishment in resource management or dynamic settings (Janssen et al., 2010).

## b. Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes

- Group contribution rates almost always increase with punishment options, but **high contributions can coincide with lower efficiency** if punishment is misapplied, costly, or triggers vendettas (Fehl et al., 2012; Rand et al., 2009).
- The frequency of punishment used declines over time as cooperation stabilizes, helping to mitigate its negative effect on efficiency in repeated games (Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006; Fehr & Gächter, 2002).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **If the control game’s efficiency is low, enabling peer punishment (with reasonably moderate costs, effective impact, and no unusual social context for anti-social punishment) is highly likely to increase average efficiency—often substantially—toward the cooperative optimum (Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gürerk et al., 2006; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).**
- **If the control efficiency is already high (near cooperative optimum), the marginal efficiency gain from enabling punishment is small or negative, depending largely on punishment costs (since there is little free riding left to correct and only the cost of unnecessary punishment can lower efficiency).**
- **Detailed prediction requires attention to:**  
    - **Punishment cost and magnitude:** High-cost, low-impact punishment can reduce efficiency; low/severe-cost, high-impact punishment is more likely to increase efficiency if targeted at defectors.
    - **Prevalence and nature of anti-social punishment:** High rates of punishment toward cooperators or excessive retaliation cycles can suppress or reverse efficiency gains (Herrmann et al., 2008; Fehl et al., 2012).
    - **Game structure:**  
      - Standard PGGs: Enabling punishment is usually efficiency enhancing, especially with repeated play and fixed group identity.
      - Dyadic/Prisoner's Dilemma: Effects on efficiency are often absent or negative due to punishment costs exceeding cooperation gains (Dreber et al., 2008).
      - CPR/resource games: If resource dynamics or communication is critical, punishment alone may reduce efficiency (Janssen et al., 2010).
    - **Availability of rewards/reputation mechanisms:** If available, may either substitute for or amplify punishment’s effects and lead to higher efficiency at lower cost.

- **Predictability from design dimensions:** The literature best supports prediction of the effect of enabling punishment as a function of punishment cost, mechanism, game round structure, and baseline efficiency. Less is known about the moderation by player count, communication, or summary feedback. Culture and group-level norms are major moderators not always included in standard design dimensions.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**a. Directly informed dimensions (with explicit manipulation and outcome measurement):**
- **player_count** – Most experimental PGGs use 4-6 player groups; some studies examine dyads or larger groups. Some evidence (Grujic et al., 2012; Gürerk et al., 2006) suggests that the positive payoff effect of punishment is strongest for groups ≥3, with diminishing returns or non-linearities for much larger groups.
- **num_rounds** – The effect of punishment accumulates across rounds; repeated interactions generally strengthen the positive effect on efficiency (Fehr & Gächter, 2002).
- **mpcr** (Marginal per-capita return) – Moderates baseline efficiency; higher mpcr supports higher baseline cooperation, so incremental effect of punishment may be reduced at high mpcr (Kümmerli et al., 2010).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech** – Costs and technical implementation (peer-to-peer, centralized, automatic) are critical moderators of the efficiency effect (Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).
- **all_or_nothing** – Most studies use continuous contributions, but some binary/all-or-nothing variants exist; effects seem directionally similar though granularity may affect frequency of punishment (Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).

**b. Indirectly informed dimensions:**
- **chat/communication** – Communication can substitute for or amplify the efficiency effect of punishment; the effect of punishment seems lower or negative when communication is available (Janssen et al., 2010).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech** – Reward as an alternative or complement to punishment is only tested in a few studies (Rand et al., 2009; Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006).
- **show_punishment_id, show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, default_contrib** – These are variably reported; limited studies explore feedback or visibility manipulation, but the core effect of punishment on efficiency is robust to most standard summary feedback treatments.

**c. Contextually discussed or effectively missing:**
- **default_contrib, show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id** – Sparsely manipulated or discussed in terms of their impact on punishment’s payoff effect.
- **Culture/group norms** – Although not always an explicit design dimension, the larger cross-society studies show that these can strongly moderate the effect of punishment (Herrmann et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2009).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Payoff outcomes are systematically reported in only a subset of studies**; many focus solely on behavioral change, which can be misleading for efficiency prediction if punishment is costly.
- **Antisocial punishment and retaliation dynamics** are context-dependent and not easily predicted from game design dimensions alone. Society-level or group-norm variables can override mechanical design effects.
- **Few studies systematically investigate interaction effects between multiple design dimensions** (e.g., how punishment cost interacts with player count or communication).
- **Parameter spaces for punishment cost, impact, and institutional setup explored in the literature are often narrow**—generalization to new parameter values is not robustly evidenced.
- **Adjacent and non-PGG designs (e.g., trust games, dyadic PDs) often do not replicate the efficiency gains from punishment** seen in canonical PGGs, cautioning against overgeneralization from adjacent settings.
- **Extensions to field, diverse cultures, or natural commons remain relatively sparse** and context effects can be large.
- **Some design dimensions required for prediction (e.g., default_contrib, reward technicialities, feedback details) are underexplored**, limiting dimension-level prediction accuracy.

---

**Summary:**  
For downstream prediction of efficiency upon enabling punishment in PGG-like environments, the literature provides strong empirical support where the baseline design matches repeated, multi-person, no-reward games with moderate punishment costs and absent/extremely rare anti-social punishment. Caution is needed for games with high baseline efficiency, very costly or misapplied punishment, potential for vendettas, or in environments with weak group norms or high antisocial punishment. Explicit efficiency measurement is lacking for some analyses and several game design dimensions remain insufficiently explored to warrant high-confidence generalization.
