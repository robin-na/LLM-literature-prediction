# 1) Evidence Base

The literature base for punishment effects in public goods games (PGGs) and close variants is large and diverse, with 120 empirical papers—primarily laboratory experiments, with a few field experiments. The set is notably strong in canonical linear PGGs, but also includes common pool resource (CPR) games, threshold/step-level public goods, trust games, and coordination games. The vast majority are empirical, not theoretical, providing direct experimental evidence.

Most relevant papers directly manipulate the availability and design of punishment and/or reward mechanisms and measure outcomes of interest. There is strong representation of empirical studies directly reporting on efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes (group payoff, earnings, welfare), as well as numerous studies focusing on behavioral measures (contribution rates, punishment frequency) rather than payoffs per se.

The coverage of game design dimensions is generally good for core features: group size, number of rounds, punishment/reward tech/cost, marginal per-capita return (MPCR), information, and feedback. Dimensions like chat, framing, and norm information are present but less systematically varied.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact:** Most papers use canonical repeated linear PGGs or close lab variants (e.g., CPRs, threshold public goods).
- **close/adjacent:** There is also coverage of variants: CPR games, trust games, coordination games, and games with exclusion or other forms of social sanctions.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact:** A substantial number of studies manipulate or introduce peer punishment, centralized punishment, third-party sanctions, or exogenous institution rules.
- **none/close/adjacent:** Some studies focus on non-monetary or costless sanctions (e.g., social disapproval), or adjacent mechanisms like exclusion, audits, or reputation systems.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact/close:** Many studies report efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation) or closely related outcomes like group earnings, surplus, or total returns.
- **adjacent/weak:** Some studies report only behavioral outcomes (contributions, cooperation) or infer payoff outcomes indirectly.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Directly relevant for prediction):**
- **Group efficiency:** Ratio of actual group payoff to the maximum possible from full cooperation (explicit in many PGG papers).
- **Group earnings/total payoff/welfare:** Direct sum of payoffs earned (numerous studies).
- **Surplus creation or reduction:** Net gains after accounting for costs of punishment/rewards.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Common, but must be distinguished):**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates:** Frequency or magnitude of contributions.
- **Punishment/reward behavior:** Amount, frequency, or type of sanctions assigned.
- **Norm compliance:** Adherence to group/institutional rules.
- **Trust, beliefs, attitudes:** Especially in trust games and studies on expectations.

Many papers measure both, but in several cases, increased cooperation does not straightforwardly map to increased efficiency due to costs of punishment, anti-social punishment, or limits on the effectiveness of punishment in varied contexts.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the studies, several consistent patterns and contingencies emerge:

- **Canonical PGGs (with symmetric, observable peer punishment):**
  - Enabling punishment almost always increases both contributions and efficiency, *provided* the cost/impact ratio is favorable (typically 1:3 or higher), anti-social punishment is limited, and information about contributions is available (Gürerk, Lauer, & Scheuermann, 2018; Dickinson et al., 2015; Fu et al., 2017).
  - Centralized punishment (e.g., by a leader or authority) is at least as effective as peer punishment, particularly in homogeneous groups or when detailed contribution info is available (Engl et al., 2021; Kube et al., 2015).
  - The efficiency effect of punishment may be entirely or more than offset if punishment is costly and misdirected (e.g., via anti-social punishment, error, or poor information), leading to little or no net gain in efficiency (Bruhin et al., 2020; Markussen et al., 2016).

- **Moderators and contingencies:**
  - **Anti-social punishment:** When prevalent, it erodes or reverses expected efficiency gains from peer punishment (Bruhin et al., 2020; Bortolotti et al., 2015). Group or cultural composition (subject pool) is a strong moderator.
  - **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** Efficiency gains from punishment are highest when the impact per unit cost is large, and when punishment is targeted and effective (Dickinson et al., 2015; Faillo et al., 2013). Low-effectiveness or high-cost punishment reduces or eliminates efficiency gains, sometimes worsening outcomes.
  - **Network structure:** Asymmetric or incomplete punishment networks (e.g., some players under-monitored) can eliminate or reverse the positive effect of punishment (Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
  - **Information and observability:** Full and accurate feedback on contributions and punishment assigned supports positive efficiency effects; noise or missing feedback increases misdirected punishment and can reduce efficiency (Nicklisch et al., 2016).
  - **Endowment or return heterogeneity:** Heterogeneous groups are prone to normative conflict, reducing the effectiveness of punishment, particularly if endowment information is not available during punishment. In such contexts, punishment may have no effect or may even reduce efficiency (Kingsley, 2016; Kube et al., 2015).
  - **Legitimacy and rule framing:** Efficiency gains are stronger when punishment is perceived as legitimate (e.g., only higher contributors can punish; social history supports legitimacy; institution is endogenously chosen) (Faillo et al., 2013; Gürerk, 2013).
  - **Centralization vs. decentralization:** Both can sustain high efficiency, but centralized punishment is more robust to anti-social punishment and informational noise, especially in larger groups or longer games (Kamijo et al., 2014; Nicklisch et al., 2016).
  - **Reward vs. punishment:** Reward mechanisms can achieve similar or higher efficiency if their structure is payoff-increasing (Gürerk et al., 2009), but do not always substitute fully for punishment, particularly over many rounds (Dickinson et al., 2015).
  - **Sanction error, severity, and probability:** Error-prone sanction regimes (especially type I errors—punishing cooperators) reduce efficiency and can make punishment net negative (Markussen et al., 2016; Qin & Wang, 2013).
  - **Dynamics and duration:** Efficiency gains from punishment may arise only in later rounds, after initial costs are paid; in shorter games, costs may outweigh gains (Masclet et al., 2013; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

- **Control efficiency as a baseline:**  
  - In most canonical settings, control (no punishment) efficiency is much lower than the treatment (punishment enabled) efficiency, but the magnitude of the increase is highly sensitive to the above moderators.

- **Payoff-neutral/costless punishment:**  
  - Costless, non-monetary sanctions (social disapproval) can increase both contributions and efficiency by mechanism (Dugar, 2013), especially when there is no cost to the group.

- **Adjunct and negative findings:**  
  - In some settings (e.g., games with a harmed minority, asymmetric exclusion costs, or pervasive antisocial punishment), enabling punishment reduces or has no effect on efficiency (Dekel et al., 2017; Dannenberg et al., 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**In PGGs matching the canonical design (continuous contribution, 3–5 players, known MPCR, observable contributions, symmetric punishment):**
- **Baseline prediction:** Enabling peer punishment tends to increase average efficiency relative to control. The size of the gain can be substantial (from <40% to 60–90% of the cooperative maximum), especially with favorable punishment parameters and limited antisocial activity (Gürerk et al., 2018; Dickinson et al., 2015).
- **Adjustment for moderators:**
    - Expect smaller or no positive effect when:
        - Antisocial punishment rates are high or group/cultural composition is uncertain (Bruhin et al., 2020; Bortolotti et al., 2015).
        - Punishment is costly and/or less effective (Markussen et al., 2016; Faillo et al., 2013).
        - Information is noisy, incomplete, or feedback is limited (Nicklisch et al., 2016).
        - The network is asymmetric, endowments are heterogeneous/unobserved, or punishment legitimacy is low (Kingsley, 2016; Faillo et al., 2013; Boosey & Isaac, 2016).
    - Positive effect is more robust when:
        - Punishment is central, targeted, and/or institutionally supported/legitimate (Engl et al., 2021; Kamijo et al., 2014; Marcin et al., 2019), or when full information and strong social learning are present (Gürerk, 2013).
    - Costless/non-monetary punishment or concurrent reward mechanisms: Both can substantially increase efficiency if structure supports high contribution without payor losses (Dugar, 2013).

**Quantitative mapping:**
- Control (no-punishment) efficiency offers a meaningful lower bound, but the literature demonstrates that design features and context can lead to less-than-expected gains or even losses when punishment is enabled.
- Generalization beyond canonical designs should be cautious; context-sensitive adjustment of the predicted treatment efficiency is required, based on specific design dimensions and awareness of population/cultural composition.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions (frequent, with quantitative evidence):**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (cost-to-impact ratio), `all_or_nothing` (continuous vs. binary contributions), `show_other_summaries` (information feedback), `chat` (communication enabled/disabled).
- To some degree: `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `chat` (as moderator or control).

**Indirectly or contextually discussed:**
- `default_contrib` (opt-in vs. opt-out) and `show_n_rounds` (horizon effects)—occasionally noted as influencing control efficiency, but less often as punishment moderators.
- `show_punishment_id` (punisher identifiability)—present in some advanced studies of higher-order punishment or retaliation, but often fixed within studies.
- Network structure (encompassing both 'who can punish whom' and the visibility of actions) and institution selection (endogenous or exogenous) are given central attention in several studies (Leibbrandt et al., 2015; Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Marcin et al., 2019).

**Effectively missing dimensions:**
- Fine-grained analyses on the impact of `default_contrib` and detailed presentation effects are sparse.
- Many potentially important psychological/contextual moderators—e.g., population-level social norms, beliefs, priming effects—are not directly coded as game design dimensions.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **External validity and generalizability:** Most high-relevance findings are from small (3–5 person), short-to-medium horizon, laboratory games. Effects in larger, heterogeneous, or field populations may differ substantially—especially due to anti-social punishment, group heterogeneity, and varying cultural norms.
- **Coverage gaps:** Some aspects of game design (e.g., chat, visibility of punishers, opt-in/out defaults, reward framing) are only sporadically manipulated, making it hard to parameterize fine-grained models across all 14 dimensions.
- **Behavioral confounds:** Increased cooperation does not always translate to increased efficiency; net payoff gains depend on punishment usage patterns, institutional cost structures, and potential for misapplied/antisocial punishment, which are not uniformly documented.
- **Contextual moderators:** Group composition (e.g., student vs. general population), cultural background, and prior history/learning are critical but not always measured or reported systematically.
- **Behavior vs. efficiency:** Many studies focus on contributions, not payoffs, and inferring efficiency effects from these may be misleading, especially when punishment is frequent or costly.
- **Rare or edge cases:** In environments with harmed minorities, high baseline cooperation, or complex network/exclusion structures, punishment may not improve or may reduce efficiency (Dekel et al., 2017; Dannenberg et al., 2020).
- **Ambiguity in ambiguous/contradictory findings:** There is often disagreement across settings (e.g., centralized vs. decentralized punishment, endogenous vs. exogenous rule-making, reward vs. punishment efficacy) and findings may conflict across cultural or network configurations.

---

**Summary:**  
The supplied literature robustly shows that enabling peer or centralized punishment in repeated, canonical PGGs increases efficiency relative to control in most lab settings, *if* punishment is not highly anti-social, is effective/cost-efficient, and contributors' actions are observable. However, design details, group composition, information structure, and cultural context are often decisive: under some conditions, punishment can reduce or fail to increase efficiency. Control efficiency provides a lower bound, but modelers must account for moderators—primarily antisocial punishment, network structure, cost/effectiveness of punishment, and the legitimacy/information environment—when using these findings for downstream prediction. Coverage of the canonical prediction dimensions is high for core mechanics, but several dimensions are under-studied or insufficiently parameterized, limiting quantitative generalization in less-standard designs or populations.
