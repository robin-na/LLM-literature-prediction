# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set contains 83 papers with a broad mix of **empirical (lab experiment)** and **theoretical** studies, but the directly relevant empirical evidence for predicting *efficiency* outcomes from design parameters in *public-goods-game-like* (PGG) environments is moderately sparse relative to the full set. Most of the direct, quantitative, efficiency-focused evidence comes from a relatively small number of lab experiments and high-precision theoretical models, while a much larger share of papers explore theoretical mechanisms, evolutionary dynamics, or adjacent outcomes (such as cooperation rates or norm compliance) using agent-based simulations or mathematical analysis. A significant number of papers focus on adjacent games (Prisoner's Dilemma, trust games, collective risk, etc.) or on non-payoff outcomes.

There is a consistent presence of studies examining punishment mechanisms—both peer and institutional—but only a subset connects these interventions to payoff/efficiency outcomes as required for the prediction task. Many papers instead report on *contribution rates*, *cooperation frequency*, or *strategy prevalence*, which while closely related, are not the same as efficiency (payoff relative to the social optimum).

# 2) Task Relevance

#### a) **PGG or Variant**
- **Relevance:** Mostly *exact* or *close* for a subset of papers; many are *adjacent* or *weak* (e.g., PD, donor-recipient, trust games).
- **Coverage:** About 20-25% of papers provide *exact* evidence on standard or well-defined variants of PGG; a larger portion use spatial PGGs or threshold/collective-risk PGGs (often *close*) and many others examine games with similar social dilemmas (*adjacent*).

#### b) **Punishment or Sanctions**
- **Relevance:** A substantial portion of papers score *exact* (peer or institutional punishment in the model/design), but many report on adjacent strategies (social exclusion, institutional controls, or combinations with reward) or focus on adjacent games without explicit punishment. Some relevant mechanisms involve exclusion or reputation-based sanctions (often scored as *adjacent* or *close*).

#### c) **Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**
- **Relevance:** *Exact* or *close* for only a modest share of the corpus (~10-15 papers); many report only on *non-payoff behavioral outcomes* or provide indirect (adjacent) evidence. *Efficiency* (total payoff relative to optimum) is directly reported or systematically compared in few papers. Other studies use group payoff, welfare, or surplus measures but often not as a function of punishment.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes:** *Efficiency* (optimality ratio), *group payoff*, *net profits*, *welfare*, *group earnings*, *probability of group success* (in threshold games), occasional *surplus* or *total coins* generated.
    - Only a limited subset of studies systematically reports how these outcomes change when punishment (vs. no-punishment) is enabled, as required for the target prediction.
    - A few papers employ group achievement or success rate as a strong proxy for efficiency (especially in threshold/collective-risk PGGs).

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:** *Contribution rates*, *cooperation rates*, *frequency of specific strategies (cooperators, defectors, punishers, loners)*, *punishment frequency*, *exclusion choices*, *norm compliance*, *reputation dynamics*, etc.
    - These measures dominate the theoretical/simulation literature and are often interpreted as leading indicators of efficiency but are not equivalent to payoff outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Enabling punishment in PGGs can—but does not always—*increase efficiency* compared to the no-punishment baseline (Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Pi et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Wang & Huang, 2022; Jiang et al., 2023; Lv, Li, & Zhao, 2023; Mondal et al., 2022; Melkonyan et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023).**
    - **Empirical lab PGGs** find that the introduction of punishment typically boosts not only cooperation but also *group payoff/efficiency* if punishment cost is moderate, especially in settings where anti-social punishment is minimal, team-based decision-making is used (especially unanimity rule), or punishment networks are structured to reduce redundant/inefficient punishment (Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Pi et al., 2022).
    - **Theoretical analyses** indicate that the *cost-to-effectiveness* ratio of punishment, the synergy factor (MPCR), punishment network structure, and conditionality of punishment are critical moderators. Too costly or ineffective punishment can lower efficiency due to wasteful sanctions, second-order free-riding, or antisocial punishment, while cheap, strong, or adaptive punishment can, under the right conditions, shift the system to a high-efficiency equilibrium (Liu et al., 2024; Lv et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Mondal et al., 2022).
    - **Threshold/collective-risk PGGs:** Efficiency (e.g., target-achievement rates or group payoff) only increases with punishment when the risk or strength of punishment is sufficiently high; weak or infrequent punishment has little impact, especially in larger groups (Jiang et al., 2023; Hua & Liu, 2023).

- **Team and network structure moderate efficiency gains:** Teams with internal coordination (e.g., unanimity rule) see higher efficiency gains from punishment due to reduced antisocial or excessive punishment (Bahbouhi et al., 2024). Incomplete punishment networks or structured punishment pathways can outperform fully connected networks due to bystander effects or over-punishment in the latter (Pi et al., 2022).

- **Punishment cost, fine/magnitude, and local versus global punishment matter:** Lower cost and higher effectiveness of punishment (fine-to-cost ratio) increase the net efficiency benefit. Local punishment or combined mechanisms (punishment plus exclusion or reward) may yield better payoffs or success rates than traditional global schemes (Lv et al., 2023; Mondal et al., 2022).

- **Non-monotonic and context-dependent effects:** Punishment is not always beneficial—in models or experiments where punishment is excessive, misdirected, or largely anti-social, overall efficiency can fall below the no-punishment baseline (Zhang & Pei, 2022). There is evidence for *bistability* or threshold effects, such that below critical punishment strength or in low-synergy environments, punishment fails to improve outcomes, but beyond a threshold, it enables high efficiency (Liu et al., 2024).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Directly Informed:** For standard or repeated PGGs, with well-characterized player number, rounds, MPCR, and punishment cost/technologies, there is strong empirical and theoretical evidence to predict that *enabling punishment* will often—but not always—increase efficiency relative to control, especially if:
    - The cost of punishment is not excessive (low to moderate),
    - Punishment is strong/effective enough to deter free-riding,
    - Antisocial punishment is low or mitigated,
    - Team or network structure suppresses destructive punishment (e.g., teams using unanimity rule),
    - Control game efficiency is moderate or low.
    - (Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Pi et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025; Wang & Huang, 2022).

- **Indirectly Informed or Moderators:** In cases of *high* punishment cost, many potential punishers (risk of redundancy/anti-sociality), low MPCR, or where group structure encourages second-order free-riding, enabling punishment may yield no efficiency gain—or could even reduce efficiency due to sanctioning costs (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Liu et al., 2024).

- **Network and design dimensions:** The effect of enabling punishment should be considered conditional on not just the existence, but also the *structure* (who punishes whom), *conditionality* (e.g., reputation-based), and *synergy* (MPCR, feedback mechanisms) in the game. Structured or incomplete punishment networks and local punishment tend to outperform complete/global punishment under some conditions (Pi et al., 2022; Hua & Liu, 2023; Wang et al., 2025).

- **Predictions for adjacent or variant games:** For collective-risk, trust, or prisoner's dilemma–like environments adjacent to PGGs, strong punishment (above a critical threshold) is generalizable as likely to increase efficiency (Jiang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Melkonyan et al., 2022; Mondal et al., 2022).

- **Use of control game efficiency:** Where the control game (without punishment) already achieves high efficiency, the scope for treatment improvement is limited. Where the control game is inefficient, punishment is more likely to yield a large efficiency gain if correctly implemented and parameterized (Liu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025).

- **Caveat:** Behavioral outcomes (increased cooperation rate, norm compliance) are good indicators, but *not* guarantees of efficiency improvement—costs of sanctioning must be considered. Many simulation and theoretical studies only report these behavioral measures, not payoffs.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**(See column definitions for dimension names.)**

- **Direct Evidence:**
    - `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` — Well covered in both experimental and theoretical work; these are the main parameters determining equilibrium outcomes in quantitative models (Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Pi et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Lv et al., 2023).
    - Less but present: `all_or_nothing`, `chat` (sometimes), `default_contrib`.

- **Indirect/Mechanism Evidence:**
    - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` — Sometimes included in models analyzing combined or comparative incentives; direct efficiency evidence is rare (Mondal et al., 2022; Kang et al., 2024).
    - `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` — Occasionally referenced as feedback mechanisms, affect norm compliance and potentially efficiency via informational effects (Pi et al., 2022).
    - `punishment_magnitude` — Directly equivalent to punishment fine/severity; analyzed in many theoretical papers as a key moderator.

- **Sparse/Missing:**
    - `chat` — Most models and experiments exclude communication, or treat it as off by default, with little attention to its interactive effects with punishment.
    - `default_contrib` — Framing effects are rarely isolated; only a few studies (Bahbouhi et al., 2024) reference contribution defaults.
    - `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` — Feedback on others’ outcomes and identity of punishers is rarely isolated as an experimental variable.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Gaps in Direct Efficiency Measurement:** The majority of studies, especially theoretical models and simulations, focus on behavioral outcomes (contributions, cooperation frequency) rather than direct efficiency or payoff-based metrics. For many papers, efficiency effects are inferred, not measured.

- **Contextual Differences and Generalizability:** Key design dimensions—communication, contribution defaults, feedback visibility—are often not varied or are held constant, limiting generalizability to settings where these features differ. Results may not transfer to PGGs with reward-only, variable default options, or strong reputation systems.

- **Moderators Often Underexplored:** Although some papers highlight network structure, team rules, or punishment conditionality, these are rarely jointly varied with punishment cost/effectiveness and MPCR in a factorial way—systematic cross-dimensional inference remains limited.

- **Ambiguity and Disagreement:** Some theoretical work notes that punishment can reduce efficiency in certain parameter regimes (high cost, antisocial punishment), while most empirical work (in well-controlled lab settings) finds positive effects when punishment is not too costly. The literature does not resolve these tensions fully, and the mapping from mechanisms to outcomes can be non-monotonic or bistable.

- **Adjacent Games and Weak Transfers:** A large portion of mechanism/theory papers use environments adjacent to (but not identical with) PGGs—e.g., prisoner's dilemma, trust games, threshold or spatial games. While these provide qualitative guidance, quantitative transfer to standard PGG efficiency prediction is uncertain.

- **Lack of Empirical Data for Some Settings:** Most evidence on complex punishment networks, institutional vs. peer punishment, or dynamic/local feedback is theoretical or simulation-based, with little experimental corroboration.

- **Underrepresentation of Certain Dimensions:** Some potentially important design features (visibility of punishment identity, summary statistics feedback, contribution framing, chat) are underexplored in the context of efficiency outcomes.

---

**Summary:**  
The current literature base provides *strong support* for the qualitative claim that enabling punishment mechanisms *often* increases efficiency (i.e., group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum), but this effect is *strongly moderated* by punishment cost, effectiveness, network/team structure, MPCR, and existing levels of efficiency in control conditions. Quantitative prediction is viable only when the relevant design parameters fall within ranges studied in exact or close-relevance empirical/theoretical work. Estimates must be interpreted carefully given that many studies focus on cooperation rates, not payoff outcomes, and generalizability to all 14 design dimensions is limited. Use of adjacent game models (PD, trust, collective risk) presents useful but not strictly transferable guidance.
