# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises exclusively theoretical papers (39/39), with no new empirical or experimental studies. The evidence base is extensive in volume and diverse in theoretical approaches, spanning evolutionary models, agent-based simulations, and analytic game theory. Most papers focus on classic public goods games (PGG) or direct variants; some extend to adjacent social dilemma contexts (indirect reciprocity, animal cooperation, N-person games). The set is broad regarding theoretical mechanism coverage relevant to punishment, but narrow empirically: no studies report new laboratory or field data. Among the theory papers, many report efficiency, welfare, or group payoff outcomes; others focus on cooperation rates or punishment behavior, providing only indirect implications for efficiency. The relevant design dimensions are often explicitly parameterized in models, but not all 14 prediction dimensions are systematically explored nor empirically validated.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**  
- *Relevance:* `exact` for the majority (core set focused on PGG or direct variants); a subset are `close` (e.g., indirect reciprocity, N-person games), with some `adjacent` (animal societies, networked dilemmas).
- *Notes:* Most high-detail findings and direct theoretical predictions are in papers modeling PGG or voluntary PGG settings.

**punishment_or_sanctions**  
- *Relevance:* `exact` for most (modeling peer or institutional punishment in PGG); several `close` (punishment-like exclusion, metapunishment, or reward for punishers); a few only `adjacent` or discuss punishment contextually.
- *Notes:* Some adjunct papers discuss punishment as part of broader cooperation norms rather than as a model mechanism.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**  
- *Relevance:* About half of the papers are `exact` (reporting efficiency, group payoff, or related welfare as central outcomes); several are `close` (payoff-based arguments without explicit efficiency ratios); the rest are `adjacent` or report only on non-payoff behaviors such as cooperation or norm compliance.
- *Notes:* Many mechanism papers draw inferences linking cooperation prevalence to efficiency, but quantitative estimates are sparse in absence of direct efficiency reporting.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (`efficiency_or_payoff`):**
  - Numerous theory papers (e.g., Sasaki et al., 2012; García & Traulsen, 2012; Perc, 2012; Rand & Nowak, 2011; Boyd et al., 2010; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Eldakar & Wilson, 2008; Fowler, 2005) directly model total group payoff, efficiency, welfare, or surplus, and outcomes are usually provided as equilibrium values, phase diagrams, or simulated averages.
  - Some models (e.g., Sigmund et al., 2010; Hauert et al., 2007; Brandt et al., 2006) use efficiency in comparing institutional versus peer punishment, or voluntary versus compulsory participation.
  - Papers such as Sigmund et al. (2001), Duéñez-Guzmán & Sadedin (2012), and Ohtsuki et al. (2009) are explicit about efficiency, but often in settings adjacent to classic PGG.
  - Close but not exact papers often focus on cooperator payoff or welfare, which is linked but not identical to group efficiency (e.g., Smaldino & Lubell, 2011; Brandt & Sigmund, 2005).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - Many theory papers target cooperation rates, defector prevalence, frequency of punishment, or strategy abundances (e.g., Boyd et al., 2003; Traulsen et al., 2009; Fehr & Fischbacher, 2003; Singer & Steinbeis, 2009).
  - Network-structured models report state occupancy (cooperation, norm establishment/collapse) but not explicit efficiency (e.g., Galan et al., 2011).
  - A substantial subset discusses motivation, reputation, and norm psychology without group payoff outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical synthesis of theory:**

- **Punishment can, in theory, increase group efficiency—but only under specific structural and parameter conditions.**  
  - Voluntary (optional) participation enables punishment to foster high efficiency with lower sanction costs (Sasaki et al., 2012; Hauert et al., 2007; Fowler, 2005).
  - The cost structure of punishment (punisher cost, punished impact) and the type of punishment (peer, pool/institutional, metapunishment) are critical. Pool-punishment is more stable, but often less efficient due to persistent overhead costs (Sigmund et al., 2010; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012).
  - Coordination and cost-sharing among punishers increase efficiency gains (Boyd et al., 2010).

- **Key moderators that determine whether punishment boosts, does not affect, or reduces efficiency:**
  - *Reputation/Information*: If players can see or infer others’ actions or punishment behavior, the efficiency boost from punishment is greater; without, punishment often fails to increase efficiency (Sigmund et al., 2001; Hilbe & Traulsen, 2012; Brandt & Sigmund, 2005).
  - *Second-order free riders*: Efficiency gains require sanctioning of those who do not punish defectors (second-order free riders); if these are not eliminated, punishment can be unsustainable and fail to raise efficiency (Perc, 2012; Sigmund et al., 2010).
  - *Antisocial punishment*: If players can punish cooperators (antisocial punishment), punishment regimes may actually reduce efficiency, with high costs incurred by all (Rand & Nowak, 2011).
  - *Initial conditions*: When the initial prevalence of punishers is low, systems can lock into low-efficiency states even with punishment available (Brandt et al., 2006).
  - *Punishment cost and magnitude*: High punishment cost relative to the punished impact (i.e., ineffective/expensive sanctions) reduces the net effect on efficiency (Eldakar & Wilson, 2008; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Rand & Nowak, 2011).

- **Complexity and sensitivity:**
  - Threshold and phase-transition phenomena are common: modest changes in punishment reach or cost can sharply shift efficiency (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012).
  - Evolutionary outcomes (and efficiency) can be highly sensitive to the mutation/exploration structure (García & Traulsen, 2012; Traulsen et al., 2009).
  - Some models observe bistability: both high- and low-efficiency states are stable depending on starting conditions (Brandt et al., 2006).

- **Reward versus punishment:**
  - Punishment is generally found more effective—often at lower cost—than reward for reaching high efficiency, but reward can still improve cooperation under some structures (Sasaki et al., 2012; Sigmund et al., 2001).

**Distinct empirical versus theory/argument evidence:**
- All findings above are from theoretical and simulation-based work; absence of laboratory/field evidence in this set means findings speak to model environments but not directly to human or field settings’ data.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**What can an analyst infer for efficiency prediction from this literature?**

- **If the game design matches core requirements (PGG, observable actions or reputation, peer or coordinated punishment, manageable punishment cost, and absence of antisocial punishment or unchecked second-order free riders), the theoretical expectation is that enabling (peer or institutional) punishment will increase efficiency over a control with punishment disabled.**  
  - The expected gain is larger with voluntary participation and mechanisms for presence/visibility of punishers; threshold phenomena (phase transitions) can occur as costs, impact, or reach cross critical values.
  - The effect size is highly sensitive to the design details: with antisocial punishment, low punishment effectiveness, high costs, compulsory participation, or excessive institutional overhead, efficiency gains may not materialize—indeed, efficiency can be flat or lower in some configurations.

- **If key conditions are not met**—e.g., anonymity, high punishment cost, lack of coordination, design allows antisocial punishment, or persistent second-order free riders—efficiency gains are unlikely, and efficiency may decline.

- **Quantitative mapping:**  
  - This literature yields qualitative or direction-of-effect predictions (improvement, no effect, or decrease) rather than parameterized effect sizes; phase diagrams and analytic results often specify boundaries, but not empirical calibration.

- **Mechanisms and design moderators must be interpreted:**  
  - For attributes like player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, and punishment_tech, the literature provides direct mapping in models.
  - For more contextually specific features (chat, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id), evidence is typically indirect, via their effects on visibility, coordination, and reputation.

- **Baseline/control efficiency matters:**  
  - The magnitude of gain from enabling punishment grows as the control (no-punishment) efficiency falls—punishment is predicted to matter most when the baseline is far from full cooperation.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**
- **player_count (group size):** Always parameterized. Effects vary; smaller groups sometimes facilitate punishment effectiveness, but robust predictions require attention to model details (e.g., Boyd et al., 2010; Eldakar & Wilson, 2008).
- **num_rounds:** Parameterized in most; more rounds can allow learning, reputation, or stable equilibrium selection, but direct empirical mapping to treatment efficiency is rare.
- **mpcr (marginal per-capita return):** Widely analyzed; higher mpcr generally facilitates the effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Core in nearly all PGG+punishment papers; lower cost/more effective punishment delivers higher efficiency, subject to boundary effects.
- **all_or_nothing:** Included in several models; some models assume all-or-nothing contributions, others allow continuous action spaces.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Directly addressed in a subset (e.g., Sasaki et al., 2012; Sigmund et al., 2001; Duéñez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012).
  
**Indirectly or contextually informed:**
- **chat:** Not directly modeled but related to coordination or reputation formation. Some results for communication as a facilitator of coordinated punishment (Boyd et al., 2010), but not to specific chat implementations.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Not directly parameterized, but findings about reputation and information depend on observability of actions—reputation/visibility mechanisms are found essential for punishment's effect on efficiency.
- **default_contrib (contribution framing):** Not differentiated in the models.

**Sparse or missing:**
- **default_contrib, show_n_rounds:** Not analyzed.
- **show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Only discussed as components of non-anonymity or reputation; not as standalone variables.
- **detailed punishment/reward implementation technologies beyond cost/impact and coverage:** Model simplifications dominate over implementation specifics.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **No empirical validation:** All evidence is theoretical or simulation-based. There is no new laboratory or field data to confirm model predictions about punishment's effect on efficiency in actual human PGG experiments.
- **Missing or under-explored design dimensions:** Several prediction features (e.g., chat, default_contrib, identity visibility, reward implementation) are only addressed indirectly, if at all.
- **Ambiguity and context dependence:** Many models find both high- and low-efficiency equilibria are stable under different initial or parameter conditions (bistability/phase transitions). Real-world prediction is uncertain when design places the system near such boundaries.
- **Treatment of behavioral and payoff outcomes:** While cooperation rates are frequently reported, they are not synonymous with efficiency. Where payoff outcomes are absent, their translation to efficiency predictions is indirect and sometimes tenuous.
- **Scope of punishment examined:** The models tend to focus on idealized forms of punishment (e.g., perfectly coordinated, with clear action observability). Realistic complications such as mistakes, noise, reluctance, or retaliation (antisocial punishment) often undermine predicted gains.
- **Parameter calibration and effect sizes:** The literature is rich in qualitative findings and phase boundaries but poor in empirical effect size estimates, limiting its value for quantitative prediction.
- **Special populations and settings:** Results for spatial, networked, or indirect reciprocity games may not generalize to standard experimental PGGs.

---

**Summary**: The literature set provides robust theoretical insights for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public goods games, with direct application to design dimensions such as group size, punishment cost, and information structure. However, its predictive value is constrained by the absence of empirical evidence, incomplete coverage of all design variables, and high sensitivity to structural and parametric moderators. Efficiency gains from punishment are contingent—not guaranteed—and strongly depend on factors like voluntary participation, action observability, coordination, and the absence of counterproductive punishment. Prediction in new game designs must carefully consider these contingencies and apply model insights with caution.
