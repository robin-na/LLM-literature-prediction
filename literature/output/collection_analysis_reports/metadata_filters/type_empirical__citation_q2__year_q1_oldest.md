# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set contains **34 studies**, primarily empirical laboratory experiments, complemented by a small number of field and observational studies. The **majority** of the evidence is **empirical** rather than theoretical, with a strong representation of experimental economics methods using either standard public goods games (PGGs) or adjacent strategic environments (e.g., prisoner's dilemmas, trust games, real-effort team tasks, ultimatum/extortion games). 

The coverage is **broad for public goods and social dilemma games overall**, but **narrower in direct relevance to the task**: only a subset of the studies report **payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, welfare, group payoff)** in **public-goods-game-like environments with punishment manipulation**. Many papers investigate **behavioral outcomes** (contributions, cooperation rates, norm enforcement) or use designs **adjacent to standard PGGs**. Several studies provide only **indirect or contextual** evidence for the key prediction task.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Three target-relevance dimensions:**

- **pgg_or_variant:**  
  *exact*: Lab experiments using standard repeated PGGs with common design features (e.g., Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Guillen et al., 2010; Chung et al., 2011; Noussair & Soo, 2008; Denant-Boemont et al., 2011).  
  *close*: Games with institutional variants, step-level (threshold) public goods, or group-level common-pool resource (CPR) setups (e.g., Colombier et al., 2011; Fatas et al., 2010; Akpalu & Martinsson, 2012).  
  *adjacent/weak*: Trust games, bargaining, extortion, ultimatum, allocation, real-world forums, and non-economic scenarios (majority of remaining papers).

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**  
  *exact*: Controlled introduction or manipulation of **peer punishment** or institutional punishment in PGG or close variant (e.g., Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Colombier et al., 2011; Fatas et al., 2010).  
  *close/adjacent*: Availability or salience of sanctions (ostracism, tax/fine, exclusion, third-party approval, etc.), often not peer-based or not endogenous (e.g., Akpalu & Martinsson, 2012; Tan & Xiao, 2012; Brick & Visser, 2010; Bolle et al., 2011).  
  *none/weak*: Studies without any punishment mechanism (e.g., Chung et al., 2011; Bravo, 2010).

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
  *exact*: Explicit reporting of group payoff or efficiency as share of maximum (e.g., Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).  
  *close*: Reporting related group earnings, surplus, or strongly-proxy outcomes (Fatas et al., 2010; Colombier et al., 2011; Corgnet, 2012; Grimm & Mengel, 2011).  
  *adjacent/weak*: Only behavioral (contribution, norm compliance) or indirect welfare/quality not converted to payoffs. Many studies in this category.

**Summary:**  
- **Directly relevant** studies (exact/exact/exact) are in the **minority**.  
- Many papers score “exact” on one or two dimensions but not all three.  
- Most studies provide **only indirect** evidence for one or more components of the downstream prediction task.  
- There is **little theoretical coverage**; most findings are empirical.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**:  
  - *Efficiency* (group earnings as % of full cooperation): measured directly in a few key PGG studies (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011), and as close proxies in others (Colombier et al., 2011; Fatas et al., 2010; Grimm & Mengel, 2011; Corgnet, 2012).
  - *Total group payoff, welfare, profits, earnings, coins generated*: sometimes reported, but often not converted to efficiency ratio.
  - *Success ratio/group quality*: used as close proxies for efficiency in some close-variant studies.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:  
  - *Contribution rate, cooperation rate*: Widely reported, often as the main outcome, especially in studies without explicit punishment or with non-standard game designs.
  - *Punishment frequency, norm enforcement, ostracism rates, trustworthiness*: Common, especially in adjacent designs (trust games, CPR, retrospections).
  - *Retaliation, compliance, strategic responses*: Focus of studies on norm psychology, retaliatory dynamics, procedural justice.

- **Distinguishing features**:  
  - Many papers use **contribution rate as a surrogate** for efficiency without reporting group payoffs or accounting for punishment costs, which can mislead predictions for efficiency outcomes.
  - A notable subset **do not report payoff outcomes at all**, even when behavioral sanctions are manipulated.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment can strongly increase efficiency**—but only under specific conditions:  
  - When **punishment is strong, exogenously imposed, and well-designed (high fine-to-cost ratio, automatic enforcement, no chat, standard PGG rules, fixed institution)**, enabling punishment moves efficiency from near-Nash levels to near full cooperation, especially for non-cooperative (low-baseline) groups (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).
  - **Endogenously set** or **weak punishment rules**, especially in non-cooperative groups or where voting is required, do **not** lead to increased efficiency—non-cooperators do not reliably self-impose effective punishment (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).

- **Punishment sometimes reduces efficiency**:  
  - If punishment is **costly**, and costs are not offset by increased cooperation, overall efficiency can **decline** (Colombier et al., 2011; Abbink et al., 2004; Bolle et al., 2011; Corgnet, 2012; Tan & Xiao, 2012).
  - Procedural constraints or hurdles (e.g., third-party approval, high opportunity for counter-punishment) can undermine positive effects (Tan & Xiao, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).

- **Design-specific moderators**:  
  - **Group size**: Larger groups potentially dilute the effectiveness of peer punishment (Bshary & Bshary, 2010; but direct efficiency evidence lacking).
  - **Game length**: More rounds (longer games) magnify the potential for positive efficiency effects via reputation (Davis & Holt, 1994).
  - **Matching structure/viscosity**: High group stability/local interaction promotes higher efficiency with punishment; random matching reduces it (Grimm & Mengel, 2011).
  - **Punishment cost/tech**: Convex or very low punishment cost increases risk of over-punishment (Bolle et al., 2011), while high cost can neutralize positive effects (Colombier et al., 2011).
  - **Communication**: Absence of chat seems associated with more positive pure punishment effects (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).
  - **Reward systems**: Reward (when available) is often found to be more efficient than punishment—sometimes the only institution that increases both cooperation and group payoff (Colombier et al., 2011; SCHMITT & MARWELL, 1970).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **For environments matching standard laboratory PGGs**—with **no communication, fixed groups, high MPCR, and externally imposed, strong, low-cost peer punishment**—enabling punishment is **very likely to increase efficiency**, often dramatically, especially if the baseline (control) efficiency is low. Use the magnitude of efficiency increase observed in these studies (from ~60–70% of maximum up to ~100%, or Nash earnings up to social optimum) as a reference for prediction (*Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011*).

- **Where punishment is weak, endogenous, subject to procedural hurdles, costly, or prone to retaliation**, **efficiency gains may be muted or erased**—and may even reverse if punishment costs are high and cooperation increases are small (*Colombier et al., 2011; Abbink et al., 2004; Bolle et al., 2011*).

- **Use control game efficiency as an important anchor**: When baseline efficiency is already high (due to institutional design, high MPCR, communication, or intrinsic motivation), the marginal gain from enabling punishment is often small or negligible.

- **Critical moderators to attend to** in prediction:
  - **Player count**: Larger groups likely dilute punishment’s efficacy.
  - **Punishment cost and punishment tech**: High punishment costs or complicated procedures impede positive efficiency returns.
  - **Matching/partner structure**: Fixed groups favor punishment effectiveness.
  - **Communication (chat)**: If present, may reduce the marginal need for punishment to sustain cooperation/efficiency.
  - **Reward presence**: If available, rewards may out-compete punishment for producing efficiency gains.

- **When outcome data is limited to non-payoff measurements** (contributions, cooperation), use caution: these do not reliably translate into efficiency unless punishment costs are accounted for (e.g., higher contributions with high punishment cost may still reduce total payoffs).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed** by at least one exact-relevance empirical study (i.e., effect of punishment on efficiency):  
- `player_count` (4–5 is typical; effect appears robust in small groups, uncertain in larger ones)
- `num_rounds` (evidence for effect of game length; more rounds enable stronger reputation dynamics)
- `mpcr` (included in almost all directly relevant designs)
- `punishment_cost` (central to all punishment manipulation studies)
- `punishment_tech` (some, especially when contrasting peer/institutional, or procedural features)
- `all_or_nothing` (standard and variant PGGs both represented)
- `chat` (normally disabled in cleanest studies)
- `show_n_rounds` (rarely manipulated, but often included)
  
**Indirectly informed** or **contextually discussed**:  
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` (rarely manipulated directly, but outcome observability sometimes alluded to)
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (comparison to reward institutions present in some studies)
- `default_contrib` (mostly fixed; not a major focus)
- `player_count` effects for larger groups inferred mostly indirectly (Bshary & Bshary, 2010).

**Effectively missing** direct evidence:  
- Systematic variation of `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`, `default_contrib`, `reward_tech`, and `reward_cost` within PGGs with documented efficiency outcomes is **rare/absent**.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Few studies directly measure efficiency as a function of all prediction dimensions**; most vary only a subset and report outcomes variably (payoff vs non-payoff).
- **Behavioral outcomes (contributions, cooperation) are often reported without corresponding group payoff calculations, especially accounting for the cost of punishment**, limiting external validity for efficiency prediction.
- **Most evidence is from small-N (four to five players) lab settings; generalization to large groups or complex real-world PGGs is untested**.
- **Reward mechanisms** and their interaction with punishment are present in only a handful of studies, making prediction for environments where both are enabled less certain.
- **Complex procedural features (e.g., punishment approved by third party, visibility/identity of punishers, communication structures, endogenous institution setting) are only occasionally studied**, often in adjacent or non-PGG designs.
- **Virtually no theoretical modeling is provided to interpolate/extrapolate findings across untested combinations of design parameters**; predictions thus rely on empirical analogs rather than first principles.
- **Self-governance and endogenous institution formation** are shown to be less effective than externally imposed punishment, but most prediction guidance presumes only externally imposed or standardized peer punishment.
- **Possible negative or null effects of punishment on efficiency are well documented in adjacent designs or when costs are high**, suggesting caution in generalizing positive effects of punishment beyond well-controlled, "classic" PGG lab settings.
- **Interpersonal, gender, or cultural context factors, while discussed, are not systematically manipulated alongside efficiency reporting**, so their effect is largely unknown for prediction.
- **No studies manipulate or systematically report all 14 design dimensions in tandem with efficiency outcomes.**

# Summary

The evidence base for predicting the **treatment efficiency** of public-goods-game-like environments when **peer punishment is enabled**, relative to control, is **strong only for specific, tightly-controlled PGG laboratory settings (few players, strong external punishment, no communication, low-cost punishment)**. In all other cases, predictions should be **hedged**, especially where **punishment is weak, costly, or endogenous**, or where the measured outcomes are not direct efficiency metrics. Evidence for the fine-grained effects of many design dimensions is **sparse or inconsistent**. Key gaps remain in **systematic multidimensional manipulation** and **direct measurement of group efficiency**, limiting the granularity of downstream prediction.
