# Evidence Base

The paper set draws from a large and diverse collection of 195 items, containing both **empirical laboratory and field experiments** as well as a substantial number of **theory and simulation-based studies**. Empirical studies dominate, particularly in the space of laboratory public goods games (PGGs) with clearly specified design parameters and outcomes. The theory papers are largely formal and simulation-based, often focused on spatial, evolutionary, or institutional models of cooperation. While the set is broad in terms of methodological approach, **the coverage is narrower—and stronger—when restricted to studies directly targeting efficiency or related payoff outcomes in public-goods-game-like environments with and without punishment**. The richest and most consistent quantitative evidence corresponds to standard, linear PGGs in laboratory settings manipulating the presence/absence of peer punishment; evidence is thinner but present for more complex, field, or institutionally rich settings.

# Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact**: The majority of studies focus directly on standard linear public goods games or close continuous-contribution variants; some work on related step-level games (threshold/tipping point, weakest-link, etc.) is also present but must be flagged as "adjacent."  
- **close/adjacent/weak**: A substantial subliterature uses Prisoner's Dilemma, Dictator, Ultimatum, trust, or helping games—these are structurally adjacent but do not map one-to-one to the PGG, especially regarding group-level efficiency and the mapping of design dimensions.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact**: Many papers manipulate or model the presence of (peer or institutional) punishment, with detailed attention to punishment cost, technology, and targeting. There is also broad coverage of related sanctions such as exclusion, ostracism, rewards, or reputation mechanisms.  
- **adjacent/weak**: Some papers analyze related mechanisms (reputation, exclusion, peer pressure, commitment) or third-party punishment, but are less directly informative on classic peer punishment in PGGs.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact/close**: A significant proportion (especially among empirical lab work) report efficiency (ratio of group earnings to social optimum), group payoff, or surplus. Some provide group earnings or welfare data, which, with reported parameters, can be transformed to efficiency for prediction. Meta-analyses and review papers often synthesize effects on cooperation/contribution (non-payoff behavior), but a subset also draw conclusions about group earnings or efficiency.  
- **adjacent/weak**: Many studies focus exclusively on contribution or cooperation rates, norm compliance, or psychological outcomes; these are important behavioral outcomes but form an indirect proxy for efficiency, sometimes but not always mapping onto payoffs due to the costliness of punishment.

# Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based outcomes:**  
  - Efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum), average individual/group earnings, welfare, surplus, and total coins/points.
  - Several studies and meta-analyses provide group efficiency under both control (no-punishment) and treatment (punishment-enabled) conditions.
  - Some theoretical studies yield explicit equations, phase diagrams, or equilibria for efficiency as a function of design variables (e.g., player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (distinguished throughout):**  
  - Contribution rate, cooperation rate, punishment frequency, intensity, targeting, norm compliance, trust, reputation, antisocial punishment, emotion.
  - A large number of studies report only these behavioral indicators; these are discussed as implications for efficiency only where justified by payoff calculations or explicit efficiency analysis.

# Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **General findings under standard lab PGGs:**
  - In repeated, linear public goods games, introducing peer punishment (when punishment is neither prohibitively costly nor weak) **robustly increases efficiency**—low baseline efficiency (control) is raised towards the social optimum (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2013; Markussen et al., 2014).
  - However, efficiency *gains* from punishment depend on the *punishment technology* (cost, fine), *baseline efficiency*, and *behavioral context* (e.g., presence of communication, mood, norm signaling, group heterogeneity, or opportunity for antisocial punishment).

- **Moderators and exceptions:**
  - **Negative/Neutral/Adverse Effect on Efficiency:**
    - If **punishment is costly and not highly effective**, the resulting punishment costs can offset or outweigh contribution gains; efficiency sometimes falls or does not increase with punishment (Wu et al., 2016; Gächter et al., 2017—negative MPCR; Kurzban et al., 2015; Barrett, 2016).
    - **Antisocial punishment** (punishing high contributors or cooperators) or **retaliation/counter-punishment** can lead to reduced efficiency (Gächter et al., 2017; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015; Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Gächter et al., 2017 (dynamic endowments)).
    - In **dynamic or endogenous games** (where wealth/endowment accumulates, or in persistently unequal groups), punishment may fail to increase efficiency or can even worsen it (Gächter et al., 2017; Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
    - **If baseline cooperation is already high**, punishment can be redundant or counterproductive.
    - **Bribery/corruption opportunities** or other means to undermine punishment drastically reduce or reverse efficiency gains from punishment (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).

  - **Positive Effect Enhanced by:**
    - **Norm signaling and communication:** Punishment combined with explicit normative messages or communication yields especially large efficiency gains (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Dal Bó & Dal Bó, 2014).
    - **Institutional design:** Efficiency is highest when *democratic processes* select punishment institutions or when *voluntary centralization* of punishment is enabled (Gross et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014; Markussen et al., 2014).
    - **Optimal venting/cooling-off:** Moderating emotional arousal before punishment can reduce wasteful punishment and raise efficiency (Dickinson & Masclet, 2015).
    - **Reward mechanisms coexisting with punishment** can, when properly structured, further boost efficiency (Chen et al., 2015; Sasaki et al., 2015), but the effect is not always additive.

  - **Critical design dimensions affecting magnitude/direction:**
    - **MPCR (multiplier per capita):** If MPCR < 1/n (i.e., cooperation is strictly inefficient), punishment increases contributions but lowers efficiency (Abbink et al., 2017).
    - **Group size:** Efficacy of punishment tends to be stronger in small groups; efficiency effects can decline with increasing player count (Wolitzky, 2013; Hilbe et al., 2014).
    - **Punishment cost/effectiveness:** Higher cost or lower impact reduces efficacy; extremely high cost can make punishment destructive (Perc et al., 2017).
    - **Heterogeneity:** Valuation heterogeneity decreases or negates efficiency gains from punishment, while capability heterogeneity can enhance the effect (Kölle, 2015).

  - **Mechanism findings:**
    - Both *peer* and *institutional* punishment can sustain cooperation and efficiency, but implementation details matter; peer punishment is more cost-effective under certain setups (Markussen et al., 2014).
    - **Exclusion (ostracism)** and *commitment mechanisms* can outperform or substitute for punishment in sustaining high efficiency (Charness & Yang, 2014; Feinberg et al., 2014; Han et al., 2017).

  - **Theory and simulation:**
    - Models map out *parameter regions* where punishment raises/lowers efficiency (Perc et al., 2017; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
    - Some models confirm second-order free-riding, anti-social punishment, and dynamical instability can reduce or neutralize efficiency gains (Hauser et al., 2014).

# Prediction Guidance

- **Empirically-driven guidance for the prediction task** (efficiency with peer punishment enabled | design dimensions, control efficiency):
  - If the control game has **low to moderate efficiency** and is otherwise a standard repeated linear PGG (3-5 players, moderate rounds, MPCR > 1/n, punishment cost-effectiveness ratio reasonable, no reward/corruption/strong exclusion), **enabling peer punishment is predicted to raise efficiency sharply**, often to near the cooperative optimum. The magnitude depends on the size of the efficiency shortfall in control, but typical treatment-control gains are large (e.g., from <50% to 80-100%).
  - If *punishment is extremely costly* or the *punishment fine is weak*, efficiency gains are minimal or negative.
  - If **antisocial punishment or counter-punishment** is prevalent (e.g., cultural context or high initial inequality), efficiency gains are eroded or reversed—efficiency may stagnate or fall (see Gächter et al., 2017; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015; Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
  - **Structural moderators**:
    - **Chat/communication**: Presence of norm signaling or communication can amplify punishment’s efficiency-boosting effect (Andrighetto et al., 2013).
    - **Exclusion**: If punishment technology is exclusionary rather than cost-imposing, and is effective and not too costly, efficiency gains are even larger.
    - **Reward options**: Adaptive combination of reward and punishment can, in theory, minimize cost for the same efficiency effect.
    - **Centralization or endogeneity of punishment**: Voluntary or democratically-chosen punishment institutions are more likely to generate high efficiency than imposed or non-democratic ones (Gross et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014).

- **Prediction for unusual/complex environments:**
  - In public goods games with *dynamic wealth*, *high inequality*, or *corruption/bribery*, the presence of punishment does not guarantee, and may inhibit, efficiency (Gächter et al., 2017; Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
  - For *threshold* or *maintenance* type PGGs, punishment often restores efficiency lost under baseline decay, but effect sizes can be contingent on group composition, visibility, and whether the game is “provision” or “maintenance” framed (Gächter et al., 2017; Pacheco et al., 2014).

# Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (strong, with empirical payoff evidence):**
- `player_count`: Multiple studies manipulate and report group size and its effect.
- `num_rounds`: Number of rounds is systematically varied; games with more rounds tend to see higher efficiency increases from punishment, up to a point.
- `all_or_nothing`: Both all-or-nothing and continuous contribution versions studied.
- `chat`: Communication/norm signaling is found to synergize with punishment.
- `mpcr`: Marginal per capita return is key—core moderator for whether punishment improves or reduces efficiency.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Generally, cost/impact of punishment is precisely varied and measured for effect on efficiency.
- `show_other_summaries`: Some studies manipulate feedback about others, showing effects on norm enforcement and efficiency.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Frequently present in theory papers modeling the interaction of reward and punishment.

**Indirectly informed or contextually discussed:**
- `default_contrib`: Default framing (opt-in/opt-out) less systematically studied, but some papers discuss its role.
- `show_n_rounds`: Finite-horizon effects are sometimes discussed, as are effects of transparency about remaining rounds.
- `show_punishment_id`: Visibility or anonymity of punishers/rewarders is highlighted as a context variable.
- `show_other_summaries`: Feedback about peer contributions is sometimes manipulated.

**Sparse or Missing:**
- Few studies systematically manipulate `default_contrib`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id` as primary moderators.
- Some design dimensions—such as multiple overlapping information treatments or highly complex institutional features—are less represented and findings are less consistent or sparse.

# Important Limitations

- **Representation of Extremely Large, Dynamic, or Real-world Groups is Limited:** Most payoff data comes from small-to-moderate lab studies; field experiments and large-group, real-stakes environments are less comprehensively covered.
- **Structural Complexity:** Many design dimensions (e.g., multi-level information structures, multiple simultaneous incentives, evolving/dynamic group or endogenous sanctioning rules) are only indirectly studied or remain underpowered for strong inference.
- **Behavioral and Contextual Moderators:** Emotional state, cultural background, inequality, and prior experience are shown to modulate punishment's effect, but are not consistently measured or controlled across studies.
- **Payoff vs. Behavioral Outcome Ambiguity:** Many studies focus on cooperation or punishment rates as the main outcome, reporting efficiency only as a secondary or inferred result.
- **Boundary Cases and Edge Parameter Regions:** The effect of punishment is highly parameter-sensitive (e.g., at critical MPCR thresholds, high punishment cost/fine ratios, or in the presence of antisocial/retaliatory punishment). Sufficient data for sharply predicting these edge cases are lacking.
- **Reporting Gaps:** Not all studies report both treatment and control efficiency; sometimes efficiency must be inferred from contribution or behavioral data.
- **Scope of Institutional and Cultural Variants:** Variants such as bribery, exclusion, centralization, or corruption are covered, but not systematically relative to every design dimension.

---

## Summary

Taken together, the literature supports **a robust, positive effect of peer punishment on efficiency in standard PGGs with reasonable parameter values and moderate group sizes,** with effect size and even direction moderated by game design (notably, MPCR, punishment cost/effectiveness, baseline efficiency, heterogeneity, presence of communication/norm signaling, provision/maintenance frame, reward/exclusion options, and information structure). **However, punishment’s effect is not universally positive: under costly, retaliatory, antisocial, or mis-targeted punishment regimes, or in games with inefficient cooperation or high inequality, punishment can fail to improve or even reduce efficiency**. The **best-informed prediction dimensions** for downstream tasks are *player count*, *number of rounds*, *MPCR*, *punishment cost/tech*, and *presence of communication/norm signaling*. Other dimensions are less well-covered or only indirectly addressed. **A cautious, context-sensitive approach is required for prediction, especially near structural or behavioral boundary conditions**.
