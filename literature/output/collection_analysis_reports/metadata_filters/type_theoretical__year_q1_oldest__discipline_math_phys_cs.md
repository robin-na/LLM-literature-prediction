# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 150 theory-driven papers, with a mix spanning canonical public goods games (PGGs), close variants, and a wide range of adjacent models (e.g., repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, indirect reciprocity, threshold games, resource management, and social network dynamics). The evidence base is heavily theoretical or simulation-based, with a strong emphasis on formal evolutionary game theory, replicator dynamics, and agent-based models. Empirical experimental work is present but not predominant, and most payoff outcomes are model-generated rather than directly observed from laboratory or field studies.

The breadth of the literature is considerable, covering a diversity of game structures, enforcement mechanisms (peer punishment, pool punishment, reward, metanorms), and contextual features (e.g., spatial structure, reputation, voluntary participation, network adaptation, information asymmetry). Coverage is robust for canonical and spatial PGGs with punishment, but there is much less direct evidence for some specific prediction dimensions (e.g., chat, show_punishment_id). Outcomes are usually presented as theoretical or simulated metrics (mean payoff, efficiency, prevalence of strategies), and direct empirical effect sizes are rare.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. PGG or variant (`pgg_or_variant`):**
- **Exact relevance**: There is strong, direct coverage of canonical PGGs and spatial PGGs with or without institutional/peer punishment in the majority of the set (e.g., Cressman et al., 2012; Eldakar et al., 2007; Gintis, 2000; Sigmund et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2010).  
- **Close/adjacent relevance**: A significant portion of the literature considers close variants: collective-risk dilemmas, repeated PD, CPR games, threshold games, indirect reciprocity, and resource allocation games, which share underlying logic but may lack certain PGG features.
- **Weak/none**: Some papers are purely adjacent, modeling bargaining, mutualism, or evolutionary selection principles without mapping onto PGGs.

**b. Punishment or sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`):**
- **Exact relevance**: Central analysis of costly peer punishment, pool (institutional) punishment, reward vs. punishment comparisons, metanorms, and punishment cost/effectiveness tradeoffs is present throughout (e.g., Eldakar et al., 2007; Rand et al., 2010; Dejong et al., 2008; Gintis, 2000; Sigmund et al., 2011).
- **Close/adjacent**: Some coverage extends to related enforcement mechanisms—ostracism, exclusion, reputation-based indirect sanctions, partner choice, and social control.
- **Weak/none**: A minority discuss only punishment as framing/background, not as a manipulable treatment.

**c. Efficiency or related payoff outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`):**
- **Exact relevance**: Many papers report mean payoff, group efficiency, welfare, or surplus directly and analytically relate these to the presence/absence of punishment (e.g., Wang et al., 2010; Zhuang et al., 2012; Gintis et al., 2001; Bowles & Gintis, 2004).
- **Close/adjacent**: Several infer efficiency from cooperation rates, prevalence of certain strategies, or dominance states. In such cases, efficiency is proxied rather than directly measured, so interpretations for efficiency must be cautious.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**: A large subset reports only strategy frequencies, cooperation/contribution rates, norm compliance, or reputation states, which are at best indirect proxies for efficiency.

In summary, the evidence base is **highly relevant** for analytical, theoretical prediction of efficiency impacts of peer punishment in PGG-like environments, with important caveats about limitations in direct empirical effect sizes, the use of behavioral vs. payoff measures, and the context of specific prediction dimensions.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes** ("efficiency" in the prediction sense):
- Group efficiency as a fraction of the cooperative optimum
- Mean/aggregate group or system payoff (coins generated, welfare, surplus)
- Stationary equilibrium payoffs
- Individual or subgroup payoffs

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (not equivalent to efficiency but important as mediators):
- Contribution/cooperation rate (mean or distribution)
- Prevalence/dominance of cooperators, punishers, defectors
- Rate/frequency of punishment assignments
- Strategy adoption or transition frequencies
- Levels of norm compliance, reputation status, ostracism/exclusion occurrences

**Distinction**: The most useful prediction evidence comes from explicit group efficiency or mean payoff outcomes with and without punishment. However, many papers require inference: e.g., if models show all-defection is replaced by all-cooperation under punishment, efficiency benefits can be deduced; if only contribution rates are reported, the link to efficiency is less direct and not always monotonic (due to punishment costs).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis—Punishment's Effect on Efficiency**

1. **General Direction of Effect**:
   - Enabling (peer or institutional) punishment in standard or spatial PGGs *typically* increases group efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline, especially in settings where the control condition is dominated by defection (e.g., Cressman et al., 2012; Eldakar et al., 2007; Sigmund et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2010; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Gintis, 2000).
   - The effect is *positive and robust* under a wide range of parameters, but not universal.

2. **Moderators and Parameter Sensitivity**:
   - **Punishment Cost and Effectiveness**: The net effect of punishment on efficiency depends critically on the cost to the punisher vs. the magnitude of the penalty to the target (punishment "technology"). Low-cost, high-effectiveness punishment leads to higher efficiency benefits (Okada & Bingham, 2008; Gintis, 2000; Nuño et al., 2010).
   - **Group Size**: Efficiency gains from punishment are more easily achieved in smaller groups; in larger groups, the benefit requires more favorable conditions (Eldakar et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2010; Sigmund et al., 2011).
   - **Number of Rounds/Repeated Interaction**: Longer or indefinite games allow for more sustained efficiency gains through punishment; in one-shot or very short games, the effect is limited (Wolff, 2012; Eldakar et al., 2007; Leimar, 1997).
   - **Spatial/Network Structure**: Spatial or networked populations provide more favorable conditions for punishment to foster efficiency compared to well-mixed populations; they can also help solve second-order free-rider problems (Perc & Szolnoki, 2012; Helbing et al., 2010; Xia et al., 2011).
   - **Retaliation and Anti-social Punishment**: If anti-social punishment (punishing cooperators) or retaliation is possible and not well-controlled, punishment may *reduce* efficiency by preventing cooperation from stabilizing or by triggering costly cycles (Rand et al., 2010; Powers et al., 2012; Janssen & Bushman, 2008; Wolff, 2012).
   - **Voluntary Participation**: When agents can abstain, efficient punishment can support dominance of cooperative strategies, but if abstainers can punish or be punished, cooperation and efficiency gains do not emerge (García & Traulsen, 2012).
   - **Information & Monitoring**: The informativeness and timing of monitoring—whether deviators are observed and punishments are credible—critically shape the effect of punishment (ABREU et al., 1991).
   - **Reputation & Metanorms**: The introduction of reputation tracking, metanorms (punishing non-punishers or rewarding punishers), and social learning mechanisms stabilizes the efficiency gains from punishment and can make them more robust (Henrich & Boyd, 2001; Kendal et al., 2006; Chalub et al., 2006).

3. **Costs of Punishment**:
   - The costs of administering punishment are paid by group members. If these costs are high relative to the benefit from increased cooperation, efficiency improvements can be neutralized or even reversed (Jaffe, 2004; Wolff, 2012). Adaptive or rare but severe punishment minimizes costs and can increase efficiency more effectively than frequent, moderate punishments (Deng et al., 2012; Perc & Szolnoki, 2012).

4. **Reward vs. Punishment**:
   - Reward mechanisms can also increase efficiency, and for a given cost, reward sometimes generates higher group payoff than punishment, especially in low-multiplier (low MPCR) settings (Zhuang et al., 2012). However, in some dynamic or anonymous environments, punishment is more robust at sustaining high efficiency than reward, especially with low entry fees and voluntary participation (Wang et al., 2011).

5. **Negative/Neutral/Conditional Effects**:
   - Several models demonstrate that enabling punishment does not guarantee increased efficiency. Efficiency is *not* improved or can be reduced when: punishment is anti-social (Rand et al., 2010; Powers et al., 2012), costs are too high, games are short or monitoring is weak, or retaliation is feasible (Janssen & Bushman, 2008; Wolff, 2012). At worst, punishment introduces costly cycles, undermining efficiency.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How this literature should inform prediction of treatment efficiency:**

- **If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low—due to defection**—and the treatment enables peer punishment that is salient, affordable (not too costly), and effective (high penalty-to-cost ratio), efficiency should be expected to rise, often substantially, as cooperators can deter defection and shift the group equilibrium toward higher cooperation/payoff.  
- **The exact magnitude depends on:**
  - **Punishment cost:** As cost increases, efficiency gains shrink and may vanish or reverse if costs outweigh cooperative gains (Jaffe, 2004; Perc & Szolnoki, 2012).
  - **Player count/group size:** Larger groups increase the threshold at which punishment is effective; effects may be diluted or require stronger punishment (Sigmund et al., 2011; Xia et al., 2011).
  - **Num rounds/repetition:** The effect is more pronounced in longer, repeated interactions, allowing punishment to influence future behavior (Leimar, 1997; Eldakar et al., 2007).
  - **Presence of retaliation/anti-social punishment:** If the environment allows for anti-social punishment or easy retaliation, anticipate little or no efficiency gain, possibly even a decline (Rand et al., 2010; Powers et al., 2012).
  - **Information/reputation:** Effective monitoring and the ability to observe or infer defections are necessary; poor monitoring weakens punishment's effectiveness (ABREU et al., 1991; De Silva & Sigmund, 2009).
  - **Other mechanisms:** The coexistence of voluntary participation, rewards, or network/clustering often enhances or moderates the gains from punishment.
 
- **Reward-only or no-sanction environments**: If reward is the only option or is combined with punishment, the literature suggests more nuanced effects with potential for higher efficiency under reward, especially if rewards can be targeted and costs are low (Zhuang et al., 2012; Sasaki & Unemi, 2011; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011).

- **Critical caveat**: Positive effects are not universal. Modelers or forecasters should be cautious in settings where anti-social punishment, retaliation, high punishment cost, or poor information make the effect ambiguous or potentially negative.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions (empirically or with detailed simulations):**
- **player_count**: Strong analytical and simulation evidence across many papers; effects often explicitly parameterized.
- **num_rounds**: Directly analyzed; longer/repeated games strengthen the efficiency impact of punishment.
- **mpcr**: Central in almost all payoff models; modulates threshold for cooperation and effectiveness of punishment/reward.
- **punishment_cost / punishment_tech (cost/effectiveness of punishment):** Consistently modeled; critical moderator.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Modeled in reward/punishment comparative analyses.
- **all_or_nothing**: Some directly manipulate—distinguish between continuous and binary contributions.
- **voluntary participation**: Analyzed explicitly in several papers (Sigmund et al., 2011; Xia et al., 2011).
  
**Indirectly informed or contextually discussed:**
- **chat**: Some context/case discussion (e.g., Ostrom, 2009) about its effect on cooperation, but rarely manipulated in payoff models.
- **default_contrib**: Discussed as part of framing or setup, but not a major analytical focus.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Information and monitoring structure addressed conceptually (ABREU et al., 1991; De Silva & Sigmund, 2009), especially in relation to punishment credibility and retaliation; direct manipulation rare.
  
**Effectively missing or only peripherally addressed:**
- **Specifics of interface features** (e.g., chat, show_punishment_id, show_other_summaries)—these are rarely a primary parameter; more often discussed in passing or as part of lab design considerations or broader environmental context.
- **default_contrib**: Very little evidence.
- **Framing, presentation, and implicit cultural features**: Not systematically varied or analyzed; known to impact behavior (Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical effect sizes are rare.** Most results are theoretical or simulation-generated; direct lab or field experiments on the full design space of dimensions are limited if present at all.
- **Behavioral outcomes are frequently conflated with or substituted for payoffs.** Many models and agent-based studies report only the prevalence of cooperators or the existence of certain strategy equilibria, not group efficiency or aggregate payoff.
- **Parameter boundary conditions are not always mapped.** Most models explore stylized regions of parameter space (e.g., low group size, continuous contribution) but may not reflect edge cases, large/nested group structures, or interaction with rarely manipulated features (e.g., chat, real-time information, default choices).
- **Antisocial or retaliatory dynamics are inconsistently incorporated.** Some studies demonstrate that the presence of anti-social punishment or retaliation can reverse or neutralize the efficiency benefits observed elsewhere, but not all models consider these possibilities.
- **Institutional frameworks and external enforcement are often absent.** The findings may not generalize to games with central authorities, legal sanctions, or mechanisms beyond peer punishment/reward.
- **Indirectness of many findings.** A sizeable portion of the literature relies on indirect inference from behavioral metrics or proxy outcomes to efficiency; thus, mapped predictions require caution.
- **Applicability to real-world heterogeneity is limited.** Many models assume homogeneous populations, perfect or simple monitoring, and lack rich variation in behavior, learning, or cultural adaptation.
- **Not all prediction dimensions are equally covered.** Key practical features like interface, framing, communication, or information salience are rarely part of the core analytical model; only a few studies treat these as first-class dimensions.

**Conclusion**: The literature overall strongly supports the expectation that, in public-goods-game-like environments, enabling peer punishment increases efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline, under the right settings (e.g., low cost, high effectiveness, minimal anti-social punishment, sufficient repetition). However, this is contingent on key game design features, and negative or null effects are possible—especially where punishment is mis-targeted, costly, or poorly supported by information or reputation mechanisms. Direct empirical outcome data are sparse, so predictions should be calibrated by theoretical models and parameter sweeps, not point estimates. The evidence is uneven across dimensions, with structural parameters (group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost) being best covered and interface/contextual features comparatively sparse.
