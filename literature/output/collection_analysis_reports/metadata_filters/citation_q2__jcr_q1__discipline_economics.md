# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 24 sources spanning lab, artefactual, and field experiments, as well as theoretical models. Most papers are empirical, with a substantial subset focusing on public goods games (PGGs) or close variants (e.g., common-pool resources, trust games, property rights dilemmas). Several theory papers address mechanism design, institutional context, or strategic behavior in social dilemma games. The breadth of this literature is moderate for the prediction task: it contains strong, directly relevant evidence for the effect of (peer) punishment on group efficiency in repeated PGG settings, but is also supplemented and in some places diluted by adjacent studies on trust games, CPRs, informal punishment, reward mechanisms, and punishment behavior outside public-good contexts. Quantitative effect sizes for the treatment effect of punishment on efficiency are frequent in empirical PGG studies, while several theoretical papers provide comparative statics or equilibrium conditions to explain moderators of punishment efficacy.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**  
- **exact**: Several empirical and theory papers use canonical linear PGGs or very close variants (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Noussair & van Soest, 2014; Stoop et al., 2018).
- **close**: Many others use CPR games with similar payoff structures and group interdependence (Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Xu et al., 2022; de Melo & Piaggio, 2015; Freytag et al., 2014).
- **adjacent/weak**: Some address adjacent environments (trust games, property-rights dilemmas, exclusion games; Kimbrough & Rubin, 2015; Ahn et al., 2016; Libois, 2022).
- **none**: A minority of papers focus entirely outside PGG or variants.

**punishment_or_sanctions**  
- **exact**: The literature robustly addresses peer or institutionally-administered punishment and sanctions, including variations in cost, targeting, mechanism, and observability.
- **close/adjacent**: Some focus primarily on reward or informal norm enforcement, or punishment in settings not structurally equivalent to PGGs (e.g., scapegoating, third-party punishment).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**  
- **exact**: Direct measurement of group efficiency, earnings, or welfare is frequent in core PGG/punishment studies (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019).
- **close/adjacent**: Others report outcomes strongly related to efficiency (group quality, welfare, resource conservation) rather than explicit payoffs.
- **weak/none**: Several only report behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, punishment frequency) or focus exclusively on punishment behavior, not efficiency.

**Summary**: The paper set contains substantial direct evidence for all three relevance dimensions, though not all papers hit all three exactly. Adjacent and contextual studies provide additional interpretive nuance.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based outcomes** (directly informing prediction):  
  - Group efficiency (group payoff as a share of the social optimum) (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Freytag et al., 2014).
  - Welfare, earnings, surplus, success ratio, quality.
  - Static and dynamic (intertemporal) inefficiency (Agranov et al., 2016).
  - Effects of punishment on group outcome distributions (Riehm et al., 2022; Kimbrough & Rubin, 2015).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (informative, but NOT efficiency):  
  - Cooperation and contribution rates, resource extraction, norm compliance.
  - Punishment frequency/type (prosocial vs. antisocial), targeting, willingness to sanction.
  - Reputation effects, motivational crowding, scapegoating, bias in punishment allocation.

The literature is careful but not always complete in distinguishing behavioral from payoff outcomes; many CPR and adjacent studies infer efficiency from changes in extraction or cooperation but only some compute and report efficiency as defined for this prediction task.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis across key themes:**

- **Direction and Moderators of Punishment's Effect**  
  - Peer or institutional punishment can increase, decrease, or have no net effect on efficiency; the direction depends on baseline cooperativeness, implementation (cost, technology, visibility), anti/pro-social punishment balance, group composition, and information structures (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Noussair & van Soest, 2014).
  - Democratic or group-level punishment mechanisms more reliably boost efficiency by limiting antisocial punishment, while individual (unilateral) punishment is prone to mis-targeting and antisocial use (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).
  - High costs of punishment, ineffective or misapplied punishment, and the possibility of extortion or anti-social punishment can negate or reverse efficiency gains (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Barron & Guo, 2021).
  - The effect of punishment is most positive when control efficiency is low. If groups are already highly cooperative, enabling punishment usually reduces net efficiency due to punishment costs (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).

- **Robustness Across Contexts**  
  - Presence of observation noise, weak norms, high group heterogeneity, or opportunity for counter-punishment all diminish the positive impact of punishment (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Noussair & van Soest, 2014).
  - In CPR and property-rights settings, institutionalized or externally imposed punishment (rather than peer punishment) also increases welfare and efficiency, often with stronger or more consistent effects than peer punishment (Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Ahn et al., 2016; Libois, 2022).

- **Role of Design Dimensions**  
  - Key moderators: punishment cost and magnitude, player count, MPCR, group information structures, punishment technology (democratic/individual/dictator), observability, and group heterogeneity.
  - Communication (chat) enhances efficiency independently and can support or substitute for punishment; its effect on prediction is robust and generally positive when present (Noussair & van Soest, 2014; Camera et al., 2013).

- **Theoretical Support for Empirical Patterns**  
  - Theoretical models clarify that punishment's efficiency effect turns on the relative magnitude of deterrence benefit to direct (deadweight) cost, distributional fairness, and possibility of strategic misuse (Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2021; Barron & Guo, 2021; Libois, 2022; Heller & Sieberg, 2010).
  - Equilibrium efficiency is increasing in the strength/efficacy of just deterrent punishment, and decreasing if institutions permit scapegoating, asymmetric application, or costly misapplication.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct guidance for predicting efficiency under punishment (from both empirical and theory papers):**

- When **control efficiency is high** (groups are already cooperative without punishment), enabling peer punishment usually **decreases efficiency** due to punishment costs outweighing marginal deterrent benefits (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).
- When **control efficiency is low**, the introduction of peer punishment will **often increase efficiency**, but the size and even direction of the effect depend on institutional design: punishment cost and magnitude; technology (democratic reduces antisocial punishment, individual is less reliable); observability of contributions; and presence or absence of retaliation or extortion opportunities (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Barron & Guo, 2021).
- **Democratic and group-level punishment**, or externally imposed/monitored sanctions, are more reliably positive for efficiency than purely individualistic peer punishment (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Ahn et al., 2016).
- Key design dimensions to track for prediction:  
  - **Player count**: Larger groups may dilute impact of any one punisher and reduce efficiency gains (Libois, 2022).
  - **MPCR**: Higher MPCR (more benefit per contribution) increases returns to punishment-induced cooperation (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).
  - **All_or_nothing**: In threshold/all-or-nothing settings, punishment focused on achieving targets can boost efficiency if control performance is poor; can backfire in continuous settings (Freytag et al., 2014).
  - **Punishment cost/punishment tech**: Higher cost or weaker/more easily misused punishment diminishes efficacy (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).
  - **Chat**: Communication independently raises efficiency, can sometimes substitute for punishment (Noussair & van Soest, 2014; Camera et al., 2013).

**Qualitative rule-of-thumb based on the literature:**  
- **If control efficiency is high:** Adding peer punishment is *likely* to lower efficiency unless the punishment mechanism is specifically tailored (democratic, scoped by group info, or limited in anti-social possibilities).
- **If control efficiency is low:** Adding a well-designed punishment option (moderate cost, effective technology, anti-social punishment minimized, observability ensured, communication allowed) can plausibly yield substantial efficiency gains.
- Where information, communication, or group composition favor coordinated prosocial enforcement, effects are larger and more reliably positive.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (targeted with payoff outcomes):**  
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `all_or_nothing`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `punishment_exists`  
  (e.g., Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019).

**Indirectly informed (with some payoff/behavioral mapping, but more context-specific):**  
- `chat` (communication)—empirically shown to raise efficiency and moderate punishment effects.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`—group observability, monitoring, or transparency sometimes discussed as moderators but not always directly manipulated or reported with payoff effects.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`—best informed in papers that analyze both punishment and reward (Noussair & van Soest, 2014; Stoop et al., 2018), though these are less frequent.
- `default_contrib`—contextually discussed in terms of framing/cooperation norms, but infrequently tied to efficiency.

**Only contextually discussed or effectively missing:**  
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `reward_magnitude`—rarely directly tied to peer punishment efficiency results.
- `show_punishment_id`—identity disclosure is analyzed as a moderator in a few studies but without systematic payoff reporting.
- Certain dimensions, especially those representing UI, protocol, or summary/identity feedback, are present only in a minority of studies, and often discussed as mechanisms rather than direct experimental contrasts.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavioral vs. Payoff Confounds:** While many papers report behavioral outcomes (contributions, extraction, norm compliance), only a subset computes group efficiency as defined for the prediction task.
- **Variation in Game Structure:** Some "adjacent" studies use trust games, property rights dilemmas, or other non-linear social dilemma settings. Extrapolation from these to standard PGG outcomes requires caution.
- **Antisocial Punishment and Extortion Not Always Modeled:** Individual punishment mechanisms are sometimes prone to antisocial misuse or extortion, which certain papers highlight but not all measure in efficiency terms. Real-world heterogeneity in enforcement power, group norms, or avenues for counter-punishment may yield lower efficiency than predicted from prosocial models.
- **Role of Information and Group Composition:** Efficiency effects depend critically on group information and cooperative baseline, but control efficiency (without punishment) is not uniformly reported or available as a predictor in all studies.
- **Sparse Quantitative Mapping:** Few papers report the exact mapping from control efficiency to treatment efficiency across many design dimensions; most report effects under a relatively narrow set of parameterizations.
- **Underrepresentation of Reward and Mixed Institutions:** The joint effects of reward and punishment, and their interaction with other mechanisms (e.g., communication, institution-building), are less well mapped than peer punishment alone.
- **External Validity:** Some evidence is drawn from field contexts with unique social norms or incentive structures (e.g., Camorra, resource extraction in India/China/Africa), which may not generalize to laboratory PGGs.
- **Design Dimensions Coverage:** Some predictors of interest (e.g., detailed UI/feedback controls, reward technology/cost) are underexplored.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature base strongly supports context-dependent prediction of efficiency effects when punishment is enabled in PGG-like games, with most design dimensions well covered for core laboratory settings. However, accurate prediction requires careful attention to baseline efficiency, punishment institution design, and moderators such as communication, group composition, and observability. Behavioral outcomes are not substitutes for payoff-based efficiency measures, and generalizations from adjacent or field contexts should be made cautiously. Many design dimensions critical for interface or protocol-level prediction are only sparsely or indirectly covered.
