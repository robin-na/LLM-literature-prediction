# Evidence Base

The paper set is reasonably **broad** for the downstream prediction task but is **heterogeneous in its primary focus**:
- There is a substantial mix of **empirical experimental (primarily lab/expt), theoretical, simulation, and some field/observational studies**.
- A **majority** of papers target **public goods games (PGG) or very close variants** with experimentally precise manipulations of punishment institutions.
- The set also includes many **theoretical works** modeling efficiency effects of punishment under varied assumptions about player preferences and game structure.
- Some papers offer “adjacent” evidence from related social dilemmas, networked games, or cooperation games with punishment-like or reward mechanisms.
- There is a **notable subset** that only measures non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates, punishment frequency), which are distinctly separated from efficiency/payoff effects.

# Task Relevance

### 1. `pgg_or_variant`
**Relevance:**
- **Exact:** ~1/3 of the papers are **directly in PGGs** with canonical design dimensions (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Salahshour et al., 2022; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013).
- **Close:** Several use **variants of PGGs or closely related games** like threshold PGGs, ROSCAs, or prisoner's dilemmas with PGG-like punishment phases (van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Koike et al., 2018; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013).
- **Adjacency:** Meaningful findings also come from models of **networked PDGs, snowdrift games, or contests**—mechanistically similar but not canonical PGGs.

### 2. `punishment_or_sanctions`
**Relevance:**
- **Exact:** Many studies have robust, explicit manipulations of **peer punishment, centralized punishment, or both** (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Roberts, 2013).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some papers focus on **reward, exclusion, or reputational mechanisms** that are adjacent to, but not the same as, direct punishment.
- **Weak/Missing:** Many works measure baseline efficiency with **no punishment**, or in adjacent dilemmas, providing only control conditions (e.g., Liu et al., 2014).

### 3. `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
**Relevance:**
- **Exact:** A **subset** of papers report **group efficiency, total payoff, surplus, or well-being**, with some giving the direct ratio defined as efficiency (Kamijo et al., 2020; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Ozono et al., 2016; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Fair number report **mean profits/earnings or similar surrogate group payoffs**, allowing indirect inference of efficiency gains/losses.
- **Weak/None:** Roughly half report only **contribution rates, behavioral strategies, or punishment frequency** with no direct efficiency or payoff variable (e.g., Lefebvre & Stenger, 2020; Paál & Bereczkei, 2015).

**Summary:** The literature covers the core prediction task well for **canonical and close-variant PGGs** with manipulated **punishment institutions** and **measured payoff/efficiency outcomes**. There is less systematic exploration of how different design dimensions moderate the effect, and many studies lack direct efficiency data. **Findings for other social dilemmas, reward-based interventions, or only behavioral (not payoff) outcomes are more contextual or suggestive.**

# Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based Outcomes:**
  - **Group efficiency** (ratio of realized to maximum group welfare) is **explicitly reported in some experimental and theoretical studies** (Kamijo et al., 2020; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).
  - **Total/group payoffs, mean profit, surplus, or earnings** are widely reported, and are frequently mapped to efficiency with caveats.
  - Some papers report **mixed** or ambiguous effects on group payoff depending on noise, institutional design, or group composition (Salahshour et al., 2022; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
  - Several theoretical models provide **explicit conditions for efficiency improvement** and illustrate parameter dependencies (Roberts, 2013; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Ye et al., 2016).

- **Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
  - **Contribution rate, cooperation rate**, and **punishment frequency** are the sole focus of many studies, especially those centered on mechanisms or psychological moderators.
  - **Antisocial punishment** (punishing cooperators), frequency of collective sanctions, or strategic use of punishment are explored behaviorally (Chapkowski, 2021; Kubena et al., 2014; Romano et al., 2024).
  - **Norm compliance, fairness perceptions, and legitimacy** sometimes mediate or moderate observed behavioral responses.

**Distinction:** While increased contributions often co-occur with higher efficiency, this **association is not always monotonic**—punishment can increase contributions but reduce or fail to improve efficiency if costs are high or punishment is antisocial.

# Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of Robust Findings:**

- **Punishment increases efficiency in standard PGGs:** When **punishment is strong/effective and not too costly**, enabling punishment reliably increases **group efficiency** compared to control/no-punishment, sometimes dramatically (Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Roberts, 2013).
    - The efficiency gain is **most marked when baseline (control) efficiency is low**, and often hinges on the **punishment cost-to-impact ratio** (Roberts, 2013).
    - **External, institutionally enforced punishment** outperforms weak, voluntary, or endogenous punishment, especially in non-cooperative groups (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013).

- **Null or negative effects under certain conditions:**
    - If **punishment is noisy, antisocial, or collective rather than targeted**, group efficiency may **not improve and can deteriorate** (Salahshour et al., 2022; van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Koike et al., 2018; Romano et al., 2024).
    - **Antisocial punishment** (punishing cooperators) and strategic, competitive punishment can **erode efficiency**, especially under conditions of punishment noise, low legitimacy, or status competition (Paál & Bereczkei, 2015; Romano et al., 2024).

- **Contextual moderators:**
    - The **effect of punishment is sensitive to game parameters:** effectiveness (cost/impact), institutional design (peer vs. pool punishment), and degree of noise/uncertainty (Salahshour et al., 2022; Roberts, 2013).
    - **Ineffective or non-costly punishment** (that is not perceived as legitimate or is easily avoided) shows negligible effect on efficiency (Koike et al., 2018).
    - Structure such as **reward mechanisms, exit options, or collective choice** can produce equal or larger increases in efficiency than punishment alone (Kamijo et al., 2020; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013).

- **Generalization to adjacent games/theories:**
    - Theoretical models suggest **thresholds** for punishment effectiveness: if below the critical threshold, punishment does not improve efficiency, and in some contexts can destabilize cooperation (Ye et al., 2016; Honjo & Kubo, 2020).
    - **Population structure, reputation mechanisms, and collective enforcement** can either enhance or substitute for punishment in maintaining high efficiency, especially in group-structured or networked populations.

**Contradictions/Ambiguity:**
- In some empirical cases, competitive use of punishment, collective sanctions, or identity-driven punishment can yield **no gain or negative effects** on group efficiency (Romano et al., 2024; Chapkovski, 2021).
- In PGGs with **inefficient public goods (MPCR<1)**, punishment does **not increase efficiency**, and only reward/complementary mechanisms can increase final payoffs (Kamijo et al., 2020).

# Prediction Guidance

**Using This Literature for Predicting Treatment Efficiency:**

- **When control efficiency is known:** 
    - **If baseline efficiency is low** and the game is a canonical PGG (continuous/all-or-nothing contributions, 3–12 players, typical MPCR range), **enabling effective peer punishment with a favorable cost-to-impact ratio (>1:2 or 1:3) is highly likely to increase efficiency**. The magnitude approaches the social optimum in strong-punishment, low-noise designs (Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
    - The **increment in efficiency** is **largest in non-cooperative groups** or low-baseline contexts; efficiency post-treatment can be predicted to move toward the optimal range if institutional punishment is robust (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).

- **Risk of null or negative effect:**
    - **If punishment is weak, costly, or highly noisy** (e.g., stochastic in impact, prone to antisocial use, or if monitoring is error-prone), **efficiency may not improve or can worsen** (Salahshour et al., 2022; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
    - In **games with very low MPCR (inefficient PGGs)**, enabling punishment typically **does not increase efficiency**—the cost of punishment outweighs cooperative gains (Kamijo et al., 2020).
    - **Collective punishment or exclusion mechanisms** may be more/less effective depending on group composition, legitimacy, and social context.

- **Design dimensions are critical moderators**: 
    - Game design dimensions like **punishment cost, punishment impact, MPCR, player count**, and **punishment technology** (peer vs. pool, deterministic vs. noisy) **directly moderate the direction and magnitude of the punishment effect on efficiency**.
    - **Other dimensions** (information feedback, chat, identity reveal, round-number transparency) have less direct, but still nontrivial, effects when they affect perceived legitimacy or targeting of punishment.

**General Principle:**
- **Punishment is most effective at increasing efficiency when:** it is **well-targeted, strong relative to cost, not antisocial, and the monitoring environment is accurate**.
- **Reward, exit, and collective choice** can serve as alternate or complementary institution designs achieving similar or better increases in efficiency.
- **The effect of enabling punishment is not universally positive or monotonic**—it is **highly conditional** on these design and context factors.

# Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
1. **player_count**: Extensively tested (n=3–12). Theory indicates group size can diminish or amplify punishment efficacy, but effects are nonlinear (Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
2. **num_rounds**: Most experiments are multi-round (10–30 rounds); longer games allow for reputational and punishment dynamics to take effect.
3. **mpcr**: Strong moderator; effectiveness of punishment declines in very low-MPCR/inefficient PGGs (Kamijo et al., 2020).
4. **all_or_nothing**: Both continuous and binary-action variants are tested. No categorical findings favoring one over the other for punishment effect.
5. **punishment_cost/punishment_tech**: **Key variables**. Cost-to-impact ratio and deterministic vs. stochastic punishment drive effects on efficiency and its directionality (Roberts, 2013; Salahshour et al., 2022).
6. **reward_exists**/**reward_cost**/**reward_tech**: Reward can substitute for or outperform punishment under certain conditions; efficiency effects of reward are robust in some settings (Kamijo et al., 2020; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013).
7. **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Sometimes varied or specified, but their effects on efficiency are indirect unless they alter targeting or legitimacy.
8. **chat**: Rarely varied systematically in high-precision PGG-punishment papers; if present, typically absent in strong-institution conditions (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).
9. **default_contrib**: Contextually discussed, not systematically manipulated in punishment-efficacy studies.

**Indirectly Informed or Sparse Dimensions:**
- **punishment_tech**: While often specified as peer punishment, treatments with collective punishment, exclusion, or centralized punishment provide indirect evidence about mechanisms and effects (Koike et al., 2018; Ozono et al., 2016).
- **reward dimensions**: Inform reward treatment efficiency more than punishment, but several papers provide direct reward vs. punishment comparisons (Kamijo et al., 2020; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013).
- **default_contribProp**: Very little direct evidence connecting framing to punishment effectiveness on efficiency.
- **show_punishment_id**: Some studies specify anonymous vs. identified punishment, with limited direct evidence for efficiency effects except where targeting is crucial (Kamijo et al., 2020).

**Missing or Only Contextually Discussed:**
- Parameters such as **information granularity**, **player heterogeneity**, and **cognitive moderators** (e.g., induced confusion, cognitive load) are sometimes discussed, but not systematically mapped to efficiency outcomes with punishment.

# Important Limitations

1. **Overrepresentation of canonical lab PGGs:** Most precision evidence comes from **multi-round, fixed-group, deterministic peer-punishment experiments**. Generalizability to other social dilemmas or real-world institutional forms is limited.
2. **Limited variation of design dimensions across studies:** Few experiments **factorially manipulate** punishment cost, player count, communication, or other vital design parameters. **Extrapolation to new parameter regions is hazardous**.
3. **Payoff vs. behavioral outcomes confusion:** Many studies report only **behavioral responses, not efficiency**, making it crucial not to infer efficiency gains solely from increased contributions (especially when punishment is costly or antisocial).
4. **Heterogeneity in reporting efficiency:** Some studies only provide group mean payoffs or earnings, not normalized efficiency, complicating cross-study effect size comparisons.
5. **Context dependence:** The effect of punishment on efficiency is **highly contingent** on the **effectiveness/cost ratio, game structure, monitoring accuracy, and social context** (competition, gender composition, resource scarcity).
6. **Reward and hybrid mechanisms:** There is **robust evidence that reward, or reward combined with punishment/exclusion, can outperform punishment alone**. Hence, predictions focused only on punishment **may underestimate the potential for efficiency gains from alternate institutions**.
7. **Neglect of dynamic or meta-institutional effects:** Longer-term dynamics (e.g., withdrawal, learning, institutional evolution) and multi-level or networked contexts are less thoroughly addressed.
8. **Selection/Publication Bias:** Most included papers, especially empirical lab studies, may be more likely to report positive or theoretically expected effects of punishment, possibly overestimating general impact.

---

**In summary:**  
This literature supports strong but **highly moderated predictions**: **Enabling punishment in canonical, repeated PGGs with effective and targeted punishment increases efficiency over control, especially when the baseline is low and antisocial punishment is minimal. Negative or null effects are observed when punishment is weak, costly, noisy, or misdirected.** Prediction should be dimension-conditional, and extrapolation across big changes in design parameters should be cautious. **Non-payoff outcomes cannot be used as direct efficiency proxies, and many design dimensions are underexplored.**
