# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists primarily of theory papers (4/8) with the remainder being empirical, either laboratory experiments (2/8) or empirical/observational studies (2/8). Theoretical work is relatively sophisticated, frequently referencing evolutionary, psychological, and institutional mechanisms underlying altruism and punishment. Of the empirical papers, one (Brick & Visser, 2010) is a public-goods-variant experiment with institutional sanctions, while another (Xiao & Bicchieri, 2012) is a dictator game study without punishment. Overall, the coverage is moderately broad regarding theoretical mechanisms but narrow in terms of direct, empirical, payoff-based evidence on the predictive task (i.e., the efficiency impact of enabling peer punishment in PGGs with particular game design features).

# 2) Task Relevance

We assess three dimensions of target relevance:

- **pgg_or_variant:** The set contains several papers with exact (Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012), close (Brick & Visser, 2010), or adjacent (Woodcock & Heath, 2002; Rosas, 2008 [multilevel selection]; Johansson et al., 2003) coverage of public goods games or related social dilemmas. Only one (Xiao & Bicchieri, 2012) is weakly relevant, and one (Clavien, 2012) is not relevant.
    - **Relevance level:** moderate, with strong theoretical PGG coverage but limited direct empirical PGG-plus-punishment-plus-efficiency data.
- **punishment_or_sanctions:** Five papers address punishment or sanctions (exact/close), but with different forms: peer punishment (Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012), institutional sanctions (Brick & Visser, 2010), and ostracism/exclusion mechanisms (Woodcock & Heath, 2002; Rosas, 2008 [multilevel selection]). Two papers have adjacent or no relevance (Johansson et al., 2003; Clavien, 2012), and one (Xiao & Bicchieri, 2012) does not address punishment.
    - **Relevance level:** moderate for peer or institutional punishment as a mechanism but not always in PGG context.
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** Only two theory papers (Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012) and one empirical paper (Brick & Visser, 2010; adjacent) substantively address efficiency or closely related group payoff outcomes. Other papers focus on behavioral or attitudinal outcomes (e.g., frequency of cooperation, norm compliance, persistence of altruism) rather than group payoff or efficiency.
    - **Relevance level:** limited, as only a minority directly measure or predict efficiency.

**In summary:** The evidence set is most relevant for theory-driven prediction in standard PGGs with peer punishment enabled, but direct empirical support for payoff effects of peer punishment in actual experimental PGGs is sparse.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (as required for the prediction task):**
    - *Efficiency* (group payoff as a fraction of the full-cooperation maximum) or direct total group payoff appears as the main outcome in Rosas (2008) and Frey & Rusch (2012) (both theory/contextualized by data), and is adjacent in Brick & Visser (2010) (who focus on compliance and earnings but not efficiency per se).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
    - *Contribution rate, cooperation, norm compliance,* and *altruist prevalence* are frequently discussed (Woodcock & Heath, 2002; Rosas, 2008 [multilevel]; Johansson et al., 2003; Clavien, 2012; Xiao & Bicchieri, 2012). These inform cooperative dynamics and behavioral mechanisms but are not equivalent to efficiency.
    - *Punishment/exclusion behaviors* and the psychological/emotional basis for cooperation are also examined.

**Explicit distinction:** Only Rosas (2008) and Frey & Rusch (2012) use efficiency or total payoff as primary outcomes, while others provide (at best) indirect or contextually indicative behavioral results.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the most relevant papers:

- **Direct costly peer punishment**:
    - *Short-run effect*: Peer punishment in standard PGGs frequently results in lower efficiency than the control condition (no punishment), especially in games of short duration or where punishment is costly and not highly effective, as both punishers and punished lose resources (Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012).
    - *Long-run effect*: With repeated interactions (many rounds), fixed groupings, and effective punishment (i.e., high impact per unit cost), the efficiency of punishment treatments improves over time. As cooperation stabilizes, punishment expenditures decline, and efficiency with punishment can surpass control (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
    - *Over-/under-punishment risk*: There is a risk of *over-punishment* (wasting resources when cooperation is high and few defectors are targeted by many punishers) and *under-punishment* (when defectors are numerous). This risk varies with group size, composition, and punishment technology (Rosas, 2008).
- **Punishment technology**:
    - *Exclusion/ostracism*: When defectors can be excluded or effectively prevented from free-riding (as opposed to just being fined), group efficiency can be more stable and higher, provided group cohesion can be maintained (Rosas, 2008; Woodcock & Heath, 2002 [simulations; not payoff measured]).
    - *Institutional sanctions*: Automatic sanctions (tax/fine for under-contribution) are highly effective at achieving compliance with group targets, but may crowd out voluntary over-contribution. Evidence for efficiency is indirect, but high compliance rates suggest improved efficiency versus voluntary or communication-only groups (Brick & Visser, 2010).
- **Design features affecting punishment’s efficiency impact**:
    - *Time horizon*: The longer the game (more rounds), the greater the likelihood that punishment increases efficiency overall (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
    - *Group structure*: Stable (partner) groups support sustained efficiency gains from punishment, unlike stranger-matching (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
    - *Punishment cost/effectiveness*: Lower cost and/or higher effectiveness of punishment increases the chance for efficiency improvement (Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012).
    - *Communication*: Enables partial cooperation but less reliable than direct punishment or institutional sanctions (Brick & Visser, 2010).
- **Behavioral mechanisms**:
    - *Reciprocity norms, adaptation*: Humans may adapt their punishment behaviors to maximize efficiency, switching punishment strategies based on group context (Rosas, 2008 [theory]).
    - *Altruist stability, norm compliance*: Papers focusing on non-payoff outcomes argue that punishment makes cooperation/altruism more robust, but do not link this to measured payoffs (Woodcock & Heath, 2002; Rosas, 2008 [multilevel]).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Dimension-informed prediction:**
    - If the control game is inefficient (low baseline cooperation), and the design features large numbers of rounds, stable (partner) groupings, and effective, not overly costly, punishment mechanisms, enabling peer punishment is likely to increase overall group efficiency—sometimes making it surpass that of the control (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
    - If the game is short, features high punishment costs (relative to effect), or lacks group stability, enabling punishment often results in lower efficiency than the control due to direct punishment costs (Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012).
    - The *type* of punishment mechanism (direct costly v. exclusionary or institutional) matters: exclusion-based or effective institutional mechanisms are typically predicted to result in higher efficiency than direct costly peer punishment (Rosas, 2008; Brick & Visser, 2010).
    - Communication improves outcomes, but not to the level of punishment-based compliance (Brick & Visser, 2010).
- **Parameter indirectness:** Most evidence is theoretical, simulation-based, or based on adjacent outcomes. There is a lack of empirical payoff data for many design features (e.g., chat, reward options, informational feedback).
- **Baselining value of control efficiency:** The literature supports using the relative efficiency of the control condition as a baseline and considering the above dimensions as modifiers when punishment is enabled.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly or strongly informed:**
- `player_count` (group size): Discussed in theory and empirical work as influencing efficiency and punishment dynamics (Rosas, 2008; Brick & Visser, 2010).
- `num_rounds`: Directly identified as a key moderator for punishment’s long-term efficiency effect (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Central to both mechanism theorizing and payoff predictions (Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Brick & Visser, 2010).
- `mpcr` (returns to contribution): Included in empirical work (Brick & Visser, 2010), although not always in the context of efficiency outcomes.
- `all_or_nothing`: Addressed as an experimental condition affecting cooperation and sanction compliance in at least one study (Brick & Visser, 2010).

**Indirectly or contextually discussed:**
- `chat`: Explored as communication, which is found to increase cooperation but is less effective than sanctions (Brick & Visser, 2010; Xiao & Bicchieri, 2012).
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`: Only contextually present in studies focusing on information-seeking (Xiao & Bicchieri, 2012), not analyzed for payoff outcomes.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Not primary subjects in the sampled papers; discussion is absent or only peripheral.

**Effectively missing:**
- `default_contrib`: Not discussed or varied in the literature set.
- `reward_magnitude`, `punishment_magnitude`: Not systematically manipulated or analyzed in the context of efficiency outcomes.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical sparsity on payoff outcomes:** Only a minority of papers analyze or report efficiency or group payoff under both control and punishment conditions, and fewer still do so for multiple design parameters relevant to prediction.
- **Predominantly theoretical mechanisms:** Much of the guidance comes from theory or simulation, rather than from systematic empirical data across the full range of design dimensions.
- **Limited treatment of variant designs:** Institutional punishment (e.g., tax/fine regimes) appears only in one adjacent empirical study, limiting predictive insight about peer versus institutional mechanisms.
- **Insufficient reporting on effects of certain dimensions:** Rewards, dynamic information provision, contribution defaults, and identity visibility are scarcely addressed, and variations in these dimensions cannot be reliably linked to efficiency effects.
- **Ambiguity in non-standard PGGs:** Results for non-standard games (e.g., all-or-nothing, heterogeneous returns) are largely inferential or based on tangentially related outcomes (e.g., compliance or norm following, not efficiency).
- **Behavioral vs. payoff conflation risk:** Several papers conflate cooperation/contribution or altruism with social welfare or efficiency, but only a subset measure the actual payoff impact.

**Summary:** The literature provides high-relevance theoretical guidance on how major design dimensions may affect the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-like games, particularly for standard-peer-punishment laboratory settings with varying group size, time horizon, and punishment type/cost. However, direct empirical support for these predictions is limited, especially for the full 14-dimension design space and for non-standard punishment or reward configurations. Care should be taken when extrapolating beyond those features most directly informed by theory and data (player count, rounds, punishment cost/technology, and group structure).
