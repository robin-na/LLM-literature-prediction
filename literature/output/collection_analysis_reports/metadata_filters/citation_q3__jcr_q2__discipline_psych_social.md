# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 47 works encompassing laboratory experiments (empirical/experimental), theory/modeling papers, and several context/field-based reports. The majority are empirical lab experiments, especially with public goods games (PGGs) or close variants, supplemented by theoretical models (especially of punishment/reward mechanisms) and a smaller number of field studies.

The set is **broad in scope**, touching on a variety of sanctioning regimes (peer punishment, pool punishment, centralized punishment, reward, commitment, etc.), payoff structures (linear PGG, step-level/threshold, CPR, n-person dilemmas, networked games), and contextual moderators (social identity, group structure, information feedback, communication, intergroup competition, etc.). However, **not all papers directly address efficiency or payoff as outcomes**, and many adjacent or theoretical contributions focus on contribution/cooperation behaviors or mechanisms, rather than direct efficiency measurement.

Empirical findings are strongest in traditional repeated linear PGGs and some close variants. Theory papers generally support or frame the findings from a mechanism perspective, but not all are directly mapped to empirical efficiency effects.

# 2) Task Relevance

## pgg_or_variant
- **exact**: A large subset of papers implement standard linear PGGs or direct variants, e.g., Gürerk et al. (2009), Gürerk (2013), Qin & Wang (2013), Fatas et al. (2010).
- **close**: Several papers use related but non-standard designs (e.g., common pool resource, team production, all-or-nothing, threshold/step-level games).
- **adjacent/weak**: Others use dyadic games, dictator/ultimatum, trust games, or theoretical models focused on non-PGG environments.

## punishment_or_sanctions
- **exact**: Multiple studies directly enable/disable punishment or implement peer/leader/centralized punishment in the game (e.g., Gürerk et al., Qin & Wang, Wu et al., Fatas et al., Aakre et al.).
- **close**: Centralized punishment/rewards, random punishment, or pool punishment fall under close relevance.
- **adjacent**: Some focus on approval/disapproval, commitment, gossip, third-party punishment, or non-material sanctions that deviate from standard peer punishment.

## efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome
- **exact**: A focused set measure efficiency, group payoff, or surplus as a primary or explicit outcome (e.g., Gürerk, Qin & Wang, Fatas et al., Eisenkopf & Bächtiger).
- **close**: Proxies like rate of public good provision or results interpretable in terms of group welfare are used in some.
- **adjacent/weak/none**: Many report behavioral/contribution rates, norm compliance, punishment frequency, or psychological outcomes, but not efficiency or payoff figures.

### Overall
The evidence base is **strong for the direct effect of punishment on efficiency in repeated PGGs or well-mapped variants**, and much weaker for environments that are highly institutionalized, field-based, or do not report on payoff/efficiency directly.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**: Efficiency (group payoff as a proportion of full cooperation), average payoff, surplus, total group earnings, and provision rates in threshold games are directly measured in several studies.
    - E.g., Gürerk et al. (2009, 2013), Qin & Wang (2013), Fatas et al. (2010), Eisenkopf & Bächtiger (2013).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**: Contribution rates, full cooperation rates, punishment frequency/intensity, norm compliance, emotional or motivational states, trust, reciprocity, approval/disapproval, and gossip effects dominate the reporting in many other works.
    - E.g., Bicskei et al. (2016), Wu et al. (2020), Allgaier et al. (2020), Fabbri & Carbonara (2017).
- **Hybrid/mixed**: Some papers infer efficiency from changes in contribution or provision rates but do not compute efficiency directly (e.g., Guillen et al. 2007, threshold games).

**Important distinction:** Outcomes on contributions and behavioral norms are often positively affected by punishment, even where efficiency does not improve (or may worsen due to direct punishment costs).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across directly relevant papers (using efficiency or payoff):

- **Enabling punishment generally increases contributions, but its effect on efficiency depends on punishment cost/structure**. In many PGG designs, peer or centralized punishment leads to higher contributions, but the associated costs can offset or even outweigh the resulting gains in group payoff, leading to a small or non-existent improvement in efficiency (Gürerk et al., 2009; Fatas et al., 2010). In some cases, non-punishment (reward-based) institutions lead to even higher efficiency, as rewards do not extract as much cost from the group (Gürerk et al., 2009).
- **Design of the punishment mechanism matters greatly**. High cost or indiscriminate punishment (e.g., random or antisocial punishment) can actually reduce efficiency (Fatas et al., 2010; Xiao & Kunreuther, 2016). Efficiency gains are maximized when punishment probability and severity are well-calibrated (Qin & Wang, 2013).
- **Contextual moderators**:
    - Access to **social history or social learning boosts the efficiency of punishment institutions** (Gürerk, 2013).
    - **Communication**—particularly when paired with punishment—substantially enhances efficiency, more so than punishment alone (Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013).
    - The presence of **both participation and compliance enforcement** is required for punishment to yield efficiency gains in multi-stage public goods settings (Aakre et al., 2016).
    - **Group structure and population partitioning** support the effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency (Noailly et al., 2009; POLLOCK, 1988).
- **Form of punishment (centralized vs. peer)**: Centralized or leader-administered punishment/coordination schemes often produce higher efficiency or more robust provision, especially in step-level/threshold games (Gürerk et al., 2009; Guillen et al., 2007).
- **Reward mechanisms** often increase efficiency more than punishment mechanisms, despite punishment being more effective at boosting contributions (Gürerk et al., 2009; Drouvelis et al., 2017).
- **Heterogeneity and social identity**: Out-group environments may see more frequent punishment, which can reduce efficiency due to increased punishment costs (Bicskei et al., 2016).
- **Information feedback, visibility, and design transparency**: These factors (e.g., showing others’ contributions, allowing chat, displaying round info) can interact with punishment to influence outcomes, but the direct impact on efficiency is less consistently measured.

A substantial number of other studies demonstrate increased **cooperation or contributions** with punishment, but do not report on efficiency, so they only indirectly support the efficiency prediction task.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the evidence:

- **Baseline control efficiency is a necessary but insufficient predictor** of treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency.
    - The **increment in efficiency from enabling peer punishment** is primarily determined by the **cost structure and targeting of the punishment**, as well as the returns to cooperation (mpcr) and the availability of complementary mechanisms (e.g., communication, social history, centralized enforcement) (Gürerk et al., 2009, 2013; Qin & Wang, 2013; Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013).
- **Most direct mapping**: When the punishment is moderate in cost, well-targeted (not random/indiscriminate), and not subject to retaliation or antisocial use, treatment efficiency is typically higher than control (no punishment).
    - However, **if punishment cost is high relative to its impact or is frequently misapplied**, group efficiency may not improve—and may decline relative to control—despite higher observable cooperation (Fatas et al., 2010; Xiao & Kunreuther, 2016).
- **Prediction should account for design details**:
    - **mpcr**, **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech** (who can punish whom, at what cost/impact), **communication** (chat), and **history information** are the best-informed dimensions for calibrating expected efficiency change.
    - **Reward_exists** and related variables must be noted: reward institutions may outperform punishment in efficiency terms.
- For **step-level/threshold PGGs**, centralized punishment can substantially increase provision rates (Guillen et al., 2007); efficiency gains depend on whether the increased provision offsets the punishment mechanism’s cost.
- If **social learning mechanisms or communication** are present together with punishment, expect a **larger positive shift in efficiency** (Gürerk, 2013; Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013).
- **If the control efficiency is already high** (near the cooperative optimum), enabling punishment may produce **little or even negative efficiency gain**, especially if punishment is costly or misapplied.
- In **large, structured, or spatially clustered groups**, punishment is more likely to produce and sustain efficient outcomes (Noailly et al., 2009; POLLOCK, 1988).

**Caution:** Many papers with positive effects on contributions do not report group efficiency, and some (notably, Bicskei et al. (2016), Wu et al. (2020), etc.) only inform behavioral or psychological mechanisms, not payoffs. Do not assume a positive efficiency effect without direct evidence.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Explicitly varied/examined in many studies (Gürerk et al., Wu et al., Noailly et al., etc.).
- `num_rounds`: Most repeated-game designs specify number of rounds.
- `mpcr`: Carefully reported and central to the mechanism in almost all PGG or variant studies.
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`: Key determinants of efficiency effect, frequently parameterized and analyzed.
- `chat`: Some studies examine explicit or enforced communication.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Reward mechanisms are manipulated or compared with punishment.
- `all_or_nothing`: Present in several designs (often noted as "binary" or "discrete choice").
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Occasionally manipulated to study the visibility and transparency of play.

**Indirectly/contextually discussed:**
- `default_contrib`: Explicit manipulation rare, but framing effects are discussed in adjacent papers.
- `show_punishment_id`: Occasionally present as part of punishment transparency, but less systematically studied.

**Sparse or effectively missing:**
- None of the papers systematically cover **all** dimensions; most are thin on `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id`, and nuanced forms of visibility or transparency (except as context).
- Reward-specific parameters (`reward_magnitude`, etc.) are less frequently specified except in explicit reward institution studies.
- Cross-dimensional interactions (e.g., chat × punishment, all-or-nothing × reward) are often not separately estimated.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse direct efficiency data**: Although contributions and cooperation rates are widely reported, direct computation of efficiency (as defined) is less common.
- **Generality to non-lab settings is unproven:** Many findings are lab-specific; field and naturally occurring group settings are relatively underrepresented, limiting prediction validity outside the lab.
- **Heterogeneity of punishment mechanisms:** There is variation in peer vs. centralized, individual vs. collective, targeted vs. random punishment, making cross-study generalization hazardous.
- **Parameter granularity:** Many design dimensions (e.g., communication, visibility of identities, reward co-existence, framing) are only partially explored or manipulated—making dimension-specific predictions difficult when these features are present or absent.
- **Non-payoff outcome dominance:** A large proportion of findings relate to non-payoff behavioral mechanisms, which are not directly translatable to efficiency predictions.
- **Limited insight into punitive misuse/abuse:** Antisocial or retaliatory punishment, which can undermine efficiency, is identified as a risk but not systematically measured or predicted.
- **Control condition variability:** Baseline/no-punishment efficiency varies substantially across studies (due to design/non-design factors), complicating the mapping from control to treatment efficiency.
- **Interaction effects rarely estimated:** Most studies examine single-dimension manipulations (e.g., only punishment cost, only chat), limiting insight into combinatorial effects.

---

**Summary:** The literature provides strong, directly relevant evidence that the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in public goods games depends centrally on punishment cost/effectiveness, game returns, group structure, and institutional complements (communication, history). However, positive effects on contribution/cooperation do not always translate into efficiency gains due to the costliness or misuse of punishment. For high-quality prediction, attention must be paid to the exact design details—not merely the presence of a punishment option. Several prediction dimensions are well-covered (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, communication, reward), but some (visibility of punishment, contribution framing) are thinly represented. Efficiency prediction is especially challenging where only behavioral or psychological outcomes are reported, as efficiency may actually decrease even with higher cooperation if punishment costs are high. Generalization outside repeated lab PGGs is limited due to context, outcome, and design diversity across studies.
