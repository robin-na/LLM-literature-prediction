# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is extensive and broad, with a heavy empirical (primarily laboratory experimental) emphasis, substantial coverage of standard linear Public Goods Games (PGGs), and a noteworthy representation of both close PGG variants (e.g., common pool resource games, trust games) and more distant adjacent paradigms. The focus is primarily on direct empirical demonstration of treatment outcomes (i.e., comparison of efficiency with/without punishment), but there is also coverage of mechanism arguments, cross-context field trials, and variations in institutional, social, and behavioral moderators.

Most evidence is concentrated on small- to mid-sized anonymous groups (3–5 players), repeated games (typically 10–20 rounds), and centralized or peer punishment technologies, with detailed parameterizations of marginal per-capita return (MPCR), punishment cost, and punishment effectiveness. The set includes high-salience cases of design variations (e.g., endogenous vs. exogenous institution choice, punishment effectiveness, communication, ostracism, counterpunishment, heterogeneity, contextual culture).

The breadth, diversity of experimental manipulations, and quantity of directly measured payoff-based outcomes make this a rich, though complex, evidence base for downstream efficiency prediction in PGG-like paradigms.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant**
- **Exact relevance:** The majority of core papers use exact PGG frameworks (linear VCMs, continuous or discrete contributions), with group sizes, MPCR, and other institutional details specified to match canonical PGG treatment-control contrasts.
- **Close relevance:** Substantial evidence comes from CPR games, ostracism/exclusion games, or closely related partner choice and trust/investment games. Mechanisms and outcomes remain comparable but with structural nuances (e.g., partner selection, resource extraction).
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Some studies discuss behavioral sanctions, information and reputation mechanisms, or single-round or two-player dilemma games, which supply helpful context but do not directly match the canonical multi-person, repeated PGG structure.
- **None:** A small fraction of studies is included for context alone and do not bear on the task.

**Punishment or Sanctions**
- **Exact relevance:** The dominant focus is on costly, explicit, peer-administered punishment after contribution choices, with nuanced exploration of cost, effectiveness, and institutional form.
- **Close relevance:** A significant number assess exclusion, ostracism, centralized or third-party punishment, or legal/leader-administered sanctions (functioning as punishment or deterrence), and examine endogenous institution formation mechanisms, all directly relevant to broadening the predictive scope.
- **Adjacent:** Informal or non-monetary punishments (disapproval, social message, gossip), as well as design variations including counterpunishment, consent/veto-based punishment, and cultural/field differences, are explored in several papers.
- **Weak/None:** Control- or no-punishment-only studies are included only for control efficiency baselines.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**
- **Exact relevance:** A large subset measures group efficiency or average total earnings relative to the socially optimal benchmark—efficiency as strictly defined for prediction purposes.
- **Close:** Total group earnings, surplus, or direct comparison of realized payoffs to maximum possible provide close proxies for efficiency.
- **Adjacent:** Some studies primarily report contribution or cooperation rates, behavioral indicators, or non-monetary sanctions, providing indirect evidence about likely efficiency effects but without direct measurement.
- **Weak/None:** Many behavioral outcome–centric studies are included for mechanism insight but do not directly support quantitative efficiency prediction.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes ("Efficiency-like")**
- Direct group efficiency (aggregate earnings as fraction of full cooperation; e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007).
- Total group earnings, welfare, or coins generated (Sutter et al., 2010; Kosfeld et al., 2009; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
- Surplus or welfare surplus (Abbink et al., 2010).
- Efficiency gains/losses from enabling punishment or institution formation (Markussen et al., 2014; Casari & Luini, 2009).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Not Efficiency)**
- Contribution rates (almost universally measured, but not always translating directly to efficiency).
- Cooperation rates, norm compliance, punishment frequency/amount.
- Targeting of punishment, patterns of anti-social vs. pro-social punishment.
- Emotional and social-psychological proxies (e.g., anger, shame) and survey measures.
- Group formation, exclusion, and partner choice behaviors.

**Distinction:** Many studies show increased cooperation or punishment with no clear positive effect on efficiency, highlighting that contribution improvements can be offset by punishment costs, retaliation, or antisocial sanctioning.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Pattern:**
- Enabling peer punishment (relative to control) in canonical repeated PGGs typically sustains higher contributions and increases group efficiency, but the magnitude and even direction of efficiency effects depend critically on punishment costs, effectiveness, targeting, counterpunishment dynamics, and institutional/pragmatic context (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007; Arechar et al., 2018).

**Positive Efficiency Effects (Under Most Laboratory PGG Conditions):**
- Peer punishment yields a robust, sizeable increase in efficiency when:
    - Punishment cost is moderate/high-impact (e.g., for every unit spent, large penalty imposed), and
    - Punishment is mainly targeted at defectors/free-riders (Masclet et al., 2003; Fudenberg & Pathak, 2010; Arechar et al., 2018),
    - Antisocial punishment and cycles of retaliation are rare,
    - Information on group behavior is available, but punisher identities are often anonymous.
- Efficiency effect is typically time-dependent: initially, punishment (with cost) reduces efficiency, but as cooperation stabilizes, the efficiency gain outweighs the cost (Sefton et al., 2007; Fehr & Gächter, 2000).

**Limitations, Negative, or Ambiguous Effects:**
- If punishment is cheap but not well-targeted, or if antisocial or counterpunishment/cycles emerge, efficiency gains disappear or may become negative, even as contributions rise (Nikiforakis, 2008; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).
- In cultural or field contexts where spiteful or antisocial punishment of cooperators is frequent, or where punishment legitimacy is low, enabling punishment can *reduce* efficiency (Vollan, 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Fehr, Hoff, & Kshetramade, 2008).
- Institutional structure is crucial: strong efficiency gains where punishment is majority-voted, consensus-based, or restricts anti-social punishment (Ertan et al., 2009; Casari & Luini, 2009; Tyran & Feld, 2006).
- Endogenous institution adoption (voting/choice) amplifies positive efficiency effects; exogenous, imposed punishment regimes—especially when not deterrent—can be ineffective or crowd out cooperation (Sutter et al., 2010; Markussen et al., 2014).
- Centralized, delegated, or leader-sanctioned punishment, when neutrally administered, enhances efficiency more than peer-to-peer; but (field) leaders driven by equality only or antisocial motives can undermine gains (Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015).
- Punishment effectiveness is shaped by heterogeneity in returns, information and feedback environment, group composition, and possibility of counterpunishment/feuds.
- Communication, when available (especially open chat or credible promise channels), interacts with or even substitutes for punishment in increasing efficiency (Bochet et al., 2006; Oprea et al., 2014).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Prediction of Treatment Efficiency (Punishment Enabled) from Control and Design Dimensions:**
- **Baseline:** Efficiency in PGG controls (no punishment) is typically far below the social optimum due to contribution decay/free riding.
- **With punishment enabled:** Efficiency is almost always increased, often substantially, but the increment can be predicted primarily by:
    - **Punishment cost/effectiveness:** High-impact punishment (punishment_tech > cost) yields greater efficiency gains; if cost approaches or exceeds impact, net efficiency gain is small or nil (Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Sefton et al., 2007).
    - **Targeting/design:** Institutions or design that censor anti-social punishment or require consensus for punishment assignment strongly increase efficiency (Casari & Luini, 2009; Ertan et al., 2009).
    - **Culture/context:** If context-specific norms support antisocial punishment or punishment legitimacy is low, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Vollan, 2008; Fehr, Hoff, & Kshetramade, 2008).
    - **Institutional choice:** Endogenous (voted) imposition of punishment (even if mild) produces larger efficiency gains than exogenous (imposed) ones; severe punishment is effective regardless of procedure (Tyran & Feld, 2006; Sutter et al., 2010).
    - **Feedback/information:** Showing contribution feedback only supports higher efficiency than showing contributions plus payoff feedback, which can encourage more punishment and lower efficiency (Nikiforakis, 2010).
    - **Counterpunishment:** If counterpunishment is possible, cycles reduce or eliminate efficiency gains (Nikiforakis, 2008; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
- **Quantitative guidance:** In canonical lab PGGs (4 players, 10–15 rounds, MPCR 0.4–0.5, punishment cost moderate, no counterpunishment), punishment typically increases efficiency by 10–20 percentage points of the optimal payoff (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007; Arechar et al., 2018). There is considerable heterogeneity; effects may be smaller or null if design or context promotes antisocial or excessive punishment, or bigger if institution is well-structured and cost/impact is favorable.
- **Dimension interaction:** Game design features (player count, rounds, information, chat, etc.) have secondary but sometimes large effects; for example, communication and punishment together often yield more than additive efficiency gains.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

Directly Informed:
- **player_count, num_rounds, mpcr:** Canonical PGG parameters extensively varied and tested, with effects on both baseline efficiency and magnitude of punishment effects.  
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Highly salient; ratio of punishment cost to impact is shown to have a critical, often non-linear effect on efficiency gains. When punishment is weak or costly, efficiency gains shrink or reverse.
- **all_or_nothing:** Both binary and continuous contribution games represented, with comparable patterns (though some design-specific effects, e.g., all-or-nothing exacerbates the impact of monitoring imperfections).
- **show_n_rounds:** Effects on endgame decay and strategic timing of contributions/punishment; included in several settings.
- **show_other_summaries:** Feedback structure (showing contributions, payoffs, or both) shown to strongly moderate punishment effectiveness and efficiency (Nikiforakis, 2010).
- **chat:** Communication as an efficiency enhancer and moderator of punishment effectiveness robustly tested (Bochet et al., 2006; Oprea et al., 2014).
- **reward_exists and reward_cost/tech:** Reward and combined reward-punishment systems explored in multiple papers; effects on efficiency generally weaker than for punishment, but sometimes synergistic.
- **punishment_exists:** Explicit on/off treatment in nearly all core studies.

Indirectly or Contextually Discussed:
- **default_contrib:** Framing and default presentation rarely tested directly in punishment-enabled PGGs.
- **show_punishment_id:** Anonymity of punishers examined; making punishment public can amplify or moderate effects (Fudenberg & Pathak, 2010; Xiao & Houser, 2011).
- **punishment_tech:** Aligned with punishment_cost in ratio; sometimes manipulated separately in multi-level treatments.

Effectively Missing:
- Some games lack extensive coverage of **reward-related dimensions** (in most studies, reward options are disabled).
- **default_contrib** and similar framing variables receive little direct experimental manipulation in the context of punishment-enabled efficiency outcomes.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Boundary Conditions**: Strong efficiency gains from enabling punishment are not universal; efficiency improvements depend critically on punishment targeting, institutional rules, cost/impact, cultural legitimacy, and the absence of counterpunishment cycles. Efficiency *can* decrease, especially outside canonical lab conditions.
- **Cultural and Real-World Transfer**: Most positive findings derive from laboratory studies with WEIRD populations. Field experiments and studies in other contexts report more frequent antisocial punishment and negative efficiency effects (Vollan, 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).
- **Design Sensitivity**: Subtle changes in design dimensions (e.g., whether punishers are anonymous/public, group heterogeneity, feedback structure) can have outsized effects on outcome. Several key dimensions (default framing, identity revelation, exclusion/reward mechanisms) are under-explored or only contextually examined.
- **Behavioral/Contribution vs. Efficiency Emphasis**: A large fraction of studies measure non-payoff outcomes only. Even where contributions rise, efficiency is only improved insofar as punishment costs, perverse punishment, and retaliation are minimized.
- **Generalization to Large Groups, Long Games**: Most core evidence is from small groups and 10–20 rounds. Results in large, long, or dynamic games (e.g., endogenous endowment/wealth growth) may differ (Gächter et al., 2017; Camera & Casari, 2009).
- **Missing Dimensions**: Some design aspects (e.g., punishment/reward framing, multi-channel information, sophisticated network structures) are incompletely tested.
- **Institutional/Implementation Complexity**: Real-world implementation of punishment may introduce further ambiguity (legitimacy, error, monitoring, context-based variation in norm perceptions).

---

**Summary**: The literature provides strong empirical guidance that enabling punishment in standard public goods games, ceteris paribus, increases efficiency relative to control, but *only* when the punishment system is effective, well-targeted, and not undermined by anti-social or retaliatory dynamics. The size and even direction of efficiency change are sensitive to a host of design and contextual moderators, many of which—such as voting, communication, or feedback structure—are addressed directly within this paper set, but some remain underexplored for quantitative prediction. Control efficiency is a useful baseline, but not sufficient for accurate prediction without careful mapping of the key game design dimensions and recognition of context-dependent moderators.
