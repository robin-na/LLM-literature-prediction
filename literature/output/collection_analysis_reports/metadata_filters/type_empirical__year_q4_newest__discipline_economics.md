# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is extensive (123 papers), with the majority being experimental lab studies and a smaller number of field experiments. The evidence base is strongly empirical, providing a broad range of experimental manipulations of public goods games (PGGs) and their variants. Most studies focus on variants of the canonical linear PGG, but there is also substantial coverage of close variants (common pool resource games, trust games, tax evasion games, etc.) and a few more distant contexts. The literature is rich in empirical results directly comparing control (no punishment) conditions with punishment-enabled treatments, and many papers report payoff-based efficiency measures. Mechanism/theory papers are rare in this set; most arguments about mechanisms are grounded in experimental results.

Despite breadth, not all studies report efficiency or directly use payoff-based metrics; many focus on contribution rates or behavioral measures. Efficiency effects are sometimes inferred rather than reported directly, and designs span a variety of group sizes, MPCRs, punishment cost/tech parameters, and institutional structures.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**  
- **Exact relevance:** The bulk of the paper set is based on standard or near-standard public goods games, with close analogs (common pool resource, trust/dyadic dilemma, market externality games) included as "close" or "adjacent".
- **Adjacent or none:** Roughly a third of the papers use adjacent designs (CPR, trust, market games, etc.), which may limit the precision of transfer for prediction but still provide important boundary information.

**Punishment or Sanctions:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Many studies directly manipulate the presence/cost/type of punishment (peer, centralized, formal/informal, third-party, ostracism, etc.).
- **Close or adjacent:** Some only study adjacent forms of sanctions (e.g. allocation, exclusion, subtle "penalties" or monitoring).
- **None:** A subset only contextualizes punishment within broader mechanisms or does not manipulate it.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**  
- **Exact/close:** Roughly half of the papers report direct payoff- or efficiency-based outcomes (total group payoff, welfare, earnings relative to optimum).
- **Adjacent:** Many others report only behavioral proxies (contribution, compliance) and must be interpreted with caution for efficiency effects.
- **Weak/none:** Some do not analyze group-level outcomes or focus on norm enforcement, heterogeneity, or individual-level behavior; these have weaker predictive value for downstream efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related Outcomes (High Relevance):**
  - Group efficiency/total payoff (e.g., Kamei, 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Nhim et al., 2023)
  - Average earnings and welfare (e.g., Chen, 2022; Krügel & Maaser, 2025)
  - Ratios of earnings to social optimum (e.g., Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022)

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Only Indirectly Inform Efficiency):**
  - Contribution/cooperation rates (e.g., Peng 2022a/b; Billinger & Rosenbaum, 2023)
  - Compliance with rules/laws
  - Frequency/type/severity of punishment (e.g., anti-social vs. prosocial punishment patterns)
  - Normative expectations, emotional/psychological measures
  - Voting, information use, communication behaviors

In many cases, efficiency must be inferred from contribution data or from the structure of payoff functions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## When and How Punishment Increases Efficiency:
- **Repeated PGGs, Homogeneous Groups, Moderate Costs:**
    - Enabling punishment reliably raises efficiency/payoff compared to control, often dramatically (Kamei, 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; JARUNGRATTANAPONG, 2022).
    - Peer or formal punishment are generally effective, especially with low punishment cost and high punishment impact.

- **Institutional and Design Moderators:**
    - Formal (centralized) punishment, when coordinated or mandated, tends to yield higher efficiency than informal or peer punishment (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Kamei, 2024).
    - Ostracism and exclusion (as social punishment) are robustly positive, but effects decline when the mechanism is removed (JARUNGRATTANAPONG, 2022).
    - Punishment effectiveness strongly depends on the probability of enforcement and network structure (Peng et al., 2023; Alt et al., 2023).
    - Leader-based punishment is more effective with prosocial (female/voting-based) leaders and responsibility framing (Jiang & Wang, 2024).

- **Conditional Effects and Limitations:**
    - In heterogeneous groups (e.g., varying MPCR/endowments), punishment often fails to increase efficiency and may decrease it (Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022, efficiency outcome; Cox & Stoddard, 2024).
    - Costly peer punishment can reduce efficiency, especially when frequency is high, punishment is anti-social, or cost-to-impact is high (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Deng et al., 2025).
    - Antisocial punishment and high punishment frequency frequently drive negative or neutral effects on efficiency (Peng, 2022; Kim et al., 2025).
    - When punishment can be avoided or “dodged” by opting out, efficiency does not improve (Del Ponte et al., 2025).
    - Task structure alters effects: large magnitude or institutional punishment increases efficiency, but small or weak punishment may not (Ye et al., 2023).
    - In multilevel or competition settings, punishment’s effect depends on prior experience and institutional design (Otten et al., 2024).

- **Special Cases—Contextual Moderators:**
    - Open group membership increases the effectiveness of formal punishment over informal (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
    - Efficiency increases are stronger when punishment is costless or covered by the institution (Bühren et al., 2025).
    - Norm uncertainty and cross-group/cross-country settings can strengthen the effect of third-party punishment (Zong et al., 2025).

## When Punishment Fails to Increase or Reduces Efficiency:
- **High Cost or High Anti-social Use:** If punishment is costly or anti-social punishment is common, efficiency typically falls or does not rise above control (Botelho et al., 2022; Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022; Deng et al., 2025).
- **CPR, Public Bads, Weak Institutions:** In resource extraction games and public bads, increased enforcement costs may outweigh gains from reduced extraction (Nhim et al., 2023; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Schaefer, 2023).
- **Implementation Issues:** If punishment mechanisms are incomplete, incoherent, or diluted via network bystander effects, efficiency gains are undermined or reversed (Peng et al., 2023; Bühren et al., 2025).
- **Contextual Factors:** Cultural norms, group heterogeneity, or specific social environments can entirely moderate or reverse the effect of punishment (Kamei, Sharma, & Walker, 2025; Praxmarer et al., 2024).

## Reward vs. Punishment:
- In heterogeneous settings, reward mechanisms may outperform punishment in increasing efficiency (Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022b).

## Robustness:
- Effects are not always monotonic: magnitude of punishment, enforcement probability, game structure, and group composition all interact to affect efficiency outcomes.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct Prediction (Best Evidence):**
    - When design matches canonical PGGs (homogeneous groups, moderate cost-effective punishment, no chat, continuous/all-or-nothing contributions, no rewards), enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency relative to control, especially in repeated games (Kamei, 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Krügel & Maaser, 2025).
    - The expected efficiency boost is strong unless anti-social punishment, high punishment cost, or group heterogeneity is present.

- **Boundary Cases:**
    - In heterogeneous MPCR or endowment groups, efficiency effects are ambiguous or negative (Chen, 2022; Cox & Stoddard, 2024).
    - If punishment cost is high, punishment rates are high, or the mechanism lacks coordination, efficiency gain is unlikely; sometimes efficiency falls below control (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024).
    - Informal, voluntary, or incomplete punishment institutions are less reliably effective.

- **Indirect/Behavioral-Only Evidence:**
    - Papers reporting only cooperation/contribution increases imply—but do not guarantee—increase in efficiency, as costs of punishment can neutralize or outweigh gains.
    - Behavioral moderators (trust, chat, information, gender, cognitive ability, culture) influence effectiveness, but must not be conflated with group payoff.

- **Implementation for Prediction:**
    - Use direct efficiency outcomes for quantitative mapping.
    - When only behavioral outcomes are reported, weight these as indirect evidence and anchor predictions to cases with similar behavioral-to-efficiency mappings.

- **For Control Efficiency-Based Prediction:**
    - When a control (no-punishment) efficiency is low and group/settings align with the canonical PGG, expect a substantial gain when enabling punishment.
    - When punishment is costly, anti-social, or easily evaded—even with low control efficiency—do not expect a net efficiency gain unless the mechanism is highly coordinated, cost-efficient, and prosocial.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Extensively manipulated and directly linked to efficiency outcomes.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Well-covered, particularly in contrasts between punishment and reward.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries:** Frequently controlled; some studies analyze the effect of known horizon.
- **chat:** Often manipulated to test effects on cooperation and norm transmission.
- **show_punishment_id:** Occasionally tested (esp. in social/psychological studies).

**Indirectly Informed / Contextual:**
- **default_contrib:** Only a few studies manipulate contribution framing (opt-in/opt-out).
- **show_punishment_id:** Studied mainly in adjacent social norm and field context.
- **Leader selection/framing/power:** Covered in some studies but not as explicit game input dimensions.

**Missing/Sparse:**
- Few studies systematically report treatment effects as a general function of default contribution framing.
- Some dimensions (e.g., fine-grained information feedback, within-round learning opportunities) are mentioned but not systematically analyzed as predictive moderators of efficiency.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Gaps in Direct Efficiency Measurement:** Many studies use contribution/cooperation, not group payoff, as the primary outcome, requiring inference to efficiency (potentially introducing bias if punishment costs are nontrivial).
- **Limited External Validity:** Lab settings may not fully capture dynamics present in larger, more complex or field contexts (e.g., long time horizons, endogenous network formation, complex social norms).
- **Sensitivity to Design Dimensions:** Small changes in punishment cost, technology (cost-to-impact), or group heterogeneity can change the sign of the effect, challenging generalizability.
- **Cultural and Institutional Context:** Many findings are context-dependent; cross-country/cultural and norm-heterogeneous studies show substantial moderation and sometimes reversal of effects.
- **Mechanism Ambiguity:** Even where efficiency increases, mechanisms can interact in unexpected ways (e.g., leader contributions vs. punishment per se).
- **Sparse Data in Some Parameter Regions:** For rarely manipulated dimensions (e.g., default_contrib, show_punishment_id), predictive inferences are less reliable.
- **Indistinguishability of Behavioral and Payoff Outcomes in Some Papers:** Some reviews and syntheses conflate contribution with efficiency—a distinction that is critical for the downstream prediction task.

**In summary:** The literature provides a rich empirical basis for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGGs, especially for standard designs and common parameter ranges. Strong positive effects are observed in canonical PGGs with moderate costs and homogeneous groups, but effects are negative or ambiguous for heterogeneous groups, high-cost or anti-social punishment, or poorly specified institutions. Prediction should be grounded in efficiency, not just behavioral outcomes, and adjusted for key design moderators as evidenced in the literature. Ambiguity, context-dependency, and missingness in certain dimensions are important constraints.
