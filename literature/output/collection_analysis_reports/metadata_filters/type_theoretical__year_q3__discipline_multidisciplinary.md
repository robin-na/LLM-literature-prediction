# 1) Evidence Base

The supplied literature set is comprised exclusively of theoretical papers (49/49), many with computational or simulation modeling, but lacking direct experimental or empirical intervention evidence. The set is broad in terms of behavioral mechanisms and modeling frameworks: most works center on exact PGGs or very close variants, though a sizeable fraction focus on adjacent social dilemmas (e.g., PD, resource dilemmas, innovation races, auctions). About half of the papers directly model explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms relevant to the prediction task, while the rest do not. Reported outcomes span efficiency (group payoff relative to optimum), cooperation/contribution rates, prevalence of strategies, and, in adjacent cases, related welfare or profit measures. However, explicit reporting of efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome is present in only a minority of studies; most focus on cooperation or related behavioral metrics.

**Strengths:**  
- Multiple exact-theory treatments exist modeling PGGs with explicit peer or institutional punishment, often with design parameters directly mapped to key prediction-task variables.
- The literature probes a rich array of moderators and mechanisms (e.g., punishment cost, effectiveness, observability, antisocial punishment, network and group structure, normative constraints).

**Weaknesses:**  
- No direct empirical or experimental studies; thus, evidence for quantitative prediction is based on theoretical or computational models, which may overstate or understate real-world effect sizes and fail to capture empirical variation.
- Some important design dimensions are rarely discussed or are available only via contextual or indirect inference.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. PGG or Variant**  
- *Exact relevance:* The majority of key findings come from models that are exact matches to PGGs (e.g., Duong & Han, 2021; Salahshour, 2021; Hintze et al., 2020; García & Traulsen, 2019).
- *Close/adjacent relevance:* Several important papers study threshold or risk-PGGs (Góis et al., 2019), or adjacent environments (leadership games, innovation races, resource games).
- *Weak/none:* About one third of the studies provide only adjacent context or focus on non-PGG dilemmas.

**b. Punishment or Sanctions**  
- *Exact relevance:* Explicit punishment or sanctions are central in the bulk of the most relevant papers. Many models enable parameterization of punishment cost, fine, observability, or strategic targeting (e.g., Salahshour, 2021; García & Traulsen, 2019; Hintze et al., 2020).
- *Adjacent/weak relevance:* Some studies discuss related mechanisms (exclusion, costly retaliation, entrance fees) or only model reward/assortment; others omit punishment entirely.

**c. Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**  
- *Exact relevance:* A subset of papers explicitly report group efficiency or equivalent payoff-based outcomes, often as a function of key design dimensions (Duong & Han, 2021; García & Traulsen, 2019; Hintze et al., 2020; Salahshour, 2021).
- *Close/adjacent:* Many papers imply efficiency effects via cooperation gains (Góis et al., 2019), or report related but not identical measures (group achievement, profitability, average earnings).
- *Weak/none:* Many models focus primarily on behavioral outcomes (contribution, strategy frequency) and do not calculate efficiency.

> **Summary:**  
Relevance to the prediction task is high for PGG structure and punishment mechanisms, but only moderate in direct efficiency-outcome reporting. Many papers offer only theoretical or qualitative (rather than quantitative) guidance for predicting treatment efficiency changes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Direct Payoff-Related Outcomes (Efficiency, Group Payoff, Welfare, Surplus):**
- *Primary efficiency modeling:* Some papers rigorously evaluate group payoff as a ratio to the cooperative optimum (Duong & Han, 2021; García & Traulsen, 2019; Hintze et al., 2020; Salahshour, 2021; Hooper et al., 2021).
- *Related measures:* Group achievement (fraction of successful groups), ex ante expected profit (Qian et al., 2018), and average earnings are used as efficiency proxies in a minority of studies.
- *Institutional cost focus:* Some theoretical treatments (Duong & Han, 2021) report on cost-efficiency from the institutional perspective, which must be translated to group efficiency.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Contributions, Cooperation Rates, Punishment Frequency, Norms):**
- *Majority of studies:* Most other papers primarily report changes in: cooperation/contribution rate, prevalence of cooperative strategies, punishment/defection frequencies, or the spread of norms.
- *Prediction caveat:* While increases in cooperation often correlate with higher group payoff, this relationship is not always guaranteed (especially when punishment costs are high or antisocial punishment is prevalent).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of Cross-Paper Findings:**

- **Enabling Punishment Often Raises Efficiency—Conditionally:**  
  Multiple theory papers show that enabling peer or institutional punishment in a PGG increases cooperation and can raise average group payoff, provided that punishment is sufficiently efficient (i.e., high impact per unit cost), public good returns are substantial, and antisocial punishment is controlled (Salahshour, 2021; Hintze et al., 2020; Duong & Han, 2021).

- **Costs and Downsides of Punishment:**  
  Efficiency gains are not automatic; the cost of administering punishment, and the occurrence of antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators), can erode or even negate overall group payoff increases. Some models show that, under certain parameter regimes (e.g., very cheap punishment, high antisocial punishment, or norm-free settings), introducing punishment may reduce efficiency (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; García & Traulsen, 2019; Lee et al., 2019; Honjo & Kubo, 2020).

- **Moderators of Punishment’s Effect:**  
  - *Punishment Cost & Magnitude:* Moderate punishment cost, with sufficient impact, is optimal—punishment that is too cheap can facilitate antisocial punishment; too costly, and punishment is too rare to sustain cooperation (Salahshour, 2021; Hintze et al., 2020).
  - *Observability/Information Availability:* The ability to observe the presence or outcomes of punishment institutions encourages conditional strategies, which can stabilize efficient cooperation; lack of observability tends to allow antisocial or opportunistic strategies to invade, reducing efficiency (García & Traulsen, 2019; Berger & De Silva, 2021).
  - *Normative/Institutional Context:* Norm-constrained or institutionally managed punishment is more likely to generate efficiency gains than unconstrained peer-to-peer punishment, which is vulnerable to misuse and efficiency losses (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Okada, 2020).
  - *Reward Co-availability:* When reward is available, it can be more cost-effective than punishment for achieving cooperation at low-to-moderate levels; at higher targets, punishment may outperform reward (Duong & Han, 2021; Góis et al., 2019).
  - *Baseline Game Parameters:* Group size, mpcr, number of rounds, and related design features moderate the marginal benefit of enabling punishment (Duong & Han, 2021; García & Traulsen, 2019; Hooper et al., 2021).

- **Contexts Where Punishment Fails or Backfires:**  
  - *Social Value Orientation:* With highly competitive agents, or in settings with frequent antisocial punishment, enabling punishment may reduce efficiency relative to control (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Honjo & Kubo, 2020).
  - *Institutional Corruption or Overregulation:* If institutions are corruptible or over-punish, efficiency can cycle or fall below baseline; expensive monitoring is required for sustained gains (Lee et al., 2019; Han et al., 2021).
  - *Strategy Structure & Memory Requirements:* Punishment mechanisms requiring coordination or high memory sometimes fail to increase efficiency if these conditions are lacking (Huang et al., 2018; Murase & Baek, 2020).

- **Reward or Alternative Mechanisms as Comparators:**  
  In several models, targeted reward matches or exceeds the efficiency gains available from punishment, particularly in pooling or threshold environments (Duong & Han, 2021; Góis et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Approach:**  
- When the control game (punishment disabled) has high efficiency, adding punishment is less likely to yield large positive efficiency effects, unless antisocial punishment is rare and the mechanism is well-calibrated (Duong & Han, 2021).
- The magnitude and direction of the treatment effect (efficiency increment) depend on interaction between key design variables:
    - *mpcr (public good profitability):* Higher mpcr amplifies the positive efficiency effect of punishment; with low mpcr, cost-effectiveness may favor reward or other interventions.
    - *punishment_cost / magnitude:* Optimal settings are required—too low a cost invites abuse, too high a cost suppresses enforcement (Salahshour, 2021; Hintze et al., 2020).
    - *observability / information:* Efficiency gains from punishment depend on whether players can observe punishment and condition strategies accordingly (García & Traulsen, 2019).
    - *norms and context:* Institutional and normative context can determine whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
    - *player_count and group structure:* Larger groups may dilute peer punishment's efficiency impact; effects of size are nuanced (Alvarez-Rodriguez et al., 2021).
    - *reward_exists / reward_cost / reward_tech:* The presence and cost-effectiveness of rewards shape the relative benefit of punishment (Góis et al., 2019; Duong & Han, 2021).
    - *show_punishment_id and show_other_summaries:* Rich information on identity and prior outcomes moderates efficiency effects via social learning and deterrence effects (Berger & De Silva, 2021).

**When to Expect Positive Efficiency Effects:**  
- Sufficiently high mpcr, moderate punishment cost and substantial impact, credible observability, constrained antisocial punishment, and robust institutional context make it likely that punishment will increase efficiency compared to control.

**Risks and Downside Cases:**  
- If antisocial punishment is prevalent, punishment is extremely cheap or highly costly, the mechanism is poorly targeted, or observability/normative support is missing, enabling punishment may fail to improve, or may even reduce, group efficiency.

**Empirical Note:**  
- All evidence is theoretical; in practice, effect sizes may be smaller and moderators likely interact in complex ways not fully captured by models.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- *player_count*: Frequently parameterized in theory models, affecting thresholds and critical group size (Duong & Han, 2021; García & Traulsen, 2019).
- *num_rounds*: Incorporated chiefly in repeated-game models and when memory/learning is relevant (Salahshour, 2021; Murase & Baek, 2020).
- *all_or_nothing*: Modeled in both binary and continuous contribution PGGs; effect depends on implementation (Hintze et al., 2020; Salahshour, 2021).
- *mpcr*: Central to nearly all direct PGG models; a core moderator for both baseline control and punishment efficacy (Duong & Han, 2021; Hintze et al., 2020).
- *punishment_cost* & *punishment_tech* (magnitude, targeting, efficiency): Explicitly modeled in most exact-relevance papers; key determinants of effect direction (Salahshour, 2021; Góis et al., 2019).
- *reward_exists*, *reward_cost*, *reward_tech*: Modeled in theoretical frameworks comparing punishment vs. reward or hybrid strategies (Duong & Han, 2021; Góis et al., 2019).
- *show_punishment_id*, *show_other_summaries*: Directly modeled when reputation, information, or observability are critical (García & Traulsen, 2019; Berger & De Silva, 2021).

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**  
- *chat*: Mentioned as a moderator in broader social norm or network structure models but not parameterized directly for efficiency (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- *default_contrib*: Rarely modeled directly; sometimes mentioned in context of framing or behavioral interventions.
- *show_n_rounds*: Incorporated in a subset of behavioral and evolutionary models as it influences learning and strategy adoption (Alvarez-Rodriguez et al., 2021; Hooper et al., 2021).

**Effectively Missing/Limited Direct Evidence:**  
- Precise calibration of *default_contrib*, explicit empirical manipulation of *chat*, and some interaction effects (i.e., how information and punishment interact in multi-dimensional design choices) are typically absent.
- Empirical evidence for the effect of "identity observability" (show_punishment_id) on efficiency via field or lab data is not present—insights are theoretical or via simulation.

---

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of Empirical Data:** All findings are derived from theory or simulation. No experimental or field data exist in this set to calibrate quantitative predictions or check theoretical predictions for robustness to behavioral or contextual noise.
- **Efficiency Reporting Gaps:** Many papers focus on non-payoff behavioral metrics (cooperation, punishment frequency) rather than efficiency or total group payoff, requiring inference or caution in translating results.
- **Antisocial Punishment and Contextual Moderators:** While frequently mentioned as a critical moderator, actual mechanisms or estimates for antisocial punishment's prevalence under different designs are not empirically substantiated here.
- **Complex Interactions and Realism:** Design dimensions are often varied one at a time in theory work; real-world settings may feature interactive effects (e.g., how mpcr × group size × punishment observability shape outcomes), for which only partial guidance is available.
- **Reward and Hybrid Mechanisms:** Literature often mixes or compares punishment and reward; under some parameters, reward may outperform punishment for efficiency. Direct comparative predictions remain underspecified.
- **Special Cases and Edge Conditions:** Some models highlight rare or edge-case parameters (e.g., corrupt institutions, overregulation, highly competitive agents) where enabling punishment reduces efficiency—suggesting real-world heterogeneity in effects.
- **Behavioral Assumptions:** Models assume rational or boundedly rational updating; effects of learning, culture, emotion, and social structure in practice may alter efficiency effects in unmodeled ways.
- **Missing Design Dimensions and Granularity:** Some relevant experimental design features (e.g., communication protocols, default contribution framing, real vs. artificial identity) are not parameterized or only contextually addressed, limiting fine-grained prediction.

> **Bottom line:** While the literature provides strong theoretical bases for predicting that enabling (peer or institutional) punishment often—but not always—increases efficiency in PGGs relative to control, effect sizes, exceptions, and moderator interactions should be interpreted cautiously and may diverge in real-world implementation.
