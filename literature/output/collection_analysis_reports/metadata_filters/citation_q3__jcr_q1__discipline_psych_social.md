# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set comprises 102 papers, with a balance of experimental/empirical studies and theoretical/mechanism-oriented models. The breadth of the set is broad in terms of collective action contexts, game-theoretic mechanisms, and sociocultural environments, but only a minority of papers provide direct, empirical evidence on the *core prediction task*: how enabling punishment affects group efficiency (payoff relative to the fully cooperative benchmark) in public-goods-game (PGG) environments. There are enough high-relevance empirical PGG punishment papers to anchor key conclusions, supplemented by theory and adjacent work, though the set is diluted by many adjacent and non-payoff-focused sources.

Most high-relevance empirical papers use lab experiments with standard linear PGGs or close variants, varying the institution (e.g., peer, democratic, leader/centralized punishment), action space, and observability. Several theory papers directly address efficiency outcomes using agent-based or analytic approaches. Many other papers explore behavioral mechanisms (e.g., motives for punishment, norm salience) or adjacent domains (e.g., common-pool resource dilemmas, trust games, partner choice), informing boundary conditions and moderators but not directly reporting efficiency outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *Exact*: About one third of the papers (especially the core lab experiments and key theory works) study PGGs or linear variants directly.
- *Close-Adjacent*: A large number address related social dilemmas (common-pool resources, spatial games, PD, trust games), agent-based models of collective action, and hybrid designs, providing conditional relevance when mechanisms or dimensions map well to PGGs.
- *Weak/None*: The remainder study unrelated games, animal models, or observational contexts without group provision or comparable structure.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *Exact*: Major empirical and theoretical papers manipulate peer or institutional punishment as a treatment.
- *Close*: Some focus on sanctions in a broader sense (exclusion, monitoring/investment in enforcement, expulsion, partner choice) or on reward; these mechanisms are often intertwined with punishment in practice and theory.
- *Adjacency/Contextual*: Many papers discuss punishment at the level of behavioral motive, norm enforcement, or as a feature of an environment, without manipulating it or isolating its effect.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- *Exact*: Only a small subset (notably, Nockur et al., 2021; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Ye et al., 2011; Cason & Gangadharan, 2013; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Chávez et al., 2018) explicitly report group efficiency or equivalent outcomes (total payoff relative to full cooperation).
- *Close*: Some report average earnings, group profit, welfare, or surplus, or provide quantifiable differences, even if not scaled to the cooperative optimum.
- *Adjacent/Weak*: A majority focus on contribution rates, cooperation, punishment frequency, or other behaviors—implying efficiency outcomes only indirectly.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff/efficiency outcomes (exact/close):**
    - *Group efficiency* (ratio of actual payout to full cooperation) is reported directly in a few experimental and theoretical studies (Nockur et al., 2021; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2013; Ye et al., 2011; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Chávez et al., 2018; Mulder et al., 2006, close).
    - *Total/group payoff or earnings*, *welfare*, or *group profit* (sometimes not normalized) are used in some studies and can often be mapped to efficiency.
    - *Payoff inequality* (e.g., Gini) sometimes accompanies efficiency outcomes.

- **Non-payoff (behavioral) outcomes:**
    - *Contribution or cooperation rates* (most common), *frequency or severity of punishment*, *norm enforcement*, *trust/trustworthiness*, *emotional responses*, and *punishment motivations* are frequently measured.
    - Many studies report only behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution, cooperation) and do not provide or infer group efficiency or total payoff.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical findings from PGG or closely related games:**
- **Peer punishment increases contributions but often reduces group efficiency due to high punishment costs**. In exact-relevance lab experiments (Nockur et al., 2021; Harrell & Simpson, 2016), the introduction of peer punishment leads to more cooperation, but high punishment use dissipates group earnings, resulting in *lower or unchanged efficiency* relative to the no-punishment baseline.
- **Institutional/centralized/democratic punishment can support both higher contributions and higher or unaffected efficiency**. When punishment is coordinated (centralized authority or group voting), punishment can deter free-riding with less overuse, leading to higher or at least *not reduced* group efficiency (Nockur et al., 2021; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Ye et al., 2011—with compensation for punishers yields highest efficiency).
- **Reward and compensation mechanisms often enhance or supplant the positive effect of punishment on efficiency** (Ye et al., 2011; Cason & Gangadharan, 2013). Rewards sometimes outperform punishment on efficiency in noisy or imperfect-monitoring contexts.
- **Heterogeneity/moderators:** The sign and magnitude of the efficiency effect depend heavily on:
    - *Punishment institution type*: Peer > high cost, risk of inefficiency; Central/democratic > more efficient.
    - *Punishment cost*: Higher cost typically reduces net efficiency gains.
    - *Pre-existing control efficiency*: If baseline (control) efficiency is high, punishment can reduce it (Nair et al., 2018; Mulder et al., 2006).
    - *Action space/defection options*: Alternative/harsher defection options can neutralize or reverse the benefit of punishment (Mulder et al., 2006).
    - *Leadership and punisher motivation*: Prosocial leaders administering punishment yield better efficiency than proself or distributed/peer punishment (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Kosfeld, 2020).
    - *Visibility/observability/reputation*: Income transparency and group summary stats can moderate sanctioning patterns and, consequently, efficiency outcomes (Hauser et al., 2021; Skatova & Ferguson, 2011).

**Theoretical and agent-based model insights:**
- **Punishment can transform social dilemmas into coordination games with higher efficiency, but only if group-wide enforcement, compensation for punishers, or credible threat structures are in place** (Ye et al., 2011; Friedman & Singh, 2004; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Tarui et al., 2008; Bhui et al., 2019).
- **Cost and effectiveness of punishment are critical**—higher fee-to-fine ratios and more targeted punishment favor efficiency (Skatova & Ferguson, 2011; Jaffe & Zaballa, 2010).
- **Group composition, structure, and information**: Larger groups may initially benefit more from punishment, but too large a group may undermine its credibility/effectiveness (Tarui et al., 2008).
- **Punishment-based reputation mechanisms can sustain high cooperation and, by inference, higher efficiency especially in large, error-prone settings** (dos Santos & Wedekind, 2015). Reward-based systems are less robust.

**Boundary arguments and exceptions:**
- **In environments with local competition or opportunities for antisocial punishment (harming, retaliation), punishment can *reduce* efficiency** (Barker & Barclay, 2016; Ahmad & Loch, 2020; Bolton et al., 2018).
- **If baseline efficiency is low due to tempting defection, punishment can increase group outcome but may still not restore efficiency to full-cooperation levels** (Nair et al., 2018; Chávez et al., 2018).
- **When enforcement is weak, noise is high, or punisher motives are corrupt/antisocial, punishment can be ineffective or harmful** (Wu et al., 2022; Spadaro et al., 2023).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **The single strongest and most reliable predictor of efficiency with punishment enabled is the combination of control efficiency (no-punishment baseline) and *type* of punishment institution**:
    - **Peer punishment often increases contributions but *reduces efficiency* or leaves it unchanged, due to excessive punishment costs.** Predict treatment efficiency as *lower than or equal to* control efficiency (Nockur et al., 2021; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2013), unless additional mechanisms (reward/compensation, coordination) are implemented.
    - **Democratic/centralized punishment, especially with effective leader or coordination, leads to *higher or at least unimpaired efficiency*—potential efficiency gains are maximized when leader is prosocial and/or punishment costs are moderate.** Predict treatment efficiency as *equal to or higher than* baseline.
    - **When punishment is paired with compensation/reward mechanisms, or when administered by a prosocial leader, efficiency is most likely to increase.** If model features a reward-for-punishers option, strengthen expectation of efficiency gains (Ye et al., 2011).
    - **Design dimensions such as punishment cost, punishment technology (fee-to-fine ratio), and information/observability moderate the outcome**:
        - High punishment cost: expect worse efficiency.
        - Efficient punishment with strong norm salience: expect better efficiency.
        - Poorly targeted, antisocial, or indiscriminately applied punishment: expect diminished or negative gains.
- **Control efficiency matters**:  
    - With high control efficiency (group already cooperating), enabling punishment can *reduce* efficiency due to unnecessary punishment costs (Nair et al., 2018; Mulder et al., 2006).
    - With low baseline efficiency (defection is tempting, much free riding), punishment may *raise* efficiency but often not to full-cooperation levels (Chávez et al., 2018).

- **Structural/contextual moderators**:
    - *Player count, number of rounds, MPCR, and action space* are frequently controlled but rarely seen to moderate the punishment effect unless they interact with punishment credibility (Tarui et al., 2008).
    - *Reward, communication, and summary information* can substitute for or moderate punishment effects, especially in environments allowing for coordination or positive reinforcement.
- **For dimensions poorly covered by the literature (e.g., chat, default contribution, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id), prediction must rely on indirect evidence or hold the effect neutral.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (frequently addressed with empirical/efficiency outcomes):**
- `player_count`: Directly manipulated in nearly all experimental studies; group size sometimes moderates effect of punishment (via credibility or diffusion).
- `num_rounds`: Standard variation in repeated games; longer games sometimes dampen/strengthen punishment effect via expectations/discounting.
- `mpcr`: Well-studied; moderate to high returns tend to make cooperation and punishment more attractive.
- `punishment_cost`: Consistently found to moderate efficiency gains; higher costs erode net benefit.
- `punishment_tech` (fee-fine ratio): Sometimes reported as directly influencing effectiveness.
- `reward_exists`: Included in several studies; joint presence alters the effect of punishment on efficiency.
- `all_or_nothing`: Action space (binary vs. continuous) is varied across studies; main findings stable, but effect sizes may vary.

**Indirectly informed/contextually discussed:**
- `chat`: Some studies manipulate or report presence; direct effect on efficiency with punishment is less well studied but may enhance coordination or forgiveness.
- `show_other_summaries` / `show_n_rounds`: Manipulated in some studies (visibility, information); clear effect on behavioral outcomes, less consistently tied to efficiency.
- `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes included; public punishment may increase norm salience (Wu et al., 2022; Kurz et al., 2014), but efficiency impact not directly quantified.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Examined alongside punishment in some studies, but evidence is thinner for their interaction with efficiency.

**Effectively missing/insufficient evidence:**
- `default_contrib`: Little evidence on effect of initial contribution framing.
- `show_punishment_id` and `show_n_rounds`: Sometimes manipulated, but not systematically studied for net efficiency effects.
- Some nuanced settings (e.g., combinations of dimensions, complex technologies) have no primary evidence; in these cases, one should be cautious in extrapolating.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited direct evidence on treatment (punishment-enabled) vs. control (punishment-disabled) efficiency, especially across the full range of design dimensions.** Most studies manipulate one or a few variables, often holding others constant or reporting only behavioral outcomes.
- **Peer punishment's net effect on efficiency is frequently negative or null—this is a robust empirical pattern, but exceptions exist for highly coordinated or compensated punishment.** This may make it difficult for prediction models trained on contribution data alone to forecast efficiency changes accurately.
- **Strong contextual and institutional moderators:** Efficiency effects are highly sensitive to punishment cost, institution type (peer/centralized/democratic), presence of reward/compensation, pre-existing cooperation levels, and information structure. Results from one context may not generalize.
- **Many studies report only contribution or behavior, not efficiency.** Extrapolation from non-payoff outcomes adds uncertainty, as high cooperation does not guarantee high efficiency (punishment can be overused or misapplied).
- **Sparse evidence for underrepresented or complex dimension combinations** (e.g., simultaneous effects of chat + reward + punishment + public identity). Prediction must be conservative in these ranges.
- **Potential external validity concerns:** Several papers demonstrate that real-world use and effects of punishment (especially third-party and in high-trust or high-relationship contexts) may diverge from lab-PGG patterns, suggesting caution in generalizing to naturalistic settings.
- **Ambiguous or mixed findings:** Some theoretical models predict net efficiency gains from punishment only under specific conditions (e.g., compensation for punishers, effective group norm enforcement) which may not be present in standard lab PGGs.

---

**In summary:**  
The available literature supports the following: In standard PGGs, enabling peer punishment typically *raises contributions* but often *reduces or does not improve efficiency* due to the high cost of punishment. In contrast, *democratic, centralized, or compensated punishment* is more likely to foster efficiency gains, sometimes equaling or modestly exceeding control efficiency, especially when the baseline is low. Key design dimensions such as punishment institution, cost, and reward mechanisms are crucial moderators. For prediction, a model should use control efficiency as a baseline and allow for negative, positive, or null effects of punishment on efficiency, weighted by institutional design and compensation/reward structure. The largest gaps in the literature are for rarely studied design combinations and settings where payoff outcomes are not reported.
