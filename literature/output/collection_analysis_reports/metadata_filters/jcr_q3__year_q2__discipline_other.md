# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base for this prediction task comprises three theoretical papers, with none offering new empirical or experimental data. These papers are broad in their conceptual approaches but limited in direct applicability to controlled public goods game (PGG) environments. Two papers draw on related models (evolutionary theory, agent-based modeling, broad resource-sharing frameworks), and only one (Angourakis et al., 2015) provides a stylized, simulation-driven perspective that touches on common-pool resource dilemmas analogous to public goods problems. The set is thus narrow in terms of direct empirical relevance to experimental PGGs with peer punishment, and the bulk of insights come from theoretical synthesis or adjacent modeling rather than direct measurement of efficiency or group payoff changes due to punishment.

# 2) Task Relevance

The paper set's relevance to the prediction task is as follows:

- **pgg_or_variant**: All three papers are at best *adjacent* to canonical public goods games. None analyze standard linear or binary PGGs directly; two approach PGG-like dynamics through the lens of resource sharing or evolutionary arguments.
- **punishment_or_sanctions**: Only Andrews & Davidson (2013) discusses punishment *exactly*, but solely as a theoretical mechanism supporting cooperation—there is no analysis of implementation details or game outcomes. Angourakis et al. (2015) deals with *adjacent* sanctioning via conditional access (exclusion), which is related to but not the same as peer punishment; Sthel et al. (2013) does not address punishment or sanctions at all (*none*).
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: All three papers are either *adjacent* or at best *close* to efficiency outcomes. Only Angourakis et al. (2015) gives indirect indications of system performance (e.g., surplus, shortage avoidance), which loosely parallel group payoff or efficiency, but they do not use the standard efficiency metric. There is no *exact* reporting of average efficiency, group payoff, or surplus as seen in PGG experiments with punishment manipulation.

**Summary**: The literature set is not a direct match for the prediction task—it provides general theoretical context and some adjacent mechanism insights regarding punishment and efficiency, but stays several steps removed from empirical predictions about PGG treatment efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**:
  - *Direct measurement*: Absent across all three papers; no empirical or simulated reporting of group payoff, efficiency, total surplus, or welfare in measurable game environments.
  - *Indirect reporting*: Angourakis et al. (2015) discusses “system performance” in terms of the probability of food shortage avoidance and the emergence of surplus, but these are presented abstractly and are not mapped to PGG efficiency metrics.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
  - *Primary focus*: Both Andrews & Davidson (2013) and Angourakis et al. (2015) focus on mechanisms supporting cooperation or norm compliance (e.g., how punishment facilitates sustained cooperation).
  - *Sthel et al. (2013)*: Conceptually discusses the benefits of cooperative systems for sustainability, without operationalizing any behavioral or payoff measures.

*Key distinction*: All empirically grounded or modeled outcomes focus on cooperation rates, norm compliance, and group sustainability, rather than payoff or efficiency in the specific sense required for the prediction task.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Across the paper set, the following findings are most salient for treatment efficiency prediction:

- **Punishment as a scaffold for cooperation**: The theoretical synthesis by Andrews & Davidson (2013) posits that punishment enables and stabilizes prosocial cooperation, underpinning collective efficiency at a population level. This aligns with the hypothesis that enabling punishment will generally have a positive effect on group cooperation and, by inference, on efficiency—though this connection is not directly demonstrated with payoff outcomes or quantified effects.
- **Optimality of intermediate sanctioning**: Angourakis et al. (2015) shows—within an agent-based common-pool resource model—that both overly strict (harsh) and overly lax sanctioning reduce system performance. Moderate sanctioning (conditional resource access as a proxy for punishment) is associated with higher rates of cooperation and better system outcomes (surplus, fewer shortages), suggesting a theoretical efficiency optimum at intermediate punishment stringency.
- **Absence of quantitative or comparative efficiency effects**: No studies in this set provide estimates of the magnitude of efficiency increase (or decrease) from enabling punishment in a PGG, nor do they provide guidance on how treatment efficiency relates to control efficiency.

**Caveat**: All effect statements are based on theoretical argument or non-PGG simulation—applicability to actual PGG efficiency outcomes with parameterized punishment is assumed but not shown.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the literature’s character, predictions about the effect of enabling peer punishment on average efficiency in PGG-like environments should be made with significant caution:

- **Interpretation**: Theoretical and simulation work supports the general expectation that punishment or sanction mechanisms foster stability of cooperation and—in qualitative terms—improve or sustain efficiency relative to unpunished settings, especially under intermediate enforcement levels (Andrews & Davidson, 2013; Angourakis et al., 2015).
- **Application to prediction task**: No direct mapping is given between the standard game design dimensions (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost) and efficiency outcomes.
  - Angourakis et al. (2015) provides indirect evidence that “harsh” or excessive punishment can suppress recovery and thus lower system performance, implying that punishment cost and rule strictness likely have non-linear, non-monotonic effects on efficiency.
  - Control game efficiency (without punishment) is conceptually relevant in that the difference attributable to punishment may depend on baseline levels of cooperation, but no paper quantifies this relationship.
- **Parameter moderation**: The literature does not empirically address how variation in player count, rounds, communication, punishment cost, reward presence, or payoff display moderate punishment’s effect on efficiency.
- **Bottom line**: The literature justifies a general prediction of positive or curvilinear punishment effects on efficiency in PGG-like dilemmas, but does not support quantitative forecasting or moderation analysis based on specific design dimensions.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

Of the 14 design dimensions relevant to the prediction task:

- **Directly informed**: None.
- **Indirectly discussed**:
  - *player_count, num_rounds*: Modeled explicitly as simulation variables by Angourakis et al. (2015) but not analyzed with respect to punishment’s marginal effect on efficiency.
  - *punishment_cost / punishment_tech*: Conceptual discussion of sanction stringency (Angourakis et al., 2015), mapped to enforcement level (rule strictness), but not to parameterized cost-magnitude tradeoffs.
  - *mpcr (mapped conceptually as storage efficiency)*: Discussed contextually in Angourakis et al. (2015) as one determinant of collective versus individual benefit, but not linked to efficiency effects of punishment.
- **Only contextually discussed**:
  - *all_or_nothing*, *chat*, *default_contrib*, *reward_exists*, *reward_cost*, *reward_tech*, *show_n_rounds*, *show_other_summaries*, *show_punishment_id*: Either absent or discussed only as background features, if at all.

In summary, most prediction-relevant dimensions are effectively missing or appear only as general context. No empirical or simulation-based findings explicitly moderate punishment’s efficiency effect by any listed dimension.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of empirical grounding**: All studies are theoretical or simulation-based, with no laboratory or field data on efficiency responses to enabling or disabling peer punishment in PGGs or direct variants.
- **Absence of payoff-based outcomes**: No study provides direct measurements or effect sizes for group payoff, efficiency, or surplus in PGGs as a function of punishment presence or structure.
- **Ambiguity of mapping between models and PGGs**: Findings from common-pool resource models (Angourakis et al., 2015) or broad evolutionary arguments (Andrews & Davidson, 2013) do not necessarily translate to parameterized PGGs with specified design features.
- **Sparse coverage of design dimensions**: Key moderators for prediction, such as punishment cost and magnitude, player count, reward features, and information display, are not empirically or mechanistically manipulated with respect to payoff outcomes.
- **Implications for downstream prediction**: Quantitative prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions remains unsupported; only broad qualitative expectations (e.g., “punishment may increase or stabilize efficiency, particularly under intermediate strictness”) can be justified.
