# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set consists of a large number (263) of primarily **empirical experimental papers**—predominantly laboratory experiments using human subjects, with some field experiments and animal studies. Very few included purely theoretical or modeling work, and almost all papers report original empirical results. The coverage is **broad and deep** for the core question of how punishment affects **efficiency and payoff outcomes in public goods games (PGGs)** or close variants; there is especially strong coverage of canonical linear PGG lab experiments with toggled punishment and detailed variation on design parameters (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost). There are also highly relevant papers on variants, such as threshold PGGs, CPRs, dyadic PDs, and related social dilemmas, as well as close and adjacent contexts (e.g., reward, reputation, exclusion, leader vs peer punishment).

However, coverage thins for **combinations of more complex or less commonly varied game design features** (e.g., chat, voluntary participation, complex info settings), and for **payoff-based outcomes** in adjacent designs like dictator or trust games, or field contexts where efficiency is not always reported. Many studies directly report or can be translated into efficiency (group payoff relative to the full-cooperation benchmark), especially for standard PGG designs.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **`pgg_or_variant`**: The literature is **highly relevant ("exact" or "close")** for standard public goods games and closely related variants (linear and threshold PGGs, CPRs, team contest games, snowdrift games, repeated PDs), with a majority of empirical studies using canonical lab PGGs or well-documented variants. Some evidence from adjacent games (dictator, trust) informs about general punishment/cooperation mechanisms but is of lesser direct value.
- **`punishment_or_sanctions`**: Relevance is very high—numerous studies directly toggle **peer, leader, centralized, second-order, third-party, and collective punishment**, with variation in cost, targeting, identifiability, and institutional rules. Many studies explicitly compare punishment to other interventions (rewards, communication, exclusion).
- **`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`**: Many studies report **group efficiency or average payoff ("exact" or "close")**, especially in the core PGG literature. Behavioral outcomes (e.g., average or marginal contribution rate, punishment frequency) are often used as proxies where efficiency is absent, but may diverge due to punishment cost.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes ("efficiency" in PGG terms):**
  - **Directly measured in a large number of PGG studies** (e.g., group payoff, efficiency ratio, earnings relative to full cooperation, welfare, or surplus).
  - **Indirectly inferred** in some papers from average contributions *when* punishment is not too costly; in others, it is necessary to note that increased cooperation does not always translate to efficiency due to punishment costs.
  - **Adjacent or absent** in a substantial subset of papers (e.g., dictator games, trust games, norm perception studies), where only behavioral, attitudinal, or neurological outcomes are measured.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - **Widely separately reported**: contribution rates, cooperation probability, punishment frequency/type/target, reciprocity, norm compliance, trust, emotion/risk preference, norm perceptions, and neural/psychological correlates.
  - **Distinction is well maintained in most studies,** but a small number conflate increased cooperation with increased efficiency (which must be flagged in prediction analysis).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## 4a) Empirical Findings (Efficiency/Payoff-Related)

### Positive Effects (Punishment Increases Efficiency vs. Control)
- In **canonical repeated PGGs with moderate player count (3-12), standard or high MPCR (≥1/n), adequate rounds, and well-designed punishment (cost-to-impact typically 1:3, peer or centralized)**, **enabling punishment usually produces substantively higher efficiency** compared to the no-punishment control (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Gürerk et al., 2006; Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006; Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).

- **Centralized/leader punishment** is often more efficiency-promoting than decentralized peer punishment, mainly due to **reduced revenge/counter-punishment, lower cost and better targeting** (Harrell, 2019; Gross et al., 2016; Ozono et al., 2016).

- **Strong/institutional punishment regimes (automated/imposed rather than endogenous peer punishment)** are most likely to close the gap between low-cooperation and high-cooperation groups and maximize efficiency gains (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).

- **Reputation and norm-signaling mechanisms augment the efficiency impact of punishment**; provision for communication/chat or explicit norm messaging can cut punishment frequency and cost while preserving high cooperation/efficiency (Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006; Andrighetto et al., 2013; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017).

### Mixed or Null Effects (Punishment May Not Increase Efficiency)
- When **punishment is costly, mis-targeted, or widely used for retaliation/antisocial motives**, the efficiency gains from higher cooperation are often **offset or even reversed** by the cost of punishment itself (Simpson et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016; Fehl et al., 2012; Herrmann et al., 2008; Molenmaker et al., 2023; Salahshour et al., 2022).

- **Antisocial punishment** (punishing cooperators, often observed in some societies, in heterogeneous groups, or under noisy feedback) can fully erase or invert efficiency gains (Herrmann et al., 2008; Salahshour et al., 2022; Kubena et al., 2014).

- In **settings where the public good is inefficient (MPCR < 1/n, or negative return games)**, enabling punishment can forcibly stabilize an inefficient or destructive norm, **reducing social welfare or group payoff compared to control** (Abbink et al., 2017; Kamijo et al., 2020).

- **Noisy, stochastic, or ambiguous punishment systems** (imperfect feedback, randomness in punishment effectiveness) greatly diminish or abolish the efficiency benefit (Salahshour et al., 2022; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).

- **Group diversity, poorly aligned local/global incentives, large group sizes** and **absence of communication or reputation mechanisms** can diminish (Ozono et al., 2020; Molenmaker et al., 2023), nullify, or reverse the expected efficiency gains.

### Negative Effects (Punishment Decreases or Does Not Improve Efficiency)
- In **dynamic CPR/commons dilemmas where resources deplete and monitoring is costly** (resource extraction, spatial games), **punishment often reduces efficiency if not paired with communication or facilitation** (Janssen et al., 2010; DeCaro et al., 2024).

- In **repeated dyadic settings (e.g., Prisoner's Dilemma), threshold games with high punishment cost, contest/team games, or where individual solutions crowd out cooperation**, punishment does **not improve and often reduces efficiency** (Dreber et al., 2008; Heine & Strobel, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Gross et al., 2020; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).

- **Corruptible or bribeable punishment authorities** can drastically reduce or negate the efficiency benefits of otherwise strong institutional punishment (Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Spadaro et al., 2023).

- **Collective/group-targeted sanctions** are generally less (or not at all) effective and may reduce efficiency compared to individual/peer-targeted ones (Chapkovski, 2021).

### Moderators / Design Dependencies
- **Punishment cost-to-impact ratio** (fee-to-fine): Lower cost or higher effectiveness generally increases efficiency effects, but excessive punishment or low cost can destabilize cooperation (Jiang et al., 2013).
- **Number of rounds / game length**: Punishment's effect on efficiency is larger in longer or indefinitely repeated games (Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017).
- **Communication/norm-signaling/chat**: Enabling communication can substitute for or augment punishment, increasing efficiency (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Janssen et al., 2010).
- **Punisher identification (show_punishment_id)**: Transparency can reduce vendettas/counter-punishment; anonymity may increase antisocial punishment.
- **Player count**: Larger group sizes can dilute punishment's effect and invite higher antisocial punishment unless coordination mechanisms or centralization are present (Gross et al., 2016; Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
- **Feedback structure (show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds)**: More feedback about others' choices increases the power of punishment to enforce cooperation, but may also increase coordination problems and punishment cost.

## 4b) Theory/Mechanism Arguments
- **Emphasis on cost-benefit tradeoff**: A punishment system only increases efficiency if the cost of implementing punishment is outweighed by the increase in group cooperation; in many cases, the costs are nontrivial or even destructive.
- **Role of norm perception, legitimacy, and context**: The legitimacy of the punishment institution, presence of repeated interaction, group structure (heterogeneity, identity), and context-specific norms critically shape whether punishment is pro-social, efficiency-enhancing, or perverse.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

## General Principles
- **If the control condition already achieves high efficiency due to strong incentives (high MPCR, communication, reputation, homogeneity, etc.), enabling punishment yields small or no additional gains and may even be detrimental** (Jiang et al., 2013; Ozono et al., 2020).
- **If the control condition has moderate or low efficiency, but the game has favorable design for targeted, low-cost, well-calibrated peer or institutional punishment, substantial efficiency gains are likely** (Gächter et al., 2017; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Gürerk et al., 2006).
- **Punishment can only increase efficiency if (a) it raises cooperation, and (b) its net cost is less than resulting extra group surplus.**
- **Design dimensions most directly supported for prediction**: player count, number of rounds, all-or-nothing vs. continuous contributions, MPCR, punishment cost and impact, centralization (institutional design), information/feedback (show_other_summaries), and communication/chat.

## When Making a Prediction
- **Use control efficiency as a ceiling for expected gains, subject to moderator effects by key design features.**
- **Adjust downward if punishment is highly costly, likely to create vendettas, or if antisocial punishment is plausible** (based on heterogeneity, social context, anonymity, etc.).
- **Adjust downward or negative if MPCR is low or negative**: punishment in destructive/inefficient PGGs enforces norms but not efficiency (Abbink et al., 2017; Kamijo et al., 2020).
- **Adjust upward only if punishment cost is low, targeting is accurate, and communication, reputation, or norm-signaling are present and well-designed.**
- **Expect null or ambiguous effects in large, fluid, or compositionally diverse groups** unless explicit institutional or behavioral mechanisms are in place to coordinate punishment or minimize its antisocial/retaliatory use.

- **Edge cases:** When control efficiency is already high, severe punishment can reduce efficiency; when punishment is administratively corruptible, betrayals can nullify or reverse expected efficiency gains; when collective/group punishment replaces peer targeting, expect reduction in efficiency.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed:**
  - `player_count`: Widely varied and heavily discussed as a moderator.
  - `num_rounds`: Heavily analyzed; longer games make punishment more effective.
  - `all_or_nothing`: Both continuous and binary games examined; PGGs with both structures included.
  - `mpcr`: Central to almost all predictions; high, low, negative MPCR conditions explored.
  - `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Most studies specify fine and cost; cost:impact ratio shown to be a strong moderator.
  - `chat`/`communication`: Many studies include or exclude, and several provide direct comparison.

- **Indirectly informed or contextually discussed:**
  - `default_contrib`: Some "opt-in/opt-out" manipulations, but not broadly varied.
  - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Reward often compared to punishment in effectiveness.
  - `punishment_tech`: Sometimes manipulated via centralization (peer vs. leader), second-order, third-party, or targeting rules.
  - `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Important moderators for coordination, monitoring, visibility, and retaliation, but less frequently directly experimented upon.

- **Effectively missing or rarely varied:**
  - Some dimensions (`default_contrib`, detailed feedback structures, fine-grained identity reveals, voluntary participation/exit/entry) are less frequently tested or only contextually analyzed, limiting direct prediction leverage.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes do not substitute for payoff/efficiency;** some studies find increased cooperation without increased efficiency due to punishment cost.
- **Many context-dependent moderators are not always experimentally manipulated:** e.g., group composition, societal context, baseline norm strength, prior experience with punishment institutions.
- **Limited variation in parameter sweeps** (e.g., simultaneous variation of multiple design dimensions is rare; most studies vary one treatment at a time).
- **Often, only "on/off" of punishment is tested**; continuous or gradated changes (e.g., linear cost:impact ratios, graded sanction strengths, mixed reward/punishment regimes) less common.
- **Field studies and non-WEIRD samples are underrepresented compared to laboratory/WEIRD settings;** antisocial punishment and retaliation may differ by society/culture.
- **Ambiguity and disagreement remain** (especially in dyadic/PDG vs. PGG, different social structures, and institutional versus peer punishment); conflicting empirical findings demand careful contextualization.
- **Rare or missing dimensions** (e.g., endogenous formation of punishment institutions, volunteer vs. mandated participation, chat + punishment + feedback interaction) limit prediction in those spaces.
- **Efficiency is sometimes only adjacent**: in many studies, only behavioral or self-reported outcomes exist, and efficiency impact must not be assumed.
- **Edge case caution:** Punishment in inefficient or negative-public-good contexts, high-cost punishment regimes, or under social conditions fostering antisocial punishment can reduce efficiency, contravening the simple "punishment = more efficiency" heuristic.

# Conclusion

The literature strongly supports that, **in standard lab PGGs with moderate MPCR and well-designed, low to moderate cost, well-targeted punishment, enabling peer punishment increases efficiency relative to control**. However, **the effect is highly sensitive to cost, antisocial use, group composition, and institutional context**. Control efficiency alone is not always predictive; **key design parameters and context must be taken into account** due to numerous observed boundary effects and failures. Adjacent but not exact evidence must be interpreted cautiously.
