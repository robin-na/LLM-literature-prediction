# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set is large (178 papers) and generally robust, with a substantial share dedicated to *empirical, lab-based experiments on public goods games (PGGs) and close variants* involving the introduction of peer or institutional punishment. While there is some *coverage of theory and field studies*, the strongest relevance for predicting efficiency outcomes comes from the experimental subset.

**Empirical vs. Theory:**  
- The bulk of direct predictive evidence (for efficiency) comes from empirical, especially lab-experimental studies in canonical linear public goods games, often with punishment phases and carefully controlled parameterization.
- Several theory papers provide explicit payoff-based models and mechanism arguments about punishment and efficiency but vary in their direct applicability to standard PGGs, with some models focusing on adjacent structures (e.g., repeated PDs, CPR games).
- A large peripheral literature addresses adjacent outcomes (cooperation rates, norm compliance, punishment patterns) or adjacent games (dictator, PD, CPR), providing valuable context but less direct predictive value for efficiency.

**Breadth for Prediction Task:**  
- The set is broad in its coverage of *punishment and public goods games and their design dimensions*, but direct, quantitative evidence on the specific prediction task—*how enabling punishment shifts group efficiency/payoff*—is provided by a narrower subset of high-quality lab and meta-analytic studies.  
- Behavioral and psychological outcome papers are overrepresented relative to strictly payoff-focused efficiency studies.
- *Close variants* (CPRs, coordination/ultimatum/team games) offer evidence but may have structural differences impacting transferability.
- Many studies include detailed reporting on specific design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.), although comprehensive factorial variation across all 14 listed dimensions is rare.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *Exact relevance* is achieved in a core set of papers that use literal public goods games, with repeated actual contributions, accurate mapping to the prediction dimensions, and direct outcome measures of efficiency or group payoff.
- *Close/adjacent relevance* is present in studies using CPRs, coordination games, teamwork/ultimatum games, and agent-based models with analogous structure, but results may not always generalize due to differences in strategic environment or payoff externalities.
- Some theory and observational studies are *adjacent or weakly relevant*, focusing on sanctioning, norm enforcement, or cooperation mechanisms without mapping to discrete PGG structures.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *Exact relevance* arises in studies explicitly manipulating the presence, cost, technology, or institutional design of peer punishment (or central/institutional punishment) within games structurally similar to the target environment.
- Other studies address *reward*, *gossip*, or *third-party punishment*—some close to peer punishment, others more peripheral.
- *Contextual or weak relevance* characterizes studies focusing on punishment motives, reputational or emotional impacts, or ethnographic/field-based norm enforcement, which lack experimental manipulation of punishment mechanisms.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- Studies vary in *primary outcome relevance*:
  - *Exact/close relevance:* Direct measures of efficiency, group payoff, welfare, total coins/earnings, or surplus.
  - *Adjacent:* Only behavioral measures (contribution/cooperation rates), typically described as proxies but not equivalent to efficiency.
  - *Weak/none:* Focus solely on punishment behavior, norm judgments, attitudinal or neural outcomes, or experimental paradigms without payoff reporting.

**Summary:**  
- The paper set contains *multiple high-quality, directly relevant empirical and theoretical pieces* for the prediction task.
- However, many studies are limited to *behavioral proxies* (cooperation rates) or adjacent environments, requiring caution in extrapolation.
- For precise prediction—especially across the full spectrum of design dimensions—*direct empirical efficiency results in PGGs with manipulations of punishment* are the most reliable subset.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (`efficiency`, `group payoff`, etc.):**
- Core studies (e.g., Zhang et al., 2024; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Joseph et al., 2025; Sparks et al., 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025) *directly report on efficiency or group payoff*, mapping actual or average payoffs onto the ratio-based efficiency metric.
- Some meta-analyses and theory papers model or regress group efficiency as a function of game parameters and the presence of punishment.
- Several studies report *mixed or negative efficiency effects* of punishment (e.g., Botelho et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022).

**Behavioral (non-payoff) outcomes:**
- Many studies focus on *contribution or cooperation rates*, *frequency/severity of punishment*, *norm compliance*, *punishment assignment patterns*, and *social evaluations*.
- These outcomes are not direct measures of efficiency, though often correlated; key prediction guidance must not conflate them with actual payoff outcomes.
- Several studies highlight the divergence: punishment may increase cooperation behavior without increasing efficiency, especially when punishment is overused or costs are high.

**Other outcomes:**
- Some papers address emotional, cognitive, neural, reputational, or attitudinal responses to punishment and cooperation (e.g., Li et al., 2022; Capraro, 2024; Gummerum et al., 2022), which are important for mechanism understanding but not for efficiency prediction.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis Across Core Evidence:**

- **Punishment often increases group efficiency, but not always:**  
  Robust experimental evidence shows that in linear repeated PGGs under standard lab conditions, enabling peer or institutional punishment typically *increases group efficiency relative to control* (Zhang et al., 2024; Sparks et al., 2024; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Joseph et al., 2025; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Harrell & Wolff, 2023). *Meta-analytic and synthetic reviews* confirm positive average effects, especially for peer punishment and centralized but targeted institutional punishment (Eichenseer, 2023).  
  - **Magnitude and time course:** Gains tend to be *delayed*, as initial efficiency can be depressed by punishment costs before contributions and earnings recover and increase.
  - **Exceptions:** Some experiments show *no improvement or even reduced efficiency*—notably when punishment is overused (especially antisocial punishment), cost-benefit ratios are unfavorable, or the institutional context fails to align use of punishment with free-riding (Botelho et al., 2022; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).

- **Punishment parameters and context are critical moderators:**  
  - *Punishment cost and effectiveness (cost-to-impact ratio):* Low-cost, high-impact punishment more reliably boosts efficiency; expensive or misapplied punishment often erodes gains (Joseph et al., 2025; Sparks et al., 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2025).
  - *Punishment type:* Peer punishment is effective if targeted; collective/institutional mechanisms may further enhance efficiency when designed to avoid unnecessary punishment and second-order free-riding (Duell et al., 2024; Prétôt et al., 2024; Zhang & Pei, 2022).
  - *Monitoring and transparency:* Punishment is more effective at improving efficiency when contributions are individually observable; hidden, noisy, or ambiguous feedback impairs impact (Eisenkopf & Walter, 2022; Chen et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2023).
  - *Group size and structure:* Larger, densely connected groups benefit more from punishment; smaller or highly cohesive groups may maintain cooperation with less reliance on punishment or may suffer from over-punishment (Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
  - *Social context:* The *shadow of prior conflict*, wealth/endowment inequality, migration, group openness, and reputation mechanisms can amplify or nullify punishment's effect on efficiency (Gross et al., 2022; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023).

- **Punishment does not always translate increased contributions into greater efficiency:**  
  - *Costly over-punishment* (including antisocial or misdirected punishment) may sustain or increase contribution but at a resource cost that outweighs gains, leaving efficiency unchanged or lower (Botelho et al., 2022; Zhang & Pei, 2022; Krügel & Maaser, 2025).
  - *Insufficient or misaligned punishment* (e.g., under peer punishment without strong norms or institutionalization) may not deter free-riding effectively, leading to little or no increase in efficiency (Prétôt et al., 2024; Botelho et al., 2022).

- **Institutional design features make a substantial difference:**  
  - *Voting/democracy/endogenous institution choice* can moderate or enhance efficiency effects when punishment regimes are well aligned with player preferences and group structure (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Duell et al., 2024).
  - *Reward* is generally less effective than punishment in sustaining high efficiency, but may avoid some welfare losses associated with punishment (Zhang et al., 2024; Peng, 2022).
  - *Commitment mechanisms and higher-order punishment* (punishing punishers, competitive selection of punishers) often further increase efficiency and reduce maladaptive punishment patterns (Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Duell et al., 2024).

- **Theoretical results confirm empirical trends and specify boundary conditions:**  
  - *Threshold effects and equilibria:* Theory papers provide formulaic and threshold-based conditions under which punishment will raise efficiency, specifying parameter regions for positive vs. negative effects (Prétôt et al., 2024; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Uchida et al., 2024).
  - *Risk of negative effects:* If cost-to-benefit parameters, social structure, or cognitive/perceptual limitations are adverse, punishment may not improve and can even harm efficiency (Botelho et al., 2022; Han et al., 2022; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Overall Approach:**  
- *Direct efficiency prediction when control efficiency is known:* Use the most closely matched experimental or meta-analytic estimates from the subset of PGG studies manipulating punishment. If the game’s control (no-punishment) efficiency, player count, MPCR, rounds, punishment cost/tech, etc., match these studies, apply the observed difference as a baseline effect.
- *Moderator adjustment:* Adjust the anticipated efficiency effect for differences in the following key dimensions:
  - **Punishment cost/effectiveness:** If the cost-to-impact ratio is higher (punishment is less efficient), expect smaller or possibly negative efficiency gains.
  - **Monitoring/feedback:** If monitoring is individual and contributions transparent, expect stronger efficiency gains. Aggregate-only or noisy feedback reduces predicted gains.
  - **Group size:** Larger, well-connected groups (≥5–12) often see stronger efficiency behavior with punishment; small groups may already sustain cooperation or may suffer from over-punishment.
  - **Game repetition:** Repeated games amplify positive efficiency effects of punishment over time; one-shot games may see little benefit or even loss.
  - **Institutional features:** Commitment, collective punishment, and democratic choice usually enhance efficiency gains; pure voluntary peer punishment is less efficient if used often.
  - **Presence of chat or reward:** Chat may sustain cooperation even without punishment; reward can supplement or sometimes substitute for punishment, particularly when costs are low.
  - **Transparency:** When player endowments or contributions are transparent, punishment's positive effect on efficiency is greater (Chen et al., 2023).
  - **Social context:** In environments with prior social conflict, inequality, or migration, or in closed "tight" groups with strong existing norms, efficiency gains from punishment may be diminished or reversed.

**When only behavioral outcomes are reported:**  
- Do not naively equate higher contributions or more cooperation with increased efficiency; adjust downward when punishment costs are substantial or anti-social punishment is common.

**When drawing from adjacent (non-PGG) or theoretical results:**  
- Apply evidence only when the game structure, incentive technology, and payoff mapping are closely analogous; otherwise, use findings as qualitative boundary conditions or to identify possible moderators, not to map quantitative prediction.

**In settings where evidence is mixed or parameter regimes are unclear:**  
- Default to *no or weak positive efficiency impact* from punishment if costs are high, monitoring is poor, and anti-social punishment has been observed.
- Explicitly note uncertainty or recommend sensitivity analysis when mapping from control to treatment efficiency.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**  
- **player_count:** Widely manipulated (3–12+), with evidence about the interaction between size and punishment effects.
- **num_rounds:** Covered in studies comparing one-shot and repeated games; repeated play increases cumulative punishment benefits.
- **mpcr (marginal per-capita return):** Commonly varied; lower MPCR (greater temptation) typically increases reliance on punishment, but may reduce its efficiency effect.
- **punishment_cost & punishment_tech:** Extensively discussed as central moderating factors; studies compare cost-to-impact ratios and alternative punishment assignment mechanisms.
- **all_or_nothing vs. continuous contribution:** Comparative results suggest continuous-action games may sustain higher efficiency, but minimum thresholds/all-or-nothing rules impose unique strategic risks (e.g., threshold failures in Joseph et al., 2025).
- **chat:** Several studies include or control for chat, frequently finding it can substitute for or amplify the effect of punishment.
- **show_other_summaries / show_n_rounds (feedback/transparency):** Transparency of contributions/payoffs and information structure are frequently discussed or manipulated; critical for effective punishment targeting.
- **reward_exists / reward_cost / reward_tech:** Several comparative studies of reward and punishment, noting differential impacts on efficiency.

**Indirectly informed / contextually discussed:**
- **default_contrib:** Framing and default settings sometimes considered, but rarely systematically manipulated relative to punishment.
- **show_punishment_id:** Some discussion in theory and mechanism papers (role of anonymity/identifiability, e.g., Zachník, 2023); less so in empirical work.

**Effectively missing or thin:**  
- Comprehensive interaction effects across the full *set of all 14 dimensions* are rarely studied in factorial fashion.
- Dimensions such as *reward_magnitude* or *detailed communication content* are less frequently varied alongside punishment.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical focus is not always on efficiency:** Many studies prioritize behavioral outcomes (contributions, norm adherence) over direct reporting of efficiency or group payoff, requiring caution when translating findings.
- **Punishment regimes not fully orthogonally varied:** Most evidence comes from canonical lab PGGs using a narrow range of punishment costs (often 1:3 cost-impact). Parameter spaces are not exhaustively mapped, limiting generalizability to untested regimes.
- **Antisocial and misapplied punishment under-explored:** While negative efficiency effects are documented in some cases, real-world rates of anti-social punishment or costly mis-targeting are not always well characterized.
- **Social and institutional context effects incompletely understood:** Effects of prior conflict, group openness, migration, inequality, and local norms are recognized as important moderators, but predictive models incorporating these are underdeveloped.
- **Interaction with alternative mechanisms:** The presence of chat, leadership, reputation, or concurrent reward mechanisms can offset, amplify, or supplant punishment effects, but multidimensional evidence is comparatively limited.
- **Translation from adjacent games (CPRs, team/coordination/ultimatum games):** While adjacent evidence is informative for mechanism arguments or boundary conditions, structural differences in these games can constrain transferability—namely, differences in payoff externalities, action sets, and the comparability of collective vs. individual efficiency measures.
- **Several theory papers offer only qualitative boundaries or parameter ranges** (e.g., critical cost/benefit thresholds); empirical calibration is often missing.
- **Some findings are culturally specific or arise from limited sample contexts:** Results may not generalize to wider or more diverse settings.
- **Little direct evidence on interaction effects among less commonly manipulated design features:** For example, the interaction of *show_punishment_id*, *reward_tech*, or *default_contrib* with punishment is scarcely addressed.

---

**In sum:**  
The literatures with strongest predictive value for treatment efficiency use *standard PGGs with direct manipulation of punishment and report efficiency/payoff*. They support the conclusion that *enabling punishment typically increases group efficiency when punishment is effective, targeted, and not too costly*, but *efficiency gains are not universal* and depend strongly on design dimensions such as punishment cost/tech, monitoring, group structure, and social context. Predictions based solely on behavioral outcomes or adjacent game structures must be treated as less reliable, and caution is warranted whenever design dimensions fall outside empirically explored regimes.
