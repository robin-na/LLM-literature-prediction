# 1) Evidence Base

The current literature base on the effects of punishment in public-goods-game (PGG) environments is extensive, with strong representation of empirical/experimental (especially lab experiment) studies, and a significant subset examining efficiency or related payoff outcomes. The evidence set is broad and spans a variety of design dimensions, with coverage of core PGGs, PGG variants (e.g., threshold, weakest-link, common-pool resource games), laboratory and field contexts, and a wide range of institutional, informational, and cultural moderators.

While the majority of high-relevance studies are experimental and focus on monetary or material payoff outcomes (i.e., efficiency), there are also many studies measuring only behavioral change (contribution rates, norm compliance), especially among adjacent or contextually similar designs. There are fewer formal theory papers, and observational/naturalistic studies are included but generally less central for direct prediction of efficiency effects from design dimensions.

A substantial number of studies report both control (no-punishment) and treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency, with sufficient detail to support conditional predictions, though many parameter combinations are untested. There is good depth on PGGs with peer punishment and institutional/structural variations, but somewhat sparser evidence across all the full range of potential game design modifiers.

# 2) Task Relevance

## PGG or Variant
- **Relevance:** `exact` for a large subset (canonical linear/repeated PGG settings predominate), `close` for threshold, weakest-link, or common-pool resource variants.
- **Detail:** The literature directly and broadly addresses the very type of environment used in the downstream prediction task, though evidence is sparser or more variable for non-canonical or highly stylized PGG variants.

## Punishment or Sanctions
- **Relevance:** `exact` for studies with explicit material cost peer or centralized punishment; several studies also include reward, ostracism, counter-punishment, or institutional variations; `adjacent` or `close` for related social sanction mechanisms (e.g., ostracism, reputational sanctions).
- **Detail:** The enabling, type, assignment, and salience of punishment mechanisms are centrally manipulated across many studies. Some papers focus on related concepts such as non-monetary sanctions, which are less directly transferrable for prediction of monetary payoff efficiency.

## Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcomes
- **Relevance:** `exact` in many high-profile lab experiments; `close` in a subset of field, threshold, and common-pool resource games where "group payoff," earnings, or "welfare" are used; `adjacent` or `weak` in studies focused primarily on behavior or non-payoff outcomes.
- **Detail:** Payoff-based outcomes are reported in many high-quality studies, but a substantial number of adjacent papers only measure behavioral responses, which may only indirectly inform efficiency effects.

# 3) Outcomes Measured in the Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (e.g., group efficiency, total earnings, welfare, surplus, coins generated) are explicitly measured or readily deduced in a substantial share of high-relevance studies (e.g., Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Wang & Qin, 2015; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Eichenseer, 2023; Castillo et al., 2021; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Dorrough et al., 2017).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (contribution rates, cooperation, norm enforcement, antisocial punishment, compliance) are more common in studies of adjacent design or with a primary psychological focus. These outcomes are not equivalent to efficiency, though often correlated (e.g., Kumakawa, 2013; Harrell, 2018; Espín et al., 2022).
- **Mixed outcomes:** Some studies infer or imply efficiency outcomes from behavioral data without direct measurement; in these cases, behavioral changes must not be equated with changes in efficiency unless payoff data are reported (e.g., Morgan et al., 2019).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant to Prediction

Synthesizing across the literature:

- **Punishment typically increases efficiency in canonical repeated linear PGGs** (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Wang & Qin, 2015; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Eichenseer, 2023; Schlösser et al., 2018; Castillo et al., 2021).
    - The magnitude of the effect varies by setting, but introducing peer or centralized punishment reliably raises efficiency above no-punishment control in most lab PGGs.
    - Effect sizes for peer punishment often approach half or more of the difference between baseline and full-cooperation (Eichenseer, 2023).
- **The effect of punishment on efficiency depends critically on game and institutional design dimensions:**
    - **Group size and network density**: Larger and more densely connected groups experience greater positive efficiency effects from punishment (Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
    - **Production function/nonlinearity**: In nonlinear or threshold PGGs (common-pool resource, weakest-link), punishment sometimes yields no gain or can reduce efficiency due to excessive costs or antisocial punishment (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Vollan et al., 2019; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
    - **Institutional details**: Efficiency gains are stronger when punishment is assigned to stable, prosocial, or democratically-selected managers, but can be lost or reversed when power is unstable or antisocial punishment is common (Castillo et al., 2021; Dorrough et al., 2017; Gross et al., 2022).
    - **Communication**: Communication or chat before or during punishment stages amplifies the positive effect on efficiency, and in some cases may substitute for or outperform costly punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Ertör-Akyazi, 2019).
    - **Information structure**: The effectiveness of punishment for efficiency depends on visibility of contributions and punishment, feedback mechanisms, and identification of violators (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Dorrough et al., 2017).
    - **Framing and context**: Cultural, group-compositional, and prior-conflict contexts moderate punishment's effects, especially where antisocial punishment is high (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Vollan et al., 2019; Gross et al., 2022).
- **Cost structure of punishment matters less than ability to enforce:** Even costly/inefficient punishment (high fee-to-fine ratio) can increase efficiency if it credibly threatens defectors (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Eichenseer, 2023), but excessive cost can neutralize or reverse the efficiency benefit in resource-destruction settings (Vollan et al., 2019).
- **Peer punishment can backfire:** High levels of antisocial punishment, instability in punishment power, high noise in monitoring/reporting, or strong group fragmentation can reduce or eliminate the efficiency gains from punishment (Dorrough et al., 2017; Vollan et al., 2019; Fischer et al., 2016).
- **Countervailing findings:** There are credible cases where enabling punishment does not increase or can reduce efficiency—particularly in resource management contexts, games with threshold/tipping points, high levels of antisocial or competitive punishment, or when punishment lacks informational salience (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Vollan et al., 2019; Dorrough et al., 2017; Gross et al., 2022; Abbink et al., 2004).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **For canonical repeated linear PGGs:** Predict a positive, and often large, increase in efficiency when punishment is enabled relative to the no-punishment baseline, controlling for design dimensions such as group size, MPCR, and information structure (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Wang & Qin, 2015; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Eichenseer, 2023).
    - Eichenseer (2023): Peer punishment increases efficiency by ~0.47 (on a normalized 0–1 scale) compared to control; centralized punishment yields a smaller but still positive effect.
    - The efficiency boost is robust, but the absolute magnitude must be adjusted by control efficiency, group size, MPCR, and repeated interaction length.
- **Include interaction terms for key design dimensions in prediction:**
    - **Group size**: Larger groups and higher network density amplify the gain from punishment (Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
    - **Communication**: Presence of chat/communication greatly strengthens the positive effect of punishment, and may substitute for it (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
    - **Feedback/identifiability**: If punishments and past behavior are not linked by feedback, efficiency gains may be lost (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).
    - **Punishment institution**: Stability and symmetry of punishment power matter; instability or inequality can eliminate the efficiency benefit (Dorrough et al., 2017).
    - **Game structure**: Linear payoff functions support stronger positive prediction; threshold/nonlinear games require caution (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Vollan et al., 2019; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
- **Beware of overgeneralization:** In environments with high resource destruction from punishment, antisocial punishment, or when institutional/cultural features support harmful punishment, punishment may fail to increase or may lower efficiency (Vollan et al., 2019; Dorrough et al., 2017; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
- **Control efficiency is a strong baseline:** The efficiency with punishment enabled should generally not be predicted below the control except in cases with strong moderators (e.g., large antisocial punishment, instability, or competitive punishment power).
- **Do not conflate behavioral outcomes with efficiency:** Many studies show increased cooperation without reporting efficiency. Only consider those that explicitly report group payoff, welfare, or efficiency.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Well-Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Widely manipulated, with strong evidence that group size can moderate punishment effects (Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Eichenseer, 2023).
- `num_rounds`: Frequently specified. Effects of punishment are in large part attributed to the ability to maintain cooperation over repeated rounds.
- `mpcr`: Regularly reported and manipulated; higher MPCR generally supports larger efficiency gains when punishment is present (Eichenseer, 2023).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Central manipulation; various studies compare different cost/impact ratios and institutional designs.
- `chat`: Several studies explicitly vary the presence or absence of communication and show its critical moderating effect (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Ertör-Akyazi, 2019).
- `all_or_nothing`: Both binary (threshold, weakest-link) and continuous variants well covered; effects on punishment efficacy are context sensitive (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
  
**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Mentioned Dimensions:**
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Some studies report these, often as part of information structure manipulations; generally indirectly discussed (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Dorrough et al., 2017).
- `punishment_magnitude`: Typically varies jointly with cost or is cited as cost/impact ratio.
- `default_contrib`: Framing effects (opt-in vs. opt-out) are seldom the focus but occasionally referenced.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Some studies include reward, or punishment plus reward, but this is a secondary focus.

**Effectively Missing Dimensions:**
- Not all possible combinations are covered; some parameter ranges (very high group sizes, rare or exotic punishment forms, unusual information structures) lack direct evidence. Some design details relevant to field environments or real-world implementation are underrepresented.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse treatment of edge-case dimensions:** Many parameter combinations, especially at the intersection of design features (e.g., large groups with complex punishment power structures, rare information regimes), are underexplored.
- **Selection bias in reporting:** There may be publication bias toward positive findings (punishment boosts efficiency); cases of null or negative effects (especially in non-linear, threshold, or high-antisocial punishment settings) are fewer but present.
- **Cultural and contextual boundaries:** Laboratory results may not always generalize to real-world or field environments, where the base rate of antisocial punishment, efficacy of institutions, and framing may differ (Vollan et al., 2019; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Noussair et al., 2015).
- **Conflation risk between behavior and payoff:** Many adjacent studies report only on contribution or behavioral norms, not efficiency; behavioral changes do not automatically imply changes in group efficiency when punishment is costly.
- **Limited reward mechanism evidence:** While a few studies compare punishment and reward (Eichenseer, 2023), most focus only on punishment, so predictions where both are simultaneously enabled are less well grounded.
- **Potential for negative efficiency effects:** There are credible, well-conducted studies where punishment reduced efficiency due to high costs, antisocial punishment, or ‘race for power’ dynamics (Dorrough et al., 2017; Vollan et al., 2019; Szekely et al., 2020; Abbink et al., 2004).
- **External validity challenges:** Repetition, anonymity, and payoff salience in lab studies may not fully capture the complexity of real-world collective action environments.
