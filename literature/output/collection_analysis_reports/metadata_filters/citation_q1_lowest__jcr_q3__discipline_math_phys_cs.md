# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 33 theory-focused papers with some simulation and a small number of empirical studies. The theoretical papers span both exact public goods games (PGG) and a range of adjacent social dilemmas (e.g., n-person prisoner's dilemma, resource dilemmas, indirect reciprocity, mutual aid, and demographically-structured models). The empirical data in this set are limited, with most studies offering analytical or simulation-based predictions but few reporting real-world or laboratory efficiency data. Simulations frequently substitute for direct measurement.

Most papers study the impact of punishment or incentives on behavioral cooperation, with varying degrees of connection to efficiency or actual payoff outcomes. A minority of papers provide direct, quantitative or parameterized predictions of efficiency under punishment treatments, while a substantial proportion report only on cooperation or contribution rates, or use proxies for payoff. The paper set is broad in its coverage of mechanisms and social dilemma structures, but relatively narrow in empirically grounded, directly mapped relationships between punishment and efficiency in standard PGGs.

---

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**  
- **exact:** Approximately a quarter directly model or analyze standard PGGs, reporting results for scenarios that match canonical game structures and design dimensions.  
- **close:** Several papers employ close variants (e.g., n-person prisoner's dilemma, mutual aid, or "others-only" games) or use similar strategic settings with minor structural deviations.  
- **adjacent/weak:** Many others situate their results in adjacent domains (e.g., reputation, exclusion, social comparison, or indirect reciprocity).  

**punishment_or_sanctions**  
- **exact:** Roughly half examine explicit, costly punishment or institutional sanctioning (peer or centralized), directly matching the intervention in the prediction task.  
- **close/adjacent:** Others analyze punishment-like mechanisms (exclusion, social disapproval, moral costs) or discuss the effect of impairing defectors without classical cost structures.  
- **weak/none:** A minority are devoid of any punishment or even adjacent mechanisms.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**  
- **exact:** Far fewer report efficiency as precisely defined (group payoff relative to fully cooperative benchmark).  
- **close:** Some papers report group payoff, average earnings, or equivalent welfare/surplus outcomes, close proxies for efficiency.  
- **adjacent:** Many more rely on non-payoff outcomes (cooperation/contribution rates) or use qualitative proxies.  
- **weak/none:** Several studies report only behavioral data or proxies, without mapping to payoffs.

**Summary:**  
The literature set offers strong support for game structure and intervention relevance (PGG and punishment), but only a subset provides direct evidence regarding the prediction target of efficiency changes following the introduction of punishment.

---

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**  
    - *Efficiency* (as group payoff normalized by maximal cooperation): Reported in a subset of theory/simulation studies, but rarely in empirical form.
    - *Total/average group earnings, welfare, or surplus:* Common in theoretical and simulation models (e.g., Vinayak, 2025; Cooney, 2025; Kristensen et al., 2025; Murase, 2025).
    - *Proxy/aggregate payoff metrics:* Some studies use related quantities (fixation probability of cooperation, expected payoff, perceived payoff), less precise but directionally meaningful (e.g., Kurokawa, 2023; Hu & Du, 2023).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
    - *Contribution rate, cooperation prevalence, fraction of cooperators:* The most common primary metric, especially in indirect or mechanism papers.
    - *Punishment frequency, assigned punishment, norm compliance, or similar:* Often reported, mainly as mediators or mechanism checks.
    - *Convergence to cooperative state, cycles of cooperation/defection:* Proxy for long-run cooperation but not direct efficiency.
    - *Psychological satisfaction, donor prevalence, or social dynamics measures:* Present but not tied to actual payoffs.

- **Reporting distinction:**  
A majority of "punishment treatment" effects in the literature are reported on behavioral cooperation, not on efficiency or group payoff; a smaller but critical subset analyzes the impact on actual payoffs, often noting where increased punishment rates can reduce overall efficiency due to the costliness of sanctions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment does not uniformly improve efficiency:**  
  - Whether punishment increases, decreases, or does not affect group efficiency (payoff) depends heavily on punishment cost, effectiveness (punishment magnitude), baseline cooperation, detectability of defection, and population structure. (Greenwood et al., 2018; Cooney, 2025; Murase, 2025)
  - There are parameter regimes where enabling punishment increases efficiency dramatically (e.g., low cost-to-impact ratio, high punishers frequency, strong institutional feedback) and regimes where it reduces efficiency (e.g., high punishment cost, low baseline cooperation, poor detection, or large group size). (Vinayak, 2025; Kurokawa, 2023; Murase, 2025)
  - Theoretical results consistently highlight non-monotonicity: Excessive or ineffective punishment can decrease efficiency due to overuse of costly sanctions, even if cooperation rates increase. (Cooney, 2025; Billard, 1996)

- **Moderators and conditional effects:**  
  - **Threshold/frequency of punishers:** Efficiency gains from punishment require a critical mass of punishers, with the threshold depending on cost and effectiveness of punishment (Greenwood et al., 2018).
  - **Design dimensions:** Efficiency benefit is strongly moderated by punishment cost, magnitude, group size, MPCR (synergy factor), and availability of coordination mechanisms (e.g., communication, partner selection, reputation, institutional feedback). (Duong et al., 2024; Larson, 2016)
  - **Group structure and multilevel selection:** Group-structured populations with demographic feedback (selective extinction) or group-level competition are more likely to see efficiency gains from punishment-like sanctions (Cooney, 2025; Kroumi, 2025; Garay et al., 2014).
  - **Detectability of defection:** Punishment is only efficiency-enhancing if defectors can be reliably identified; otherwise, its costs may outweigh its benefits (Murase, 2025).
  - **Punishment implementation:** Direct, targeted (partner-chosen) and justified punishment outperforms indiscriminate or third-party punishment in efficiency outcomes (Dasgupta & Musolesi, 2025).
  - **Reward mechanisms:** Joint presence of punishment and reward may shift equilibrium toward maximal efficiency if policy parameters are sufficient (Vinayak, 2025), but costs must also be considered.

- **Context-dependent null effects and reversals:**  
  - In some regimes, enabling punishment provides little to no efficiency benefit, or can even reduce group payoff if the cost is high or if it triggers excessive cycles of mutual punishment (Kurokawa, 2023; Cooney, 2025).
  - The presence of communication, partner selection, or reputation systems can substitute for or enhance the effect of punishment (Billard, 1996; Larson, 2016).

- **Empirical and quantitative gaps:**  
  - Most findings are theoretical or simulation-based; few provide empirical effect sizes for real PGGs with punishment.
  - Direct mapping from control efficiency and design parameters to treatment efficiency is often scenario-, threshold-, and mechanism-dependent rather than universal.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **General rule:**  
  - **Punishment can, but does not always, increase efficiency** in public-goods-game-like environments. The effect is strongly dependent on game structure and parameters. "Punishment-enabled" does not guarantee higher efficiency than control.

- **When is efficiency likely to improve with punishment?**  
  - Punishment cost is not excessive relative to the benefit of cooperation (Greenwood et al., 2018; Liu & Guo, 2010).
  - Punishment is impactful enough to deter defections, yet not so severe or frequent as to be collectively costly (Cooney, 2025; Murase, 2025).
  - There is sufficient ability to (a) detect defectors reliably and (b) target punishment to defectors (Murase, 2025).
  - A critical fraction of the population is willing to punish, exceeding a threshold determined by parameter values (Greenwood et al., 2018).
  - Baseline efficiency (in control) is low to moderate and can be plausibly improved by reducing defection.
  - Reward or exclusion can further support or sometimes substitute for the efficiency effect of punishment (Vinayak, 2025; Inaba et al., 2016).

- **When is efficiency likely to stay flat or decline with punishment?**  
  - Punishment is too costly, indiscriminate, or misapplied, particularly in large groups or when the number of rounds is high (Kurokawa, 2023; Cooney, 2025).
  - Defection is easily detected and already rare; introducing punishment offers little incremental deterrence and only adds costs (Murase, 2025).
  - Social or structural factors (e.g., high perception error, poor network targeting, lack of coordination) make punishment ineffective or undermine its justification (Inaba et al., 2016; Larson, 2016).
  - Population composition does not sustain the necessary fraction of punishers (Greenwood et al., 2018).

- **How to use control game efficiency?**  
  - **If control efficiency is high:** Punishment may not improve and can even reduce efficiency if costs are incurred for little deterrence.
  - **If control efficiency is low:** Punishment is more likely to shift the equilibrium towards greater contributions, possibly yielding substantial efficiency improvement, provided punishment is affordable and justified.

- **Integrating design dimensions:**  
  - For prediction, incorporate player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_magnitude (or overall effectiveness), frequency and type of punishment, chat/communication, presence of partner selection or reputation, and group structure (demography, kin, or network).
  - Use reported or implied thresholds: e.g., "if (punishment magnitude * probability of being punished) > marginal gain from defection, and punishment cost is not prohibitive, expect efficiency gain." (Vinayak, 2025; Greenwood et al., 2018)

- **Ambiguity and caveats:**  
  - Ambiguity remains due to lack of empirical studies, context variability, and complex non-linear or threshold effects.  
  - Aggregate findings do not guarantee any individual instance will show an efficiency gain.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**  
- **player_count:** Frequently varied or analyzed as a substantive moderator of both behavioral and payoff outcomes.
- **num_rounds:** Common in repeated/intertemporal game analyses.
- **all_or_nothing:** Explicitly specified in many models.
- **mpcr (synergy factor):** Central in nearly all formalized models; commonly analyzed for interaction with punishment's effectiveness.
- **punishment_cost and punishment_tech (type/magnitude):** Detailed treatment across most relevant papers; recognized as key moderators.
- **reward_exists/cost/tech:** Addressed in several multi-intervention models.
- **show_n_rounds:** Occasionally modeled or controlled; less commonly theorized as a moderator of efficiency.
- **show_other_summaries:** Sometimes present (particularly in reputation and linking strategies).
- **show_punishment_id:** Rarely manipulated; usually not a focal parameter, but sometimes discussed.

**Indirectly informed or contextually discussed:**  
- **chat:** Occasionally included as a communication/coordination facilitator.
- **default_contrib:** Seldom directly analyzed; some discussion of framing/opt-in vs. opt-out, but not as a central parameter.

**Effectively missing:**  
- **Quantitative, empirical mapping:** Direct, parameterized, real-world-efficient predictions from design + control efficiency to treatment efficiency are rare.

**Special design mechanisms:**  
  - **Partner selection, reputation systems, group structure, detection accuracy, and institutional mechanisms** emerge as critical moderators in some papers, suggesting their importance for future empirical data collection and modeling.

---

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical data deficiency:**  
  - The prevailing reliance on theory and simulation means that real-world variability, psychological nuance, and institution-specific effects are not well captured.

- **Efficiency vs. behavior:**  
  - The preponderance of behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates) without conversion to group payoff or efficiency restricts the strength of prediction for the task target.

- **Context and external validity:**  
  - Many findings are conditional on idealized game structures, mechanistic assumptions, and simplified population traits.

- **Complex, non-monotonic relationships:**  
  - The effect of punishment on efficiency is often non-linear, with both beneficial and harmful regimes possible depending on parameters; simple rules ("punishment always helps") are not justified.

- **Incomplete coverage of design features:**  
  - Several relevant dimensions (e.g., punishment/reward framing, information sharing of punishment actions, chat, default contribution) are under-modeled or omitted from most theories.

- **Ambiguity/disagreement:**  
  - Some papers predict strong positive effects; others show that punishment can be net costly, especially with high cost, poor targeting, or low initial cooperation.

- **Lack of generalizable formulae:**  
  - While some papers provide analytic thresholds and formulas, these are not always applicable across the diversity of PGG designs seen in empirical practice.

---

**In sum:**  
The literature set provides substantial mechanistic and conditional insight into when and how punishment might change efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, but direct quantitative prediction remains limited by the lack of empirical studies and the frequent focus on behavioral rather than payoff-based outcomes. Predictors should use findings as guides to plausible direction and magnitude, not as guaranteed mappings.
