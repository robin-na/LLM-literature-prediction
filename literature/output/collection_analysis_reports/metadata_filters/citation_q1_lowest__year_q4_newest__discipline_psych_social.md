# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is broad, incorporating 120 papers. The core consists mainly of empirical, experimental (primarily lab-based) studies directly manipulating punishment mechanisms in public goods games (PGGs) or close variants. These are augmented by theory/modeling papers and a substantial body of contextually related or adjacent-game studies (e.g., trust games, CPRs, minimum effort games, dictator/ultimatum games, and reviews). The evidence base is strongest for lab PGGs with classic linear contribution structures, group sizes from 3–5, standard marginal per-capita returns (MPCR), repeated rounds, and options for costly peer punishment. A notable number of papers analyze payoff-based (efficiency/welfare) outcomes, but many focus on behavioral/psychological endpoints. Direct evidence on efficiency is available for the core PGG designs; theoretical work complements this with explicit mechanism analyses and conditions. Evidence thins for more complex or real-world settings, large groups, and reward/punishment hybrids, with many adjacent or nonevidence papers primarily offering mechanisms, conceptual frameworks, or context.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact:** A substantial subset (approx. 20–25 studies) are exact matches: standard or near-standard linear PGGs (e.g., Zhang et al. 2024; Ye et al. 2023; Joseph et al. 2025; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Botelho et al. 2022; Cobo-Reyes et al. 2022; Sparks et al. 2024; Duell et al. 2024; Prétôt et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2025).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Many others use CRDs, CPRs, threshold games, coordination games, trust/ultimatum/bargaining games, or other social dilemmas. These inform mechanisms or moderators but are less directly mappable to standard PGGs.
- **Weak/None:** Some review/theory/conceptual works discuss PGGs only obliquely, or use no game-theoretic structure.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact:** Most experimental PGG papers study peer- or institution-based costly punishment, manipulating presence, cost, effectiveness, or structure. Some include higher-order punishment, endogenous institution choice, or compare punishment to rewards.
- **Close/Adjacent:** Several focus on third-party or indirect punishment, antisocial punishment, or reputation-based sanctions, less directly relevant to peer punishment predictions in PGGs.
- **Weak/None:** A minority only reference punishment theoretically or thematically (conceptual papers, field ethnography).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact:** A good proportion (e.g., Zhang et al. 2024; Sparks et al. 2024; Joseph et al. 2025; Krügel & Maaser, 2025) report efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus explicitly.
- **Adjacent:** Many others report only contribution/cooperation rates or punishment/reward behavior, inferring efficiency indirectly or not at all.
- **Weak/None:** Substantial theory, mechanism, and context papers do not analyze or report payoff-based outcomes.

**Summary:**  
While the evidence base is rich for PGG+pgnishment, only about one-third provides exact matches for the full core outcome (efficiency after enabling punishment). Several highly cited studies and reviews emphasize that behavioral increases in cooperation are far more commonly reported than net efficiency gains.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes:** Group efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation), average earnings, total coins/welfare, and surplus are often reported in high-quality experimental PGG studies (e.g., Zhang et al. 2024; Joseph et al. 2025; Sparks et al. 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025).
- **Behavioral Outcomes:** Contribution rates, cooperation ratios, frequency/targeting of punishment (prosocial/antisocial), coordination, norm compliance, and emotional drivers (anger, fairness) are exceedingly common. Some meta-analyses focus exclusively on contributions or norm compliance, not payoffs.
- **Structural/Process Outcomes:** Voting on institutions, group convergence (ICC), preference shifts, strategic choice mechanisms, perception of legitimacy/fairness, and norm evolution.
- **Non-Payoff Outcomes:** Many theory or mechanism papers (and some field studies) analyze attitudes, psychological motivations, or institutional/structural adaptations, not payoffs.

**Distinction:**  
It is important to note that increased cooperation/contribution does not always result in higher efficiency, since costs of punishment may offset or surpass any welfare gains. Several papers explicitly note this (Botelho et al., 2022; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical synthesis:**

- **Classic PGG findings:**  
  - Enabling costly peer punishment often increases contributions and, in many cases, results in higher group efficiency (Zhang et al., 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Sparks et al., 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Duell et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025). However, positive efficiency effects are not universal—some studies show either no change or even reduced efficiency due to the direct costs and frequency of punishment (Botelho et al., 2022; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022).
  - The effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent on several design dimensions (punishment cost and impact, group size, rounds, information, monitoring accuracy, transparency), and whether punishment is sparingly or indiscriminately used.
  - **Early-stage costs:** In repeated games, initial rounds may see lower efficiency with punishment (due to sanction costs), but later rounds often recoup and surpass control efficiency as cooperation stabilizes (Sparks et al., 2024). The time path matters.
  - **Magnitude/effectiveness of punishment:** Large or highly effective punishment tends to produce stronger efficiency gains, while small/weak punishment may not and can even be counterproductive (Ye et al., 2023).
  - **Institutional/punishment structure:** Institutional (collective or monitored) punishment, formal sanctions, or competitive/deliberated punishment mechanisms usually yield greater efficiency than peer-to-peer punishment, especially under imperfect monitoring conditions (Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Duell et al., 2024; Prétôt et al., 2024).
  - **Enforcement credibility:** Punishment increases efficiency only when its enforcement is credible and perceptible (Alt et al., 2023; Eisenkopf & Walter, 2022).
  - **Transparency:** Outcomes and payoffs are improved when relevant quantities (e.g., endowments) are visible; under secrecy, punishment is less effective (Chen et al., 2025).
  - **Costly vs. costless sanctions:** Costly punishment robustly sustains higher efficiency; non-costly sanctions (disapproval) have little or only temporary effect (Sparks et al., 2024).
  - **Antisocial punishment:** The presence of antisocial punishment dampens or reverses positive effects on efficiency (Zhao et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025).

**Theory and modeling:**

- Theory work (Prétôt et al., 2024; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Uchida et al., 2024) establishes precise conditions (cost:benefit, monitoring structure, player cognition) under which punishment mechanisms produce efficiency gains. Generally, institutional/collaborative punishment with sufficient investment or credible threat can achieve efficient cooperation, while sparse, costly, non-institutional punishment often cannot.
- Theory also emphasizes the role of psychological biases (prospect theory, cognitive constraints) which may amplify or dull the effect of weak punishment (Uchida et al., 2024).

**Scope and exceptions:**

- Efficiency improvements from punishment are **not universal**: In some settings—especially when punishment is used heavily and costs are high, or group/norm structure is fragile or migratory—punishment can reduce efficiency (Botelho et al., 2022; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022).
- The effect of punishment is strongly moderated by institutional complexity (e.g., open vs. closed societies, endogenous institution choice, migration; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022), and information structure (transparency, feedback on contributions: Chen et al., 2025; Eisenkopf & Walter, 2022).
- Adjacent game types (minimum effort, CPRs, ultimatum, trust games) generally corroborate these findings in their domains, but their quantitative results may not always transfer to classic PGGs.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General guidance from the literature:**

- **Enablement of punishment in a standard PGG** (with similar player_count, num_rounds, MPCR, etc. to those studied) is most likely—statistically and theoretically—to increase average efficiency over control, especially when punishment is:
    - Costly to use, but with high effectiveness (at least 1:3 or 1:2 impact-to-cost ratio).
    - Used sparingly—i.e., if group cooperation quickly rises and the frequency of actual punishment falls after initial enforcement.
    - Institutionalized or collectively implemented, with high monitoring accuracy and credible enforcement.
    - Accompanied by transparency (visible endowments and contributions).
    - Embedded in a stable group over multiple rounds, with no opportunity for exit/migration.
- **Quantitative calibration:** Papers like Zhang et al. (2024), Sparks et al. (2024), Joseph et al. (2025), Krügel & Maaser (2025) report efficiency gains of 5–15 percentage points over control, but there is considerable variation depending on the parameters mentioned above.
- **Moderators of null or negative effect:** The efficiency gain from punishment may be null or negative if:
    - Punishment is low-cost and/or anti-social punishment is prevalent (frequent punishment of non-defectors)—see Chen et al. (2025), Zhao et al. (2024).
    - Costs to group from punishment exceed cooperation gains (Botelho et al., 2022; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022).
    - Indirect, weak, or reputation-based punishments are used in settings with high social capital or closed groups (Goto & Matsui, 2025).
    - Transparency or monitoring is undermined or endowments are secret (Chen et al., 2025).
- **Baseline efficiency matters:** If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is already high (e.g., >85–90%), enabling punishment may provide little or even negative efficiency change, especially if punishment becomes costly and overused (Botelho et al., 2022).
- **Reward and hybrid mechanisms:** Presence or absence of reward options, and their relative cost/effectiveness, influence both contributions and efficiency (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Peng, 2022). However, classic prediction focuses on punishment vs. no-punishment.

**Uncertainty and Disagreement:**  
- The direction and magnitude of the efficiency effect are not uniform across all studies, especially as design features vary and in more complex or field environments.
- Empirical findings and theory both indicate strong interaction effects between design dimensions, requiring multivariate consideration for accurate prediction.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**
- **player_count:** Most studies cover 3–5 players; evidence is rich and covers possible group size effects but is sparser for large groups.
- **num_rounds:** Effects differ by length; longer games often show larger efficiency gains as learning and coordination accumulate.
- **mpcr (Marginal Per-Capita Return):** Strongly moderated; lower MPCR (higher temptation) requires stronger/more credible punishment to achieve efficiency gains.
- **punishment_cost / punishment_tech:** Extensively manipulated; cost-to-impact ratio, mechanism (peer/leader/collective), credibility, and frequency are all well-documented as critical.
- **all_or_nothing:** Less often varied; most studies use continuous contributions, though several exceptions exist.
- **chat (communication):** Some studies control for or allow/no chat; generally, no-chat conditions are more represented in punishment experiments.
- **show_other_summaries, transparency dimensions:** Direct feedback and transparency of contributions and wealth are shown to strongly affect the effectiveness of punishment.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Occasionally studied as cross-conditions or in designs where both reward and punishment may be enabled.

**Indirectly informed or contextually discussed:**
- **default_contrib:** Only occasionally directly manipulated (e.g., opt-in/opt-out framing).
- **show_n_rounds:** Sometimes manipulated for time horizon salience; less commonly primary focus.
- **show_punishment_id:** Only a few studies specify anonymity or visibility of punishers; not a consistent focus.

**Effectively missing or underexplored:**  
- Some dimensions (e.g., fine granularity of identity visibility in punishment, rich communication about sanctioning) are less systematically varied.
- Few studies address the full interaction of all 14 dimensions; most focus on a smaller subset.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency outcomes are underreported** relative to contribution/cooperation rates, leading to possible overestimation of punishment’s benefits on welfare.
- **Over-representation of small, homogeneous lab groups.** Findings for larger groups, diverse populations, or field settings are less secure.
- **Limited study of anti-social punishment, real-world norm variation, migration, and exit options.** Where these do appear, they complicate or reverse predictions (e.g., in open societies, settings with group exit, or where anti-social punishment predominates).
- **Non-linear and non-monotonic effects:** Theory and empirical studies suggest the efficiency effect of punishment is highly non-linear; too costly, too weak, or mis-targeted punishment structures can depress efficiency.
- **Reward and punishment hybrids are less explored.** Important for real-world designs or complex environments, but not systematically integrated in the literature.
- **Generalizability is restricted:** Most findings derive from stylized lab experiments with decontextualized stakes and anonymous matching.
- **Some critical prediction-relevant dimensions are inconsistently reported or manipulated.** For example, default contribution framing, identity visibility in punishment, and round-number salience.
- **Ambiguity/disagreement remains**—a minority of high-quality studies find that punishment reduces efficiency, especially when costs are high and cooperation is already at a moderate level.
- **Theory outpaces empirical calibration:** Many theoretical/mechanism results specify necessary/sufficient conditions but map less clearly onto empirical parametric predictions.

---

**In summary:**  
Direct, robust evidence supports that enabling peer punishment in standard public goods games frequently—but not universally—increases group efficiency above the control, conditional on critical game design features such as punishment cost/effectiveness, monitoring, transparency, and group baseline cooperation. Nonetheless, the presence of exceptions, contextual moderators, and reporting biases toward behavioral outcomes require careful, dimension-sensitive, and uncertainty-adjusted prediction.
