# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set for predicting treatment efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments with and without punishment is **broad, diverse, and methodologically rich**. It includes both **empirical laboratory experiments** and a substantial number of **theoretical models**. Of the sample provided, roughly half of the empirical papers are direct laboratory experiments in repeated linear or variant public goods games; others examine adjacent environments such as common pool resource (CPR) dilemmas, networked games, and social dilemmas with endogenous partner choice, or provide theoretical analysis of evolutionary and repeated-game dynamics.

The **empirical evidence is strongest for standard linear PGGs in small groups (n=4–5, 10–30 rounds)**, with clearly specified punishment, cost, and monitoring regimes. **Theoretical papers** supplement these findings by mapping out how key parameters (punishment cost, effectiveness, monitoring accuracy, group size, network structure, etc.) determine equilibrium efficiency. There are important studies of nonlinear production functions, centralization of punishment, endogenous institution formation, legitimacy and trust, and the interaction of punishment with communication and agreement.

Overall, the evidence base is **direct and informative** for the prediction task in canonical PGG designs, but **gaps remain** for large groups, field settings, nonlinear payoff structures, and designs with more complex institutions, social ties, or ambiguity in monitoring.

# 2) Task Relevance

We assess each dimension of target relevance as follows:

- **pgg_or_variant**: **exact to close relevance** for most of the core studies (lab experiments and theory papers) focusing on voluntary contribution mechanisms, repeated linear PGGs, and direct variants (VCM, weakest-link, step-level, CPR, etc.). Numerous adjacent papers expand to dynamic networks, exclusion/ostracism, or nonlinear payoff structures.
- **punishment_or_sanctions**: **exact relevance** dominates—many studies directly manipulate or theorize peer- or centralized punishment, both as treatment and control conditions. There is also close and adjacent evidence regarding reward, exclusion, endogenous monitoring, democratic legitimacy, and informal sanctions like exclusion or information-based punishment.
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: **exact relevance** is strong but not universal. Many empirical papers report direct measures of efficiency or group earnings, but a significant number (especially in adjacent domains) report only behavioral outcomes (contributions, cooperation rates, punishment frequency) or imply efficiency through changes in contributions and costs without directly quantifying it. Some theoretical models focus on equilibrium payoffs, which map reasonably well to efficiency.

In sum: **Coverage is best for canonical PGGs with exact punishment manipulations and direct efficiency/welfare outcomes. Adjacent and weakly relevant findings provide valuable context but are limited for precise prediction.**

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Direct payoff-based outcomes (primary interest):**
- Efficiency: Group payoff as a percentage of the social optimum, net of costs and punishment expenditure.
- Group earnings/welfare/surplus: Total or average payoffs across all players per round or session.
- Surplus relative to maximum: As in most laboratory PGGs.

**Closely related but indirect outcomes:**
- Contribution/provision rates: How much is contributed, which is mechanically related to (but not identical with) group payoff, as punishment/reward costs may offset the benefits of cooperation.
- Appropriation/extraction rates: Particularly for CPR or threshold cases.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (important, but distinct):**
- Punishment frequency/intensity
- Norm compliance
- Retaliation/counter-punishment/antisocial punishment
- Group selection, reputation scores, partner selection rates
- Communication content or consensus
- Approval/acceptance of rules/institutions

Notably, **many studies document increases in contribution or cooperation that do not translate into greater efficiency due to the costs of sanctioning**—a crucial distinction for the prediction task.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### Cross-paper synthesis of findings for core prediction:

- **Punishment frequently increases cooperation rates**, but **does not always increase efficiency**. The net effect depends on the balance between increased public good provision and the costs incurred through sanctioning (punisher and target) (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Colombier et al., 2011).

- **Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators)**, common in some cultures or environments, can negate or reverse the efficiency benefits of punishment (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Fischer et al., 2016).

- **Communication is a powerful moderator**: Enabling chat or normative messages in the presence of punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency and, crucially, neutralizes the efficiency loss otherwise observed with counter-punishment or retaliation (Andrighetto et al., 2016).

- **Game production technology matters:** In standard linear PGGs, punishment often increases contributions but can have a neutral or negative effect on efficiency due to high sanction costs. In weakest-link or complementary production functions, punishment has large, positive efficiency effects; in nonlinear or best-shot technologies, effects can be neutral or negative (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Karakostas et al., 2023; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).

- **Group size (player_count):** The effect of punishment on efficiency can be either positive or negative in larger groups, depending on the design of punishment institutions, legitimacy, possibility for coordination, and monitoring regime (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
    - In well-designed centralized or legitimate punishment regimes, large groups may see greater efficiency gains from punishment.
    - In decentralized/anonymous punishment with low legitimacy, efficiency may fall in larger groups.

- **Punishment institution design is critical:**
    - **Centralization:** Centralized punishment can eliminate perverse punishment but does not guarantee higher efficiency versus decentralized peer punishment—interaction with information/noise matters (Fischer et al., 2016).
    - **Legitimacy/identifiability:** Revealing punisher identity or instituting legitimacy (democratic selection, bystander approval) increases the effectiveness and efficiency of punishment with increasing group size (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Gatiso & Vollan, 2017).
    - **Stability/equality of power:** Punishment power that is unstable or unequal leads to races for control and reduced efficiency (Dorrough et al., 2017).

- **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** High punishment cost relative to effect may reduce or neutralize any positive efficiency impact (Kranz, 2010; Wang & Lv, 2019). Efficient (high impact per cost) punishment increases the likelihood that enabling punishment will enhance efficiency.

- **Monitoring/Information structure:** Imperfect or noisy monitoring undermines the positive effect of punishment and may increase wasted punishment expenditure, reducing efficiency (Gallo et al., 2022; Fischer et al., 2016).

- **Role of agreements and norms:** The efficiency benefit of punishment is greatly increased in the presence of a clear, group-level agreement or norm (especially a full cooperation standard) that can be enforced—punishment alone often fails to improve efficiency unless such a standard is present (Dannenberg, 2016).

- **Reward vs. Punishment:** Reward mechanisms are frequently less costly and more efficiency-enhancing than punishment (Colombier et al., 2011), but this is less the focus for the main prediction task.

### Disagreement and heterogeneity:

- There is **disagreement about baseline effects:** Some papers find robust positive effects of punishment on efficiency (Dutta et al., 2021; Kranz, 2010), while others report mixed or negative average effects, especially in the presence of antisocial punishment, costly decentralized punishment, or nonlinear payoffs (Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Colombier et al., 2011; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).

- **Network structure and endogenous monitoring:** The effectiveness and efficiency impact of punishment depends on network density and structure (Shreedhar et al., 2020; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should this literature inform prediction of treatment efficiency (with punishment enabled), given design dimensions and control efficiency?**

- **Baseline control efficiency is informative but not determinative:** Knowing control efficiency is helpful, but the treatment effect of punishment is highly contingent upon design dimensions such as group size, punishment institution, cost/tech, communication, monitoring, and public good technology/common pool resource structure.

- **Key moderators/triggers for efficiency boost with punishment:**
    - **Low to moderate group size with clear, legitimate, and effective punishment institution (well-calibrated costs/impact; high legitimacy/identifiability); communication is enabled; or a clear group agreement exists.**
    - **High/complex group size and/or anonymous, illegitimate, or highly decentralized punishment will frequently not improve, and may reduce, efficiency—particularly if antisocial punishment or noise is a risk.**
    - **Centralized or authority-administered punishment is not automatically superior; effectiveness is context-dependent and may benefit by elimination of antisocial punishment, but is susceptible to issues of legitimacy, corruption, and instability (Fischer et al., 2016; Dorrough et al., 2017).**

- **When to expect efficiency to rise with punishment:**
    - **Standard linear PGGs with small to medium groups, robust monitoring, moderate punishment cost and strong effectiveness, especially if accompanied by communication or explicit agreement.**

- **When to expect efficiency to stay flat or fall:**
    - **Large or very small groups with low punishment legitimacy, costly or unstable punishment regimes, high antisocial punishment, or high monitoring noise/information uncertainty.**
    - **Nonlinear production or best-shot tasks may see neutral or negative efficiency effects of punishment, as do environments with strong sanctioning but no communication/legitimacy.**

- **Design dimensions should be used actively in the prediction model:** Not all dimensions are equally informed (see below), but the empirical and theoretical evidence strongly supports interactive/moderated modeling—no single dimension is sufficient to predict the impact of punishment on efficiency.

- **Beware of overestimating positive effects:** The literature repeatedly shows that increased cooperation or contribution rates are not equivalent to efficiency gains when punishment costs are nontrivial or poorly targeted.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

### Directly informed:
- **player_count (group size)**: Strongly discussed; effects shown to vary with group size and structure.
- **num_rounds**: Main findings often concern repeated games over 10–30 rounds; effects of endgame dynamics and repeated play are established.
- **chat (communication enabled/disabled)**: Shown to be a critical moderator (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Kamei, 2019).
- **mpcr**: MPCR is core to defining public goods incentives and is structurally varied in several empirical/theory papers.
- **punishment_cost / punishment_tech**: Frequently manipulated; central to theoretical models' predictions and observed empirical effects.
- **all_or_nothing (continuous vs binary contributions)**: Specified in many experimental designs; nonlinear/binary variants show differing sensitivity to punishment.
- **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech**: Included in some papers for comparison (Colombier et al., 2011), but most focus on punishment.
- **punishment institution specifics (anonymity, centralization, legitimacy)**: Though not always neatly parameterized, evidence about public vs anonymous punishment, centralization, legitimacy (democratic selection), and identifiability is strong.
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id**: Occasionally manipulated; finding that feedback and identifiability affect efficiency via legitimacy/retaliation/compliance.

### Indirectly or contextually discussed:
- **default_contrib**: Rarely manipulated directly; some studies comment on contribution framing's indirect effects.
- **endogenous institution formation / monitoring regime**: Strongly discussed conceptually in several key papers.
- **network density/structure**: Addressed explicitly in networked/structured population studies (Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Shreedhar et al., 2020).
- **production technology**: (Best-shot, weakest-link, linear) Identified as a major moderator of punishment's efficiency effect, but operationalized under various labels.

### Effectively missing or under-informative:
- **show_punishment_id**: Specific manipulation rare, though some studies discuss identifiability at a high level.
- **Reward parameters in the context of punishment**: Most punishment-focused studies do not systematically vary reward dimensions (some adjacent evidence exists).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Underrepresentation of large-group, field, and real-world policy settings**: The overwhelming majority of high-relevance studies are laboratory-based, with n=4–5 and finite rounds. Generalizability to large-scale public goods or CPR problems in the field is uncertain; group dynamics and institution legitimacy may differ.

- **Sparse coverage of nonlinear production technologies and complex/realistic institutions**: Most strong efficiency-related findings are for linear PGGs. Games modeling ecological, nonlinear, or best-shot/weakest-link production are less well studied and show more ambiguous or negative effects for punishment.

- **Antisocial punishment and cultural heterogeneity often matter but are not always measured/discussed**: Cross-cultural studies identify strong variation in the prevalence and effect of antisocial punishment, but are too few for systematic calibration.

- **Ambiguity in monitoring and information environment**: Many studies hold monitoring constant or assume perfect information; real-world settings often feature costly, imperfect, or delayed feedback about contributions.

- **Lack of full factorial experimental designs**: Few studies vary all relevant design parameters simultaneously, making detailed interactive inference challenging.

- **Efficiency is often not directly measured or only indirectly inferred**: A nontrivial proportion of the literature uses behavioral proxies (e.g., contribution, cooperation rate), which overestimate the welfare impact when punishment is costly.

# Final Guidance

**Prediction accuracy will be highest when control efficiency is paired with careful attention to:**
- Group size,
- Number of rounds,
- Institution legitimacy and identifiability,
- Communication availability,
- Production technology (linear vs. nonlinear),
- Punishment cost/effectiveness,
- Information/monitoring structure.

**Beware uniform assumptions:** Punishment does not always increase efficiency—and can even reduce it—especially when poorly targeted, costly, or in environments with strong antisocial punishment or instability in institution design. Cross-paper evidence supports a nuanced, interactive view: **punishment’s efficiency effect is highly contingent on the specifics of the game and its institutional/behavioral context**.
