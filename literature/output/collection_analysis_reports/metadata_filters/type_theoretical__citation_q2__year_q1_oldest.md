# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set consists entirely of theoretical and simulation-based papers, with no new experimental or empirical studies. The coverage is broad in terms of models and game-theoretic frameworks, including standard and spatial public goods games (PGGs), various repeated social dilemmas, and public-goods-like environments with adjacent mechanisms (e.g., network rewiring, reputation systems, or voluntary participation). Many papers model punishment directly, exploring its costs, mechanisms, and efficacy in sustaining cooperation and affecting payoffs or efficiency; others focus on mechanisms such as reward, social structure, or network dynamics. The set is strongest for theoretical insights into how design dimensions interact with punishment to affect efficiency, and it offers a range of generalizable mechanisms and conditions under which punishment is expected to increase, limit, or sometimes reduce efficiency.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:** The majority of the papers are labeled as `exact` for public goods games or close variants, with many models matching canonical experimental PGGs either directly or in structure. Some papers are marked as `close` or `adjacent`, often focusing on repeated prisoner's dilemma, social networks, or indirect reciprocity which, while not PGGs, share analogous strategic environments and insights. Only a minority are `none` for direct PGG relevance.

**punishment_or_sanctions:** There is strong coverage of punishment, with a large number of papers labeled `exact` or `close`, focusing on peer punishment, pool punishment, metanorms, or adjacent mechanisms such as ostracism and exclusion. Several papers discuss variations of punishment (e.g., direct costly, exclusion-based), design features, and their strategic consequences. Some papers discuss reward as an alternative or complementary mechanism.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** About half the paper set targets efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes as the primary result—labeled as `exact`, `close`, or `adjacent`. These include explicit analyses of group welfare, average payoff, surplus, or system efficiency. The other half focus mainly on behavioral outcomes such as cooperation rate, contribution rate, punishment frequency, or norm compliance, which are relevant but not synonymous with efficiency. It is common for payoff-based outcomes to be discussed or implied but not measured directly.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes:**  
  - Many papers provide explicit modeling and analysis of group efficiency, average payoff, or welfare (e.g., Zhuang et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2011; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Kranz, 2010; Nakao, 2009; Heller & Sieberg, 2010; Nuño et al., 2010). Some report mean payoff in simulation, social welfare, or equilibrium outcomes as a function of design parameters.  
  - In several cases, efficiency is inferred from model equilibrium or the prevalence of full cooperation, but is sometimes only indirectly discussed.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  - A significant proportion of papers report as primary outcomes the frequency of cooperation, defection, or punishment (e.g., Shutters, 2012; Zhang et al., 2010; Prietula & Conway, 2009).  
  - Other behavioral metrics include norm emergence, metanorm compliance, retaliatory cycles, or rates of reputation updating.

- **Distinctions:**  
  - Papers often conflate or move between behavioral and payoff outcomes, but the distinction is clear in those focused specifically on group efficiency or payoff as a model target.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Findings** (from simulations/theory):

- **General Effect of Punishment:**  
  - The dominant result is that enabling punishment in PGGs or close variants increases efficiency relative to control (no punishment), often approaching the full-cooperation benchmark—conditional on sufficient punishment effectiveness, manageable punishment cost, and favorable group behavioral composition (Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Wang et al., 2011; Kranz, 2010; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012).
  - However, the efficiency effect can be small or negative if punishment is too costly, ineffectively targeted, or if antisocial punishment/retaliation is prevalent (Wolff, 2012; Rosas, 2008; Nakao, 2009).

- **Modifiers and Threshold Effects:**  
  - Efficiency gains from punishment exhibit threshold dynamics based on: (i) coverage/reach of the punishment mechanism, (ii) fraction of initial cooperators, (iii) punishment cost/effect ratio, (iv) group/network structure, (v) noise/retaliation. (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Wolff, 2012; Langlois & Langlois, 2004; Chassang & Takahashi, 2011).

- **Role of Game Design Dimensions:**  
  - Number of rounds (longer games favor efficiency gains from punishment; effects may not materialize in short games) (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
  - Player count and group structure modulate the efficacy of punishment: Small groups or those enabling targeted punishment yield higher efficiency benefits; in large or diffuse groups, individual incentive to punish diminishes (Kritikos & Bolle, 2004).
  - Mechanisms for rewarding, reputation, and voluntary participation can substitute for or interact with punishment, with reward sometimes yielding greater efficiency than punishment (Zhuang et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2011).

- **Cost and Technology of Punishment:**  
  - Lower punishment cost and higher punishment effectiveness increase the probability that punishment will increase efficiency (Kranz, 2010; Heller & Sieberg, 2010).
  - Forms of punishment matter: exclusion or reputation-based punishment can be more efficient than direct costly punishment (Rosas, 2008; De Silva & Sigmund, 2009).

**Theoretical/Mechanism Arguments:**
- Credible and appropriately designed punishment mechanisms can shift equilibria towards cooperation and higher payoff, but excessive punishment (or too much resource expenditure on it) reduces net efficiency (Nuño et al., 2010; Wolff, 2012).
- Network structure and the possibility of partner selection or rewiring can either amplify or substitute the effect of punishment (Zschache, 2012; Shutters, 2012).
- The mechanisms by which punishment increases efficiency are well articulated: it deters defection, supports norm enforcement, and stabilizes cooperative equilibria, but can also provoke cycles of retaliation and reduce group outcomes if poorly implemented.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

The evidence base supports the following for predicting group efficiency with punishment enabled, conditional on control efficiency and game design:

- **Expectation of Efficiency Gain:** Introduction of peer punishment should, on average, increase efficiency over control in standard PGGs, provided punishment is not too costly and is effective (Kranz, 2010; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010).

- **Magnitude Moderators:** The size of the efficiency effect varies systematically with:
  - **Punishment Cost and Effectiveness:** Higher cost or lower effectiveness diminishes efficiency gains; in fact, with excessive cost or weak impact, net efficiency can decrease (Nakao, 2009; Nuño et al., 2010).
  - **Number of Rounds:** Longer games allow initial punishment costs to be amortized and for cooperation to stabilize, making efficiency gains more likely over time (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
  - **Player Count/Group Structure:** Smaller groups, or structures allowing targeted punishment and information flow, yield larger positive effects; large/diffuse groups may dilute the effect (Kritikos & Bolle, 2004; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012).
  - **Baseline Cooperation:** If control efficiency is already high, marginal gains from punishment are smaller. If the control is dominated by defectors, punishment has greater potential to improve efficiency (Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Congleton & Vanberg, 2001).
  - **Type of Punishment Mechanism:** Exclusion and reputation-based punishment are associated with greater efficiency than direct costly punishment, particularly if over-punishment is a risk (Rosas, 2008; Langlois & Langlois, 2004).
  - **Presence of Reward, Reputation, and Voluntary Participation:** These can enhance or substitute the effect of punishment; in some dynamic or anonymous settings, reward alone may not sustain efficiency, but punishment with entry fees can be highly effective (Wang et al., 2011; Zhuang et al., 2012).
  - **Coverage/Reach:** There may be critical coverage thresholds, below which punishment is ineffective and above which there is a sharp transition to high efficiency (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012).
  - **Retaliation and Antisocial Punishment:** If punishment can be easily retaliated or misapplied, efficiency gains are diminished and may reverse (Wolff, 2012).

- **Key Predictive Dimensions:** For forecasting, the most informative design dimensions are: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech (effectiveness/severity), group structure (spatial/reputation), and, if available, baseline (control) efficiency.

- **Transfer to Adjacent Settings:** In repeated games or public-goods-like environments, similar moderation applies, but with more uncertainty. The logic and trend of positive efficiency gains hold so long as punishment is credible and not too costly.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed (modeled explicitly):**
  - **player_count:** Modeled in most papers, with explicit attention to its effect on punishment efficacy (Kranz, 2010; Wang et al., 2011).
  - **num_rounds:** Explicit in longitudinal models; shown to moderate the time course and stability of efficiency gains (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
  - **all_or_nothing:** Some models use binary contributions; effects compared with continuous-action models (Carpenter & Matthews, 2010).
  - **mpcr:** Standard parameter, key determinant of cooperation's attractiveness and punishment's cost-benefit (Kranz, 2010).
  - **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Core to almost every model; variations analyzed systematically; punishment effectiveness is a top moderator.
  - **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Included in models directly comparing punishment and reward, showing both are mechanisms for efficiency but sometimes with different effect sizes or conditions (Zhuang et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2011).
  - **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries:** Sometimes included as information structure; informative for models with reputation or repeated interactions (Chassang & Takahashi, 2011).

- **Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
  - **chat:** Discussed as a moderator for cooperation and punishment's impact in some mechanism/explanatory papers (Brandts & Fatas, 2012), but rarely modeled explicitly.
  - **default_contrib:** Framing is mentioned for behavioral outcomes, but rarely as a direct payoff moderator.
  - **show_punishment_id:** Punisher identification is discussed as part of reputation/third-party punishment, but with limited modeling.

- **Effectively Missing/Sparse:**
  - **game interface features** (such as interface manipulations distinct from structural game design), **nonstandard summary visibility**, or detailed **information treatments** are typically not modeled with reference to their isolated impact.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Absence of Empirical Data:** All findings are from theory or simulation. There are no experimental manipulations or observed effect sizes from human subjects.
- **Indirect Evidence for Some Dimensions:** While core dimensions (player count, mpcr, punishment cost/tech) are well represented, evidence is often indirect or missing for dimensions such as chat, default contribution framing, and explicit interface features.
- **Outcome Ambiguity:** Many papers report on cooperation rates or norm compliance as the primary outcome, not efficiency or payoff. Predicting efficiency from non-payoff outcomes requires inference and caution.
- **Transfer to Real-World Settings:** Theoretical and simulated results may not account for real-world cognitive biases, social preferences, or unmodeled sources of noise and antisocial behavior (e.g., antisocial punishment, mistaken attribution, or behavioral heterogeneity).
- **Nonlinear and Threshold Effects:** Many models predict sharp transitions or threshold effects (e.g., in punishment reach, cost, or initial conditions), making prediction sensitive to parameters but hard to extrapolate across design changes.
- **Design Feature Interactions:** Several moderators (e.g., group structure, presence of reward, possibility of retaliation) interact in complex ways; single-dimension variation may not capture the joint effect on efficiency.
- **Generalizability to Non-PGGs:** Results for adjacent environments (repeated PD, indirect reciprocity, exclusion, reputation) support mechanism hypotheses but require caution in quantitative translation to PGGs.

---

**Summary:** The theory- and simulation-heavy literature supports a robust qualitative prediction that adding peer punishment to standard PGGs increases efficiency—contingent on punishment being effective and not prohibitively costly, and absent widespread retaliation. Quantitative magnitude and the details of design moderation (especially for less-standard dimension configurations) remain uncertain in the absence of empirical benchmarking. Game design dimensions most directly and consistently informing efficiency prediction are player count, round number, cost and effectiveness of punishment, marginal per-capita return, and group structure. Dimensions such as chat, information displays, or contribution framing are less well covered. Many behavioral analyses (contribution rate, cooperation) are only loosely predictive of actual efficiency outcomes, so care must be taken not to over-interpret them for efficiency prediction. The main limitations are the lack of empirical outcome data and the necessity to extend model-based findings with caution to predict real-world or experimental efficiency.
