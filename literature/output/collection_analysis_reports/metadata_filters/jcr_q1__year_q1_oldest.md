# 1) Evidence Base

The supplied literature set is both diverse and extensive, incorporating over 300 papers spanning empirical lab and field experiments, evolutionary and game-theoretic models, and conceptual/theoretical reviews. For the present prediction task—assessing the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments compared to punishment-disabled controls—the evidence base is robust and high-quality on laboratory public goods games with and without punishment. It remains moderate to high for close variants (e.g., CPR games, indirect reciprocity games, spatial PGGs) and substantial for adjacent two-player social dilemmas (Prisoner’s Dilemma, trust games).

Empirical evidence is concentrated in standard lab PGGs with well-specified parameters (especially group size 3–5, 10–30 rounds, well-defined MPCR, and classic punishment cost/technology). There is also strong coverage of key moderators like group composition, punishment scope/targeting, monitoring accuracy, cultural context, and the presence of communication, rewards, or reputation. Theoretical work extends generalizability, explores parameter boundaries, institutional variants, and mechanism arguments.

However, for the full set of 14 prediction-relevant design dimensions, coverage is uneven—some (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, communication) are highly informed; others (default contribution, visibility of punishers, endogenous institutional choice, reward technology, showing number of rounds, feedback structures) are less consistently manipulated or reported. Payoff-related (i.e., efficiency) outcomes are well measured for canonical PGGs, but many behavioral or adjacent literature only provide indirect inference.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:  
- Relevance is **exact** for a large core of the literature focused on standard linear public goods games (VCM), repeated interaction, and classic punishment treatments.  
- **Close** for CPR games, threshold games, expulsion, contest/competition variants, and indirect reciprocity settings where shared dilemmas exist but are structurally distinct.  
- **Adjacent** for repeated PDs, trust games, and one-shot bargaining, which share mechanisms but differ in group structure.

**punishment_or_sanctions**:  
- **Exact** for studies manipulating peer punishment (costly, direct, typically with 1:3 or similar cost-to-impact ratios), including both exogenous and endogenously chosen institutions.
- **Close** for experimental and computational treatments involving expulsion/ostracism, pool punishment, centralized punishment/monitoring, or self-commitment mechanisms.
- **Adjacent** or **weak** for studies dealing with symbolic punishment, reputation/information effects, or adjacent regulatory sanctions (tax/fine schemes).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- **Exact** for studies reporting group payoff, total earnings, welfare, “efficiency” as a fraction of cooperation maximum, or directly comparable net payoff measures.
- **Close** where earnings or group payoffs are reported but not directly scaled to social optimum.
- **Adjacent/weak** where only contribution rates, norm compliance, or punishment/reward frequencies are reported, requiring inference for efficiency predictions.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes** (directly informing prediction):  
- **Efficiency or group efficiency:** Measured as the actual group payoff divided by the payoff from perfect cooperation in the given game.
- **Total group earnings/welfare/surplus:** Sum of all players’ earnings per group.
- **Aggregate coins generated, group net profit:** Direct monetary proxies for efficiency.
- Some high-quality studies report **treatment–control efficiency differences** explicitly (usually in % or normalized units), especially in lab PGGs.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (not direct proxies for efficiency):  
- **Contribution/cooperation rates:** Frequency/amount of public goods contributions.
- **Punishment/reward frequency/amounts:** How often and how much players punish or reward.
- **Norm compliance, variance/equality in contributions, trust, behavioral response types.**
- **Punishment targeting (prosocial vs. antisocial).**
- Many studies focus on these as main outcomes. They often correlate with efficiency but are not substitutes—especially since punishment can increase cooperation but reduce efficiency via resource destruction.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Enabling peer punishment in standard repeated PGGs generally increases group efficiency (average payoff relative to maximum) compared to no-punishment controls,** especially when baseline (control) efficiency is low (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Gintis et al., 2003; Sutter et al., 2010).
    - The size of the efficiency gain typically ranges from 10–40% in lab settings, but effect magnitudes vary widely (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Gintis et al., 2003).

- **The positive effect of punishment on efficiency is robust but not universal.** Important moderators and exceptions:
    - **Punishment is less effective or can reduce efficiency** when:
        - Monitoring is noisy/imperfect (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Grechenig et al., 2010; Abreu et al., 1991).
        - Punishment is misdirected (antisocial punishment is prevalent; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Herrmann et al., 2008; Fehr et al., 2008), or when spiteful punishment is common.
        - Groups are large or heterogeneous in motivation/payoff, leading to feuds and counter-punishment (Nikiforakis, 2008; Nikiforakis et al., 2012).
        - Punishment is weak, uncoordinated, or costly relative to its impact—sometimes reversing its effect entirely (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Bochet & Putterman, 2009).
        - Communication is absent; when present, communication often amplifies efficiency gains or substitutes for punishment (Ostrom et al., 1992; Janssen et al., 2010).

- **Severity, cost-effectiveness, and targeting of punishment matter greatly:**  
    - High-impact, low-cost (efficient) punishment yields higher efficiency gains—low-impact or high-cost punishment can reduce efficiency (Page et al., 2005; Egas & Riedl, 2008).
    - Unrestricted punishment can reduce group efficiency due to wasteful/antisocial use (Ertan et al., 2009).
    - Targeted punishment (restricted to low contributors/free riders) is most effective for efficiency (Ertan et al., 2009).

- **Some institutional or design features further moderate punishment's efficiency effect:**  
    - Centralized (delegated) or endogenous punishment (by group vote/choice) can outperform decentralized peer punishment (Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Kosfeld et al., 2009).
    - Exclusion/ostracism mechanisms often yield even greater efficiency gains than standard monetary punishment—with the threat alone sufficient (Cinyabuguma et al., 2005; Masclet, 2003).
    - Conjunction with reputation or communication mechanisms increases the efficiency return of punishment (Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006; Bochet & Putterman, 2009).

- **In certain contexts, enabling punishment reduces efficiency:**
    - If the social or cultural environment fosters antisocial punishment or destructive retaliation (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Fehr et al., 2008).
    - If the group experiences feuds, vendettas, or high counter-punishment rates (Nikiforakis, 2008; Fehl et al., 2012).

- **Control (no-punishment) efficiency is a strong but imperfect predictor of treatment (with punishment) efficiency:**  
    - The efficiency gain from punishment is larger when control efficiency is low (i.e., when baseline cooperation decays).
    - If control efficiency is already high (due to communication, reputation, or external incentives), additional gains from enabling punishment may be small or negative (Ostrom et al., 1992).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct and reliable guidance:**
- In repeated, small- to moderate-group, linear PGGs with clear monitoring, enabling peer punishment is highly likely to increase efficiency relative to punishment-off controls—by 10–40%—unless punishment is grossly misapplied or too costly (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Gintis et al., 2003; Sutter et al., 2010).
- The predicted treatment efficiency can be estimated by applying an additive or multiplicative improvement (based on prior studies’ magnitude and game design match) to the control game’s efficiency, **moderated by the specific parameterization of punishment cost, effectiveness, and group features**.

**Critical dimension-level moderators:**
- **Player count (group size):** Effect strongest in small to mid-sized groups (3–6); weaker or negative in larger groups unless punishment is centralized or coordinated (Nikiforakis et al., 2012; Saak, 2012).
- **num_rounds:** Longer games allow punishment effects to emerge and stabilize cooperation, supporting sustained efficiency gains (Frean & Abraham, 2004; Fehr & Gächter, 2000).
- **chat/communication:** Absence of chat reduces the positive effect of punishment; communication alone can substitute for or amplify punishment effects (Ostrom et al., 1992; Bochet & Putterman, 2009).
- **mpcr:** Lower MPCR (i.e., higher temptation to defect) amplifies the efficiency benefit of punishment; very high MPCR may make punishment less necessary (Kosfeld et al., 2009).
- **punishment_cost/punishment_tech:** Favourable cost:impact ratios (e.g., 1:3 or higher) yield positive efficiency effects; high-cost, low-impact punishment does not (Egas & Riedl, 2008).
- **show_punishment_id:** Transparency (visible punishers) can moderate antisocial punishment (not directly isolated in most studies, but reputation/punishment visibility is a key moderator in theory).
- **reward_exists/combining rewards:** Reward alone or in combination with punishment can sometimes achieve even higher efficiency (Rand et al., 2009; Sutter et al., 2010).
- **information/monitoring quality:** Perfect monitoring of others’ contributions is necessary for a positive effect. Noise or indirect monitoring may reverse the effect (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Grechenig et al., 2010).
- **Cultural/contextual moderators:** Prevalence of antisocial punishment, group composition, and societal norms can completely reverse “typical” punishment effects (Herrmann et al., 2008; Barclay, 2004).

**If evidence is based only on non-payoff behaviors (contribution, cooperation rates):**  
- It should be used with caution for payoff-based prediction. Higher contributions may not yield higher efficiency if punishment is frequent and costly.

**Treatment effect is contextually conditional and not always monotonic:**  
- Strong, well-targeted punishment increases efficiency only up to an optimum; excessive or misapplied punishment reduces efficiency.
- The benefit of adding punishment is greatest when control efficiency is low and group-level enforcement is otherwise weak.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (i.e., manipulated and payoff measured):**  
- player_count, num_rounds, mpcr (and related production functions), punishment_cost & punishment_tech (cost-to-impact ratio), show_n_rounds (sometimes, when finite horizon is manipulated), chat (though less commonly allowed).
- feedback structures (show_other_summaries) are well characterized in classic lab PGGs.

**Indirectly or occasionally informed:**  
- all_or_nothing (binary vs. continuous contribution choices)—directly tested in a subset.
- reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech—sometimes included, especially in studies comparing or combining reward and punishment, but less commonly than core punishment dimensions.
- default_contrib—rarely manipulated.
- show_punishment_id—occasionally, and sometimes confounded with reputation/visibility treatments.

**Only contextually discussed or missing:**  
- Endogenous institutional choice (punishment chosen by group vs. imposed) is considered in a few key papers but not systematically covered for all designs.
- Cultural and social context, group identity, and baseline trust or propensity for antisocial punishment are acknowledged as key moderators, but rarely as design dimensions directly.
- Some important dimension interactions (e.g., endogenous vs. exogenous punishment size, design of monitoring structures) are less consistently isolated in the literature.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Strongest evidence is for canonical lab PGGs with peer punishment in small, short-to-mid-length repeated games, and full monitoring.** Predictions outside this envelope (very large groups, high noise, field settings, threshold/nonlinear production, CPRs, institutional punishment) require careful qualification and may not generalize.
- **Punishment can both raise and lower efficiency, depending on design and context.** There is no universal “positive effect”—the literature contains robust counterexamples (especially under noisy monitoring, high antisocial punishment, costly, ineffective, or weakly targeted punishment, or in specific cultural settings).
- **Most empirical studies do not manipulate all 14 prediction dimensions simultaneously;** most focus on a subset. Interactions among parameters (e.g., between chat and punishment, or institution selection and punishment cost/tech) are incompletely mapped.
- **A significant subset of studies measure only non-payoff behaviors.** These cannot be safely translated into efficiency effects unless supporting efficiency data or robust mapping is available. In some cases, increased cooperation comes entirely at the cost of increased punishment, leaving group welfare unchanged or lower.
- **Real-world and field settings, as well as large-group or dynamic population applications, are less well mapped,** and findings can diverge sharply from lab results (e.g., crowding-out effects, cultural variation in punishment targets).
- **Most studies focus on peer punishment;** centralized or delegated punishment, hybrid or institutionally chosen sanctions (e.g., via voting), and exclusion/ostracism variants are less extensively covered, but show systematically different effects.
- **Efficiency effects are rarely reported as a function of all moderating design dimensions** (especially for institutional arrangements, default contribution, and feedback structures).

**In summary:** The literature supports strong, but not universal, expectations that peer punishment increases efficiency in standard repeated public goods games with clear monitoring and small–moderate group size, especially when baseline efficiency is low. The effect is strongly contingent on key design dimensions, context, and the presence of antisocial punishment, misdirected sanctions, or noisy monitoring. Direct mapping from control efficiency and design dimensions to treatment efficiency is possible in well-characterized contexts but subject to the above caveats. Payoff improvement should not be inferred solely from increased contributions unless efficiency is directly measured or the cost structure is known.
