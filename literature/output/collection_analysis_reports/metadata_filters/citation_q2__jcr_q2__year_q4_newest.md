# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises a mix of experimental (lab and field) and theoretical papers, with a strong emphasis on empirical laboratory public goods games (PGGs) and related social dilemmas. Of the 43 papers, a minority directly report **efficiency or payoff outcomes** in public goods games with and without punishment; most focus on **behavioral outcomes** (e.g., contribution rates, punishment assignment, or cooperation frequency). A few high-quality field experiments and simulation studies supplement the empirical base. Theoretical papers often address evolutionary game dynamics, mechanisms of punishment, or adjacent strategic settings (e.g., prisoner’s dilemma, trust games), and provide qualitative or mechanistic predictions but rarely explicit efficiency data.

Overall, the set is **narrowly focused on laboratory and simulation environments**, offering high internal validity for standard PGGs and direct variants, but is **less broad regarding contextual, ecological, and applied environments** or less common game designs. The base is strongest for configurations with 3-4 players, repeated rounds, linear MPCRs, and canonical punishment implementations.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact**: Many papers conduct lab experiments or simulations in standard PGGs or very close variants (e.g., Peng, 2022; Pi et al., 2022; Wang & Huang, 2022). Some field experiments relate to resource dilemmas with PGG-like structures (Ntuli et al., 2023).  
- **adjacent/close**: Several studies employ adjacent designs (threshold PGGs, CPRs, PDs with group punishment, trust games, bargaining games); theoretical and simulation-based studies are often adjacent or close rather than exact.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact**: A notable subset directly manipulates peer punishment, exclusion, or related sanction mechanisms (e.g., Peng, 2022; Pi et al., 2022; Wang & Huang, 2022), including both individual and group punishment.
- **close/adjacent**: Some papers discuss punishment in the context of centralized sanctions, expulsion, or peer reporting, which are analogous but not identical to canonical peer punishment in PGGs.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact/close**: Only a handful of papers directly report **efficiency** (the key prediction outcome), group payoff, or total welfare as the main metric (e.g., Pi et al., 2022; Peng, 2022; Wang & Huang, 2022; Ntuli et al., 2023).
- **adjacent/weak**: Many more report on behavioral outcomes (contribution or cooperation rates, frequency of punishment), and sometimes draw inferences about likely efficiency changes but without directly presenting efficiency data.

**Summary**:  
The **relevance is highest** when predicting punishment effects on efficiency in standard or slightly modified PGGs, with some strong papers directly informing the task. However, much of the literature is indirect—most outcome data are **behavioral, not payoff-based**; thus, estimates of efficiency effects must often be inferred.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Efficiency, Group Payoff, Welfare, Surplus, Earnings):**  
- **Directly measured:** Some key experimental studies measure and report efficiency or group payoffs as a primary outcome (e.g., Pi et al., 2022; Peng, 2022; Wang & Huang, 2022; Ntuli et al., 2023; Melkonyan et al., 2022 [theory]; Lim & Capraro, 2022 [theory]).
- **Indirectly measured/adjacent:** Quite a few papers measure closely related but not identical outcomes (e.g., group achievement, resource stock, compliance, average payoff) in adjacent but not canonical PGGs.
- **Not measured or only inferred:** The majority report **non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (e.g., contribution rate, punishment assigned, cooperation frequency, norm compliance).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
- **Very common:** Many studies focus on mechanisms, antecedents, and moderators of cooperation, punishment, and group norm enforcement, as well as psychosocial variables (emotion, social identity, instruction comprehension).
- **Inferences about efficiency:** Often, increases in cooperation rate or punishment frequency are interpreted as likely to affect efficiency, but without direct calculation or reporting of overall group payoffs or efficiency ratios.

**Clarity on Outcome Types:**  
There is a **clear distinction** between studies that directly enable efficiency-based prediction and those that require extrapolation from behavioral metrics.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing the **directly relevant empirical** and **theoretical findings**:

- **Directly Measured Efficiency Effects:**
  - **Punishment does not always increase efficiency, even if it increases cooperation:**  
    In standard lab PGGs, introducing punishment mechanisms often raises contribution rates, **but the costs of implementing punishment frequently offset these gains**—resulting in no significant improvement, or only minor improvement, in group efficiency (Peng, 2022).  
  - **Network Structure Moderates Punishment Effects:**  
    The structure of punishment opportunities (who can punish whom) critically affects efficiency: incomplete punishment networks (circle, pairwise) can yield higher efficiency than complete networks, possibly due to reduced bystander effects and more effective targeting of punishment (Pi et al., 2022).
  - **Cost and Technology of Punishment Matter:**  
    Lower punishment costs or mechanisms that reduce the cost of effective punishment (e.g., reputation-based, institutional support) are predicted, both empirically and theoretically, to generate larger efficiency gains (Pi et al., 2022; Kang et al., 2024 [theory]; Lim & Capraro, 2022 [theory]; Melkonyan et al., 2022 [theory]).
  - **MPCR as a Moderator:**  
    The marginal per-capita return influences both baseline cooperation and the effectiveness of punishment; high-MPCR games may already have high control efficiency, muting possible gains from punishment, while low-MPCR games exhibit larger punishment effects when the mechanism is strong or reputational (Pi et al., 2022; Ntuli et al., 2023).
  - **Communication (Chat):**  
    Where communication is allowed, average efficiency can be higher in both punishment and control conditions; punishment remains effective at increasing efficiency even with chat (Ntuli et al., 2023).
  - **Empirical exceptions:**  
    In some designs, higher punishment *reduces* efficiency, especially if antisocial punishment is prevalent or if punishment is not well targeted (Zhang et al., 2022 [adjacent, behavioral]).

- **Theoretical/Simulation Insights:**  
  - **Synergy and Nonlinearity:**  
    Synergistic punishment (joint punishment by multiple group members) or nonlinear punishment effects can enhance the stability of cooperation, particularly in structured populations (Lv et al., 2022; Lim & Capraro, 2022).
  - **Credible Threats Increase Efficiency:**  
    The ability to credibly commit to punishment (even if costly) is crucial for efficiency gains (Melkonyan et al., 2022).
  - **Combination with Rewards:**  
    Systems combining positive (reward) and negative (punishment) incentives are generally predicted to be more effective at increasing cooperation and, by extension, efficiency, but only when cost structures are favorable (Pan et al., 2022; Zu et al., 2022 [indirect]).

- **Behavioral Moderators and Indirect Evidence:**
  - **Instruction Clarity and Comprehension:**  
    The efficacy of punishment for efficiency is higher when participants thoroughly understand the game structure (Ramalingam & Stoddard, 2024).
  - **Cultural, Social, and Psychological Factors:**  
    Cross-cultural differences in response to punishment may influence efficiency, but direct evidence is limited; where the effect of punishment on contributions is muted (Weber et al., 2023), efficiency gains may be absent.

- **Field and Policy-Relevant Evidence:**
  - **Real-world CPR Games:**  
    In resource management settings, costly punishment-imposed quotas can cause **large, positive, and robust gains in efficiency, even when baseline efficiency is low** (Ntuli et al., 2023).

- **General Ambiguity and Divergence:**
  - **Conflicting Effects Across Contexts:**  
    There is **ambiguity and disagreement** about whether punishment increases or fails to increase efficiency, even when it reliably increases cooperation. This is highly sensitive to **punishment costs**, **structural design**, and behavioral context.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Based on this literature, prediction of treatment efficiency in PGG-like environments should proceed as follows:**

- **Punishment’s effect on efficiency is conditional and often nonlinear**—it depends not only on its presence, but on the fine structure of punishment technology, costs, network structure, and baseline efficiency.
- **Direct empirical evidence** shows that:
  - **Enabling punishment increases efficiency** versus control *when* punishment costs are non-excessive and when network structure allows targeted, effective sanctioning (Pi et al., 2022; Wang & Huang, 2022; Ntuli et al., 2023).
  - **If punishment is costly to assign** and poorly coordinated (or antisocial), it may *not* increase efficiency—in some cases, efficiency can stay flat or decrease (Peng, 2022).
- **High control efficiency** (i.e., when the no-punishment game already achieves near-maximum returns) generally limits the observed effect of punishment on efficiency; largest gains are seen when baseline efficiency is low due to high rates of free riding (Ntuli et al., 2023; supported by theory).
- **Punishment design details strongly moderate its effects.** For prediction, game dimensions that are well-supported (player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech), as well as network structure allowing or constraining who punishes whom, should be prioritized.
- **Where direct efficiency data are not available, behavioral outcomes (contributions, cooperation rates) are only proxies.** While higher cooperation rates often go together with higher efficiency, this is not always the case due to the cost of punishment, which may offset or exceed the gains from increased cooperation.

**Quantitative prediction should be anchored in studies reporting both control and treatment efficiency (Pi et al., 2022; Ntuli et al., 2023), with careful adjustment for matching design dimensions.** Where only behavioral-proxy data are available, uncertainty should be explicitly increased.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- **player_count**: Frequently manipulated/reported in both experimental and theoretical studies (range: 2–5 most common). Effects on efficiency are directly documented, especially with regard to network structure.
- **num_rounds**: Standard repeated games; typical range 5–20; effects on learning, stability, and anticipation of end-game.
- **mpcr**: Central moderator; high/low MPCR contexts probed for baseline efficiency sensitivity and for how punishment interacts with the tendency for free riding.
- **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech**: Many papers manipulate or model punishment cost per unit, targeting, and decision rules; **network structure of punishment** is particularly salient (Pi et al., 2022; Lv et al., 2022).
- **chat**: Presence/absence of chat (communication) is reported in several papers, with general evidence that communication increases baseline efficiency but does not eliminate the efficacy of punishment (Ntuli et al., 2023).
- **all_or_nothing/contribution mechanism**: Both binary and continuous decisions examined.
- **show_n_rounds**, **show_other_summaries**: Sometimes specified; their effect is less systematically probed but often held constant.

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**
- **default_contrib**: Rarely manipulated; a few adjacent studies consider opt-in vs. opt-out effects (Civai et al., 2022, context only).
- **show_punishment_id**: Experimental visibility of punishers and group summary statistics is reported in some studies (occasionally in mechanisms discussion).
- **reward_exists**, **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**: Occasionally discussed/modelled, especially in theory papers combining reward and punishment; seldom central to payoff analysis.

**Effectively Missing:**
- **No systematic evidence** on the effects of varying defaults (default_contrib), reward magnitude/cost, or visual displays beyond summary reports.
- **Sparse data** on the effect of explicit display of the number of rounds, identities, or summaries in direct translation to efficiency.
- **Punishment magnitude (punishment_magnitude)** is rarely varied independently of punishment cost, and is thus not isolated as a key moderator.

# 7) Important Limitations

**Specific to this literature set:**
- **Few studies report both control and treatment efficiency** for matched conditions, limiting the base for quantitative prediction. Most studies report changes in cooperation or punishment rates, requiring indirect inference.
- **Heterogeneity in design and reporting**: Even among similar lab PGGs, variations in player numbers, MPCR, group assignment, and punishment protocol make direct cross-study aggregation challenging.
- **Ambiguity in behavioral-proxy interpretation:** Efficiency changes cannot reliably be inferred from cooperation increases unless punishment costs are precisely reported and analyzed.
- **Sparse coverage of less common game dimensions:** Defaults, identity revelation, reward features, and certain information conditions are largely absent or contextually discussed only.
- **Limited field and real-world evidence:** Generalizability to population-scale, field, or applied policy environments is weak; laboratory findings may not translate to large, heterogeneous social groups or networks.

**In sum:**  
The literature supports careful, dimension-sensitive prediction of treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency in PGG-like games, particularly where baseline efficiency is low and where punishment is well-designed (moderate cost, structured targeting). In most other cases, the direction and size of efficiency effects depend critically on design features—most importantly punishment cost, network structure, and underlying cooperative incentives. Prediction in un- or underexplored parts of the design space remains highly uncertain.
