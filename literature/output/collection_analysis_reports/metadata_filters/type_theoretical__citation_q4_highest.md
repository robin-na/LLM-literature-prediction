# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is theoretical and simulation-based (i.e., pure theory, models, and evolutionary game simulations), with **no direct empirical or laboratory experimental studies** included. All included studies report results derived via mathematical modeling, evolutionary simulation, or analytic derivation. The focus is broad within the public goods game (PGG) literature and theoretical social dilemma research, but **all evidence is indirect** with respect to experimental or observational data. The set is broad as regards the diversity of game design parameters and types of social dilemmas modeled, but **narrow in empirical grounding**—findings are about predicted or simulated efficiency impacts, not measured ones from actual lab or field experiments.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**: **exact**. All core papers model public goods games or close formal variants, frequently with extensions (e.g., spatial structure, optional participation, exclusion, incentives).
- **punishment_or_sanctions**: **exact to close**. All papers analyze costly punishment, sanctions, or closely analogous mechanisms (e.g., exclusion, policing, ostracism, commitment enforcement) as an explicit variable in the model.
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: **exact to close**, with some **adjacent**. Key outcomes are always efficiency, group payoff, average group earnings, surplus, or equilibrium fitness—i.e., direct or closely-linked payoff metrics. Several studies also discuss or focus on the **mechanisms** leading to efficiency, but nearly all report efficiency changes as a modeled output. Some studies focus primarily on the dynamical or evolutionary stability of cooperation, but tie these to aggregate payoffs as a proxy for efficiency.

In summary, the literature is **highly relevant** for theoretical and simulation-based predictions of how enabling punishment affects efficiency in PGG-like environments, especially as a function of game design dimensions.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (primary, as required by the prediction task):**
  - **Efficiency** (group total payoff divided by maximal payoff under full cooperation): This is the main reported outcome.
  - **Average group payoff, total welfare, surplus, average fitness**: Used interchangeably or as explicit proxies for efficiency.
  - **Cost-effectiveness metrics** (especially for institutional schemes): Sometimes used, e.g., institutional cost per cooperation achieved.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (secondary):**
  - **Cooperation rate, fraction of cooperators/punishers, stability of cooperation/defection**: Frequently reported, but always distinguished from efficiency.
  - **Frequency of punishment, types of strategies, oscillation or phase transitions among strategies**: Used to explain or contextualize payoff outcomes, but not substitutes for efficiency as the main predicted variable.
  - Some studies analyze **mechanisms** (e.g., emergence of prosocial norms, robustness of equilibrium, invasion conditions), often as moderators for payoff outcomes.

The distinction is generally clear: efficiency or group payoff is the outcome of interest for prediction; behavioral outcomes are mechanistic/adjunct.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Enabling costly punishment** in standard public goods games (without complicating features) generally **increases group efficiency** compared to the control/no-punishment baseline, often substantially ((Fehr & Gintis, 2007); (Gintis, 2000); (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999); (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013)).
    - The efficiency gain depends crucially on the **cost and impact of punishment**: punishment must not be prohibitively costly for punishers and must be sufficiently effective at deterring defectors for large efficiency gains (Perc et al., 2017; Gintis, 2000).
    - In most models, inefficient or excessively costly punishment, or very large group size, can limit or negate efficiency gains (Barrett, 2016).
- **Variants and moderating factors**:
    - **Antisocial punishment** (punishing cooperators) can eliminate or even reverse expected efficiency gains ((Rand et al., 2010); (Hauser et al., 2014)), unless network structure or second-order effects suppress antisocial punishers (Szolnoki & Perc, 2017).
    - **Peer exclusion** (social exclusion of defectors) is often more effective and robust at raising efficiency than direct costly punishment, especially in spatial populations or optional participation setups (Sasaki & Uchida, 2013; Liu et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2019).
    - **Reward mechanisms** can complement or (for low cooperation thresholds) outperform punishment in cost-effectiveness, particularly for increasing cooperation from low baseline (Duong & Han, 2021; Chen et al., 2015; Góis et al., 2019).
    - **Voluntary participation** and **institutional context** (e.g., public visibility, central monitoring) can enhance punishment effectiveness or alter cost thresholds for achieving high efficiency (Sasaki et al., 2012; Hauert et al., 2007; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).
    - **Retaliation against punishers** and lack of punisher anonymity reduces the efficiency-enhancing effect of peer punishment, especially in large groups (Janssen & Bushman, 2008; Bendor & Swistak, 2001).
    - **Group size, mpcr, and repetition**: Increasing group size or decreasing mpcr generally makes cooperation and efficiency harder to sustain unless coordinated or institutional punishment is available (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; BOYD & RICHERSON, 1992; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
    - **Monitoring and reputation**: Information visibility (showing who punishes, summary stats) is critical; the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is greatly reduced or absent when monitoring is weak (ABREU et al., 1991; dos Santos et al., 2011; Kandori, 1992; Sigmund et al., 2001).
    - **Combination with rewards/other mechanisms**: Adaptive hybrid schemes ('first carrot, then stick') tend to minimize total cost and maximize efficiency for establishing and maintaining cooperation (Chen et al., 2015). Combining punishment and reward does not usually outperform optimal use of either mechanism under non-extreme cost conditions (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
- **Exceptions and negative/mixed efficiency effects**:
    - With **high punishment costs**, **pervasive retaliation**, or **antisocial punishment**, enabling punishment can actually reduce average payoff or group efficiency (Barrett, 2016; Gintis, 2011; Hauser et al., 2014; Kurzban et al., 2015).
    - **Institutional punishment (pool punishment)** can often sustain cooperation but at such cost that net efficiency is not improved, or is improved only under certain parameter regimes (Sigmund et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2019; Perc, 2012).
    - **Second-order free riders** (non-punishing cooperators) can undermine the sustainability of punishment, leading to unsustainable or oscillatory outcomes unless punished or excluded themselves (Perc, 2012; Sasaki & Uchida, 2013; Szolnoki & Perc, 2017).
    - In some models with risk or resource growth (public goods as common-pool), punishment of over-users is only effective at high or intermediate resource growth rates (Chen & Szolnoki, 2018).
    - **In repeated/partner choice games**, benign or forgiving types of punishment (forgiveness, temporary exclusion) yield higher efficiency than harsh, irreversible punishment (Ali & Miller, 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **For typical PGGs**: Enabling peer or institutional punishment (with sufficient cost-to-effectiveness ratio and in the absence of pervasive antisocial punishment or retaliation) should be expected to **increase group efficiency compared to the control**—often up toward the fully cooperative optimum, depending on the strength and visibility of punishment (Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Gintis, 2000; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999).

- **Prediction from control (no-punishment) efficiency, moderated by design dimensions**:
    - If control efficiency is low (group dominated by defection), enabling punishment typically increases efficiency substantially—**unless punishment is prohibitively costly, ineffective, or easily retaliated against**, in which case efficiency is unchanged or may decrease (Barrett, 2016; Kurzban et al., 2015; Sethi & Somanathan, 2003).
    - The **magnitude of efficiency gain** depends on: 
        - **Punishment cost** and **effectiveness** (how large the penalty per cost, and ratio to benefit of defection/cooperation)
        - **Group size** (with larger groups requiring more efficient punishment or institutional support)
        - **mpcr** (lower synergy needs stronger or more efficient punishment to achieve similar gains)
        - **Information/reputation structure** (public feedback, summary stats, punisher identification)
        - **Possibility of antisocial punishment** or counter-punishment
        - **Nature of the sanction (peer vs. institutional, exclusion vs. deduction)**
        - **Optional participation/volunteering vs. enforced play**
        - **Presence of rewards or hybrid incentive schemes**
        - **Number of rounds and possibility of strategizing over time**
    - If initial conditions (e.g., initial fraction of punishers, or cooperating clusters) matter, as in evolutionary or spatial models, predictions must take them into account: punishment can be ineffective if not present in sufficient frequency to invade (Brandt et al., 2006; Sasaki et al., 2012).

- **Indirect or Mechanism Guidance**: 
    - If only behavioral outcomes (contribution/cooperation rate) are available, they can be treated as **proxies**—gains in cooperation typically correlate closely with gains in efficiency in canonical PGGs, but must be **distinguished** when punishment is so costly that it outweighs cooperation gains (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Gintis, 2011).
    - In evolutionary simulations, payoffs and efficiency are typically tracked explicitly, allowing mapping between behavioral transitions and efficiency outcomes.

- **Contextual Caveats**: 
    - In **spatial/network PGGs**, local clustering or partner choice mechanisms may substitute for or enhance punishment; in some cases, social exclusion or partner switching is more efficient than costly direct punishment.
    - For **institutional punishment (pool punishment)**: high efficiency is achieved only if second-order free riders can be effectively deterred, and only if the equilibrium is dynamically stable (Perc, 2012; Liu et al., 2019). Efficiency can drop dramatically (sometimes discontinuously) if these conditions fail.
    - In **collective-risk or threshold PGGs**, both punishment and reward can improve group success (proxy for efficiency), but the relative effectiveness depends on the risk, the threshold, and group size (Góis et al., 2019; Vasconcelos et al., 2013).
    - **Anti-social punishment** and **retaliation** can greatly reduce or even eliminate efficiency gains from punishment—even if cooperation is increased, the cost may be too high for net improvement (Rand et al., 2010; Hauser et al., 2014).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

Which of the **14 prediction dimensions** are supported:

- **Directly Informed (frequently modeled as core parameters):**
    - **player_count** (group size): Most models explicitly vary group size.
    - **num_rounds** (number of rounds/game iteration): Critical in repeated games, evolution, and norm dynamics.
    - **mpcr** (marginal per-capita return, synergy): Usually central; all theory papers parameterize this.
    - **all_or_nothing** (binary vs. continuous contribution): Specified in nearly all models.
    - **punishment_cost** (cost for punisher): Always present when punishment is analyzed.
    - **punishment_tech** (effectiveness of punishment/fine): Almost always analyzed.
    - **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech**: Reward interventions and hybrid incentives analyzed in several studies (Duong & Han, 2021; Chen et al., 2015; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
    - **show_n_rounds**, **show_other_summaries**, **show_punishment_id** (information structure/reputation/feedback): Some models, especially those analyzing reputation, explicitly model feedback/visibility, punisher identification, public/private monitoring, etc.

- **Indirectly Informed (sometimes parameterized, less emphasis):**
    - **chat** (communication): Occasionally modeled as a type of information or coordination channel; more often discussed as a context modifier.
    - **default_contrib** (opt-in vs. opt-out framing): Framing effects mentioned in some models, but not a central theoretical parameter.
    - **optional participation** (voluntary entry, 'all-or-nothing' in participation): Highly discussed and explicitly modeled in work on voluntary public goods games and evolution of punishment/emergence (Hauert et al., 2007; Sasaki et al., 2012).
    - **Retaliation/identifiability**: Modeled as part of feedback/information; directly addressed in papers on retaliation and anti-social punishment.

- **Only Contextually Discussed/Speculative:**
    - **chat**: Not formally parameterized, but recognized as relevant for institution formation, coordination, or reputation.
    - **Information/monitoring structure**: Monitoring probability and public vs. private monitoring are sometimes formalized, sometimes only discussed as context.
    - **Intrinsic/extrinsic motivation**: These psychological dimensions are only occasionally parameterized.
    - **Endogenous institution formation, election, or voluntary punishment regime choice**: Occasionally modeled, otherwise discussed contextually.
    - **Reward/positive sanctions**: Rewards sometimes folded into models; in others, only mentioned as a contrast to punishment.

- **Effectively Missing (not parameterized or analyzed in this set):**
    - **default_contrib**: Explicit default framing (opt-in/opt-out) is very rarely modeled.
    - **Real-world communication, rich chat, or endogenous institution design**: Not formalized in these theory papers.

In summary: **player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists/cost/tech, and feedback/monitoring parameters** are all well covered for prediction. **Chat**, **default contribution framing**, and some nuanced information structures are under-theorized or only discussed, not modeled.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of empirical calibration**: All findings are theoretical or simulation-based. Actual behavioral or empirical validation is minimal or missing.
- **Generality vs. specificity**: Mechanically, models cover a wide range of design dimensions, but may not capture real-world factors (bounded rationality, error, limited information, implementation errors, heterogeneity in motives).
- **Behavioral realism**: Theory papers often assume rational or evolutionary agents; response to framing, emotions, changing group composition, and real-world communication are often ignored.
- **No direct mapping to parameter values**: While models specify critical parameter regions, the effect sizes cannot be directly mapped to typical lab or field experiment values without translation; magnitude and threshold effects are context-sensitive.
- **Uncertainty for practical implementation**: Key moderators identified (monitoring/reputation, anti-social punishment, retaliation, second-order free riding, group structure) are often simplified or idealized.
- **Intervention mechanisms may differ**: Many analyzed mechanisms (ostracism, exclusion, commitment, institutional incentives) differ materially in practice from canonical peer punishment designs; direct translation to standard PGG protocol is sometimes unclear.
- **Neglect of some design dimensions**: Dimensions such as rich chat, opt-out framing, voluntary institution creation, or dynamic group formation are only speculatively linked.
- **Potential for ambiguous or conflicting results**: In some models punishment can both increase contributions/cooperation and **reduce payoff/efficiency** if costs are high or anti-social punishment is present (Barrett, 2016; Hauser et al., 2014; Kurzban et al., 2015), highlighting the need to preserve this ambiguity in predictions.

# **Conclusion**

Theoretical and simulation-based literature provides **strong and nuanced support** for the expectation that enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments **often increases group efficiency** over the no-punishment control, **conditional on key design dimensions**: especially punishment cost, effectiveness, information/reputation structure, absence of anti-social punishment, and, for larger or less-cohesive groups, the presence of coordination or institutional support. 

**Critical design dimensions—player count, mpcr, punishment cost/effectiveness, and feedback structure—are directly modeled and interpreted.** Where only behavioral cooperation outcomes are available, they are strongly associated with efficiency unless punishment is so costly (or misapplied) as to destroy gains from higher cooperation.

**Prediction guidance from this literature should be interpreted mechanistically and with a focus on parameter region:** If the baseline efficiency is low and punishment is reasonably effective and not subject to high cost, anti-social use, or retaliation, models predict large efficiency gains; if punishment is costly or easy to exploit or retaliate against, or if monitoring is weak, efficiency gains may be absent or negative.

**Empirical calibration, context-specific psychological factors, and rich real-world or experimental features are missing**—so care must be taken if applying these predictions to non-theoretical settings.
