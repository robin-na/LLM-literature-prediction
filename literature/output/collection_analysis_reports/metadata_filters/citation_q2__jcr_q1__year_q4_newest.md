# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base consists of a large, diverse set of 113 papers, including empirical laboratory experiments, observational studies, and a substantial number of theoretical and model-based investigations. Empirical results are dominated by controlled lab experiments in both standard and variant public goods games (PGGs) and related collective resource management environments. A significant portion of theoretical contributions directly model PGGs or close variants. The breadth of the paper set is considerable, with high coverage of key mechanisms (punishment, sanctions, reward, exclusion) across a broad variety of institutional, ecological, and network settings. However, many papers focus on adjacent environments (e.g., collective-risk dilemmas, trust games, or prisoner's dilemma variants) or on behavioral outcomes rather than the primary prediction outcome (efficiency). Thus, while the paper set is broad for understanding mechanisms and moderators, the subset supplying direct, quantitative efficiency evidence is more focused, especially for classic PGG parameters.

# 2) Task Relevance

Task relevance is assessed on three axes:

**a) PGG or variant relevance:**  
- Many papers evaluate classic linear PGGs or standard close variants (e.g., threshold/timed, spatial or networked PGGs, or CPR games), giving broad coverage. The most robust efficiency results come from exact or close PGG settings.
- Some papers operate in adjacent games (Prisoner’s Dilemma, donation games, trust games), and should be interpreted cautiously for direct prediction.
- **Relevance labels:** pgg_or_variant=exact (many), close (many), adjacent (numerous), weak/none (a fair fraction).

**b) Punishment or sanctions relevance:**  
- Experimental studies and theory papers directly manipulate the existence, cost, or implementation of punishment/sanctioning mechanisms, including peer punishment, centralized punishment, reputation-based exclusion, or attack options.
- Many others discuss reward, exclusion, indirect punishment, or hybrid mechanisms, which are adjacent but may not translate quantitatively to classic peer punishment.
- **Relevance labels:** punishment_or_sanctions=exact (core subset), close (reward/exclusion; sizable), adjacent/weak (some signaling/reputation or structural analogues), none (many papers outside the main scope).

**c) Efficiency or payoff outcome relevance:**  
- The best-aligned studies report efficiency, group payoff, or earnings as their primary outcome.
- Many more focus on contribution/cooperation rates, norm compliance, punishment frequency, or strategic behavior. These are **not efficiency** outcomes and should be noted as such.
- **Relevance labels:** efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome=exact (less than half), close (group welfare, welfare proxies, group success; several), adjacent (contribution rates and behavioral proxies; frequent), weak/none (numerous, especially theory).

Direct evidence for the prediction task—estimating average game efficiency before and after enabling punishment in PGGs—is supplied by a limited but high-quality subset. Many other papers provide important mechanistic or contextual moderators, but with indirect or adjacent outcome measures.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**  
- Efficiency as the ratio of realized group payoff to the social optimum is reported in a moderate number of core experimental and theory papers (e.g., Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Salahshour et al., 2022; Eichenseer, 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Lv et al., 2023).
- Studies on group earnings, welfare, surplus, or total coins generated also provide directly relevant measures (e.g., Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Yaman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022 JPCM; Libois, 2022; Mondal et al., 2022).
- Some studies report related but less direct payoff proxies: probability of loss avoidance (collective-risk games); resource pool sustainability; group success rates.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (not efficiency):**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**: Most common outcome; many spatial/theory papers report only frequencies of cooperative strategies.
- **Punishment frequency/strategy frequencies**: How often players punish or choose certain strategies; prevalent in evolutionary models.
- **Norm compliance, fairness, trust, reputation**: Focused on behavioral/psychological outcomes, commonly in adjacent or dyadic games.
- **Punisher/rewarder reputation/signaling**: Some experimental studies focus on social perception and signaling effects rather than group outcomes.

**Ambiguity:**  
Where outcome labelling is ambiguous (e.g., “public good abundance” vs. efficiency), analysis must rely on context—explicit payoff comparisons or statement that efficiency is the primary outcome.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the literature:

**a) Enabling punishment in standard repeated PGGs:**
- **Peer punishment in standard PGGs (fixed group size, rounds, linear returns)** robustly increases efficiency relative to no-punishment baselines, provided punishment is not too costly, is targeted at defectors, and is not prone to antisocial use (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Eichenseer, 2023; Lv et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024).
- Group efficiency gains are typically realized after an initial learning period, as punishment suppresses free-riding and supports sustained cooperation (Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
- Efficiency improvements are sensitive to institutional details: cost-to-impact ratio (punishment_cost), targeting/noise (punishment_tech), and whether the mechanism deters antisocial punishment (Salahshour et al., 2022; Bahbouhi et al., 2024).

**b) Moderators and boundary conditions:**
- **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** Lower cost and higher impact favor greater efficiency gains. If punishment is excessively costly or inefficient, efficiency gains are small or negative (Wang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Salahshour et al., 2022; Libois, 2022).
- **Antisocial punishment or noise:** Noisy or antisocial punishment (punishment aimed at cooperators or random target assignment) can sharply reduce or reverse efficiency gains, sometimes leading to lower efficiency than the control (Salahshour et al., 2022; Gross et al., 2022).
- **Institutional alignment and learning:** Effectiveness increases when the scale of punishment matches the problem scale and when learning/memory opportunities are present (Vasconcelos et al., 2022).
- **Group structure/context:** Post-conflict social contexts or networked environments where punishment is misallocated or counterproductive can nullify gains or render punishment ineffective (Gross et al., 2022; Romano et al., 2024).
- **Decision rules/player heterogeneity:** Teams using unanimity rule to assign punishment tend to have higher efficiency, as destructive (especially antisocial) punishment is filtered out (Bahbouhi et al., 2024).
- **Threshold/resource feedback/collective-risk games:** In collective-risk settings, punishment increases group success (proxy for efficiency) only if sufficiently strong and credible; in some cases, overall payoffs may actually decrease due to the direct costs of sanctions (Jiang et al., 2023; Grimalda et al., 2022).

**c) Adjacent/close variants:**
- In resource management, trust games, and structured population models, similar patterns generally hold: punishment increases efficiency as long as it is strong enough to support cooperation and not so costly that its expense outweighs the cooperative gains (Wang et al., 2022 PRSB; Libois, 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Yaman et al., 2023).
- In other situations (e.g., costly contest, attack options, or contestable punishment), enabling punishment can reduce efficiency (Romano et al., 2024).

**d) Magnitude and qualification:**
- Experimental meta-analyses report sizeable average gains in efficiency when punishment is enabled. For example, Eichenseer (2023) finds enabling peer punishment increases group efficiency by about 0.47 (relative to optimum), centralized punishment by 0.26, and reward by a smaller amount.
- The presence of reward or exclusion sometimes substitutes or complements punishment, and the combined effect often depends on parameter interaction (Mondal et al., 2022; Lv et al., 2023).

**e) Negative or ambiguous effects:**
- If punishment is misdirected, costly, subject to antisocial use, or implemented in a normatively unclear environment (e.g., post-conflict or in-group contests), efficiency can decline (Salahshour et al., 2022; Romano et al., 2024; Gross et al., 2022; Grimalda et al., 2022).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**a) Direct prediction of treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency:**
- For standard PGG designs, enabling peer punishment is likely to increase average efficiency, *conditional on* punishment being affordable, targeted, and not antisocial. If the control game has very low efficiency (persistent free-riding), larger post-punishment gains are possible, but may also be offset by punishment costs if not well-targeted or if antisocial punishment is prevalent.
- Key design dimensions moderating the magnitude of improvement are: punishment cost and effectiveness (punishment_cost, punishment_tech), marginal per-capita return (mpcr), group size (player_count), and possible behavioral router mechanisms (e.g., default_contrib, team decisions).
- If control efficiency is already high (e.g., with communication, reputation, or high baseline cooperation), marginal gains from enabling punishment may be modest (see Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Eichenseer, 2023).
- Where design dimensions match those of high-quality empirical studies (e.g., group size, mpcr, punishment ratio similar to Lo Iacono et al., 2023), quantitative effect sizes can be transferred with more confidence.
- Efficiency gains are much less likely (or even negative) when (i) punishment is stochastic/noisy, (ii) punishment is costly and not efficient, (iii) social context undermines targeting of punishment (conflict history, group status, ethnicity), or (iv) antisocial punishment occurs with substantial frequency (Salahshour et al., 2022; Gross et al., 2022; Romano et al., 2024).
- In collective-risk and threshold environments, group success (a proxy for efficiency) increases only if punishment is strong/credible and group size does not dilute incentive to cooperate/punish (Jiang et al., 2023).
- The effect of reward, exclusion, or other mechanisms may substitute or interact with punishment, and care should be taken in applying findings to hybrid or non-standard designs.

**b) Use of indirect evidence:**  
Where only cooperation or contribution rates are available, these may support qualitative predictions about the direction of efficiency change, but should **not** be treated as quantifying efficiency itself (explicitly flagged in, e.g., Ding et al., 2025; Ling et al., 2025).

**c) Model-based results:**  
Theoretical models reinforce the empirical findings: as punishment cost decreases and its effectiveness increases, the likelihood that punishment increases efficiency (compared to the no-punishment baseline) rises. Nonlinearities (thresholds, bistability, feedback) can produce discontinuities or context-dependent effects (Lv et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024; Libois, 2022).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**  
- **player_count**: Robustly manipulated in many studies; group size tends to dilute incentives for both cooperation and punishment, with larger groups often needing stronger punishment or lower costs to achieve efficiency gains (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Eichenseer, 2023; Jiang et al., 2023).
- **num_rounds**: Most lab and model studies use repeated settings; the build-up of efficiency gains relies on sufficient repetition for cooperation to stabilize.
- **mpcr**: Variations in synergy/return rates are key moderators; higher MPCR generally increases efficiency and reduces need for punishment, but effect sizes are reported (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024).
- **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech**: Extensively studied; cost-to-fine ratios are decisive in determining whether punishment increases or reduces efficiency (Salahshour et al., 2022; Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Vasconcelos et al., 2022).
- **all_or_nothing**: Addressed explicitly in several models; discrete vs. continuous contributions can affect whether punishment maintains cooperation (Yan et al., 2023).
- **show_other_summaries**, **show_n_rounds**: Sometimes reported as context or feedback variables, less frequently subject to formal manipulation but noted to affect learning and strategic behavior (Vasconcelos et al., 2022).

**Indirect or contextually discussed dimensions:**
- **chat**: The presence of communication typically increases efficiency in control games and can reduce the incremental value of punishment (Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Bernhard & Cushman, 2022).
- **default_contrib**: Explored in only a few studies; opt-in vs. opt-out framing can affect baseline cooperation.
- **reward_exists**, **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**: Several papers address the role of reward (sometimes in combination with punishment); efficiency outcomes are directly compared (Mondal et al., 2022; Eichenseer, 2023; Wang et al., 2024 PRSB).
- **show_punishment_id**: Manipulated in a few lab studies; transparency of punishment source can suppress antisocial punishment and boost efficiency, but evidence is less general.

**Missing or under-informed dimensions:**
- The majority of spatial, evolutionary, or agent-based models do **not** systematically vary or report all 14 prediction dimensions. For example, **default_contrib**, **show_other_summaries**, and **show_punishment_id** are often absent from reporting or analyses.
- Contextual moderators (e.g., prior conflict, group identity, socio-ecological context) are discussed but not always parameterized.
- Feedback mechanisms, environmental coupling, and cross-scale institutions are represented in theory papers but less so in experiments.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Outcome specification**: A large fraction of the literature focuses on behavioral rather than payoff outcomes; contribution/cooperation rates should not be conflated with efficiency. Where only behavioral outcomes are available, predictions about efficiency must be qualified accordingly.
- **Generality and mapping**: The robust efficiency gains from punishment are clearest for standard, well-specified PGGs. Direct transfer of effect sizes to non-standard, spatial, or hybrid games should be done with caution.
- **Parameter ranges**: Many influential papers fix certain parameters (e.g., punishment ratio, group size, MPCR) and do not explore their interaction or boundary effects. The absence of systematic cross-design variation means that dimension-level predictions outside observed ranges are less certain.
- **Social and cultural moderators**: Although some studies address context (e.g., prior conflict, group status, culture), broad generalization across cultures or settings may not be warranted without qualification.
- **Negative and ambiguous results**: Some studies identify conditions under which punishment reduces efficiency—particularly when punishment is costly, not well-targeted, antisocial, or when game structure invites competitive or status-driven punishment rather than norm enforcement.
- **Indirect evidence**: Adjacent games (e.g., threshold/CRD, trust games, prisoners’ dilemmas with punishment) are not always reliably transferable to PGG efficiency prediction, especially when group dynamics or institutional features differ.
- **Reporting gaps**: Several relevant design dimensions are only sparsely addressed, which constrains fine-grained prediction in designs with, for example, unusual contribution modes, feedback structures, or identity transparency.

**Summary**:  
This literature base provides strong support for the robust positive effect of punishment on efficiency in standard repeated PGGs—conditional on punishment being well-designed, affordable, and well-targeted. However, generalization across all game designs, participant contexts, and institutional arrangements should be made cautiously, keeping clear the distinction between efficiency and cooperation rates, the specific parameter ranges studied, and plausible boundary conditions where punishment may not increase or can even reduce efficiency.
