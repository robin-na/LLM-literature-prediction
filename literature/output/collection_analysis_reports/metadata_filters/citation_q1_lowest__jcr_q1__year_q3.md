# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set includes 49 papers, drawn from both experimental (lab and field) and theoretical sources, with a substantive minority of agent-based and mathematical models. Overall, the set is broad in scope but with substantial empirical weight on standard and variant public goods games (PGG), and also includes a variety of adjacent paradigms (trust game, contest, coordination, spatial and reputation games).

Within this set, a significant number of papers (including Harrell, 2019; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Nicklisch et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2020) provide *direct empirical or theoretical evidence* for the impact of (peer or centralized) punishment on efficiency or related payoff outcomes in PGGs. Others contribute adjacent or indirect evidence, such as through non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution, cooperation, punishment assignment), mechanisms (e.g., emerging norms), or in settings that differ in crucial respects from standard PGGs.

Empirical studies tend to measure both behavior (contribution/cooperation) and, less consistently, group efficiency or total earnings. Theory papers often model payoff outcomes directly but may focus on evolutionary stability or expected payoffs rather than realized group earnings.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant**  
- **Exact relevance:** Multiple papers directly study standard linear or binary PGGs (Harrell, 2019; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Nicklisch et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2020).
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Other studies use close relatives—trust games, spatial or coordination games, threshold public goods, contests, or repeated social dilemma tasks.
- **Weak/none:** Some non-PGG settings (animal studies, biological markets, etc.)—these are not relevant to the target prediction task.

**Punishment or Sanctions**  
- **Exact relevance:** Many empirical and theory studies include peer or centralized punishment as a treatment or mechanism (Harrell, 2019; Nicklisch et al., 2021; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Gao et al., 2020).
- **Adjacent/close relevance:** Some analyze related sanctions (exclusion, legal remedies, strategic commitment, costly reputation assignment) or focus on reward or other incentive mechanisms.
- **Weak/none:** Several studies do not manipulate punishment as a variable or only reference it contextually.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcomes**  
- **Exact relevance:** Papers explicitly measuring group efficiency, total earnings, or payoff (Harrell, 2019; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Nicklisch et al., 2021; Heine & Strobel, 2020; Cagala et al., 2019; Berger & De Silva, 2021).
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Some provide adjacent outcomes—final wealth, welfare, net profit, income, or game-specific analogs.
- **Non-payoff outcomes:** Many otherwise relevant studies report only behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution, cooperation) and not group efficiency.
- **None:** A subset neither reports nor models payoff-based outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes**
- **Directly measured:** Group efficiency (earnings as share of social optimum), total group/payoff, profit, welfare (e.g., Harrell, 2019; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Nicklisch et al., 2021; Heine & Strobel, 2020).
- **Indirectly measured:** Income, net profit, average final wealth, surplus, welfare proxies, etc.
- **Modeled:** Expected payoffs, stationary abundance of strategies (theoretical and simulation studies).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**
- Contribution and cooperation rates (most studies).
- Punishment frequency, assignment, and selectivity.
- Punishment/reward decisions.
- Norm compliance, solidarity, or related prosocial behavior.
- Perceptions, learning or effort judgments.
- Social information choices and reactions.

**Distinction:**  
Even where increased contributions or cooperation are observed with punishment, these do not necessarily translate to higher group efficiency, especially if punishment is costly or misdirected. Some studies emphasize this dissociation (e.g., Kanitsar, 2019; Heine & Strobel, 2020).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Enabling punishment generally increases group efficiency in standard PGGs**, relative to control (no punishment), but the *magnitude* and *reliability* of this effect are strongly moderated by institutional design:
    - *Peer punishment* often improves efficiency, but the net gain is eroded by wasted expenditure from redundant, retaliatory, or spiteful punishment (Harrell, 2019; Nicklisch et al., 2021).
    - *Centralized (leader/manager) punishment* typically yields higher efficiency than peer punishment, due to lower aggregate punishment costs and less counter-punishment (Harrell, 2019; Castillo & Hamman, 2021). The efficiency benefit is robust across different mechanisms for leader selection (elected vs. exogenous) and presence/absence of accountability (Castillo & Hamman, 2021).
    - *Monitoring costs* and *information availability* are critical: punishment is only effective in sustaining efficiency when accurate, affordable monitoring is available (Nicklisch et al., 2021; Berger & De Silva, 2021).
    - *Feedback structure* matters: full transparency regarding punishment and contributions can reduce cooperation and efficiency (Engel, 2019; Berger & De Silva, 2021).
    - *Theoretical models* confirm that efficiency gains depend on willingness to punish, punishment cost and effectiveness, consensus thresholds, and conformity/collective enforcement (Gao et al., 2020).

- **Punishment does not always raise efficiency and may sometimes lower it:**
    - In *contest environments* (Heine & Strobel, 2020) or where baseline contributions are already high, adding punishment may reduce efficiency due to costly over-punishment and rent dissipation.
    - Costly or misdirected punishment (e.g., jealousy-motivated, parochial) can undermine or even reverse efficiency gains (dos Santos & Knoch, 2021; Matsuzawa & Tanimoto, 2018).
    - Some centralized punishment institutions impose costs that outweigh the cooperative gains (Safarzynska, 2020).
    - The mere presence of a strategic or punishment-enabling institutional feature is insufficient when control efficiency is already high or punishment is not cost-effective (Harrell, 2019; Berger & De Silva, 2021).
    - In spaces with high transparency or rich reputation information, deterrence via punishment can be undermined (Berger & De Silva, 2021).

- **Evidence on other design moderators:**
    - *Punishment cost*: Lower cost relative to severity enhances efficiency via increased deterrence; high cost can erode or reverse gains.
    - *MPCR*: Higher returns to cooperation generally increase the effectiveness of punishment in improving efficiency.
    - *Player count and num_rounds*: Most direct evidence comes from small group (3-6) and moderate round (10-20) settings. Theoretical results extend to larger groups, but empirical confirmation is sparse.
    - *Availability of rewards* as a comparison: In some environments, rewards are more effective and welfare-enhancing than punishments, especially when punishment costs are high (Heine & Strobel, 2020).
    - *Information structure* (who sees what—contributions, punishers' identity) and communication (chat) are critical contextual features but have mixed and design-dependent effects.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- If the game is a *standard PGG* (well-formed, linear payoff, moderate group size, standard MPCR), **enabling peer punishment typically increases efficiency relative to baseline**, **unless** the punishment mechanism is highly costly, poorly targeted, or the baseline (no-punishment) efficiency is already near the maximum (Harrell, 2019; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Nicklisch et al., 2021).
- **Centralized punishment** (leader/manager) is expected to increase efficiency *more* than peer punishment, due to lower aggregate punishment costs and better coordination, all else equal (Harrell, 2019; Castillo & Hamman, 2021).
- The **effect size** depends on punishment cost, monitoring accuracy, and design: affordable and accurate monitoring, low punishment cost relative to impact, and well-calibrated institutional design maximize gains (Nicklisch et al., 2021; Gao et al., 2020).
- **If baseline efficiency is already high**, or if punishment is misapplied or is itself welfare-reducing (e.g., in competitive, contest or exclusionary environments), **enabling punishment may not improve efficiency and can reduce it** (Heine & Strobel, 2020; Safarzynska, 2020; Bar-Gill & Engel, 2018).
- **Behavioral outcomes** such as increased contributions generally correspond with higher efficiency, but only when punishment costs do not outweigh the group gains (Harrell, 2019; Kanitsar, 2019; Langenbach & Tausch, 2019).
- **Where direct evidence is absent for a design dimension (e.g., all_or_nothing, default_contrib, show_punishment_id, reward tech/cost), prediction should be cautious.** The effect should be interpolated from similar designs, or weak priors applied.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**  
- **player_count**, **num_rounds**: Direct evidence across lab experiments and models.
- **mpcr**: Explicitly manipulated and shown to moderate efficiency outcomes.
- **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech** (severity/targeting, monitoring mechanism): Shown to strongly affect net efficiency.
- **chat**: Often controlled for; presence/absence occasionally tested, with some effects on baseline cooperation but less on efficiency in punishment-enabled games.
- **all_or_nothing** (binary vs. continuous): Directly varied in some experiments; most commonly continuous.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Sometimes manipulated, typically as counterfactuals; rarely reported as jointly present with punishment.
- **show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Information and feedback structures are directly studied (e.g., Engel, 2019; Berger & De Silva, 2021).
- **show_n_rounds**: Occasionally varied, mostly as part of standard instructions; not generally highlighted as a main moderator.

**Indirectly/contextually discussed:**
- **default_contrib**: Not centrally analyzed as a framing variable, but implied in some studies (opt-in/opt-out).
- **reward_cost/reward_tech**: Addressed in studies of combined reward/punishment (Heine & Strobel, 2020), but less in isolation.

**Effectively missing in this set:**
- **Explicit manipulations or analyses of default_contrib, show_punishment_id, detailed reward_tech/cost** are sparse; the interaction effects of these rarely appear in payoff/efficiency analyses.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Non-payoff outcomes dominate in some empirical work:** Many studies provide only behavioral (contribution/cooperation) outcomes without corresponding group efficiency or payoff data. This limits the precision of efficiency predictions, especially where punishment costs are significant.
- **Generalizability is constrained:** Most direct evidence comes from small-group, moderate-round, laboratory PGGs with standard parameters. Results may not extrapolate directly to large groups, real-world settings, or highly diverse design features.
- **Joint manipulations of multiple dimensions are rare:** Multivariate and interaction effects (e.g., effect of punishment cost *and* reward availability *and* feedback structure) are not well covered.
- **Contest, trust, and coordination “adjacent” papers** sometimes produce results that differ from standard PGGs (e.g., punishment reducing efficiency in contest or trust settings), but generalizability to linear PGGs is ambiguous.
- **Ambiguity and disagreement remain:** Some studies find null or negative effects of punishment on efficiency, especially where punishment is costly, misdirected, or when baseline efficiency is high. Others demonstrate large positive effects under precise conditions.
- **Sparse evidence on certain design features:** Dimensions such as chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, show_punishment_id, or the concurrent presence of reward and punishment lack extensive empirical payoff data.
- **Reward, exclusion, and alternate incentive mechanisms:** Less evidence exists for how these directly compare to or interact with punishment in predicting efficiency.
- **The role of group heterogeneity, wealth inequality, or social value orientation:** While some studies highlight their importance (Zhang et al., 2020; Gërxhani et al., 2021), these are not comprehensively analyzed together with punishment-enabled efficiency.

---

**Summary:**  
For predicting the effect of enabling peer punishment on group efficiency in PGG-like games, the literature offers strong—but context-dependent—empirical and theoretical support for a positive effect relative to control, especially for well-designed, low-cost punishment mechanisms with accurate and affordable monitoring. Centralized punishment typically increases efficiency more than peer punishment. However, the efficiency effect is offset or even reversed when punishment is costly, misapplied, or when the baseline (control) efficiency is already high. Game design dimensions such as player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost and monitoring, and feedback structure are well evidenced as moderators; other features are less informatively covered in this corpus. Non-payoff behavioral improvements with punishment are common, but efficiency gains depend critically on the cost and institutional design of punishment mechanisms. Ambiguity, context-dependency, and gaps in coverage of certain design features warrant caution and nuanced, conditional prediction.
