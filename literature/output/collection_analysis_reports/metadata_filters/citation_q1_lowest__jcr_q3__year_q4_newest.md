# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set (n=48) is broad, encompassing a mix of **empirical (lab and field experiments), theoretical, and simulation studies**. There is a strong representation of standard and variant public goods games (PGG), as well as studies on adjacent social dilemmas (such as common-pool resources [CPR], Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, and collective-risk dilemmas). Many papers include direct experimental comparisons of punishment versus control, while others are theory-driven or simulation-based explorations of sanctioning mechanisms.

- A **minority (~25%) of papers are empirical studies** that measure efficiency or related payoff outcomes under controlled manipulation of peer punishment.
- A **substantial number of theory/simulation papers** focus on mechanistic or evolutionary models (often with explicit payoff formulas).
- Several papers report only **behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, cooperation frequency)** or focus on adjacent dilemmas, not directly addressing efficiency.
- Some contributions are **conceptual or mechanism-focused** without direct measurement of efficiency.
- A **few notable empirical studies** include field experiments (e.g., in farming or governance contexts).

Overall, while the breadth is substantial, **papers directly measuring and comparing baseline and punishment-enabled efficiency in canonical PGGs are in the minority**, and there is marked heterogeneity across studies regarding population, incentive structure, and reporting standards.


# 2) Task Relevance

**Assessment on Target-Relevance Dimensions:**

- **pgg_or_variant:**
  - *exact* — About half the papers (e.g., Chen 2022; Sparks et al. 2024; Prétôt et al. 2024; Falvey et al. 2025; Oechssler et al. 2022) use standard or direct PGGs. Many others are *close* (common-pool, donation, or binary PGG), and a sizeable minority are *adjacent* (e.g., IPD, resource allocation, collective-risk).
  - *none* — A few papers (e.g., reputation theory essays) are not PGGs nor variants.
- **punishment_or_sanctions:**
  - *exact* — Several papers manipulate peer punishment or institutional sanctions as their core treatment (e.g., Chen 2022; Sparks et al. 2024; van Klingeren & Buskens 2024; Prétôt et al. 2024).
  - *close/adjacent* — Many papers involve non-peer (institutional, tax-based, automatic, or group-level) punishment, or discuss reward versus punishment.
  - *none* — About a third of the studies have no punishment/sanctions component.
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
  - *exact/close* — Only a subset directly report efficiency, group payoff, or welfare outcomes (e.g., Chen 2022; Sparks et al. 2024; Cooney 2025; Suzuki & Ishiwata 2022), though some theory papers derive steady-state payoffs or efficiency bounds.
  - *adjacent/weak* — Many focus on cooperation/contribution frequencies, not efficiency; some only model or discuss mechanisms without outcome measurement.

**Summary:**  
**A limited core of studies is directly task-relevant** (punishment, PGGs, efficiency), while many more are adjacent or informative only for mechanistic insight or baseline estimation.


# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related Outcomes (directly relevant):**
  - *Efficiency (ratio versus full cooperation)* — Measured in several empirical and theoretical studies (e.g., Chen 2022; Sparks et al. 2024; Cooney 2025; Suzuki & Ishiwata 2022; Vinayak 2025).
  - *Group payoff/earnings, total profit/welfare, surplus* — Used as proxies for efficiency or reported alongside it.
  - *Explicit formulas for equilibrium payoff* — In referenced theory papers.
- **Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
  - *Contribution rate/cooperation rate* — Most frequently reported measure, especially in agent-based and theory/simulation studies (e.g., Guo et al. 2023; Du et al. 2023; Kido & Takezawa 2024).
  - *Punishment frequency/assigned, norm compliance, strategy type prevalence*.
  - *Leader/follower behavior, coalition formation, honesty rates, or network responses*.
- **Conceptual/Mechanistic Outcomes:**  
  - Commitment, trust, perceived fairness, or qualitative group dynamics (often studied without quantifying payoffs or efficiency).

*Distinction is maintained:* For some theoretical or field studies, behavioral investment (e.g., in irrigation, conservation, or honest reporting) is interpreted as a proxy for efficiency, but not explicitly calculated.

**Key Gaps:**  
**Many papers do not measure efficiency or group payoff after enabling punishment**; instead, they infer likely effects based on changes in behavior, contribution, or cooperation rates.


# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesized Findings from Task-Relevant Literature:**

### Empirical Evidence (Direct PGGs with Efficiency Outcomes)
- **Effectiveness of Peer Punishment:**
  - *Heterogeneous Groups:* Enabling punishment does not improve, and may decrease, group efficiency relative to control (Chen 2022).
  - *Homogeneous Groups, Longer Games:* Costly peer punishment leads to sustained increases in both cooperation and efficiency, with effects stronger over longer time horizons and after initial resource destruction from punishment costs (Sparks et al. 2024).
  - *Common-pool Games with Endogenous Sanction Choice:* Graduated or strict punishment can increase efficiency and resource sustainability, especially when sanctioning is chosen endogenously by the group (van Klingeren & Buskens 2024).
  - *Field Setting:* Punishment can crowd out intrinsic motivation to cooperate and *reduce* group investment, sometimes lowering efficiency compared to control (Amirova et al. 2022).

- **Effect of Punishment Cost/Severity:**  
  The impact of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost-to-harm ratio. High punishment costs or poorly targeted punishment may lower efficiency even as cooperation rises (Cooney 2025; Kurokawa 2023; Murase 2025).

- **Alternatives and Moderators:**
  - *Institutional Punishment:* Theoretical and some empirical results indicate that institutionally organized, collectively funded punishment (or group-level sanctions) can overcome second-order free riding and produce stable, efficient cooperation, but only above a threshold investment (Prétôt et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2023).
  - *Communication/Coalition:* In many cases, alternatives such as chat, coalition formation, or conditional contribution mechanisms match or exceed the efficiency gains of punishment (Ball et al. 2025; Oechssler et al. 2022; Falvey et al. 2025; Herne et al. 2023).
  - *Punishment Technology (targeted/involuntary vs. voluntary):* Punishment is only efficiency-enhancing in large groups if it can be targeted and is involuntary (Sugaya & Wolitzky 2023).
  - *Antisocial or Misapplied Punishment*: Ineffective or antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors or for non-normative reasons) is a key explanation for neutral or negative efficiency effects (Chen 2022; Kurokawa 2023).

- **Game Structure Moderation:**
  - *MPCR/Enhancement Factor:* When returns from cooperation (MPCR) are low, punishment rarely increases efficiency and may exacerbate losses.
  - *Group Size:* In larger groups, punishment is less likely to enhance efficiency unless institutionally managed or precisely targeted (Kurokawa 2023; Sugaya & Wolitzky 2023).
  - *Reward:** Pure reward or hybrid schemes may be as effective or superior to punishment for improving efficiency in some designs (Chen 2022; Wang et al. 2023; Duong et al. 2024).

### Theory/Mechanism Arguments
- Models establish explicit boundaries and formulas, indicating:
  - *Non-monotonic/potentially negative efficiency effects* if punishment is expensive or frequent (Cooney 2025; Murase 2025).
  - *Institutional context, monitoring efficacy, and investment thresholds* determine whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency (Prétôt et al. 2024).
  - *Detectability of Defection:* Punishment improves efficiency only when defection is hard to observe; otherwise, its costs often outweigh the gains (Murase 2025).

### Areas of Ambiguity or Disagreement
- **Empirical studies disagree on whether enabling punishment increases efficiency**, with several studies showing positive effects (Sparks et al. 2024; van Klingeren & Buskens 2024) and others reporting neutral or negative effects, especially where punishment is misapplied, antisocial, or in the field (Chen 2022; Amirova et al. 2022).
- **Theoretical models tend to support punishment's positive role, but only under specific design parameter combinations** (punishment must be targeted, cost-effective, and/or collectively supported).

### Behavioral Outcomes vs. Payoff
- Many studies report *increases in contribution rates* with punishment, but this does **not necessarily translate to higher efficiency or group payoff** (e.g., Kingsley & Smith-Walter 2024; Guo et al. 2023; Kido & Takezawa 2024).


# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct, Quantitative Prediction:**  
  Papers with *exact relevance* (e.g., Chen 2022; Sparks et al. 2024; van Klingeren & Buskens 2024) provide the **strongest quantitative basis for predicting the efficiency effect** of enabling punishment in PGG-like environments, *conditional on design dimensions* like group size, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and baseline control efficiency.
  - In settings with **homogeneous groups, low-to-moderate punishment costs, longer time horizons, and effective monitoring**, enabling peer punishment is likely to *increase* efficiency relative to control.
  - In **heterogeneous groups**, with high opportunity for antisocial punishment or unequal benefits, enabling punishment may have *no effect or decrease efficiency* compared to control.
  - **Institutional/involuntary punishment** (e.g., fines administered by a third party; tax-funded sanctions) generally increases efficiency if costs are not excessive and if defection is difficult to detect.
  - **Punishment cost and targeting**: If punishment is costly or misapplied, it can lower efficiency even if contributions rise (Cooney 2025, Murase 2025).
- **Strong moderators include:** group size, heterogeneity (benefit and endowment inequality), punishment cost and magnitude, structure (peer vs. institution), and monitoring/targeting technology.
- **When only behavioral results exist (e.g., changes in cooperation/contribution), use caution**: Increases in contributions do not assure efficiency gains if punishment is wasteful, antisocial, or costly.
- **If only control efficiency is known and treatment design matches one of the task-relevant studies, use that paper as the primary analog**, adjusting for fit on the 14 design dimensions.

**When evidence is conflicting, predictions should be qualified**:  
- For heterogenous groups or real-world/field contexts, predict *no effect or possible efficiency reduction* with punishment (Chen 2022; Amirova et al. 2022).
- For lab-based, homogeneous settings with well-designed punishment, predict *small-to-moderate efficiency gains* (Sparks et al. 2024; van Klingeren & Buskens 2024).
- Always contextualize with reference to control efficiency, punishment structure, game length, and potential for misapplied/antisocial punishment.


# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count` (group size): Most task-relevant studies analyze its moderating influence on punishment efficacy.
- `num_rounds`: Game horizon is frequently reported; longer games show greater possibility for efficiency recovery (Sparks et al. 2024).
- `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return): Key moderator—low MPCR limits potential for efficiency gain from punishment.
- `punishment_cost`/`punishment_tech`: Nearly all mechanism and empirical studies emphasize the importance of these (cost, magnitude, who can punish, institution vs. peer).
- `all_or_nothing`: Binary/continuous contribution structure is addressed in several papers.
- `reward_exists`: Explored as an alternative or complement to punishment in several theory and empirical studies.
- `chat` (communication): Explored as an alternative efficiency-boosting intervention.
- `show_n_rounds`/`show_other_summaries`: Sometimes noted, but rarely analyzed as key moderators.
- `show_punishment_id`: Occasionally described, but not systematically varied in most studies.

**Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
- `default_contrib`: Framing is sometimes mentioned (e.g., opt-in vs. opt-out studies).
- `punishment_tech` (`targeted`, `involuntary`, capability): Critical in theory, but less so in empirical studies.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Reward as an alternative only in some, not the main focus in most.

**Effectively Missing/Sparse:**
- Some dimensions, especially `show_punishment_id`, `default_contrib`, and details on summary feedback, are rarely manipulated or discussed systematically.
- Contextual framing, personal/normative beliefs, and real-time monitoring are sometimes discussed but not systematically analyzed alongside efficiency or payoff.


# 7) Important Limitations

- **Scarcity of Fully-direct Evidence:**  
  Only a small subset of studies offers *exact comparisons of control and punishment-enabled efficiency* with fine-grained reporting of all relevant design dimensions.
- **Reporting Gaps and Ambiguity:**  
  Many otherwise-relevant studies report only behavioral proxies or omit efficiency/group payoff as outcomes, limiting applicability for quantitative prediction.
- **Design-specific Effects:**  
  The evidence base includes both classic, homogeneous lab games and heterogeneous or field settings; findings do not always generalize across these contexts.
- **Heterogeneity of Punishment Mechanisms:**  
  Confounding of peer, institutional, automatic, targeted, and antisocial punishment reduces comparability across studies and settings.
- **Limitations of Theory Papers:**  
  While theoretically informative, many models make idealized assumptions (e.g., perfect monitoring, well-mixed populations, infinite rounds) that may not map directly to typical PGG experiments.
- **Confounding with Reward and Coalition Mechanisms:**  
  Some of the largest efficiency effects are observed with reward-based or coalition mechanisms, not with punishment per se.
- **Absence of Field RCTs with Direct Efficiency Measures:**  
  Most field and real-world studies focus on behavioral change or investment, not measured payoffs or total welfare.
- **Ambiguity in Real-world Applicability:**  
  Several studies (e.g., Amirova et al. 2022; Chen 2022) suggest that punishment may backfire in naturalistic, heterogeneous groups, but these settings are underrepresented.
- **Lack of Systematic Variation on Several Design Dimensions:**  
  Some configurational variables (e.g., show_punishment_id, default_contrib) are sparsely manipulated, making it difficult to quantify their effects.

---

**In summary**, this literature provides some strong, design-specific guidance for predicting the efficiency consequences of enabling punishment in PGG-like settings, with important caveats about generalizability, context sensitivity, and mechanism. For designs matching those with exact or close evidence, efficiency predictions can be made with reasonable confidence, especially if control efficiency is known. For other cases, substantial uncertainty remains due to reporting gaps, heterogeneous mechanisms, and the frequent reliance on behavioral (not payoff-based) outcomes.
