# 1) Evidence Base

The corpus comprises 150 papers, with a strong mix of empirical lab and field experiments and a large set of theoretical/simulation-focused works. The set is broad in terms of diversity of game structures (public goods games [PGGs], close and adjacent variants like threshold games, IPD/PDGs, trust/coordination games, common-pool resource games, and information-sharing settings), institutional forms (peer, pool, and centralized punishment and reward), and population structures (well-mixed, spatial, networked, group-structured). The majority of theory and simulation papers include explicit quantitative or qualitative predictions about outcomes, whereas the empirical subset offers key causal evidence, especially in standard PGG environments. Many papers provide detailed mappings to game design dimensions, but a sizable portion addresses only cooperation rates and behavioral mechanisms without reporting explicit efficiency or payoff outcomes.

**Summary:** The evidence base is broad and deep for PGGs with peer, pool, or institutional punishment, but empirical, payoff-level outcome data are less extensive than theory/simulation, and non-payoff behavioral outcomes are more commonly reported than efficiency.

---

# 2) Task Relevance

### a) PGG or Variant
- **Exact relevance:** Most theoretical and simulation studies (and a subset of empirical experiments) focus directly on standard or spatial PGGs (e.g., Cobo-Reyes et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2025; Yang & Yang 2024; Cui et al. 2022).
- **Close relevance:** A substantial body generalizes to close variants—threshold games, IPD, trust games, coordination/weakest-link, collective-risk, and common-pool resource games—which share key strategic properties but may differ in update rules or incentive structures.
- **Adjacent/weak relevance:** Numerous papers analyze repeated PDGs or related dilemmas, or simulate only behavioral patterns without direct mapping to PGGs.
  
### b) Punishment or Sanctions
- **Exact relevance:** Many papers directly manipulate or model peer punishment, pool punishment, or institutional (tax-based/governmental) punishment, including central vs. decentralized forms.
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Some consider reward mechanisms, hybrid reward-punishment, or indirect sanctions (e.g., exclusion, partner choice, or environmental feedback), which may inform punishment effects analogously.
- **Weak/none:** Some control- or reward-only interventions, or settings entirely without sanctioning, are present (notably in the baseline/empirical samples and many adjacent mechanism studies).

### c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome
- **Exact relevance:** A limited but important subset, especially among theory and analytical simulation, reports group efficiency, welfare, or average payoff directly (e.g., Sun et al. 2025; Yang & Yang 2024; Lu et al. 2024; Gioffré & Tampieri 2025).
- **Close/adjacent:** Many more report contribution rates, cooperation frequencies, or proxy measures (sometimes with analytical or qualitative links to efficiency), but without payoff accounting (e.g., prevalence of cooperation, reduction in defection, evolution of punishers).
- **Weak/none:** Several influential empirical and theoretical works report only behavior, norm compliance, or psychological mechanisms, complicating direct transfer to efficiency.

---

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**A. Payoff-related (Directly Matching Prediction Task):**
- **Efficiency** (group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum) appears in a number of theoretical, simulation, and empirical studies, primarily those with direct mapping between contributions, costs, and payoffs (e.g., Sun et al. 2025; Vinayak 2025; Ishikawa & Fontanari 2025; Jia & Wang 2024; Lu et al. 2024; Gioffré & Tampieri 2025).
- **Related Payoff Outcomes** such as total group earnings, welfare, surplus, or net coins/resources, are variably reported and can usually be mapped onto the efficiency target when parameters are provided.

**B. Non-Payoff (Behavioral/Mechanism Outcomes):**
- The vast majority of simulation and experiment papers, especially those focused on dynamic models, report **contribution rates**, **cooperation frequency**, **fraction of punishers/defectors**, or presence/stability of cooperation. While these often correlate with efficiency, the mapping is not always monotonic, especially when considering explicit punishment costs, antisocial punishment, or non-targeted sanctions.
- A few studies also focus on psychological outcomes (social norm enforcement, perception of punishment, fairness preferences), network effects, or the evolution of social structure.

**Note:** It is common for research to infer likely efficiency outcomes based on behavioral measures, but direct measurement (including accounting for punishment/reward costs) is less widespread.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## General Patterns

- **Enabling Punishment:**
  - **Most PGGs and close variants** find that adding peer, pool, or institutional punishment increases group efficiency **relative to low-efficiency controls**—but only **if punishment is not excessively costly, is sufficiently effective, and does not generate large antisocial or misapplied punishment** (Sun et al. 2025; Yang & Yang 2024; Cui et al. 2022; Ohdaira 2025; Cooney 2025; Vinayak 2025; Gioffré & Tampieri 2025).
  - **Efficiency gains** are **largest when the control game has low efficiency** (i.e., baseline cooperation is low), the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) is moderate, and both punishment cost and risk of antisocial punishment are low.
  - **Pool/institutional punishment** generally outperforms peer punishment in terms of efficiency, especially in **open/migratory societies** or where group formation is endogenous (Cobo-Reyes et al. 2022).
  - **Reward mechanisms** may increase efficiency even more than punishment (Yang & Yang 2024), and hybrid or threshold-triggered interventions can be especially effective (Lu et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2024).

- **Limits and Moderators:**
  - **Punishment may reduce or not improve efficiency** if punishment is costly (punishment cost ≈ penalty delivered), if antisocial punishment is common, or if group baseline efficiency is already high without punishment (Gao et al. 2025; Ishikawa & Fontanari 2025; Calabuig et al. 2024; Herne et al. 2022).
  - **Large groups and high cost-to-harm ratios** make it harder for punishment to be efficiency-enhancing; efficiency gains are more likely in small groups or with strong centralized sanctions (Kurokawa 2023).
  - The **structure and feedback rules for punishment** (negative feedback, reputation-based targeting, fine redistribution) noticeably moderate both cooperation and efficiency gains/losses (Ohdaira 2025; Sun et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025).

- **Contextual Factors:**
  - **Resource feedback, environmental dynamics, and group structure** (spatial/networked vs. well-mixed; open vs closed groups; kin structure; presence of migration) shape whether punishment increases, diminishes, or has no effect on efficiency (Cui et al. 2022; Jia & Wang 2024; Chica & Santos 2023).
  - **Critical threshold and bi-stability** phenomena: Many models find **tipping points**—above which punishment triggers high efficiency (all-cooperation), but below which it might be neutral or detrimental.

- **Empirical Strength:** Direct experimental evidence for efficiency effects (net of punishment costs) is significant but narrower in scope than theory. Where reported, **experimental findings support a positive or mixed effect** of punishment on efficiency, often contingent on punishment cost/effectiveness and design (Cobo-Reyes et al. 2022; Pancotto et al. 2023; Gueth & Otsubo 2023; Lec et al. 2023).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Best-Informed Scenarios:** Directly relevant results recommend that, for PGGs or close variants with known design dimensions and observed control (no-punishment) efficiency, enabling peer or institutional punishment will:
    - **Increase efficiency** if baseline efficiency is low, punishment is not prohibitively costly, and strategic or group structure supports effective targeting of defectors.
    - **Have little to no effect** if baseline efficiency is already high, or if structural or psychological factors reduce the impact or occurrence of punishment.
    - **Reduce efficiency** if punishment is implemented with high frequency, misapplied, or has ineffective targeting or high cost-to-impact ratio.

- **Key Moderators (by Design Dimension):**
    - **player_count:** Effect varies non-monotonically; increased inefficiency and exploitation risk in large groups (threshold for punishment-based efficiency increases rises with group size; Kurokawa 2023; Ishikawa & Fontanari 2025).
    - **mpcr:** Higher MPCR (more efficient PGG) makes cooperation and efficiency gains from punishment less necessary; at low MPCR, punishment can trigger efficient equilibria that would not otherwise emerge.
    - **punishment_cost:** Lower cost to punishers and higher fine-to-cost ratios produce greater efficiency gains. High costs or misapplied punishment can negate benefits (Cooney 2025; Sun et al. 2025).
    - **punishment_tech:** Institutional or pool punishment often yields greater efficiency gains compared to peer punishment, especially when combined with central authority or taxes (Cobo-Reyes et al. 2022; Yang & Yang 2024).
    - **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech:** Presence of reward can amplify or sometimes outperform punishment for efficiency (Yang & Yang 2024; Lu et al. 2024).
    - **show_punishment_id and related observability dimensions:** Visibility and reputation mechanisms can modulate both norm enforcement and the effectiveness/appropriateness of punishment, shaping efficiency outcomes (Li et al. 2023).

- **From Control to Treatment Efficiency:** The magnitude of the predicted efficiency increase is highest **when control efficiency is low** and punishment can shift the group from a defection-dominated to a cooperation-dominated equilibrium. When control efficiency is already moderate or high (due to network reciprocity, communication, or other mechanisms), the predicted efficiency gain from punishment is smaller or absent (Gao et al. 2025; Cui et al. 2022).

- **Limiting Conditions:** Settings with high punishment cost, frequent execution, or mis-targeted punishment can result in **lower efficiency with punishment than without** (Calabuig et al. 2024; Herne et al. 2022; Cooney 2025).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed:**
    - **player_count:** Explicitly specified in most simulation and empirical works; findings depend strongly on group size.
    - **mpcr:** Central in virtually all PGG studies; often mapped exactly to model parameters.
    - **punishment_cost/punishment_tech:** These are core variables in most theory and many experiments, with explicit modeling of cost, fine, and implementation details.
    - **num_rounds:** Frequently specified; evolutionary outcomes daily depend on repetition/finite horizon (supporting predictions about sustainability).
    - **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech:** Often modeled in parallel with punishment (especially hybrid or tax-based interventions).

- **Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
    - **chat:** Rarely manipulated systematically (some evidence that absence of chat increases reliance on punishment).
    - **all_or_nothing:** Some studies examine binary vs. continuous contribution; impact is sometimes discussed but less often a main variable.
    - **default_contrib:** Framing of default as opt-in/opt-out is infrequently specified/measured.
    - **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Information and observability features are sometimes specified (notably in empirical experiments and theory on reputation/feedback), but only a subset directly tests their influence on efficiency/punishment effectiveness.

- **Missing or Sparse:**
    - **Detailed mapping of reward/punishment implementation technology (who can punish whom under what conditions),** reward cost/technology (apart from studies directly comparing reward and punishment).
    - **Heterogeneity in player attributes, endowment, or payoff structure** is discussed conceptually but less often manipulated experimentally.
    - **Dynamic adjustment (variable punishment cost or fine, endogenous adaptation):** Some recent models address, but this remains a less populated area for empirical games.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavioral vs. Efficiency Outcomes:** Most theory and simulation studies focus primarily on cooperation rates, which **do not always translate directly to efficiency**, especially when the costliness of punishment is high or when antisocial punishment is endemic.
- **Limited Empirical Payoff Data:** Direct, experimental studies on group efficiency/net payoff with and without punishment, net of punishment costs, are relatively rare; theory and simulation far outnumber lab evidence for efficiency outcomes.
- **Design Dimension Gaps:** Some key design moderators (information structure, contribution framing, and visibility of punishment) are underexplored or only discussed contextually, rather than as systematically manipulated variables.
- **Generalizability:** Many findings are **context-dependent**: e.g., the effect of punishment may depend on spatial/network structure, cultural/institutional environment, and specific parameterizations (e.g., specific MPCR, group size, or punishment cost ratios).
- **Complex and Non-Monotonic Effects:** Evidence is **not always unidirectional or monotonic**—advantages of punishment for efficiency can reverse under high cost or ineffective targeting, and threshold/bi-stable behaviors mean that control efficiency and initial conditions can strongly moderate treatment effects.
- **Reward vs Punishment:** Some studies find that **reward (or hybrid reward-punishment)** regimes can outperform punishment for efficiency; implications for mapping from control to treatment efficiency may differ under reward-enabled protocols.
- **Adjacency and Transferability:** A substantial fraction of evidence comes from close but not exact PGGs (e.g., repeated PDG, threshold games, or non-monetary sanctioning); **not all results are immediately transferable** to standard lab PGGs.

# **Bottom Line**

- The literature set gives **strong theoretical and moderate empirical support that, in standard and spatial PGGs, enabling punishment generally increases efficiency when control efficiency is low, punishment is not too costly, and punishers can effectively target defectors**.
- **Efficiency gains are not universal:** High punishment costs, group size effects, poorly targeted or antisocial punishment, or already-high baseline cooperation can diminish or negate efficiency improvements.
- **Most critical for prediction:** MPCR, punishment cost, punishment implementation technology, group size, baseline (control) efficiency, and presence/type of reward mechanisms.
- **Uncertainty remains:** Several dimensions (especially information/framing, observability, heterogeneity) and complex boundary conditions are insufficiently mapped, and empirical net efficiency evidence remains limited. Where behavioral or norm compliance outcomes (not efficiency) are reported, inferences should be cautious.
- **For treatment-vs-control efficiency prediction,** modelers should weight design dimensions by the existing coverage and attend to moderators evident in the literature, especially those influencing the cost-effectiveness of punishment.
