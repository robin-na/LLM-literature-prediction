# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is moderately broad, with 26 papers spanning empirical lab and field experiments, theoretical and evolutionary modeling, and a few observational studies. The mix tilts toward standard PGG and close variants, with several theory papers addressing population dynamics, evolutionary mechanisms, or social structure. Roughly one-third of the papers are strictly empirical with direct manipulation of punishment; the remainder are either theory/modeling, empirical studies without punishment, or adjacent/observational in non-human or natural contexts. The majority of empirical papers use clear, game-theoretic frameworks with directly measured payoff-based outcomes, though several studies focus on behavioral or process outcomes. Direct evidence for the prediction task is present in several core PGG lab and field experiments and a handful of closely related theoretical models.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**  
- *exact*: The literature provides a solid base of exact matches, including standard repeated linear public goods games and close variants (weakest-link, threshold, and sequential CPR games) with explicit manipulation of punishment (e.g., Fischer et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
- *close*: Other papers adapt the PGG framework for natural resource dilemmas (e.g., irrigation, field common pool resources), dynamic or evolutionary settings, or bacterial models (Sääksvuori, 2014; Javaid & Falk, 2015; Inglis et al., 2014).
- *adjacent/weak*: Some papers analyze dyadic donation, trust, and ultimatum games, as well as observational studies of animal cooperation. These provide mostly conceptual or contextual relevance.

**punishment_or_sanctions**  
- *exact*: Multiple empirical lab/field experiments and theory papers directly manipulate or model punishment or sanctioning (peer/centralized, ostracism, external, third-party), including cost structures and institutional design (Fischer et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Javaid & Falk, 2015; Sääksvuori, 2014).
- *close/adjacent*: Some studies include related mechanisms (ostracism as a group-level social sanction, reputation-based or signaling “punishment,” anti-social punishment) or discuss enforcement roles and threats rather than payoff-relevant sanctions.
- *none/weak*: A minority of studies (mostly observational or on unrelated game forms) do not manipulate or analyze punishment.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**  
- *exact*: Several studies report group earnings, efficiency (payoff as a fraction of the social optimum), welfare, or aggregate returns as primary outcomes (Fischer et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Javaid & Falk, 2015; Sääksvuori, 2014; Eldakar et al., 2013; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).
- *close/adjacent*: Some model-based papers report stability, invasion success, or evolutionary abundance, which must be interpreted indirectly as efficiency proxies (Kaiping et al., 2016; Inglis et al., 2014).
- *weak/none*: Several behavioral studies focus only on contribution rates, punishment frequency, or norm compliance, not payoffs.

**Summary**: The base is strongest for exact PGG designs with explicit punishment and measured efficiency, moderate for CPR or dynamic evolutionary close variants, and weaker for empirical studies with only behavioral/mechanism outcomes or adjacent problem domains.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**
- *Directly measured:*  
  - Efficiency (group payoff/maximum possible), total group earnings, welfare, surplus, coins generated (e.g., Fischer et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Javaid & Falk, 2015; Sääksvuori, 2014; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).
  - Several theory papers provide analytical results or simulations of average group fitness/payoff as a function of design dimensions (Eldakar et al., 2013; Lee & Iwasa, 2014).
- *Indirect/proxy measures:*  
  - Evolutionary models report the prevalence of cooperative/punishing strategies, indicative but not identical to group payoff (Kaiping et al., 2016; Inglis et al., 2014).
  - CPR and resource extraction studies occasionally require inferring efficiency from extraction or cooperation frequencies (de Melo & Piaggio, 2015).
- *Absent:*  
  - Some empirical/observational studies and those focused exclusively on behavior or cognition do not report payoff outcomes.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- Contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency, antisocial punishment, ostracism actions, signaling behavior, norm enforcement, social roles, etc.  
- While often predictive of payoff, these outcomes are not payoff-based and are reported as such in several field and theoretical studies (e.g., Kaiping et al., 2016; Konishi & Ohtsubo, 2015; Inglis et al., 2014; Perez et al., 2015).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General effect of punishment on efficiency:**  
- **Mixed evidence** for the efficiency impact of punishment in standard repeated PGGs:
    - *No reliable gain*: Several lab experiments find that enabling peer punishment does not increase—and can decrease—efficiency, especially when antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is common, or in environments with high baseline control efficiency (Fischer et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Javaid & Falk, 2015).
    - *Positive effects in certain contexts*: Punishment raises efficiency when the design prevents or minimizes antisocial punishment, when communication is possible, or under complementary production functions (weakest-link games, not linear PGGs) (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Sääksvuori, 2014).  
    - *Institutional design matters*: Centralized/“pool” punishment and ostracism are generally more efficient than decentralized/peer punishment, especially when information is noisy or anti-social punishment is present (Fischer et al., 2016; Sääksvuori, 2014; Gao et al., 2015).
    - *Theory aligns*: Models show that punishment’s positive effect on efficiency is greater when costs are low, group size is small, and rounds are numerous (Eldakar et al., 2013). Graduated punishment and redistribution of penalty rewards further increase efficiency (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).

**Critical moderators from literature:**
- **Communication (chat):** Enabling chat or even limited normative messaging during the punishment phase reliably increases efficiency when punishment exists, and offsets efficiency losses due to counter-punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016).
- **Production function:** Complementary/weakest-link structures lead to large efficiency gains from punishment; linear PGGs show little to no gain and can suffer from antisocial punishment (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
- **Baseline (control) efficiency:** When control groups (no punishment) are already efficient—due to social information or norms—adding punishment can lower efficiency by introducing confusion, waste, or coordination problems (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
- **Anti-social punishment:** High rates lead to reduced efficiency gains from punishment or even net losses (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; de Melo & Piaggio, 2015; Gao et al., 2015).
- **Punishment cost and design:** Efficiency gains from punishment are maximized when punishment is low-cost, well-targeted, centralized, or graduated (Eldakar et al., 2013; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015). Weak, probabilistic, or uncoordinated punishment can reduce group welfare (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
- **Group structure and social context:** The presence of migration, group competition, or community structure moderates long-run outcomes (Kaiping et al., 2016; Sääksvuori, 2014).

**Empirical vs. theory:**  
- Empirical studies provide direct measures of the effect of punishment in specified experimental settings, but often show mixed or context-dependent results.
- Theory frequently demonstrates stronger or more reliable efficiency gains given assumed conditions (low cost, no anti-social punishment, centralized institutions), often providing explicit boundary conditions for effectiveness.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Punishment is not reliably efficiency-increasing in standard linear PGGs**: Unless the design or context limits antisocial punishment, allows chat, or uses a complementary production function, enabling peer punishment often yields little or no improvement in efficiency over control (Fischer et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
- Efficiency gains from punishment are **more likely when**:
    - Chat or normative communication is available (Andrighetto et al., 2016).
    - The punishment system is centralized, graduated, or otherwise well-coordinated (Fischer et al., 2016; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).
    - The control condition exhibits low baseline efficiency; otherwise, added punishment may crowd out pre-existing efficient norms (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
    - The game uses a weakest-link or complementary production function (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
- **Critical design dimensions** to use in prediction (those identified as direct moderators):  
    `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `chat`, `all_or_nothing`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, and—where available—information structure (`show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`), as they are shown to condition punishment’s impact.
- **Predictive formula**: Use the control efficiency as a baseline, then adjust predicted treatment efficiency positively only if design features favor effective, coordinated, and pro-social punishment (communication, complementary production, centralized punishment); adjust downward or do not change if antisocial punishment is likely, punishment is costly, or control efficiency is already high.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (meaningful empirical/theoretical support):**
- `player_count`: Both empirical and theory studies vary group size and report its effect on efficiency and punishment effectiveness (Eldakar et al., 2013; Fischer et al., 2016).
- `num_rounds`: Directly manipulated in repeated games and theory, longer repeated interactions support more positive effects from punishment (Eldakar et al., 2013).
- `chat`: Shown to have significant moderating effects (Andrighetto et al., 2016).
- `all_or_nothing`: Directly analyzed (binary contributions vs. continuous) in several studies (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Andrighetto et al., 2016).
- `mpcr`: Directly manipulated and theoretically modeled (Eldakar et al., 2013).
- `punishment_cost`: Central to both empirical and theory results (Fischer et al., 2016; Eldakar et al., 2013).
- `punishment_tech`: Peer vs. centralized vs. external punishment is a key moderator (Fischer et al., 2016; Sääksvuori, 2014; Gao et al., 2015).

**Indirectly informed (discussed or inferred):**
- `default_contrib`: Rarely manipulated, but relevant via framing in a subset of experiments.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Information treatments in some experiments are highlighted as moderators, but not in most.
- `show_punishment_id`: Occasionally addressed in terms of anonymity and social information affecting punishment behavior (Andrighetto et al., 2016).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Some models and experiments include or contrast rewards, but empirical evidence focuses mainly on punishment.

**Contextually discussed or missing:**
- Details such as the exact contribution of summary statistics or the effect of default contribution framing are underexplored.
- The optimal calibration of `punishment_tech` beyond peer vs. centralized (e.g., hybrid or probabilistic systems) is not experimentally addressed in depth.
- `reward_*` dimensions are rarely central, mostly noted where both punishment and reward options are introduced as experimental controls.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Antisocial punishment and norm ambiguity**: Many field and lab environments suffer from antisocial punishment, often destroying or even reversing efficiency gains from punishment. Predicting this depends on social context (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; de Melo & Piaggio, 2015).
- **Baseline efficiency dependency**: High control efficiency (due to strong norms or social information without punishment) means added punishment may decrease, not increase, efficiency (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
- **Limited exploration of full design space:** Certain prediction dimensions (reward mechanisms, default contribution, summary info) are seldom experimentally varied or analyzed for efficiency impact.
- **Theory–Practice gap:** Theoretical models predict larger, more robust effects than are often found empirically, due to idealized assumptions (no anti-social punishment, well-calibrated punishment cost/effectiveness, homogeneous populations).
- **Generality to naturalistic/socially heterogeneous settings**: Heterogeneous population structure, varying local institutions, and real-world complexity (e.g., migration, evolving norms) are most often captured in theory, not experiments.
- **Lack of direct payoff outcome reporting:** Several papers rely on non-payoff behavioral measures (cooperation rates, strategy frequencies) as proxies—caution is needed in interpretation for prediction.
- **Dynamic and long-run outcomes:** Many models focus on evolutionary stability or long-term cooperation, which may not align directly with experimental lab timescales relevant for efficiency prediction.
- **Sparse data for interaction effects:** While key predictors like chat, punishment type, and cost are individually explored, systematic exploration of their interactions (e.g., communication × institution × cost) is rare.

---

**In summary**, this literature base offers strong, but nuanced, guidance for predicting the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in PGG-like environments, grounded in both empirical and theoretical studies. The main implication is that punishment only increases efficiency under certain design and social conditions; key game dimensions (communication, institution structure, cost, and baseline efficiency) must be considered in any quantitative or qualitative prediction. Some design dimensions remain underexplored or are only indirectly supported, and antipredictive contexts (high antisocial punishment, high initial efficiency) should decrease confidence in positive treatment effects of punishment.
