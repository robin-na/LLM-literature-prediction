# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is broad, with a mix of **empirical (lab and field experiments), simulation, and theory papers** focused largely on public-goods-game (PGG) or closely related environments (n ≈ 25-30 directly on PGGs with payoff outcomes; many more providing adjacent support or covering structural variants, e.g., step-level or threshold PGGs, PDs, or Snowdrift games). Empirical work is heavily represented in lab settings, employing standard design manipulations (player count, rounds, punishment on/off, cost/impact, information, matching, etc.), but also includes field experiments testing some coordination/punishment structures. There is also a substantial body of theoretical and simulation work modeling punishment’s effect on cooperation, payoff, and stability across a wide swathe of game parameters.

The evidence spans both **directly relevant studies**—standard linear PGGs with efficiency outcomes measured under control and punishment-enabled conditions—and a large group of **adjacent studies** focusing on punishment's effect on cooperation, norm compliance, or related behaviors, with or without explicit measurement of group efficiency or payoff. There is robust cross-validation between experimental data and theory/simulation in many cases, but also some structural gaps (not all 14 prediction dimensions are covered uniformly, and not all outcome definitions map precisely onto “efficiency” as required by the prediction task).

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) pgg_or_variant**  
- **exact:** The core of the literature is on standard and linear PGGs (e.g., Simpson et al., 2017; Gächter et al., 2017; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017; Andrighetto et al., 2013; Wu et al., 2016; Gross et al., 2016).
- **close:** Many studies use close variants—step-level PGGs, pool/peer punishment, threshold PGGs, networked/structured PGGs, snowdrift games, or multi-person PDs—often preserving the strategic incentives but introducing modifications (e.g., Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013; Jiang et al., 2013).
- **adjacent:** Extensive simulation and theory work considers repeated PDs, Ultimatum Games, resource management dilemmas, or helping games, which generate insight into mechanisms (e.g., peer punishment, exclusion, partner switching) but are not PGGs per se (e.g., Ohdaira, 2016; Wang et al., 2014).
- **none/weak:** A minority of papers are unrelated to PGGs and cannot inform PGG-specific prediction.

**b) punishment_or_sanctions**  
- **exact:** Many papers manipulate peer punishment on/off, vary its cost/efficiency, or compare peer punishment to alternatives (reward, ostracism, institutional punishment, moral/approval/disapproval judgments).
- **close/adjacent:** Some focus on reward, exclusion, or partner selection as alternative sanctioning mechanisms; others examine coordination solutions or moral judgment interventions, touching on the same behavioral targets (e.g., punishment as social pressure).
- **none/weak:** Some studies examine only non-punitive modifications; these are not task-relevant.

**c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**  
- **exact/close:** Several directly report group payoff, efficiency, surplus, or group earnings in control and punishment-enabled conditions (e.g., Simpson et al., 2017; Gächter et al., 2017; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013; Abbink et al., 2017). Some provide precise efficiency ratios or percentage-of-optimal benchmarks.
- **adjacent/weak:** Many report only contribution rates or norm compliance, requiring careful mapping from contribution patterns to efficiency (with explicit caveats for indirect inference).
- **none:** A significant set of behavioral- or mechanism-focused papers do not report payoffs/efficiency and are therefore only contextually useful.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**
  - **Group efficiency** (total earnings over theoretical maximum): directly reported in several PGG lab experiments.
  - **Group surplus/payoff/earnings:** used interchangeably with efficiency in many experimental and theoretical works.
  - **Average individual earnings:** often provided as a proxy for group efficiency.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - **Contribution/cooperation rates:** almost universally reported, often as a main behavioral outcome. Need explicit mapping (and caveats) if used as payoff proxies.
  - **Punishment frequency/intensity; types of punishment (altruistic, antisocial):** frequent focus, sometimes not tied to collective payoffs.
  - **Norm compliance, approval/disapproval, reputation:** measured explicitly in studies involving non-material sanctions, social signaling, or indirect reciprocity.
  - **Other:**
    - Spillover/learning/trust effects in subsequent games.
    - Retaliation and antisocial punishment dynamics.
    - Distributional and equity outcomes (inequality, variance in earnings).

**Only studies explicitly reporting group efficiency, total payoff, or earnings should be treated as primary evidence for efficiency prediction. Behavioral outcomes should be clearly flagged as non-payoff for the prediction context.**

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis across the Evidence Base:**

- **Peer (Costly) Punishment—Standard PGGs:**
  - Enabling costly peer punishment often *increases cooperation*, but the group efficiency effect is variable and design-dependent:
    - **No efficiency gain** is found when the cost of punishment offsets the benefits of higher contributions (Simpson et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016). In many lab PGGs (4-5 players, 9-30 rounds, MPCR=0.4–0.5, 1:3 cost/impact), **group earnings with punishment frequently do not exceed control**.
    - **Positive efficiency effect** is observed when punishment successfully deters free riding at low use cost (sufficiently high impact, moderately low p/q ratios), especially in longer games or when punishment power can be centralized (Gächter et al., 2017; Gross et al., 2016; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017; Hilbe et al., 2014). This efficiency gain is *not immediate*: efficiency may start lower (due to early punishment costs) but can catch up or surpass other interventions over time.
    - **Efficiency losses** result when punishment is costly, poorly targeted, or leads to cycles of retaliation/antisocial punishment, which can occur under: noisy monitoring (van Miltenburg et al., 2017), negative or low MPCR (Abbink et al., 2017), or substantial opportunities for antisocial use (Kubena et al., 2014).

- **Non-Material Sanctions (Moral Judgment, Approval/Disapproval, Gossip):**
  - **Costless sanctions** (moral approval/disapproval, gossip) can *outperform costly punishment*, yielding higher efficiency (more net payoff) and greater trust downstream (Simpson et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016).
  - **Combining norm-signaling with punishment** (explicit expectation communication plus sanctions) further increases efficiency relative to punishment alone, by reducing both the frequency and intensity of costly punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2013).

- **Punishment Structure/Mechanism Effects:**
  - **Power centralization** (allowing group members to delegate punishment ability) can dramatically raise efficiency in peer-punishment PGGs, relative to standard to peer punishment (Gross et al., 2016).
  - **Institutional punishment** (leader/fund-based), especially when involving follower support, can increase profits above control and peer-punishment conditions, but is at risk of efficiency loss if corruption/bribery is possible (Ozono et al., 2016; Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Abdallah et al., 2014).
  - **Second-order punishment** (punishing non-supporters of punishment) is an important moderator; it is critical for compliance and efficiency in some institutional PGG contexts (Hilbe et al., 2014; Ozono et al., 2017; Sasaki et al., 2015).
  - **Severity of punishment** needs to match the baseline cooperation: in "easier" games with higher control efficiency, mild punishment is as effective or more efficient (Jiang et al., 2013).

- **Moderator Effects—Game Structure and Design Dimensions:**
  - **MPCR:** When MPCR << 1/n, punishment can enforce *inefficient* or even destructive norms (Abbink et al., 2017); when MPCR > 1/n, punishment can meaningfully improve efficiency (Gächter et al., 2017).
  - **Group size, rounds, matching:** Longer repeated interactions and fixed group composition increase the payoff effectiveness of punishment (Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017; Gross et al., 2016).
  - **Information and noise:** Noisy monitoring undermines efficiency benefits of punishment (van Miltenburg et al., 2017). Communication and norm-awareness channels moderate returns (Andrighetto et al., 2013).
  - **Presence of rewards:** Reward alone rarely outperforms punishment; combined reward and punishment can synergistically increase efficiency (Sasaki et al., 2015; Dong et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2014).
  - **Other structures:** In step-level, threshold, or contest PGGs, or where between-group competition exists, findings replicate the trade-offs above but with context-specific moderators (Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013; Jiang et al., 2013; Gavrilets & Richerson, 2017).

- **Theoretical Models—Confirmation and Boundary Effects:**
  - Simulations consistently show **punishment increases efficiency when it is cheap and effective**, but can *reduce efficiency* when it is expensive or triggers retaliation.
  - Efficiency gains from punishment depend on escaping second-order free rider traps and, in some structures, require complementary mechanisms like reward or norm-internalization (Sasaki et al., 2015; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Ye et al., 2016).
  - Nonlinear production (e.g., increasing returns to scale) amplifies the effectiveness of punishment (Ye et al., 2016).
  - **Payoff/impact ratios and threshold conditions** are often explicitly specified for predicting when punishment will (or won’t) increase efficiency (Roberts, 2013; Barrett, 2016; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013).

- **Special Cases and Negative Effects:**
  - **Competitive environments:** Punishment becomes a tool for rivalry, not norm enforcement, leading to no improvement or even decline in efficiency (Paál & Bereczkei, 2015).
  - **Antisocial punishment:** Common in larger groups or low-trust populations, can erase or reverse efficiency gains (Kubena et al., 2014; Abbink et al., 2017).

- **Indirect and Conditional Evidence:**
  - When efficiency is not reported, increased contributions under punishment—with cost parameters known—imply possible gains, unless punishment costs clearly dominate (need care in interpretation).
  - Networked, spatial, and evolutionary variants suggest that cooperation and efficiency gains from punishment are robust to structure if punishment is designed correctly, but nonmonotonic or phase-transition-like effects may arise (Perc et al., 2013; Quan et al., 2017).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Expect asymmetric and design-specific effects.**  
  The predicted efficiency of a PGG with peer punishment enabled, given control efficiency, depends strongly on punishment cost/impact, group size, MPCR, rounds, and information/noise structure.

- **Punishment is not guaranteed to increase efficiency.**
  - When **control efficiency is low**, and punishment is low-cost/high-impact, enabling punishment will usually raise efficiency—sometimes dramatically (Gächter et al., 2017; Gross et al., 2016; Roberts, 2013).
  - When **control efficiency is moderate or high**, or punishment is expensive (high cost, low impact), efficiency gains may be small, absent, or negative (Simpson et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016; Jiang et al., 2013).
  - If the environment enables **moral/non-material sanctions or norm-signaling**, these can outperform costly punishment and yield net efficiency gains (Simpson et al., 2017; Andrighetto et al., 2013).
  - **Noisy, competitive, or negative-sum environments** (low MPCR, high monitoring error, presence of antisocial punishment or competitive incentives) often result in efficiency losses when punishment is enabled (Abbink et al., 2017; van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Paál & Bereczkei, 2015).

- **Design dimension mapping:**
  - **Dimension-level guidance is best for:** player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, chat, group matching, and summary information. These are the predictors most often explicitly reported and manipulated.
  - **Indirectly or partially informed:** punishment/reward identity visibility, all_or_nothing (continuous vs. binary choices), default_contrib, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries (but data are sparser).
  - **Contextually mentioned or rarely varied:** effect of chat/communication, default contribution framing.
  - **Sparse/missing:** Some output dimensions (reward_tech, show_punishment_id) are not commonly reported or directly tested.

- **Interpretation notes:**
  - Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., increased cooperation rates) **do not guarantee increased efficiency** if the costs of punishment are high.
  - **Retaliation, antisocial punishment, and design features enabling strategic misuse** can convert cooperation gains into efficiency losses; prediction should explicitly consider these risks.

- **Caveats:**  
  Numerical effect sizes should be drawn only when both control and treatment efficiency are reported for the same game structure.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count**: Widely varied; studies report effects for small (2+) to larger groups (8–12), document nonlinearity (efficiency benefits of punishment may fade in larger groups or in threshold games).
- **num_rounds**: Most PGG experiments use from 4 to 50 rounds; longer games see greater positive effects for punishment due to learning/history.
- **mpcr**: Central moderator; the threshold for punishment’s efficiency benefit is often found near MPCR = 1/n.
- **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech**: Fee-to-fine ratio, cost/impact structure, and enforcement mechanism (peer, pool, leader-based) are core variables.
- **reward_exists**, **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**: Several studies compare reward-only, punishment-only, and combined mechanisms (e.g., Wu et al., 2014; Sasaki et al., 2015).
- **all_or_nothing**: Both binary and continuous contribution PGGs are studied; effect size and punishment effectiveness can differ across structures.
- **chat**, **norm-signaling**, **information features**: Communication and norm-awareness interventions (e.g., explicit messaging, show_other_summaries) are manipulated, shown to interact with punishment.
- **punishment specification**: Peer vs. centralized, second-order punishment, delegated power, and corruption/bribery are tested.

**Indirectly Informed or Sparse:**
- **default_contrib**, **show_n_rounds**, **show_punishment_id**: Manipulated only in a limited subset; typically not a primary moderation factor in existing efficiency measurements.
- **show_other_summaries**: Sometimes reported or manipulated, especially for feedback’s effect on norm salience.

**Effectively Missing:**
- **Edge cases of dimension combinations** (e.g., large n, high punishment cost, with chat and reward enabled) are rare.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Heterogeneity of outcomes:** Not all studies report group efficiency; many focus on contribution rates, requiring cautious inference.
- **Measurement alignment:** “Efficiency” is not always defined as the ratio to fully cooperative payoff; in some studies, only total earnings or profits are reported, which may under- or overstate efficiency if marginal returns are not at 100%.
- **Generalizability:** Most high-quality, directly relevant evidence comes from laboratory PGGs with small-to-medium group sizes, moderate MPCR, and short to moderate repetition.
- **Sparse coverage of high-dimensional design space:** Many prediction input dimensions are only partially covered—especially rare or complex combinations, e.g., high player count with chat and second-order punishment and visible identities.
- **Contextual and framing effects:** Social, psychological, and cultural moderators (e.g., presence of competition, norm uncertainty, subject experience) are shown to have large effects but are not always operationalized as prediction dimensions.
- **Adverse selection and antisocial punishment:** Efficiency can be substantially reduced by antisocial punishment, retaliation, or strategic misuse of punishment in competitive or low-trust environments. Prediction models must treat these as risks where relevant design features are present.
- **Control condition ambiguity:** Some studies report only relative effects, not both control and punishment-enabled efficiencies for the same design. Control group efficiency may be inflated by misunderstanding or behavior not driven by social preferences (Burton-Chellew et al., 2016).
- **Cross-paper comparability:** Differences in outcome definitions, secondary manipulation, and framing (provision vs. maintenance, pool vs. peer punishment, etc.) limit direct effect size transfer.
- **Field vs. lab differences:** Field studies are rare; generalizing lab effects to real-world or large-scale social dilemmas should be done cautiously.
- **Multiple interacting mechanisms:** Communication, rewards, exclusion, reputation, partner choice, and third-party signaling can all function as substitutes or complements to punishment, complicating isolated inference.

---

**In summary:**  
Direct, empirical evidence supports a nuanced, design-dependent expectation: enabling punishment in a PGG environment can substantially increase efficiency relative to control *only* when punishment is cheap/effective and well-targeted, and the baseline (control) efficiency is low. In many typical lab setups, average efficiency does not rise above control due to punishment costs, unless structural or contextual features enhance impact or reduce misuse. Non-material sanctions, norm-signaling, and power centralization often outperform standard peer punishment. Prediction must be modulated by the specific game design dimensions; not all important moderators are covered in the literature, and many studies report only behavioral, not payoff, outcomes.
