# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of three theory papers—Sripada (2005), Nakao & Machery (2012), and Campbell (1991)—with no direct empirical or experimental contributions. All sources address the broader topic of punishment or sanction systems and their functions within human societies or other natural systems. The set is broad in its philosophical and theoretical discussion of punishment, extending to evolutionary, anthropological, and cultural mechanisms, but narrow in terms of direct applicability to experimental public-goods games or direct measurement of efficiency or group payoff outcomes. No quantitative, controlled, or comparative empirical results are reported.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**: The literature is rated as `adjacent`. None of the papers focus directly on public goods games (PGGs) or experimental variants; rather, they discuss systems akin to PGGs—moral norms in large groups, cooperation in evolutionary and cultural contexts, and group-level adaptations for coordination.

**punishment_or_sanctions**: The relevance is `exact` for punishment or sanction mechanisms; all three papers focus on the presence, functions, and evolutionary or social logic of punishment in groups, although often in broader contexts than laboratory PGGs.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: The papers are `adjacent` to `weak` for efficiency-related outcomes. Their discussions connect to the notion that sanction systems might increase group effectiveness or cohesion (in a general sense corresponding loosely to efficiency), but none provide experimental or analytic quantification of efficiency or measured group payoff changes due to punishment.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

The outcomes addressed are primarily **non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
- **Norm compliance and cooperation** are central themes (Sripada, 2005; Campbell, 1991).
- **Evolutionary functions** of punishment (Nakao & Machery, 2012), with emphasis on mechanisms such as loss-cutting and behavior modification.
- Occasional reference to group “effectiveness” or “cohesion” but not operationalized as efficiency or total group payoff.

**Payoff-related outcomes** such as total earnings, efficiency, welfare, or surplus are not directly measured or reported in any paper.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment as a support mechanism for cooperation or compliance**: The papers agree, in theory, that the existence of punishment and/or reward systems can sustain compliance with social norms and promote group cohesion or cooperation (Sripada, 2005; Campbell, 1991). These mechanisms are seen as especially important for large groups, where reciprocity is less feasible (Sripada, 2005).
- **Ambiguity about actual efficiency gains**: Nakao & Machery (2012) challenge the assumption that punishment always evolves to promote cooperation or group efficiency, noting that its function may often be to impose costs or cut losses, regardless of its behavioral effect on others.
- **Divergent mechanisms—beyond increasing group payoff**: Campbell (1991) highlights supernatural or “invisible” sanctions as a widespread cultural adaptation for curbing individual selfishness, again with the implication (but not evidence) that this benefits group success.
- **No direct empirical support** for claims about group payoff, efficiency, or measurable surplus effects of punishment in PGGs or analogs; all findings remain theoretical, making only general and often ambiguous suggestions about positive group-level effects.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

This literature provides only **theoretical, broad-brush support** for the idea that punishment mechanisms may make compliance and cooperation more sustainable in public-goods-game-like environments, which could in principle translate to higher efficiency when peer punishment is enabled compared to no-punishment controls. However, the papers caution against a blanket expectation that punishment will always increase efficiency, citing evolutionary and contextual variation where punishment serves functions other than improving group payoff (Nakao & Machery, 2012).

Given the absence of empirical or quantitative results, predictions of treatment efficiency must be based on generic theoretical plausibility arguments—mainly that enabling punishment can support norm compliance (often a prerequisite for high group efficiency), especially in larger, more anonymous groups or those lacking direct reciprocity opportunities (Sripada, 2005). However, it remains an unresolved theoretical issue whether these effects are realized in all designs, as the possibility of costly or misapplied punishment reducing group resources (“anti-social punishment”) is not excluded by these accounts.

Because payoff outcomes are not directly addressed, any prediction about the **average efficiency of treatment games as a function of design parameters and control efficiency** would be highly speculative and should be characterized by substantial uncertainty.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed**:
  - `punishment_cost` (Sripada, 2005; Nakao & Machery, 2012): Theory discusses the importance of the costliness of punishment for its stability and effectiveness, though without quantitative thresholds or gradients.
  - `player_count`, `num_rounds` (Sripada, 2005): Cited as salient structural factors—the theory argues that punishment mechanisms become more critical in large groups and over repeated interactions, but these are contextually rather than empirically supported.
  - `punishment_tech` (Sripada, 2005; Nakao & Machery, 2012): Forms of punishment (direct, exclusion, reputation) are discussed conceptually.

- **Indirectly or contextually discussed**:
  - `reward_exists` (Campbell, 1991): The role of both punishing and rewarding sanction systems is considered in a broad anthropological context, not operationalized as experimental game design features.
  
- **Effectively missing**:
  - All other dimensions: `all_or_nothing`, `chat`, `default_contrib`, `mpcr`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`. There is no mention of these in the theoretical discussions, nor of empirical results concerning their modulation of punishment’s effect on efficiency.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of empirical evidence**: No paper in the set reports experimental, observational, or simulation-based efficiency or group payoff outcomes resulting from enabling punishment.
- **Reliance on theory**: All claims rest on plausible but untested or only contextually supported arguments about human or biological societies; no operationalization for predicting quantitative outcomes in experimental PGGs.
- **Payoff-behavior conflation**: The strong emphasis on behavioral outcomes (norm compliance, cooperation) without separation from actual payoff improvements leaves ambiguity about relevance for efficiency prediction.
- **Sparse coverage of design dimensions**: Most of the 14 dimensions relevant for prediction are not discussed or are only mentioned in passing, limiting any nuanced or parameterized guidance.
- **Potential for negative or null effects**: Nakao & Machery (2012) explicitly warns that not all punishment supports improved cooperation or efficiency, introducing uncertainty that the theoretical consensus is overly optimistic or context-dependent.
- **Transferability**: Because the theory draws from anthropology, evolutionary biology, and broad social science rather than controlled econometric or laboratory game settings, external validity for modern PGG experiments is uncertain.

**Conclusion**: This literature set offers only theoretical and tangential support for the idea that enabling peer punishment in public-goods-game-like environments may improve group efficiency; it provides neither quantitative results nor operationalizable design-variable relationships. There is substantial uncertainty and insufficient evidence for precise efficiency prediction.
