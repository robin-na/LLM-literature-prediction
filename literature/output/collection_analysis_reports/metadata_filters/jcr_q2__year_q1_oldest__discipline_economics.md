# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set includes a mix of **empirical laboratory and field experiments** and a substantial body of **theoretical and simulation research** on public goods games (PGGs) and related social dilemma environments. The empirical component (about 40–50% of papers) focuses heavily on **laboratory PGGs** with and without punishment, with diverse game design variations (player count, rounds, communication, heterogeneity, and sanctioning mechanisms). There is also representation from field experiments and context-specific studies (e.g., fisheries, schools, cross-cultural settings). The theoretical papers cover a spectrum from equilibrium characterizations and evolutionary models to mechanism design arguments.

**Scope:** The evidence base is relatively **broad for standard PGGs with punishment** and for direct payoff/efficiency outcomes, but it is more sparse or adjacent when considering step-level games, CPR variants, nonmaterial sanctions, large-N groups, or complex institutional designs diverging from the standard lab PGG environment.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) PGG_or_variant:**   
- **Exact Relevance:** Most empirical lab studies focus on standard PGGs or very close variants (VCM, CPR). Theory papers also model the canonical PGG structure or natural analogues (e.g., repeated joint production, local interaction).
- **Close to Adjacent:** Several studies use coordination, trust, or step-level games that are structurally related but not exact PGGs; these provide only **adjacent** or **close** relevance.

**b) Punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact Relevance:** There is a strong emphasis on **peer punishment** (costly and voluntary), with some papers also exploring formal/monetary, informal/social, and centralized punishment mechanisms.
- **Close to Adjacent:** Others discuss alternatives (e.g., ostracism, social disapproval, reward, monitoring/exclusion), often as "sanctioning" but not always material punishment.

**c) Efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact Relevance:** A subset of key experiments directly measure and report **efficiency** (payoff as a percentage of the social optimum) or closely related outcomes (group earnings, total payoff, welfare, surplus).
- **Close to Adjacent:** Many papers focus on **contribution rates** or behavioral markers, only inferring efficiency from these or reporting group payoffs less precisely.

**Summary:** The literature's overall relevance to predicting **payoff-based efficiency change** from enabling punishment is **high but not exhaustive**; it is strongest for lab PGGs with standard parameter ranges and weakest for environments with unique sanction technologies, strong contextual moderators (e.g., culture, network), or nonmaterial sanctions.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

### *Payoff-Related Outcomes*
- **Group Efficiency:** Reported as group payoff relative to the full-cooperation optimum in many core experiments (e.g., Sefton et al., 2007; Casari & Luini, 2009; Nikiforakis et al., 2010).
- **Welfare/Earnings/Surplus:** Used interchangeably with efficiency in reporting total coins, average team income, or expected earnings.
- **Provision Rate:** In step-level games, efficiency is often proxied by the frequency with which the public good is provided (Guillen et al., 2007).

### *Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Distinguished Explicitly)*
- **Contribution/Cooperation Rate:** Almost universally measured, but not always directly translatable into efficiency due to the cost of punishment.
- **Punishment Behavior:** Rates, motives, and targets (e.g., anti-social punishment, perverse punishment).
- **Norm Compliance, Trust, Reputation Effects:** Often behavioral, used to explain or predict contribution dynamics.

### *Indirect or Weakly Related Outcomes*
- Some field and adjacent studies infer efficiency from behavioral changes or context without direct measurement.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### *Core Empirical Patterns*
- **Punishment increases contributions, but the translation into efficiency depends on the cost and structure of punishment.** In many standard PGGs, efficiency initially declines upon enabling punishment (due to punishment costs), but may recover or surpass control as cooperation stabilizes and the need for active punishment decreases (Sefton et al., 2007; Noussair & Tucker, 2005).
- **Cheap/effective punishment (high impact per cost) is more likely to yield net efficiency gains (Nikiforakis et al., 2010; Casari & Plott, 2003).** Punishment tech is thus a crucial moderator.
- **Anti-social or perverse punishment (e.g., punishment of cooperators) can negate efficiency gains or even reduce efficiency below control** (Bochet et al., 2006; Kocher et al., 2012).
- **Contextual moderators are critical:** Communication, group composition, baseline trust, culture, and institutional design (e.g., consensus punishment, ostracism vs. simple peer punishment) decisively affect efficiency effects (Casari & Luini, 2009; Maier-Rigaud et al., 2010; Kocher et al., 2012).
- **Punishment mechanisms that require consensus or filter out anti-social punishment are more likely to increase efficiency** (Casari & Luini, 2009).
- **In one-shot or very short games, or when baseline cooperation is high, punishment may decrease efficiency** (Kocher et al., 2012; Kocher et al., 2008).
- **Centralized punishment or leader-administered sanctions can increase contributions but not necessarily efficiency** if the mechanism is redistributive rather than strictly punitive (Gürerk et al., 2009).

### *Key Theory Insights*
- The **cost/benefit structure, patience (discount factor), population composition, and network connectivity** mediate whether punishment can support efficient equilibria (Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Congleton & Vanberg, 2001; Haag & Lagunoff, 2006).
- **Spatial and local punishment can sustain high efficiency in larger and structured populations** (Noailly et al., 2009, 2007; Grimm & Mengel, 2011).
- **Punishment can backfire** when opportunities for counter-punishment or feuding exist (Nikiforakis & Engelmann, 2011).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

## Synthesized Guidance

- **Directly informed prediction** is strongest when the control (no-punishment) game is a repeated, small-group, linear PGG, with well-specified MPCR, known rounds, standard (costly, peer) punishment, and *no chat or communication*. Here, **enabling punishment will likely increase efficiency relative to control**—after any initial punishment cost phase—with the magnitude of gain depending on the *punishment_tech* (cost/impact) and MPCR (Sefton et al., 2007; Noussair & Tucker, 2005; Kroll et al., 2007).
- **If punishment is expensive, weak, or anti-social punishment is common**, **efficiency gains may be absent or negative** (Casari & Luini, 2009; Bochet et al., 2006; Tan, 2008). Mechanism design (e.g., consensus requirements) mitigates this risk.
- **Communication (chat), endogenous rule-making, and strong group identification** substantially enhance the efficiency effect of punishment, sometimes *more* than punishment itself. The combination is super-additive (Ostrom, 2006; Bochet et al., 2006).
- **Increasing group size tends to reduce the per capita effect of punishment on efficiency**, unless institutional arrangements sustain coordination (Carpenter et al., 2009; Kritikos & Bolle, 2004). Evidence suggests diminishing returns to punishment with greater anonymity and larger groups.
- **Effects are time-dependent:** Initial periods may reflect efficiency loss (punishment is used extensively), but as cooperation stabilizes, efficiency can surpass control levels—especially in extended games (Nikiforakis et al., 2010; Sefton et al., 2007).
- **Reward mechanisms alone are less effective or even counterproductive for efficiency**; punishment is generally necessary to sustain high cooperation/efficiency (Sefton et al., 2007; Gürerk et al., 2009).
- **Institutional details** (punishment consensus, anonymity, targeted vs. random assignment) profoundly alter the efficiency effect, so prediction should not generalize across different punishment_tech or institutional settings.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count:** Core moderator; most evidence is for small groups (3–6); larger groups show weaker or more context-dependent effects.
- **num_rounds:** Longer sequences allow for stabilization and emergence of efficiency gains.
- **mpcr:** Higher MPCR increases the benefits of cooperation and can make punishment more effective.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** The cost and effectiveness (cost/impact ratio) of punishment are explicit and central moderators; optimal efficiency gains require punishment to be both effective and not excessively costly.
- **chat:** Communication is frequently manipulated and shown to have a large impact (often greater than punishment).
- **all_or_nothing:** Both all-or-nothing and continuous games studied; findings are consistent across types but continuous amounts allow more nuance.

**Indirectly or Occasionally Informed Dimensions:**
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds:** Often specified but not always varied.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Explored as comparisons or adjuncts; reward alone is less useful for sustaining efficiency.
- **show_punishment_id:** Identity revelation affects anti-social punishment, retaliation, and hence efficiency, but studies are fewer.
- **default_contrib:** Rarely a focus; opt-in/opt-out effects on efficiency are not well illuminated.

**Effectively Missing:**
- **Complex combinations:** e.g., simultaneous manipulation of multiple dimensions (e.g., chat plus varying punishment tech) is rare.
- **Real-world complexity:** Heterogeneous populations, strongly endogenous network formation, and large group or field settings are limited.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Heterogeneity and group context are underrepresented:** While some field and cross-cultural experiments are included, most findings are drawn from Western student samples in anonymous lab environments.
- **Sparse coverage of complex or hybrid institutions:** Institutional arrangements, such as consensus punishment, ostracism, or combined reward-punishment environments, are explored in only a few studies, yet have outsize importance in moderating efficiency outcomes.
- **Limited evidence for large-N and highly repeated games:** Most evidence is for small, finite groups over short to moderate lengths.
- **Behavioral outcomes dominate in some papers:** Many studies measure contributions rather than efficiency, and the effect of punishment on efficiency is often inferred rather than directly reported.
- **Ambiguity and disagreement remain, especially regarding anti-social punishment, group composition, and cultural context:** Some studies report positive efficiency effects, while others show neutral or negative effects, often explained by anti-social punishment or institution-specific moderators.
- **Lack of fine-grained data on the interaction among design dimensions:** While individual dimensions are studied, combinatorial effects are less well investigated.
- **Some dimensions are confounded:** For example, player identification, chat, and round structure are sometimes varied together, making it hard to isolate dimension effects.

**In summary:**  
- **Where the literature is exact and directly applicable, predictions of increased efficiency with punishment are robust, conditional on favorable punishment design and group parameters.**  
- However, **efficiency gains are not universal**, and risk of negative effects exists in one-shot games, settings with weak or costly punishment, strong anti-social punishment, or adverse social context.  
- **Dimension-specific prediction is best supported by evidence on group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and chat; other dimensions are less well covered.**
- **Predictions outside the range of the literature's core parameterizations—large groups, very weak or very strong punishment, complex hybrid mechanisms—should be made with caution.**
