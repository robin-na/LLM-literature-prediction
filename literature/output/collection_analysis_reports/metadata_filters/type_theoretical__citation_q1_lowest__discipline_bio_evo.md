# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 41 papers, all theoretical in nature—no papers report empirical or experimental results. The scope is relatively broad for evolutionary models of social dilemmas, with a heavy emphasis on public goods games (PGGs) and closely related frameworks (e.g., N-person prisoner's dilemma, repeated games, indirect reciprocity, group-structured populations). Most works focus on variations in population structure, incentive mechanisms (punishment/reward), cognitive biases, and error rates. A significant number of papers explore institutional versus peer enforcement, the evolution of cooperation strategies, and group-level sanctions or demographic feedbacks. However, true "exact" empirical evidence on the quantitative effect of peer punishment on group efficiency in PGGs is missing; the literature is dominated by theory with some simulation studies. Most papers that address efficiency do so through model-derived outcomes, such as equilibrium group payoff, welfare, or fixation probabilities, rather than direct experimental or observational measurement.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:  
- *Exact relevance*: ~6-7 papers focus squarely on standard PGGs or indistinguishable N-person prisoner's dilemmas with closely matched parameters and game structures (e.g., Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Powers et al., 2023; Greenwood et al., 2018; Liu & Guo, 2010).
- *Close or adjacent relevance*: Many others model games with similar incentives (e.g., threshold public goods, mutual aid, indirect reciprocity, group-structured prisoner's dilemmas, division of labor, or resource management games). Several papers are adjacent or close in their game structure but differ from exact PGGs in decision options, payoff structure, or core mechanisms.

**punishment_or_sanctions**:  
- *Exact relevance*: About a third of the corpus models explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms (either peer or institutional), including parameters for cost, effectiveness, and targeting (e.g., Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Powers et al., 2023; Greenwood et al., 2018; Cooney, 2025; Murase, 2025).
- *Close/adjacent*: Many others discuss related mechanisms (exclusion, reputation, partner-switching, reprobation, or social pressure), which may be functionally similar to punishment but implemented differently from standard peer punishment in PGGs.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- *Exact/close*: Most relevant theoretical papers analyze efficiency or directly calculate group payoff/welfare under different strategy mixes and interventions. Some use proxy measures (e.g., mean welfare, fixation probability of cooperation, maximum sustainable yield) as direct analogs of efficiency as defined in the prediction task.
- *Behavioral (non-payoff) outcomes*: Roughly half the papers report mainly on cooperation or contribution rates, punishment frequencies, or frequencies of strategy types, providing only indirect evidence for efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**:
    - Frequently: Group efficiency (as defined by mean group payoff or surplus).
    - Occasionally: Fixation probabilities of cooperation, maximum sustainable yield, expected lifespan, abundance of cooperators, or explicit formulae for average group payoff.
    - Sometimes: Institutional or intervention costs as related to achieving cooperation/welfare targets.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
    - Very common: Contribution rate, cooperation rate, frequencies of cooperation versus defection and/or punishment, stability of cooperative states, prevalence of norm internalizers, changes in partner choice or group composition, and rates of strategy adoption.
    - Mechanistic variables: Norm compliance, level of exclusion, retaliation, and information/observation structure.

Importantly, multiple papers (e.g., Greenwood et al., 2018; Odouard et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2025) report only on behavioral outcomes and do not quantify efficiency or total group payoff, making their findings relevant only as indirect evidence for the prediction task.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis across papers with exact or close relevance**:

- **Punishment can increase efficiency, but only under favorable parameter settings**. Enabling punishment (peer or institutional) in a PGG can lead to sustained high-efficiency equilibria—but only if the punishment cost is low relative to its deterrence (fine imposed or benefit preserved) and if the group is able to reach a critical mass of punishers (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Greenwood et al., 2018; Liu & Guo, 2010; Cooney, 2025).
    - *Empirical finding*: This relationship is mechanistically derived from model equilibria—when per-punisher cost is too high, or the effectiveness of punishment is too low, efficiency gains evaporate or punishment can even reduce efficiency (Kurokawa, 2023; Cooney, 2025).

- **Cost-sharing and institutional arrangements expand the efficiency impact of punishment**. When punishment costs are spread among many or imposed at the institutional level (not borne by lone individuals), the region of parameter space allowing for efficiency gains is much larger (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Powers et al., 2023).

- **Group size/player count** is a strong moderator:
    - In standard theoretical PGGs, large groups *can* reach high efficiency with institutional punishment and shared costs, but peer punishment in large groups often fails or is unsustainable because of second-order free-riding (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Greenwood et al., 2018; Kurokawa, 2023).
    - The critical threshold of punishers (or the minimum group investment in punishment) typically increases with group size.

- **Punishment does not inherently guarantee efficiency gains**. Simply enabling punishment is not sufficient; the baseline composition of the group, parameters governing cost, magnitude and targeting, and error rates matter critically (Greenwood et al., 2018; Murase, 2025).

- **Under some conditions, punishment can reduce efficiency**. When punishment is poorly targeted, too costly, or overused, its cumulative cost may outweigh the deterrence benefit and reduce total group payoff (Kurokawa, 2023; Cooney, 2025; Murase, 2025).

- **Information, observability, and error rates moderate the effect**:
    - Low detectability of defection can expand the region where punishment improves efficiency (Murase, 2025).
    - Accurate identification and punishment of defectors (with low error rates) makes punishment more likely to increase efficiency.
    - Group structure, limited information, or imperfect knowledge can sometimes make punishment more robust (Larson, 2016), but can also undermine its deterrent effect if defectors are frequently misidentified.

- **Role of norms and social environment**:
    - The presence of norm internalizers, strong social norms supporting punishment, and institutionalized enforcement promote stability of high-efficiency, high-cooperation states (Odouard et al., 2023; Kido & Takezawa, 2024).
    - Sanctioning mechanisms relying on social pressure or community enforcement, especially when backed by group selection or selective extinction, can also increase efficiency, sometimes functionally substituting for explicit costly punishment (Furuzono et al., 2013; Kroumi, 2025).

- **Adjacency: Partner choice, exclusion, and demographic feedbacks** can serve as alternatives or supplements to punishment in achieving high efficiency; their relative effects are context-dependent (Graser et al., 2025; Ghachem, 2016; Kurokawa, 2022; Inaba et al., 2016).

- **Reward mechanisms and hybrid incentives**: When modeled, introduction of reward or hybrid reward-punishment schemes also enhance group efficiency—with optimal system design depending on the relative costs and effectiveness of each (Duong et al., 2024).

**Ambiguities, caveats, and model disagreements**:
- Some models predict that as group size increases, punishment's positive effect on efficiency diminishes sharply unless costs are institutionally buffered or other mechanisms (e.g., hierarchy or information stratification) are available (Powers et al., 2023; Greenwood et al., 2018).
- In rare cases, spiteful or non-targeted punishment (i.e., 'spite' rather than true norm-enforcing punishment) consistently lowers efficiency, particularly when groups are large or costs are high (Kurokawa, 2023).
- The mapping from increased cooperation rate (behavioral outcome) to group efficiency is context-dependent and not always monotonic, due to the offsetting costs of punishment.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should these findings inform prediction of treatment efficiency from design dimensions plus control efficiency?**

- **Strongest, most direct guidance** comes from theoretical models that explicitly calculate group efficiency as a function of game parameters with and without punishment. Wherever possible, predictions should:
    - Use parameter thresholds and explicit payoff formulas from such models (e.g., Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Greenwood et al., 2018; Murase, 2025; Cooney, 2025) to identify regimes where enabling punishment is likely to increase, decrease, or leave unchanged the average efficiency relative to control.
    - Recognize that *the sign and size of the efficiency effect from enabling punishment are not universal* but depend on: player count, marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost, punishment magnitude, and whether punishment is peer or institutional (with cost sharing).

- **Inductive inference from control efficiency**:
    - If the control game (punishment disabled) exhibits high baseline efficiency (near full cooperation), models generally predict little or no efficiency benefit, and possibly an efficiency loss, from enabling costly punishment—because the costs of punishment are incurred without much gain (Cooney, 2025; Kurokawa, 2023).
    - If control efficiency is low (pervasive defection), enabling punishment can in theory shift the game to a cooperative, high-efficiency equilibrium—but only within favorable parameter regions (low punishment cost, high effectiveness, sufficient initial frequency of punishers, and/or institutional cost sharing) (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Greenwood et al., 2018).

- **Design dimensions to prioritize in prediction**:
    - **player_count, mpcr**: Larger groups require more decentralized or institutional solutions; higher mpcr generally makes cooperation/punishment more viable.
    - **punishment_cost, punishment_tech (i.e., magnitude, targeting, effectiveness)**: Lower punishment cost or higher impact per unit spent increases the likelihood of efficiency gains.
    - **punishment_tech details**: Whether punishment is peer or institutional, targeted or blanket, fixed or per-interaction, and ability to identify defectors.
    - **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Introduction or coexistence of reward can change the equilibrium landscape.
    - **Error rates in identification/implementation**: High error rates undermine the efficiency benefits of punishment.
    - Other dimensions—e.g., **chat, show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id**—are discussed in some papers mainly in behavioral terms, with sparse direct evidence on efficiency.

- **Qualitative rules and exceptions**:
    - Enabling punishment is more likely to raise efficiency when costs are low, targeting is accurate, and group structure/information are favorable.
    - Enabling peer punishment is likely to fail to improve (or can even hurt) efficiency in large groups or at high cost unless supported by institutional arrangements.
    - In models where punishment is not targeted or is "spiteful," enabling punishment tends to lower efficiency (Kurokawa, 2023).
    - The most transferable predictions arise from models with phase diagrams or explicit formulas relating all five of: control efficiency, player count, mpcr, punishment cost/magnitude, and group structure.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed**:
- **player_count**: Modeled in almost all relevant theory papers; often a major moderator of whether punishment improves efficiency (e.g., Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Powers et al., 2023; Greenwood et al., 2018).
- **mpcr**: Central to almost all payoff or efficiency calculations.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech (including magnitude/type)**: Addressed in explicit model parameters in the vast majority of punishment-focused work.
- **all_or_nothing**: Modeled in many cases, both as binary and continuous choice.
- **num_rounds**: Discussed in relation to repeated versus single-shot PGGs and the stability of cooperation; sometimes key for threshold dynamics.

**Indirectly Informed**:
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Occasionally included or discussed as alternative or additional mechanisms; hybrid incentive designs in a few models (Duong et al., 2024).
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id**: Sometimes discussed as aspects of information environment or transparency, mainly for their effect on cooperation rates, with weak linkage to efficiency.
- **chat**: Rarely directly modeled; when present, analyzed for effects on behavioral outcomes (cooperation) rather than efficiency.

**Only Contextually Discussed or Sparse**:
- **default_contrib**: Framing effects (opt-in/opt-out) not systematically modeled in PGG theories here.
- **show_punishment_id**: Occasionally referenced in discussions about observability and the deterrence/retaliation trade-off, without direct efficiency results.

**Effectively Missing**:
- Most models do not address the effects of *within-round communication*, fine-grained framing, non-standard contribution defaults, or many detailed UI/game mechanics elements as design variables for efficiency prediction.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of empirical calibration**: The entire evidence base is theoretical; empirical validation or quantification of effect sizes/ranges for treatment efficiency versus control is missing.
- **Over-representation of idealized models**: Most models assume homogeneous agents, perfect rationality (or evolutionary stability), and stylized cost/benefit trade-offs, with limited incorporation of bounded rationality, learning, or real-world noise.
- **Scarcity of peer punishment models in large, repeated, continuous-contribution PGGs**: Most closely modeled cases are either institutional punishment or variants with discrete choices and finite group structures; standard peer punishment as experimentally implemented (e.g., Fehr & Gächter) is underrepresented.
- **Ambiguous mapping from behavioral to payoff outcomes**: Many papers report only on cooperation or contribution rates, leaving the actual efficiency outcome dependent on unmodeled cost/benefit trade-offs.
- **Limited coverage of some design dimensions**: Some input variables required for precise downstream prediction (e.g., chat, contribution framing, UI transparency) are only lightly treated or not treated at all.
- **Context-dependency and parameter sensitivity**: Theoretical results are highly sensitive to parameter values and initial group composition, making predictions outside the parameterized regime hazardous.
- **Potential for mismapping adjacent results**: Several important findings concern partner choice, exclusion, demographic feedback, or indirect reciprocity rather than explicit punishment, which may not transfer quantitatively to peer punishment-enabled PGGs.

---

**In summary**:  
This literature set provides a detailed, mechanism-rich, but theoretical account of how game design features moderate the impact of punishment on group efficiency in PGG-like contexts. The absence of empirical data on efficiency changes and sparse attention to some real-world design elements should temper confidence in quantitative predictions, but the set is highly informative about the qualitative patterns and model-based thresholds that should govern downstream efficiency effects for different punishment treatments.
