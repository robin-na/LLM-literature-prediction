# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is composed entirely of theoretical analyses—none of the 54 papers report original empirical or experimental data. This makes the evidence base highly formal, offering mathematical and simulation-based proofs, comparative statics, and model-based mechanisms, but not direct experimental estimates or measured effect sizes. Coverage across the literature is broad, encompassing exact (public goods game) models, close variants (common-pool resources, helping games, trust games, repeated and spatial dilemmas), and more contextually adjacent environments (organizational theory, market analogs, evolutionary models). Some papers provide explicit equilibrium formulas and simulation findings; others focus on conceptual arguments and mechanism logic.

Given the lack of empirical papers, the literature is strong on theoretical mechanism and qualitative moderation but weak on precise, calibrated prediction or empirical calibration.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessed on the three key dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - *exact*: A minority of papers directly analyze standard public goods games (PGG) and their variants (Levine & Modica, 2016; Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Huang et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2024; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Benchekroun & Van Long, 2008; Chessa & Loiseau, 2024; Congleton & Vanberg, 2001).
  - *close*: Many extend to closely related repeated, threshold, or spatial cooperation games (e.g., repeated PD, CPR, helping games, team production).
  - *adjacent/weak*: Roughly half the set approaches from more distant analogs: market games, familial/social network games, etc.
  - *none*: A few are purely conceptual and do not model cooperation or payoff at all.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - *exact*: Papers with explicit costly punishment/peer sanctions modeled in PGGs (e.g., Levine & Modica, 2016; Huang et al., 2024; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Hwang & Bowles, 2012).
  - *close*: Numerous studies feature analogous enforcement—social sanctions, exclusion, social disapproval, monitoring, institutional/political punishment.
  - *adjacent/weak*: Some discuss incentive structures or refer to non-punitive sanctions (rewards, reputation, partner selection).
  - *none*: Several have no punishment mechanism at all.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - *exact*: Several papers (especially those modeling PGGs or variants) directly analyze efficiency—group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum.
  - *close*: Many papers provide welfare, surplus, or total payoff as a key outcome, often directly translatable to efficiency.
  - *adjacent/weak*: Some discuss behavioral outcomes (contribution/cooperation rates), evolutionary dynamics, or only allude to efficiency.
  - *none*: A handful are purely on behavioral/psychological mechanisms, with no efficiency or payoff analysis.

**Conclusion**: The evidence base is strongest for theoretical mechanisms in exact and close variants of PGG, with explicit punishment and well-defined efficiency outcomes. It is notably weak on empirical generalization and on direct coverage of all relevant game design dimensions.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**:  
  - *Directly measured*: Efficiency (group payoff/maximum payoff), welfare, surplus, mean or aggregate earnings (Levine & Modica, 2016; Huang et al., 2024; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Noailly et al., 2009, 2007; Benchekroun & Van Long, 2008; Congleton & Vanberg, 2001).
  - *Closely related*: Simulated or theoretical average payoffs, frequency of reaching efficient equilibria, or 'welfare' in repeated games (Haag & Lagunoff, 2006; Sethi & Somanathan, 2003).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:  
  - Contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequencies, norm compliance, and strategy evolution are often reported and discussed, but are not the focal outcome of the prediction task.
  - Several papers provide only behavioral or psychological analysis (e.g., antisocial punishment prevalence, mechanisms of reciprocity, willingness to punish, network dynamics).

*Distinction maintained*: Only those papers focusing on total (group) payoffs or ratios to the social optimum are considered as having measured (even theoretically) the prediction-relevant outcome.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Strong theoretical consensus (within exact/close PGG*)**:
- *Enabling peer punishment increases efficiency* (payoff) relative to no-punishment settings, provided the punishment cost is not prohibitively high and the technology is effective (Levine & Modica, 2016; Huang et al., 2024; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Noailly et al., 2009, 2007; Congleton & Vanberg, 2001).
- *Efficiency gains are moderated* by:
  - **Punishment cost and effectiveness**: High punishment cost reduces or eliminates efficiency gains; low or targeted punishment can enable maximal or near-maximal efficiency.
  - **Group size**: Punishment is required to sustain efficiency as group size increases (Levine & Modica, 2016; Huang et al., 2024; Kritikos & Bolle, 2004). Coordination difficulties can reduce effectiveness in large, uncoordinated groups (Kritikos & Bolle, 2004).
  - **Population composition**: High rates of altruists or reciprocators can paradoxically lower efficiency by reducing the willingness to punish, undermining the punishment mechanism (Hwang & Bowles, 2012; Dong et al., 2024; Sethi & Somanathan, 2003).
  - **Structure of punishment/reward**: Targeted (e.g., minimal, focused on worst contributors) and endogenous/shared punishment is more efficient and less costly than blanket or maximal punishment (Huang et al., 2024; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
  - **Social and network structure**: Effective punishment regimes are easier to sustain in environments with sufficient monitoring, alignment, and connectivity, but can backfire/lose effect in excessively heterogeneous or poorly connected groups (Haag & Lagunoff, 2006; Camera & Gioffré, 2017, 2025).
  - **Antisocial punishment and context**: High antisocial punishment, cultural context, or weak institutions can negate or reverse efficiency gains from enabling punishment (Sylwester et al., 2013; Schunk & Wagner, 2021; Stoelhorst & Richerson, 2013).

**Ambiguity/conflict**:
- *Social context and non-material sanctions*: In some settings, monitoring or social sanctions may crowd out intrinsic motivation and reduce efficiency (Orr, 2001), or be less effective than expected unless designed very carefully.
- *Effectiveness of reward vs. punishment*: Reward mechanisms can substitute for or complement punishment, and maximal efficiency is sometimes achieved with rewards and minimal necessary punishment (Huang et al., 2024; Janus & Lim, 2009).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based on synthesized evidence, for the task of predicting average efficiency with peer punishment enabled (given game design dimensions and control efficiency):

- **Direction of effect**: Expect a positive effect of enabling peer punishment on group efficiency relative to control without punishment, *unless* moderators intervene (e.g., high punishment cost, strong antisocial punishment, group composition).
- **Sensitivity to design dimensions**:
  - *Player count*: Efficiency gains from punishment are more necessary in larger groups (Levine & Modica, 2016).
  - *Number of rounds*: Repeated interaction increases scope for enforcement and efficiency gains, especially under sufficiently long horizons (many-papers).
  - *MPCR*: Moderate to high MPCR helps punishment sustain cooperation; extremely low or high values can destabilize enforcement (Choi, 2007).
  - *Punishment cost/tech*: Central for predicting the *magnitude* of efficiency gain (Kol'veková et al., 2021; Sethi & Somanathan, 2003).
  - *Reward exists & structure*: Efficient designs combine maximal feasible rewards with minimal, targeted punishment (Huang et al., 2024).
  - *All/or/nothing, endogenous mechanisms*: Designs that enable voting or endogenous sharing of punishment costs yield high efficiency at lower cost (Kol'veková et al., 2021).
  - *Summary and visibility mechanisms*: Information availability and transparency (show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id) can strengthen enforcement and efficiency, especially by facilitating reputation effects and coordination (Noailly et al., 2007).

- **Use of control efficiency**: If efficiency is already high without punishment (e.g., due to high baseline cooperation, strong intrinsic motivation), enabling punishment may yield small or null efficiency gains, and in some theoretical settings, can even reduce efficiency (Orr, 2001; Hwang & Bowles, 2012).

- **Caveats**: Effects are contextually moderated—antisocial punishment, high cost, or motivational crowding can cause the effect to be negligible or negative.

- **Quantitative prediction**: Some models (Levine & Modica, 2016; Huang et al., 2024; Kol'veková et al., 2021) yield explicit formulas or comparative statics, supporting parameter-based predictions when mapped faithfully to the game design dimensions in the task.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed**:
  - player_count
  - num_rounds
  - all_or_nothing
  - mpcr
  - punishment_cost
  - punishment_tech (effectiveness, targeting, sharing)
  - reward_exists
  - reward_cost/tech (less central, but addressed in some, especially when compared to punishment)

- **Indirectly/contextually discussed**:
  - show_n_rounds
  - show_other_summaries
  - show_punishment_id
  - default_contrib (framing/opt-in-out): Not precisely parametrized, but some theory refers to action space restrictions or framing (Chessa & Loiseau, 2024).

- **Effectively missing/sparse**:
  - chat (explicit communication): Largely missing; reputation, monitoring, indirect communication are sometimes discussed but not implemented as "chat".
  - default_contrib: Rarely modeled in standard form.
  - reward_cost, reward_tech: Sometimes analyzed, especially when compared to punishment; quantitative modeling sparse.
  - all finer strategic information and behavioral observation controls: only mentioned in broad qualitative terms.
  
- **Dimensions best informed for payoff prediction**: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, and—when relevant—reward_exists/cost/tech.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **No empirical calibration**: All findings are theoretical (proving mechanisms, equilibria, stability conditions), not experimentally measured. The magnitude of effects and the real-world distribution of moderators (antisocial punishment, group composition, institutional context) are unknown.
- **Limited coverage of all design dimensions**: Some task-relevant variables (e.g., chat, information displays, contribution framing) are rarely if ever modeled with the granularity found in experimental setups.
- **Assumptions of models**: Many results crucially depend on full rationality, common knowledge, or specific structures of population composition, which may not hold in practice.
- **Ambiguity regarding context and moderators**: Substantial variation across models in the predicted sign/magnitude of effect when group composition, culture, or context is unfavorable to punishment; general direction is positive but with notable qualifications.
- **Limited mapping to one-shot or heavily finite settings**: Many theoretical results assume repeated, indefinitely repeated, or evolutionary dynamics, and may not generalize to short, finite, or one-shot games.
- **Indirect relevance of adjacent-context papers**: Close and adjacent analogs expand the conceptual mechanisms but reduce confidence in quantitative prediction for exact PGG.

---

**In sum**: The literature robustly supports that, in theoretical public goods game and many close variants, enabling peer punishment increases group efficiency, but only under specific, parameter-dependent conditions regarding punishment cost, targeting, group structure, and population composition. The effect is not universally guaranteed and can reverse in settings with high intrinsic cooperation, motivational crowding, high antisocial punishment, or poor punishment design. Explicit efficiency outcomes are available for only a subset of design dimensions. The predictions are theoretical and not empirically calibrated.
