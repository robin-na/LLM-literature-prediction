# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set (n = 253) is broad, covering a wide range of experimental, observational, and field studies on public goods games (PGGs), common pool resource games, and related social dilemmas. The core evidence base for the prediction task consists of a rich collection of **lab experiments** that directly test the effect of punishment (peer, centralized, third-party, exclusion/ostracism) on **payoff-based outcomes** (including efficiency, group earnings, welfare, surplus).

A significant subset of papers provide **exact or close empirical evidence** on the prediction problem—estimating the change in group efficiency when peer punishment is enabled, conditional on game design and baseline/control efficiency. Many other papers are **mechanism-focused, theoretical, or behavioral**, addressing contribution rates, psychological processes, or norm dynamics without reporting efficiency or earnings. Adjacent and weakly relevant papers offer context, moderators, or social psychological insights but have little to no direct data on the payoff consequences of punishment.

Key characteristics of the evidence base:
- Strong on **experimental evidence** for standard lab PGGs (groups of 3–12 players, 10–30 rounds, clear MPCRs, various punishment and reward technologies, and control conditions).
- Covers **both peer and centralized punishment**, as well as other sanctioning institutions (exclusion, ostracism, etc.).
- Includes a handful of **meta-analyses and cross-societal comparisons**, which are strong behaviorally, but less often report payoff effects.
- Contains several field/lab-in-the-field experiments in real groups, adding context and external validity.
- Theoretical and adjacent works are numerous; many offer mechanism or process insights (e.g., on motivation, norm compliance), but few report group payoff/efficiency outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:
- Relevance: **mostly exact or close**. Many studies use standard or modified PGGs; a subset employ close CPR, trust, or dictator games. Some studies focus on all-or-nothing (binary) choices, others on continuous contributing.

**punishment_or_sanctions**:
- Relevance: **exact or close** for roughly a quarter of the papers; these manipulate peer, central, third-party, or ostracism-based punishment, and distinguish between types (e.g., peer vs. central), costs, and technologies. Many more papers (adjacent/weak) discuss punishment, but only as a secondary variable or in non-PGG settings.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
- Relevance: For **fully payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, earnings, surplus)**, approximately 20–30 studies are **exactly relevant** and report the required outcome for the prediction task. Many more report on cooperation, contribution, or norm compliance without direct efficiency or payoff measures (**adjacent**).

Summary: **Direct relevance is high** for the core dimensions, but decreases for specific moderating dimensions, institution types (peer vs. central), and in studies focusing only on behavior or on non-PGG games.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (directly relevant):**
- **Efficiency:** (group earnings / social optimum). Several multi-lab and high-profile studies, meta-analyses, and some field studies.
- **Group/individual earnings, welfare, surplus, total coins generated:** Many lab experiments report average or total payoffs.
- **Surplus over baseline/control:** Used in studies with endogenous institution choice or multi-level treatments.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (common, but not efficiency):**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates:** Universally reported; often the primary outcome.
- **Punishment frequency, level assigned, targeting:** Measures who punishes whom and under what circumstances.
- **Norm compliance, convergence, trust, norm enforcement rates:** Mechanism/process variables, not direct payoffs.
- **Inequality metrics (e.g., Gini), satisfaction, fairness evaluations:** Reported in minority of studies.

Note: The literature often **distinguishes** between increased contributions and increased net efficiency. Because punishment incurs costs, **efficiency may not always track contribution rate**; e.g., some studies find that punishment increases contributions but reduces or leaves efficiency unchanged due to high punishment costs.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical Findings Across Studies
- **Enabling costly peer punishment in standard lab PGGs usually increases group efficiency (payoff) compared to controls** without punishment, especially when baseline efficiency is low due to contribution decay (Gintis et al., 2003; Gächter et al., 2017; Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
- **The efficiency gain from punishment is often large and robust** for 3–5 player, 10–30 round, linear PGGs with MPCR 0.3–0.5 and punishment cost-to-impact ratios between 1:3 and 1:4.
- **However, the effect is not universally positive.** Several studies show that:
    - **Peer punishment can reduce efficiency** when (a) punishment is overused/antisocial, (b) punishment is inefficiently targeted, or (c) punishment costs are high relative to contributions or to the gain in cooperation (Nockur et al., 2021; Harrell & Simpson, 2016).
    - **Centralized/leader punishment is often more efficient** than peer punishment (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Eichenseer, 2023), especially when the leader is prosocial/democratically chosen and has authority to punish.
    - **Ostracism/exclusion as sanction can increase efficiency as much as or more than monetary punishment,** often with fewer actual punishments assigned—sometimes the threat is sufficient (Masclet, 2003; Sääksvuori, 2014).
- **Framing, information, and social context moderate the effect:** Transparency of endowments, visibility of others' contributions, history of conflict, and group composition can amplify or reverse the effect of punishment on efficiency (Chen et al., 2025; Gross et al., 2022; Barclay, 2004).
- **In CPR-like and variant games (threshold, all-or-nothing, or with alternative defection options), the effect of punishment is highly context-dependent:** Sometimes positive (Cason & Gangadharan, 2013; Chávez et al., 2018), sometimes zero or negative when punishment costs outweigh gains or trust/institutional coordination is weak (Ostrom et al., 1992; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022).
- **Institutional design is critical:** The presence of commitment, democratic selection of punishment regimes, or the ability to coordinate punishment (as opposed to uncoordinated peer punishment) clearly increases the efficiency impact of enabling punishment (Duell et al., 2024; Eichenseer, 2023; Krügel & Maaser, 2025).
- **Communication is often a stronger moderator of efficiency than punishment:** When chat or face-to-face communication is permitted, efficiency can rise to near-optimal levels even without punishment (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; Mak et al., 2015).
- **The cost-to-impact ratio (punishment cost/punishment severity) is a key moderator.** Low-cost, high-impact punishment is typically more likely to increase efficiency, but risk of over-punishing or antisocial punishment may increase if punishment is too cheap (Chen et al., 2025).

## Theory, Mechanism, and Indirect Evidence
- **The long-run efficiency impact of punishment is greater in longer, repeated games with high partner stability.** Initially, efficiency gains may be offset by punishment costs, but as contributions stabilize and punishment is used less, net group payoffs rise (Balliet et al., 2011; Gächter et al., 2017).
- **The presence of rewards, communication, or reputation mechanisms can substitute for or strengthen the effect of punishment on efficiency** (Feinberg et al., 2014; Hamman et al., 2011; Stoop et al., 2018).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based on the synthesized literature, the following **guidance applies when predicting average group efficiency (payoff ratio) in PGGs with peer punishment enabled, given control efficiency and game design:**

### General Rule
- **If the game is a repeated, 3–5 player, linear PGG (10–30 rounds, moderate-to-low MPCR), and control efficiency is substantially below optimum due to contribution decay, enabling peer punishment is likely to increase efficiency—sometimes by large amounts (20–40% of the maximum),** especially if the punishment cost-to-impact ratio is favorable (e.g., 1:3 or 1:4), and contribution/reward visibility is high (Gintis et al., 2003; Gächter et al., 2017; Barclay, 2004; Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
- **The increase in efficiency is often not immediate; it may take a few rounds for contributions and payoffs to converge upward.** In short games, the net gain is smaller; in long games, punishment costs often decrease as norm compliance stabilizes.
- **If punishment is centralized (by a prosocial leader) or coordinated, expect a higher efficiency gain per unit punishment cost than in peer punishment settings.** Peer punishment can lead to self-reinforcing cycles of antisocial punishment, which may reduce efficiency (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Eichenseer, 2023; Nockur et al., 2021).
- **If baseline (control) efficiency is already high (due to strong communication/chat, high social norm, visible endowments, or a cooperative setting), adding peer punishment may offer little or even negative incremental efficiency** due to unnecessary punishment costs (Balliet et al., 2011; Nair et al., 2018).
- **In environments with high antisocial punishment rates, conflicting group interests, or the possibility of destructive punishment, the efficiency effect can be reduced, eliminated, or reversed** (Milinski & Marotzke, 2022; Goette et al., 2012; Pleasant & Barclay, 2018).
- **Framing, payoff structures, and transparency matter:** Peer punishment is more likely to improve efficiency when (a) endowments and contribution rates are visible, (b) all can be sanctioned (no immunity), and (c) the social context supports prosocial or norm-based punishment (Chen et al., 2025; Barclay, 2004; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).

### Dimensional Moderators
- **Punishment cost/impact:** Lower cost and higher impact amplify efficiency gains, but too low a cost can increase antisocial punishment and backfire (Chen et al., 2025).
- **Player count:** Peer punishment is most effective (vs. costless or anonymous) in small groups (≤5); in larger groups, exclusion/ostracism or centralized mechanisms perform better (Kerr et al., 2009).
- **Institution selection and participation:** When groups can choose to commit (collective punishment with commitment), efficiency gains from punishment are higher (Duell et al., 2024).
- **Information structure:** Transparent endowments and actions (show_other_summaries) increase the efficiency effect of punishment (Hauser et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2025).
- **Baseline efficiency:** The relative gain from punishment is greatest when control efficiency is low; in high-control-efficiency settings, gains may be zero or negative (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; Nair et al., 2018; Balliet et al., 2011).
- **Reward mechanisms:** When both punishment and reward are available, reward can dampen use of punishment and sometimes yield equal or better efficiency (Stoop et al., 2018; Kiyonari & Barclay, 2008).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions** (i.e., repeatedly and quantitatively varied or analyzed for efficiency effects):

- `player_count`             : Well covered (experiments from 3–12 players).
- `num_rounds`              : Well covered (10–30+ rounds; robustness in repeated/long games).
- `mpcr`                 : Well covered (typically 0.3–0.5; varied in some meta-analyses, critical for baseline efficiency and marginal incentives).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` : Directly varied (cost, impact, threshold, targeting, peer vs. central).
- `all_or_nothing`            : Binary/continuous choices; effects on efficiency discussed.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Sometimes directly manipulated (transparency, information, endgame effects).
- `show_punishment_id`        : Directly studied in a minority of papers (e.g., impact on antisocial punishment).

**Indirectly informed dimensions**:

- `chat`                 : Heavily studied as a moderator/alternative; regular finding that it increases efficiency even in absence of punishment.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Studied as competing or augmenting interventions in some papers (e.g., Stoop et al., 2018).
- `default_contrib`           : Sometimes included as framing (opt-in vs. opt-out) but not always isolated.
- `punishment_exists`: The central manipulation throughout.
- `show_other_summaries`: Indirectly, as many studies manipulate feedback/info, but not always cleanly.

**Sparsely addressed or contextually discussed dimensions**:

- `default_contrib` (framing effect rarely isolated, though opt-in vs. opt-out studies exist).
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (especially joint manipulation with punishment—less commonly isolated).
- `show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds` (identity and info salience sometimes tested, but their interaction with punishment's effect on efficiency is less thoroughly quantified).
- `punishment_tech` (tech often described, but detailed parametric sweeps of fine levels and targeting are less common than 'cost/impact ratio' or binary peer vs. central distinctions).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited multi-dimensional sweep:** Most high-quality efficiency results are for a narrow range of game parameterizations, especially 3–5 players, 10–30 rounds, moderate MPCR, and standard (1:3/1:4) punishment cost/impact. Generalization to larger groups, fewer/more rounds, alternative punishment techs, or different framing is less supported.
- **Peer punishment is most studied; centralized and coordinated sanctions are less common but often more efficient.** Subtle institutional details can reverse the effect of enabling punishment.
- **Many papers report only contribution/cooperation rates, not efficiency or payoff**. Using contribution as a proxy for efficiency can mislead where punishment costs are high.
- **Contextual moderators—communication, endowment transparency, cultural factors, incentives for antisocial punishment—are sometimes critical, but not fully mapped** onto the 14-dimension prediction space.
- **Field studies, lab-in-field, and complex variants are underrepresented** in direct efficiency reporting.
- **Reporting gaps for certain dimensions**: e.g., opt-in vs. opt-out framing, alternative technologies for punishment/reward, and information display variables.
- **High variance in the effect of punishment depending on baseline efficiency**: When control efficiency is already high, adding punishment can hurt (via costs); when baseline is low, punishment often helps. Not all studies provide both control and treatment payoffs.
- **Heterogeneity in operationalization of "efficiency":** Some studies use net payoff, others focus on social optimum ratio, some on aggregate earnings or surplus; definitions and calculation methods sometimes differ.
- **Behavioral mechanism papers cannot be safely used for quantitative efficiency prediction.** Many studies supporting the general social effectiveness of punishment do not report efficiency.

---

**In summary:**  
- The literature base is strong for standard PGG designs with repeated peer or central punishment and well-modeled cost/impact/MPCR variations.
- Peer punishment typically increases efficiency when baseline efficiency is low, but effects depend critically on cost/impact ratio, social context, communication, type of institution, and control efficiency.
- Predicting efficiency effects from design dimensions should weight evidence most heavily from papers measuring payoff/efficiency outcomes and controlling for punishment's direct and indirect costs, adjusting for key moderators (e.g., group size, repeated interaction, institution design, transparency).
- Absence of direct efficiency reporting (using contribution as a proxy) should be flagged as a limitation, and predictions in poorly evidenced design regions should be treated with caution.
