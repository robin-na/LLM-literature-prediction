# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is composed entirely of **theoretical works**—there are no new empirical or experimental studies present. Theoretical methods dominate, with analyses drawing on equilibrium concepts, mathematical models, simulation, and formal review. The set covers both **narrow, directly relevant models** (e.g., exact public goods games with punishment and group payoff analysis) and a range of **adjacent models** (volunteer’s dilemmas, repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma, coordination games, indirect reciprocity, network games), some with partial overlap in structure or payoff concepts.

Overall, the evidence base is **broad in theoretical mechanism coverage** but **narrow in experimental validation**. Payoff outcomes (efficiency or total group payoff) are clearly addressed in several theory papers, but quantitative predictions are not calibrated or cross-checked by experimental data. Many models generalize to, but are not always tailored for, the exact public goods game structure relevant to peer-punishment environments.

# 2) Task Relevance

The paper set’s relevance to three core dimensions is as follows:

**a. PGG or Variant**
- **exact:** Several papers model or review exact public goods games (e.g., Botta et al., 2021; Eldakar et al., 2018; Thöni, 2014; Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- **close/adjacent:** Many others analyze repeated games or social dilemmas closely related to PGGs, such as repeated PD, volunteer’s dilemma, or networked coordination (e.g., Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015).
- **weak/none:** Some focus on trust games or other social games without direct mapping to the group contribution context (e.g., Bellucci, 2022; Beraldo & Sugden, 2016).

**b. Punishment or Sanctions**
- **exact:** Multiple papers model explicit peer or institutional punishment as a formal mechanism (e.g., Botta et al., 2021; Eldakar et al., 2018; Thöni, 2014).
- **close/adjacent:** Many theoretical models allow punishment-like mechanisms (trigger strategies, community enforcement, sanctioning, etc.), though framing and structure can differ from laboratory PGG punishment (e.g., Annen, 2011; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Uchida et al., 2024).
- **weak/none:** A few papers focus exclusively on reward, coalition formation, or self-regulation, with punishment only contextually mentioned (e.g., Skarzhinskaya & Tsurikov, 2021; Lütz et al., 2023).

**c. Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**
- **exact:** Several models directly predict or analyze group efficiency, group payoff, or welfare (Botta et al., 2021; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Uchida et al., 2024).
- **close/adjacent:** Papers focusing on equilibrium properties, welfare under alternative sanction forms, or payoff comparisons for repeated dilemmas (Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Andersson, 2020; Forges et al., 2016).
- **weak:** Several focus mainly on behavioral or norm-based outcomes (contribution rates, norm stability) with limited treatment of payoff (Thöni, 2014; Okada, 2020).
- **none:** A few are purely conceptual or focus on affect, learning, or signaling without payoff analysis (Golman, 2016; Hernández, 2021).

**Summary:**  
- **Direct, theory-based relevance:** Strong on all three axes for a minority of papers.
- **Indirect or adjacent coverage:** Frequent, especially for efficiency in settings structurally similar to PGGs.
- **Weak or missing:** No new empirical efficiency data; efficiency is less consistently analyzed than cooperation rates or norm compliance.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**  
- **Efficiency:** The primary focus in a subset of theory papers (e.g., Botta et al., 2021; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Uchida et al., 2024), defined as the ratio of realized payoff to the full-cooperation benchmark.
- **Group payoff/welfare/surplus:** Used variously as group sums or welfare-maximizing solutions (often interchangeable with efficiency in theory).
- **Total coins generated or analogous metrics:** Mathematical derivations always tie back to these constructs.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
- **Cooperation/contribution rate:** Many models report or theorize effects on average contributions but make weaker or no claims on payoff (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Thöni, 2014).
- **Norm compliance, punishment/reward frequency, stability of cooperation:** Described in mechanism-focused papers, especially on indirect reciprocity, signaling, or network effects (Okada, 2020; Golman, 2016).
- **Emotional or learning dynamics:** State transitions or adaptive behaviors (Andersson, 2020; KRAINES & KRAINES, 1993).

**Distinction:**  
- Payoff-based outcomes are the minority but are conceptually prioritized for this prediction task. Most models clarify that higher cooperation rates do **not always** translate to higher group efficiency, especially if punishment is costly.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of Key Theory Results:**

- **Punishment Often Increases Efficiency, but Only Conditional on Cost and Structure.**  
  In exact or close PGG models, enabling punishment (particularly when above a critical threshold of severity or frequency) generally increases cooperation and raises efficiency—sometimes to the optimal level (Botta et al., 2021; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Annen, 2011; Uchida et al., 2024).  
  - Effect is **mediated by punishment cost**: If punishment is expensive relative to its deterrence, or is overused (antisocially), efficiency gains may be **attenuated or reversed** (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Thöni, 2014).
- **The Presence of Antisocial or Selfish Punishment Can Lower Efficiency.**  
  If punishment is driven by motives unrelated to norm enforcement (revenge, spite), not only does it fail to increase cooperation, it reduces group payoff by imposing net losses (Thöni, 2014; Eldakar et al., 2018).
- **Community/Wide-Base Punishment and Symmetric Power Favor Efficiency.**  
  When punishing power is distributed evenly, efficiency gains from enabling punishment are much higher; concentrated or “selfish” punishment leads to lower productivity (Eldakar et al., 2018).
- **Impact of Game Structure:**
  - Optional participation and use of loner strategies moderate the effect—a large enough punishment probability leads to stable high-efficiency equilibria (Botta et al., 2021).
  - The effect of punishment is greater if feedback, monitoring, or reporting are reliable (Annen, 2011).
  - **Threshold and Volunteer’s Dilemma:** Only action-based punishment reliably induces efficient equilibria (Friehe & Tabbach, 2018).
  - Peer punishment often carries a significant social cost, which can negate or outweigh efficiency gains unless parameters (cost, probability, magnitude) are well chosen (Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- **Reward and Combined Mechanisms:**  
  Rewards alone are less effective in raising efficiency unless large; combined reward and punishment can strengthen efficiency gains (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Skarzhinskaya & Tsurikov, 2021).
- **Psychological Moderators:**  
  Players’ probability weighting (prospect theory) can magnify the effect of even infrequent or mild punishment, leading to positive shifts in efficiency (Uchida et al., 2024).

**Empirical Findings:**  
None—no experimental or observational payoff/effect data is analyzed in this paper set.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

For the downstream task—predicted efficiency in punishment-enabled treatment, conditional on design and control efficiency—this theoretical literature suggests:

- **Directionality:**  
  **Enabling** peer punishment will usually **improve efficiency relative to control, but only if**:
    - The punishment is not so costly (in resource terms or social cost) as to offset cooperative gains.
    - Antisocial/selfish punishment is rare or structurally discouraged.
    - Power to punish is widely distributed (not concentrated among self-interested minorities).
    - The punishment probability or magnitude is above the relevant deterrence threshold.
    - The control game is characterized by low baseline cooperation/efficiency (gains are larger when starting lower).
- **Dimension Moderators:**  
  Outcomes are especially sensitive to:
    - **player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Large groups and higher MPCR generally lower required punishment severity for cooperation. High punishment cost can neutralize or reverse efficiency gains. The functional form of punishment (probabilistic, deterministic, institutional/peer) matters.
    - **num_rounds, show_n_rounds:** Longer repeated interaction makes punishment more effective as a deterrent, provided players are sufficiently patient.
    - **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech:** Effective reward mechanisms can substitute for or augment the efficiency gains from punishment.
    - **chat, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** These may influence effectiveness via information/coordination, but direct evidence is sparse.
- **Non-monotonic Effects and Threats to Efficiency Gains:**  
  If punishment is too weak or too strong, or dominated by antisocial motives, efficiency may not improve or may worsen.
- **Quantitative Prediction:**  
  The literature is able to provide **qualitative directional guidance and formulas (in principle) linking dimension values to expected efficiency**, mainly in theory but not empirically derived.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed by Theory Evidence:**
- **player_count:** Modeled as group size; alters threshold for punishment required.
- **num_rounds:** Shapes repeated interaction; addressed in models using repeated games.
- **mpcr:** Central in all public goods models; thresholds for cooperation and punishment are directly functions of MPCR.
- **punishment_cost:** A critical moderator; high costs restrict positive impact on efficiency.
- **punishment_tech:** Explicitly included—the form, probability, and institutionalization matter to deterrence and cost.
- **all_or_nothing:** Modeled in several papers, especially for binary participation or transfer games.
- **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech:** Modeled where selective incentives or dual mechanisms are considered.

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**
- **show_n_rounds:** Addressed in repeated game models in terms of knowledge of horizon (affecting patience/discount).
- **show_other_summaries:** Modeled as reporting or information sharing; relevant for community enforcement models.
- **show_punishment_id:** Mentioned in passing as affecting observability, but not a focus.
- **chat:** Only contextually discussed as influencing communication; no formal modeling.
- **default_contrib:** Not directly analyzed; some framing implications in optional vs. compulsory participation models.

**Effectively Missing or Very Weakly Addressed:**  
- **reward_magnitude, punishment_magnitude:** Sometimes implied or adjustable parameters but without specific quantitative predictions.
- **default_contrib, show_punishment_id:** Generally not modeled in the theoretical literature.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **No Empirical/Experimental Evidence:**  
  All findings are theoretical, so predictions may not capture human behavioral noise, implementation issues, or idiosyncrasies evident in lab or field experiments.
- **Ambiguity in Direction and Magnitude:**  
  Theory papers do not agree in all cases; the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is **not always positive**. Predicted direction can reverse with high punishment cost, prevalent antisocial punishment, or asymmetric power.
- **Exact Parameter Mapping Is Lacking:**  
  Explicit formulas exist for some scenarios (e.g., punishment thresholds), but only for a subset of design dimensions. Most models require further parameterization to be used as quantitative predictors in arbitrary game settings.
- **Limited Coverage of Some Dimensions:**  
  Information, communication, and default settings (e.g., chat, show_n_rounds) are under-theorized in their effect on efficiency. Reward-related parameters are contextually addressed, not systematically analyzed.
- **Adjacent Games:**  
  Many findings are in repeated PD, volunteer’s dilemma, social contract, or network games; results may not transfer quantitatively to standard PGGs without caution.
- **No Heterogeneity or Cultural Variation:**  
  Human subject variability, cultural factors, and stochastic behavioral deviations are unmodeled.
- **Focus on Stationary/Equilibrium Outcomes:**  
  Dynamic adjustment paths, transient inefficiencies, or learning effects are not generally addressed.
- **No Direct Comparison with Control Efficiency:**  
  The literature often establishes conditions for possible efficiency increases with punishment but does not systematically forecast the quantitative delta from a punishment-disabled control game.

---

**In summary:**  
This paper set provides **rich theoretical insight and qualitative guidance** regarding the determinants and boundaries of punishment’s impact on efficiency in public-goods-like environments, with strongest support when punishment is reasonable in cost, broadly distributed, and not susceptible to antisocial use. However, **direct quantitative prediction from game design to efficiency gain** is not empirically calibrated, and some design dimensions are only partially or contextually addressed. Theoretical ambiguity and potential for negative or null efficiency effects under certain parameterizations should be preserved in any downstream modeling or forecasting applications.
