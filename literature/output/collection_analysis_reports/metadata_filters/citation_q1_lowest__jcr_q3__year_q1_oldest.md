# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set includes 12 papers spanning both empirical (lab and field experiments) and theoretical (modeling and simulation) work. Theoretical contributions (e.g., Liu & Guo, 2010; Corriveau, 2012; Billard, 1996; Castro et al., 1998; Ziegler, 1997; Ma et al., 2009; Aruka & Akiyama, 2009) predominate, with a notably smaller subset of experimental studies that actually measure outcomes in public-goods-like games (e.g., English, 2012; McEvoy, 2012; Loukopoulos et al., 2006; Stiff, 2008).

The set is broad in terms of conceptual treatments of social dilemma games and mechanisms (punishment, reward, spillover, reputation effects) and varied in empirical/theoretical mix. However, for the specific prediction task—public goods game (PGG) efficiency effects of peer punishment—the number of papers with direct, quantitative, empirical payoff-based evidence in the canonical PGG is limited, and few papers report quantitative efficiency outcomes. Many papers provide adjacent, theoretical, or qualitative evidence relevant to PGGs with punishment, but often measure non-payoff behavioral outcomes instead of actual group efficiency.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *exact*: A minority of papers study canonical PGGs or very close variants (Liu & Guo, 2010; English, 2012).  
- *close*: Several model or test close relatives of PGGs—involving coalition formation (McEvoy, 2012), public-goods-like resource dilemmas (Loukopoulos et al., 2006), or social dilemmas with public contributions (Billard, 1996; Corriveau, 2012; Castro et al., 1998).  
- *adjacent/weak*: Others treat n-player games or dyadic trust/prisoner’s dilemma settings with similar incentive structures (Stiff, 2008; Ma et al., 2009; Ziegler, 1997; Aruka & Akiyama, 2009). A few are only contextually related or mention PGGs without substantive analysis (Nikiforakis, 2010).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *exact*: Some papers explicitly manipulate or theoretically model punishment mechanisms (Liu & Guo, 2010; McEvoy, 2012; Corriveau, 2012; Ziegler, 1997; Castro et al., 1998).  
- *close*: Several examine indirect sanctions (reputation effects, reprobation) or discuss punishment as a theoretical possibility, but not as a directly manipulated variable (Loukopoulos et al., 2006; Billard, 1996; Stiff, 2008; Ma et al., 2009).  
- *mention_only/weak*: Some mention punishment conceptually but do not model or empirically evaluate it (Aruka & Akiyama, 2009; Nikiforakis, 2010).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *exact*: Very few studies empirically report efficiency or total group payoff (English, 2012; Corriveau, 2012: theory/modeling).  
- *close/adjacent*: Several infer efficiency improvements from increased cooperation or group welfare in models/theory (Liu & Guo, 2010; Castro et al., 1998). Empirical studies more often report contribution rates or behavioral measures rather than direct efficiency.  
- *none/weak*: Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, norm compliance, punishment frequency) dominate in many empirical studies (Loukopoulos et al., 2006; McEvoy, 2012; Billard, 1996; Stiff, 2008).

**Summary:** The literature is only partially relevant: it has significant coverage of PGGs and punishment in theory, with scarce empirical studies reporting the required payoff-based efficiency outcome (especially both with and without punishment in otherwise-matched designs).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes:**  
  - *Efficiency or total group payoff*, group earnings, welfare, and surplus are directly measured or theoretically derived in a limited subset (Liu & Guo, 2010 [theory/approximation]; English, 2012 [empirical, without punishment]; Corriveau, 2012; Castro et al., 1998).
  - Payoff outcomes are often inferred from models or assumed based on increased cooperation, but not directly measured in most empirical studies.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  - *Contribution rates, cooperation rates, norm compliance,* frequency of punishment, and incidence of defection are the main endpoints in most experimental papers (Loukopoulos et al., 2006; McEvoy, 2012; Stiff, 2008; Billard, 1996).
  - Many theoretical models focus on prevalence or stability of cooperative strategies rather than aggregated group payoff.

- **Mixed/Indirect Outcomes:**  
  - Some studies treat proxies: e.g., abundance of "persistent cooperators" as a welfare proxy (Liu & Guo, 2010), or "fitness" as group payoff (Castro et al., 1998).

**Conclusion**: Accurate mapping from behavioral outcomes to efficiency is often assumed rather than measured. Direct reporting of efficiency changes due to punishment is rare.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Synthesis Across Studies

- **Punishment Tends to Increase Cooperation, and Sometimes Efficiency:**  
  - Theoretical models largely concur that, if punishment costs are moderate and the benefits of cooperation are substantial, enabling punishment mechanisms can foster higher cooperation and (by inference) group payoff or efficiency (Liu & Guo, 2010; Corriveau, 2012; Castro et al., 1998).
  - Empirical studies of *behavioral outcomes* (not efficiency) find that punishment increases cooperation rates and reduces free-riding, including among those not directly punished (Loukopoulos et al., 2006).

- **The Net Efficiency Effect Depends On Punishment Cost:**  
  - The cost of administering punishment is crucial. When punishment is too costly, efficiency gains from increased cooperation may be negated or reversed (Liu & Guo, 2010; Ziegler, 1997; Billard, 1996).
  - In models, explicit parameter boundaries are offered: punishment increases efficiency when its cost is not excessive compared to the marginal per capita return (MPCR) and the benefit of cooperation (Liu & Guo, 2010).

- **Structure and Credibility of Punishment Matter:**  
  - Merely enabling punishment is not always enough—punishment must be credible or structured (coalition-based, committed) to promote higher cooperation/efficiency (McEvoy, 2012).
  - Indirect/reputational or mixed (direct+indirect) punishment systems can be robust in sustaining high cooperation (Corriveau, 2012; Stiff, 2008).

- **Empirical Results on Efficiency Are Scarce or Inferential:**  
  - Direct improvement in payoff-based efficiency from introducing punishment in a PGG is mostly found in theoretical or modeling papers, not in reported fruitfully-designed empirical studies.
  - Empirical studies more often show improved cooperation rates than higher efficiency, and do not always assess or report efficiency when punishment is enabled (Loukopoulos et al., 2006; McEvoy, 2012).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Quantitative Prediction**: The literature provides qualitative and theoretical support that enabling peer punishment in a PGG is likely to increase efficiency, *conditional* on punishment costs being moderate relative to the marginal returns of cooperation (Liu & Guo, 2010; Corriveau, 2012; Castro et al., 1998). Precise quantitative effects or a formula for predicting efficiency in the punishment-enabled treatment, given control efficiency, are not offered by empirical data.

- **Control Efficiency as a Predictor**: The English (2012) study shows that, in the absence of punishment, efficiency varies primarily with participant expectations. While this is informative for setting a baseline, the effect that punishment has in increasing efficiency above this baseline is only theoretically modeled in the other studies. Thus:
  - If control efficiency is low due to low expectations or high defection, enabling punishment *can* raise efficiency, but the magnitude is sensitive to punishment cost, the credibility of sanctioning, and benefit/cost ratios.
  - Prediction should be modulated by design features influencing punishment's credibility, cost, and observability.

- **Key Moderators for Prediction**:
  - **Punishment Cost**: Lower punishment cost predicts larger efficiency gains.
  - **MPCR**: Higher MPCR (returns to cooperation) increase the likelihood of punishment improving efficiency.
  - **Credibility/Structure**: Structured, committed, or observable punishment systems are more effective than voluntary/ambiguous ones.
  - **Communication and Feedback**: While not directly tied to efficiency, design dimensions such as chat, reputation/feedback, and summary information may interact with punishment's effects but are underexplored.

**Caveat**: The absence of robust empirical data on efficiency in PGGs with and without punishment, holding other factors constant, means predictions are based on strong theoretical priors but not direct empirical evidence.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

## Directly Informed
- **player_count**: Modeled in virtually all theoretical and many empirical papers; larger groups tend to require stronger or more credible punishment mechanisms (Liu & Guo, 2010; Corriveau, 2012; Ziegler, 1997; Castro et al., 1998).
- **num_rounds**: Essential in repeated games and theoretical models of sustained cooperation under punishment threat (Corriveau, 2012; Liu & Guo, 2010).
- **mpcr**: Explicitly modeled as a critical moderator of payoff structure and thus the net benefit of punishment strategies (Liu & Guo, 2010).
- **punishment_cost and punishment_tech**: Central in all models/theories treating punishment effects; the cost level and mechanism design strongly affect outcome (Liu & Guo, 2010; Ziegler, 1997; Castro et al., 1998).
- **all_or_nothing**: Manipulated in several studies (McEvoy, 2012; Loukopoulos et al., 2006).

## Indirectly or Contextually Informed
- **chat**: Modeled or mentioned as a situational moderator of cooperation but its interaction with punishment/efficiency is mostly theoretical (Billard, 1996; Loukopoulos et al., 2006).
- **show_other_summaries**, **show_n_rounds**: Discussed in theoretical models exploring the role of shared feedback or observability for enforcement (Corriveau, 2012).
- **reward_exists**, **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**: Discussed by Billard (1996) and others as analogues or complements to punishment, but not directly tied to efficiency-outcome predictions in PGGs.

## Effectively Missing
- **default_contrib**: Not addressed across the paper set.
- **punishment_magnitude**: Rarely specified—some models imply a linear or proportional effect but most focus on punishment existence/cost rather than magnitude.
- **show_punishment_id**: Not directly manipulated or discussed.
- **reward_magnitude**: Not systematically treated.
- **others (e.g., specifics of reward dynamics, opt-in/opt-out framing)**: Largely unexamined.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse Empirical Data on Efficiency**: There is a notable gap in empirical, direct, and quantitative data comparing matched PGGs with and without punishment, in terms of efficiency (not just contributions).
- **Behavioral Over Emphasis**: Most studies measure contributions or norm compliance rather than actual group payoff, meaning efficiency effects must often be inferred rather than measured.
- **High Structural and Contextual Variance**: Many models feature stylized or alternate game structures, such as coalition formation, repeated n-player reciprocity, or adjacent trust games, thus limiting generalizability to canonical PGGs.
- **Uncertain Magnitude of Effects**: Even where theory predicts efficiency gains under punishment, the absence of empirical magnitudes hampers quantitative prediction.
- **Cost Sensitivity and Nonlinearity**: Models stress that positive effects of punishment are parameter-sensitive and can become negative as punishment costs rise, which complicates extrapolation to new designs.
- **Missing or Underexplored Dimensions**: Several key design variables (e.g., default contribution framing, reward/punishment magnitude, identifiability) are rarely, if ever, systematically studied with respect to efficiency effects.
- **Ambiguity in Generalization**: Some findings are ambiguous, noting that punishment’s efficacy depends on credible commitment, other design features, and player heterogeneity—factors often not systematically varied.

---

**In summary:**
- The literature provides strong theoretical and qualitative support that introducing peer punishment to a PGG can increase efficiency, especially when punishment cost is low and the system is credible.
- Empirical validation is scarce—especially studies reporting efficiency outcomes rather than only contribution rates.
- Critical design parameters for prediction (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) are well-theorized, but not all parameters are empirically or systematically tested.
- Prediction from this literature should be cautious, qualitative, and retain parameter sensitivity and ambiguity where empirical evidence is missing.
