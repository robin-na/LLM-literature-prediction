# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of 26 papers ranging from laboratory experiments and empirical observations to formal and computational theory. The empirical papers primarily use laboratory experiments focused on economic games (public goods games, trust/ultimatum games, coordination games, and investment/team tasks), while the theory papers model repeated games and evolutionary environments. The set is broad in addressing collective action, cooperation, and sanctioning institutions; however, it is relatively narrow in the number of standard multi-player, repeated PGGs that directly report efficiency outcomes with and without punishment. Several papers focus on adjacent environments (trust games, minimum effort/coordination, delegation/ultimatum, team investments) or consider broader institutional and cultural mechanisms without direct experimental manipulation of punishment in PGGs.

**Empirical vs. Theory composition:**  
- Approximately 11 papers provide empirical laboratory experiments, most directly manipulating punishment or reward options with outcome measures related to group payoff or efficiency.
- About 9 papers are formal or computational models/theory, focusing on the mechanisms and equilibria of (sometimes adjacent to PGG) sanctioning schemes and collective action.
- The remaining papers are observational, ethnographic, or broad contextual/theoretical discussions without controlled manipulation of punishment or explicit measurement of efficiency.

While the overall corpus is diverse in context and methods, only a modest subset provides direct, empirical, PGG-based, efficiency-outcome evidence—the ideal for the intended prediction task.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Target-relevance assessment (per dimension):**

- **pgg_or_variant:**  
  - *exact*: ~5 papers (e.g., Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Zhang & Pei, 2022; Pancotto et al., 2023; Peng, 2022)
  - *close or adjacent*: ~10 papers—trust games, delegation, minimum effort/coordination, team investment, shirker’s dilemma, repeated PD.
  - *weak/none*: The remainder are observational, theory in non-game settings, or descriptive.

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**  
  - *exact*: Direct manipulation of punishment or sanctions in most experimental/theoretical papers.
  - *adjacent*: Several examine reward, partner choice/walk-away, or refer to sanctions contextually (not experimentally).
  - *none*: Few (e.g., Montoya-Lozano et al., 2024).

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
  - *exact*: Only a limited subset directly report group efficiency or total payoff as the main outcome (e.g., Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Peng, 2022; Pevnitskaya & Ryvkin, 2022; Herne et al., 2022).
  - *close/adjacent*: Many report contributions or cooperation frequencies and infer payoff indirectly, or focus on adjacent outcomes (group productivity per unit work, welfare, payoff of one group member).
  - *weak/none*: Several provide only non-payoff behavioral measures or no quantitative outcome at all.

> **Summary:**  
This literature set is moderately but not fully task-relevant. About one-third of papers provide direct evidence on how punishment affects efficiency specifically in PGGs or close variants. The remainder either (i) focus on adjacent but not identical games, (ii) report behavioral rather than efficiency outcomes, or (iii) discuss mechanisms theoretically or contextually without quantitative efficiency data.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes (group efficiency, welfare, earnings, surplus, total coins):**  
  - *Directly measured*: In select empirical papers (e.g., Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Herne et al., 2022; Peng, 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024; Lec et al., 2023). Some theory papers model efficiency as equilibrium payoff or welfare.
  - *Inferred/indirectly measured*: When only contributions or cooperation rates are reported, efficiency is often assumed to rise with them, but this can be misleading, especially when punishment is costly.
  - *Absent*: In several adjacent/observational papers.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (cooperation or contribution rates, punishment frequency, norm compliance):**  
  - *Prevalent*: Most experimental and theory papers report contribution rates or behavioral changes in response to punishment/reward, sometimes only discussing payoff implications.
  - *Distinguished*: Zhang & Pei (2022), explicitly warn that increased cooperation may reduce efficiency if punishment is costly.

- **Other outcomes (norm appropriateness, fairness perceptions, trust, intentions):**  
  - *Measured*: Especially in observational/contextual/ethnographic reports (Sequeira, 2023; Bharti & Malik, 2023; Clark, 2022). These do not address efficiency or payoff directly.

> **Key distinction:**  
Even when punishment increases cooperation or reduces free-riding, the net effect on group efficiency depends on the balance between gains from cooperation and costs of punishment—often only directly reported in a subset of studies.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis across the literature:**

- **Peer vs. institutional punishment:**
  - *Empirical and theory consensus*: Formal or institutional punishment (administered centrally, cost shared) is more likely than peer punishment to increase group efficiency, especially if punishment cost is low and fines are high (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
  - *Peer punishment*: Often increases contributions/cooperation but can either increase, decrease, or not change efficiency depending on magnitude and cost of punishment (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Herne et al., 2022). Context and design details determine direction.

- **Cost and effectiveness of punishment:**  
  - *High punishment cost or low effectiveness*: Can erode or negate efficiency gains (Herne et al., 2022; Zhang & Pei, 2022).
  - *Favorable cost/benefit*: Institutional punishment, or peer punishment with high fines for low cost, can lead to stable high efficiency (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).

- **Game openness and group stability:**  
  - *Migration/group formation*: Formal sanctions are especially effective in open/migratory groups (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
  - *Partner choice/walk-away*: Functionally adjacent to sanctioning and increases efficiency, especially when defection is possible due to context or resource absence (Kurokawa, 2022; Sequeira, 2023).

- **Punishment observability and coordination:**  
  - Visible and credible punishment signals increase deterrent effects, coordination, and efficiency, even in adjacent game settings (Li et al., 2023; Gueth & Otsubo, 2023; Lec et al., 2023).

- **Interaction with other mechanisms (reward, democracy, communication):**  
  - *Reward*: Can increase efficiency, sometimes more robustly than punishment if the reward is large or voting is majority-based (Peng, 2022). Peer reward alone is less effective.
  - *Democratic process*: Increases productivity and may raise perceived group efficiency even without changing total output/payoff (Kamei & Tabero, 2025).
  - *Communication*: Costless disapproval or chat rarely achieves lasting efficiency improvements without the presence of sanctioning options (Lec et al., 2023).

- **Group size, rounds, MPCR, and all-or-nothing:**
  - Effects of punishment on efficiency are moderated by group size, marginal per-capita return (MPCR), and contribution granularity. Increase in group size tends to decrease control efficiency; punishment’s effect on efficiency can either scale up or dissipate with size depending on institutionalization and punishment cost/effect ratio (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Peña et al., 2024).

- **Efficient equilibria and initial conditions:**  
  - Many models/theories support "bistability"—either high- or low-efficiency equilibria can be stable; punishment increases the basin of attraction for the efficient equilibrium but doesn't guarantee it (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Uchida et al., 2024).

- **Behavioral factors:**  
  - Psychological phenomena like prospect theory can amplify punishment effects (Uchida et al., 2024). Social norms and gender can affect willingness to punish but don't directly predict efficiency (Dato & Friehe, 2025).

> **Overall empirical pattern:**  
  - *Punishment-enabled treatments usually increase efficiency relative to control when costs are low and punishment is formal/institutional or well-designed.*
  - *Peer punishment often increases behavioral cooperation but may, depending on cost and frequency, fail to increase or even decrease efficiency due to lost resources.*
  - *The effect is maximized when punishment is credible, visible, and not overused; mitigated or reversed otherwise.*

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the mixed but informative evidence, the following guidance is warranted for predicting treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency:

- **If control efficiency is high (near full cooperation):**  
  - Enabling punishment (especially peer punishment with nontrivial cost) is unlikely to further increase efficiency and may reduce it if used for costly sanctioning of minor deviations (Herne et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).
- **If control efficiency is low (widespread defection):**  
  - Institutional punishment or peer punishment with low cost and high efficacy, particularly in open or unstable groups, is likely to increase efficiency—potentially markedly (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).
- **Design features amplifying punishment’s positive effect:**  
  - Central/facilitated punishment, cost-sharing, credible precommitment, visibility of punishment, moderated group size, and low baseline efficiency.
- **Design features reducing or reversing effect:**  
  - High punishment cost, antisocial/retaliatory punishment, lack of coordination or communication, all-or-nothing contributions, and high efficiency in the control.
- **Peer punishment:**  
  - Should be expected to increase behavioral cooperation but only increase efficiency if the punishment cost/benefit is favorable and antisocial punishment is rare.
  - May not notably increase efficiency in small, homogeneous groups with already moderate cooperation, or may reduce it in cases of excessive costly sanctioning.

- **Caveats:**  
  - Predictions must adjust for reported ambiguities—changing game details (e.g., optional participation, information disclosure, chat, round horizon) can reverse or dilute effects.
  - Empirically, efficiency increases with punishment are neither universal nor reliably large; context, mechanism, and baseline matter.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions (many papers with experimental/theoretical variation):**  
- `player_count` (group size): Modeled and manipulated; clear evidence that group size moderates both baseline efficiency and punishment effectiveness.
- `num_rounds`: Repeated vs. one-shot games and their horizon are common treatments; longer games allow equilibria to emerge.
- `all_or_nothing`: Manipulated in some; all-or-nothing often leads to sharper phase transitions.
- `mpcr`: Often specified; theory shows it is central to cost/benefit tradeoffs and cooperation emergence.
- `punishment_cost`/`punishment_tech`: Almost always specified in punishment-enabled studies; directs both frequency and impact of punishment, fundamental mediators.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Some papers systematically compare reward and punishment institutions.

**Indirectly or only contextually discussed dimensions:**
- `chat`: Included or disabled in several lab designs; generally, lack of chat makes cooperation/punishment effects more pronounced.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes manipulated; the visibility/feedback features affect deterrence but are less universally varied.
- `default_contrib`: Rarely manipulated as a design choice; but opt-in vs. opt-out framing is discussed conceptually in few papers.
- `punishment_magnitude`: Specification of punishment effectiveness (fine-to-cost ratio) is common, but not always labeled as such.
- `reward_magnitude`: Usually set in studies involving reward.

**Effectively missing or not systematically varied:**
- Detailed framing/psychological manipulation (Normative salience, prompt framing).
- Fine-grained variation of information symmetry or partner-matching protocols outside of a few focused studies.
- Simultaneous variation of multiple dimensions (full factorial experimental designs are rare).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Coverage gaps:**  
  - Only a minority of studies provide all the required elements: standard PGG, direct manipulation of punishment, and efficiency (not just behavioral) outcomes. Many informative studies are adjacent, not exact, to typical PGG prediction scenarios.
- **Outcome ambiguity:**  
  - Many results about the effect of punishment on efficiency are inferred from behavioral measures (contributions, cooperation) rather than direct payoff, risking misestimation especially in peer-punishment settings where costs can offset cooperation gains (Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- **Parameter sensitivity:**  
  - Effects of punishment on efficiency are parameter-sensitive (cost, magnitude, visibility, group structure). Single-study generalizations are risky when design details shift.
- **Peer punishment vs. institutional:**  
  - Many studies focus on peer punishment, but significant evidence and theory highlight institutional punishment as qualitatively distinct, with findings not always transferrable.
- **Heterogeneity in adjacent games:**  
  - Several papers model or test in environments adjacent (not identical) to PGGs; while mechanisms may be similar, structural differences (asymmetry, sequentiality, real effort) limit generalizability.
- **Lack of direct parametrized estimates:**  
  - Few studies report quantitative shifts in efficiency by dimension, making precise prediction from design variables challenging, especially for machine learning or predictive modeling purposes.
- **Contextual/cultural factors:**  
  - Observational/ethnographic/cultural studies suggest broader social norms may affect punishment’s efficacy (e.g., aversion to punishment), but these are not systematically tied to game outcomes.

> **In summary:**  
This literature base is informative and suggests cautious, qualified prediction rules, especially regarding punishment’s variable effect on efficiency. However, gaps in direct, multidimensional PGG evidence, reliance on adjacent behavioral outcomes, and context-dependence limit its precision for downstream prediction in new or finely detailed design settings.
