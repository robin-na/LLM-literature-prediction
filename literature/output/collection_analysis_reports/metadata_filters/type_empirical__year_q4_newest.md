# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises a **large collection of empirical, primarily laboratory experimental papers**, with a heavy focus on *public goods games (PGGs) and their direct variants*, and an extensive set of closely related social dilemma environments (CPR games, threshold games, etc.). The vast majority of studies are laboratory experiments systematically varying PGG design features—including the presence and form of peer or institutional punishment, group size, number of rounds, MPCR, feedback, chat, and others.

- **Empirical-Experimental Focus**: Nearly all cited studies use real-payoff lab experiments, with direct manipulation of punishment treatments and clear measurement of efficiency or its close proxies.
- **Scope and Breadth**: The evidence base is *broad for PGGs with and without peer or institutional punishment*, with dozens of studies directly reporting payoff-based efficiency outcomes, and additional studies providing mechanism-level or adjacent results in closely related games.
- **Direct vs. Indirect Outcomes**: While many studies measure group efficiency or average earnings/payoff, a nontrivial subset focuses on contribution, cooperation, or norm compliance rates, which are not equivalent to efficiency and must be distinguished.

Within this rich empirical base, there is limited use of purely theoretical mechanism papers; the set focuses on *empirical demonstrations*, with some studies offering mechanism explanations (e.g., on antisocial punishment or crowding out) but grounded in experimental observation. The paper set is accordingly **highly relevant and well-powered for modeling efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments**, but still leaves some areas/dimensions less directly covered (see below).

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) PGG or Variant**  
- **Relevance: exact/close for >80%** of empirical studies. Nearly all core studies manipulate classic *linear PGGs*, leader/follower PGGs, or dynamic/threshold/CPR variants directly analogous for prediction.
- **Adjacent/weak/none relevance** applies to studies examining dyadic trust/ultimatum games, discrimination games, or abstract allocation games. These are treated cautiously and only inform adjacent mechanisms.

**b) Punishment or Sanctions**  
- **Relevance: exact for the majority**. Most studies directly manipulate the enablement of peer or institutional punishment, varying punishment cost, tech, network reach, or making punishment endogenous.
- **Close relevance** in CPRs, climate games, public bads with penalties, and studies examining weak/collective/third-party/reputation-based sanctions.
- **Adjacent or weak**: Many non-payoff or mechanism studies focus on social/psychological aspects of punishment, or on rewards, exclusion, group formation, or informal gossip as substitutes.

**c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**  
- **Relevance: efficiency or direct payoff is reported in a substantial fraction of studies** (see Outcomes below), enabling direct mapping to the prediction task.
- **Close/adjacent relevance**: Some studies report cooperation or compliance (not efficiency), or only present raw earnings, group output, or related proxies.
- **Weak/none**: Many mechanism-focused or psychological outcome studies do not report payoffs or efficiency, limiting their use for the prediction task.

**Summary:** The literature set is **highly relevant to all three target-relevance criteria**, with a few commonly encountered limitations (use of behavioral outcomes, use of adjacent games, and sparse mapping for some design dimensions).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

## **Payoff-Related Outcomes (Directly Informing Prediction)**
- **Efficiency**: Ratio of observed group payoff to the full-cooperation payoff (reported directly or computable from earnings data) in many core PGG/punishment studies.
- **Group Payoff/Total Earnings/Welfare**: Most studies reporting 'group welfare,' 'average group earnings,' or similar can be mapped to efficiency with knowledge of design parameters (MPCR, rounds, endowments).
- **Surplus/Coins Generated**: Includes total output of group, reduced by punishment/reward costs.
- **Net Earnings (after punishment/reward costs)**: Critical to distinguish from gross payoff, especially where punishment is frequent and costly.
- **Threshold/Success Rates** (for collective-risk games): Used as proxies for aggregate welfare or loss avoidance in some variants.

## **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Not Directly Efficiency)**
- **Contribution Rate/Cooperation Rate**: Frequency or average level of voluntary donations is widely reported, but must be explicitly distinguished from group efficiency (as high contributions can be offset by high punishment cost).
- **Punishment Frequency/Assigned Punishment**: Often used to explain mechanisms or moderate effects; not a final outcome.
- **Norm Compliance, Antisocial Punishment, Retaliation Rates**: Important moderators/mechanisms.
- **Emotional/Normative Responses/Reputation**: Growing use as outcomes in adjacent or mechanism studies; not payoff-based.

**Explicit mapping is critical in the prediction context: behavioral improvements (e.g., higher contributions) do not always translate into higher efficiency if punishment is costly and/or misapplied.**

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## **Synthesis Across Core Dimensions**

- **Punishment Increases Efficiency in Many Standard PGGs**:  
  In canonical repeated, linear PGGs, **enabling peer or centralized punishment typically increases group efficiency** compared to no-punishment control, especially with:
  - Moderate to high MPCR (≥0.4)
  - Low to moderate punishment cost and high effectiveness (e.g., 1:3 cost-impact ratio)
  - Deterministic, observable, and targeted punishment tech
  - Short to moderate group size and number of rounds (e.g., 4-12 players, 5-40 rounds)
  - No severe group heterogeneity or antisocial punishment (Zhang et al., 2024; Kamei, 2024; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Jiang & Wang, 2024; Sparks et al., 2024; Joseph et al., 2025).
  Quantitative effect sizes: peer punishment *adds 0.15-0.50 in efficiency* (range: baseline to near-optimal) depending on design (Eichenseer, 2023).

- **Punishment Can Reduce or Fail to Improve Efficiency in Other Contexts**:
  - **When punishment is costly and use is frequent, and/or antisocial punishment occurs (punishing cooperators, group heterogeneity), efficiency gains from higher contribution are offset or reversed by direct costs** (Chen, 2022; Botelho et al., 2022; Peng, 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Nhim et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025).
  - Negative or zero effects are observed for **heterogeneous groups** (payoff or identity), **noisy or unjust punishment**, and **polycentric/subgroup punishment institutions** (Molenmaker et al., 2023; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022; Peng & Fan, 2023).

- **Strong Moderating Role of Game Design Dimensions**:
  - **Punishment Tech**: Network structure (who can punish whom), observability, effectiveness/cost ratio, and noise (stochasticity) are decisive. Complete peer punishment networks can reduce efficiency relative to incomplete networks due to diffusion of responsibility (Peng & Fan, 2023; Pi et al., 2022; Salahshour et al., 2022).
  - **Group Composition/Structure**: Efficiency gains are larger in homogeneous groups with aligned interests; group composition in identity or background can render punishment ineffective or counterproductive (Molenmaker et al., 2023).
  - **Information/Transparency**: Public feedback, transparency (e.g., of endowments or punishment identity), and monitoring tech enhance the positive effect of punishment (Chen et al., 2025; Chen, 2022; Eisenkopf & Walter, 2022).
  - **Institutional Context (Voting, Facilitation, Endogenous Choice)**: Institutions arising from group choice (democracy, facilitated meetings) or coordinated norms can make punishment substantially more effective, or vice versa (Krügel & Maaser, 2025; DeCaro et al., 2024; Kamei et al., 2023).
  - **Punishment Cost and Efficacy**: Low-cost, high-impact punishment increases efficiency; high-cost or low-impact punishment (including collective, non-targeted) is much less effective or even detrimental (Nhim et al., 2023; Schaefer, 2023).
  - **Reward Mechanisms**: Where reward is available, it often matches or exceeds punishment in boosting efficiency, with fewer negative side effects (Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022).
  - **Form and Strength (Graduated, Formal, Peer)**: Graduated and formal institutions can be more effective under particular conditions; peer punishment is highly sensitive to parameters and context.

- **Dynamic and Context Effects**:
  - **Effect of Repeated Play**: Punishment's impact typically strengthens over time (once learning/cooperation is established and punishment use declines), but high punishment use in early periods can negate gains.
  - **Forward Spillovers**: Punishment's effect often decays or is sensitive to end-game effects (cooperation collapse when punishment is removed; JARUNGRATTANAPONG, 2022).
  - **Endogenous Group Formation (Migration/Open Groups)**: Punishment more robustly increases efficiency in open/migratory societies compared to closed/fixed groups (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).

- **Negative Findings/Trade-offs**:
  - **Punishment Can Lower Efficiency**: Via resource destruction, especially with high antisocial punishment, group heterogeneity, or lack of coordination (Botelho et al., 2022; Chen, 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024).
  - **Sanction Regime Details Matter**: Institution choice, possibility of counter-punishment, collective vs. individual, credible enforcement, and democratic legitimacy all moderate effects.
  - **Mechanism/Process Explanations**: Ineffectiveness is often due to antisocial punishment, lack of strategic/targeted use, or group processes (diffusion, discrimination).

- **Adjacent/Supporting Results**:
  - Evidence in adjacent or alternative environments (CPRs, all-or-nothing games, climate clubs, market externality games) generally concords with main findings: **enabling effective, credible, targeted punishment increases efficiency relative to control only under specific, favorable conditions**.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Implications for Predicting Treatment Efficiency from Design Dimensions & Control Efficiency:**

1. **Punishment almost always increases efficiency relative to control** in standard repeated, linear PGGs when:
   - Punishment cost is moderate to low and effectiveness is high;
   - Network of punishment is complete or covers all (but is not excessively large, to avoid diffusion);
   - Game lacks severe group heterogeneity or strong identity-based conflict.

2. **The magnitude of the effect is strongly moderated by:**
   - **Punishment cost/impact ratio**: Higher cost or lower impact reduces or reverses gains.
   - **Network structure of punishment**: Incomplete targeted networks can prevent efficiency loss from antisocial or redundant punishment.
   - **Antisocial punishment prevalence**: Higher antisocial punishment, common in heterogeneous or conflictual groups, negates efficiency gains.
   - **Type of punishment (peer vs. formal/institutional, deterministic vs. stochastic, individual vs. group-level, graduated vs. strict)**.
   - **Presence of reward/alternative mechanisms**: May substitute or surpass punishment in efficacy.

3. **Control Efficiency is an informative baseline, but insufficient alone**:
   - In homogeneous, moderate-MPCR, well-designed PGGs, treatment efficiency can be predicted as control efficiency plus a positive increment determined by punishment tech and institutional context.
   - In heterogeneous or problematic contexts, control efficiency may not rise—or may fall—when punishment is enabled.

4. **Important "Red Flags" Requiring Caution:**
   - **Designs with costly, frequent, or antisocial punishment (without effective targeting or coordination) can result in no efficiency gain or even negative net effect** (Botelho et al., 2022; Chen, 2022).
   - **Heterogeneous MPCR/endowments, group identity conflicts, or historical conflict** sharply diminish or reverse the efficiency effect.
   - **Noisy, ambiguous, or stochastic punishment, or institutions lacking credibility/enforcement, reduce or eliminate positive effects** (Salahshour et al., 2022; Alt et al., 2023).

5. **Dimension-specific Guidance Supported by Evidence**:
   - **Player Count**: Larger groups sometimes see stronger relative gains from punishment (Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Lo Iacono et al., 2023), but only if punishment keeps pace with free-riding.
   - **Number of Rounds**: Longer games see greater gains as learning and decline in punishment costs accumulate.
   - **All-or-Nothing**: Binary (all-or-nothing) contribution can amplify the impact of punishment depending on the joint effect with MPCR and group size.
   - **MPCR**: Higher MPCRs make cooperation more profitable, increasing the room for punishment to restore/raise efficiency.
   - **Punishment Cost & Tech**: See above—these are especially critical and often directly measurable from experimental results.
   - **Institutional Design** (e.g., formal vs. peer, endogenous choice, democracy) has large, sometimes decisive moderation effects.

6. **When Evidence is Based on Non-Payoff Outcomes:**  
   - If only contribution or norm compliance is measured (not group payoff), **do not infer efficiency effects except in designs where punishment use is minimal or the cost is clearly negligible.**
   - In PGGs with high punishment frequency, efficiency and contribution are *not* isomorphic.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `all_or_nothing`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` — **Strong empirical grounding; effects on efficiency modulated in multiple papers.**
- `chat` (communication): Well studied as a moderator of both baseline efficiency and the impact of punishment.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Addressed in several studies, especially on comparative efficacy and interaction with punishment.
- `default_contrib` (opt-in/opt-out): Variably studied, with some evidence on framing effects.

**Indirectly or Contextually Discussed:**
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` — *Information structures* are sometimes specified and found to moderate effects (transparency, feedback, monitoring), but are less frequently subject to systematic variation in the context of payoff outcomes.
- Heterogeneity in endowments, MPCR, or group composition: Directly analyzed in multiple papers, with clear findings on moderation of punishment efficiency.

**Effectively Missing or Sparse:**
- Systematic variation/comparison of `punishment_tech` (network structure, targeting) beyond a handful of recent studies.
- Detailed manipulation of information display dimensions (apart from broad chat or transparency conditions).
- Rich contextual features (real-world group composition, social proximity, political legitimacy) are typically only addressed in single papers or mechanism discussions.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Variable Outcome Definitions**: Many studies use contribution, cooperation, or compliance as primary outcomes; only a subset provide the group efficiency or payoff ratios directly required for prediction. Careful mapping is required to avoid inferring efficiency gains from contribution increases in settings with high punishment costs.
- **Payoff Generalizability**: Effects documented in standard linear PGGs may not generalize straightforwardly to:
  - Heterogeneous or asymmetric games
  - All-or-nothing or threshold games
  - Games with extensive payoff or group composition complexity (e.g., multilevel, networked, dynamic CPRs)
- **Contextual Moderation**: Effects of punishment are frequently amplified or reversed by context (group history, identity, prior conflict, endogenous migration, open/closed societies, institutional legitimacy), but generalization across contexts requires caution.
- **Design Parameter Gaps**: Some design dimensions (detailed feedback, summary information, punishment observability) are underexplored in direct relation to efficiency, and must be calibrated indirectly or with caution.
- **Sparse Evidence at Scale/Field**: While there are some field, cross-cultural, and lab-in-the-field studies, most direct payoff results are from small-group, incentivized lab settings; external validity to field or organizational environments is less certain.
- **Contributor Behavior ≠ Efficiency**: The field is replete with behavioral results that cannot be directly used for payoff-based prediction.
- **Limited Evidence on Mechanism Interactions**: Many papers test single design feature manipulations; complex, interacting designs (e.g., chat + punishment + reward + endogenous group formation) remain less well explored.

---

**In Summary:**  
The literature base provides **rich, direct evidence for many main design dimensions and PGG variants relevant to predicting the efficiency boost (or reduction) from enabling punishment** across a range of environments. **Punishment often increases efficiency, but only under specific, favorable parameter regimes** (moderate cost, high impact, strategic targeting, minimal antisocial use, and supportive context/institution). **Negative or null effects are frequently observed—especially in highly heterogeneous, conflictual, noisy, or poorly coordinated settings.** Empirical findings robustly demonstrate that control efficiency and game design dimensions jointly determine the likely treatment efficiency, but that generalization beyond standard laboratory PGGs must be done with care, drawing only on results from studies matching the prediction context.
