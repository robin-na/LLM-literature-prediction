# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is broad in scope, encompassing 64 primarily theoretical papers, with a mix of adjacent, close, and (less often) exact-relevance analyses for public goods games (PGGs), sanctions/punishment, and efficiency/welfare outcomes. The majority are formal models or conceptual/theoretical arguments without empirical experiment or field data. The coverage is wide regarding mechanisms, moderators, and variants (including institution vs. peer punishment, group structure, repeated games, and signal/reputation mechanisms), but direct experimental assessments of efficiency outcomes in PGGs with and without punishment are rare. Most of the evidence is theoretical and simulation-based; empirical validation or direct group payoff data is limited or absent.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:** The set contains several exact-relevance (PGG or linear/threshold PGG) theory papers (e.g., Prétôt et al., 2024; Greenwood et al., 2018; Liu & Guo, 2010), many more close/adjacent (trust games, repeated prisoner's dilemma, CPR, resource games, team trust games, etc.), and some with only weak or contextual connections.

**punishment_or_sanctions:** Exact-relevance punishment/sanction mechanism is addressed in a subset of papers (Prétôt et al., 2024; Greenwood et al., 2018; Cooney, 2025; etc.), with others discussing reward, ostracism, exclusion, or informal/social pressure as analogues. Many papers distinguish between peer punishment and institutional (collective) punishment.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** Direct measurement/modeling of group efficiency or related payoff/welfare is present in a moderate subset (e.g., Prétôt et al., 2024; Cooney, 2025; Duong et al., 2024; Vinayak, 2025; Kurokawa, 2023; Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023). Many papers instead focus primarily on cooperation/contribution rates, norm compliance, or strategy frequencies (non-payoff behavioral outcomes) and only infer likely efficiency effects.

**Summary:** The evidence set is strongest for theoretical/mechanism exploration of PGG-like games with punishment, but has gaps with respect to empirical validation and direct efficiency/total payoff results in experimental PGGs.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

### Payoff-related outcomes (`efficiency`, `group payoff`, `welfare`, `total coins/earnings`):
- Explicit efficiency/total payoff is modeled or reported in key theory papers (Prétôt et al., 2024; Greenwood et al., 2018; Cooney, 2025; Vinayak, 2025; Kristensen et al., 2025; Kurokawa, 2023; Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023; Corriveau, 2012; Laclau & Tomala, 2017; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015).
- Some closely related work treats payoff as expected group fitness, evolutionary success, or sustainable yield.
- Most institutional modeling papers link behavioral outcomes to group welfare but do not always present explicit efficiency ratios.

### Non-payoff outcomes (`contribution rate`, `cooperation rate`, `punishment assigned`):
- Many theory/simulation papers use the fraction of cooperators, successful punishment events, or norm compliance as proxies for system performance (e.g., Wu & Sun, 2022; Du et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2023; Kido & Takezawa, 2024; Solferino et al., 2018).
- Some papers use "abundance of persistent cooperators" (Liu & Guo, 2010) or similar measures as proxies for group welfare.

### Mixed outcomes:
- Some papers discuss both strategy frequencies (cooperation/defection) and group-level payoffs as linked but distinguishable.

**Distinction:** While non-payoff outcomes may correlate with efficiency, only explicit efficiency or group payoff results should be considered direct evidence for the prediction task.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across papers:

**(a) Enabling Punishment Can Increase Efficiency—But Only Under Restrictive Conditions**
- Multiple theory papers (Prétôt et al., 2024; Greenwood et al., 2018; Vinayak, 2025; Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023; Corriveau, 2012; Cooney, 2025; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015; Celik & Peters, 2016) conclude that punishment strongly raises efficiency *if* it is:
   - Sufficiently strong/effective relative to the gain from defection;
   - Not excessively costly;
   - Properly targeted at defectors (minimizing errors and not punishing cooperators/second-order free riders);
   - Supported by appropriate monitoring or information structure;
   - Sometimes, coordinated or institutional rather than purely peer-based.
- Institutional punishment (tax- or pool-funded, collective monitoring) is more reliably efficiency-enhancing than peer punishment, which is vulnerable to second-order free-rider problems and low equilibrium efficiency (Prétôt et al.; Greenwood et al.; Wang et al., 2023).

**(b) The Efficiency Impact is Moderated by Cost Structure, Monitoring, and Sanction Technology**
- If the punishment mechanism imposes high costs or is easily misapplied, efficiency gains may be offset or the net effect may be negative (Cooney, 2025; Kurokawa, 2023; Mihm & Toth, 2020; Antoci & Zarri, 2015; Laclau & Tomala, 2017).
- Monitoring structure (who can see whom, immediacy of detection, public vs. private) is critical; rich or public monitoring enables more efficient use of punishment (Mihm & Toth, 2020; Laclau & Tomala, 2017; Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023).
- Peer punishment is generally less efficient unless mechanisms are present to solve the second-order free rider problem (Cooney, 2025; Greenwood et al., 2018; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).

**(c) Necessary Thresholds and Non-linear/Tipping Effects**
- There exist threshold conditions (governed by punishment cost, strength, population structure, and baseline cooperation) that determine whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency (Prétôt et al., 2024; Greenwood et al., 2018; Vinayak, 2025).
- Increasing punishment beyond a certain point can reduce efficiency due to excessive cost (Cooney, 2025).
- The effect of punishment is often non-linear—small changes in parameters can dramatically change system efficiency.

**(d) Population Structure and Group Size**
- Larger groups may require different sanction thresholds or structures to preserve efficiency; targeted institutional punishment is more scalable (Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023; Corriveau, 2012; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015).
- In large groups, absence of punishment collapses efficiency, especially if some commitment/defection types exist; strong, targeted punishment can restore efficiency if implemented well (Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023).

**(e) Second-Order Free Riding and Robust Mechanism Design**
- Fragility of punishment effects is a recurring theme: presence of unconditional cooperators, mal-targeted punishment, or unpunished non-punishers leads to breakdowns in efficiency (Antoci & Zarri, 2015; Greenwood et al., 2018).
- Some models identify "punish non-punishers" or "mixed reciprocity" as more robust strategies for maximizing efficiency (Olcina & Calabuig, 2015; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).

**(f) Monitoring/Information, Reputation, and Network Effects**
- Efficiency gains from punishment are higher when players have sufficient information to target defectors, but not so precise as to enable strategic abuse or retaliation (Mihm & Toth, 2020; Larson, 2016).

**(g) Reward Mechanisms**
- In some models, institutional rewards are as effective or more effective than punishment in increasing efficiency (Wang et al., 2023).

**(h) Indirect or Social Punishment**
- Social pressure (psychological, reputational, exclusion) can act analogously to material punishment, but effect sizes and robustness may differ (Castro et al., 1998; Furuzono et al., 2013).

**Summary:** 
- The introduction of punishment will increase efficiency relative to control only if key conditions (cost, severity, accuracy, monitoring, and group structure) are met. Otherwise, the effect may be minimal, fragile, or negative.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct Predictive Implications:**
- For a given control efficiency, *enabling* punishment should increase treatment efficiency **if**:
   - The cost of punishment is moderate, relative to the marginal benefit of cooperation (Prétôt et al., 2024; Greenwood et al., 2018; Vinayak, 2025; Cooney, 2025);
   - Punishment technology is effective and not easily misapplied;
   - There is adequate monitoring, so defectors are reliably detected;
   - The group size and number of rounds are not so large that peer or decentralized punishment breaks down;
   - Institutional (collectively funded) punishment exists or strong coordination among punishers is possible;
   - Reward mechanisms are considered and, if available, may be as effective or more so than punishment.

- **If these conditions are not met** (e.g., high punishment cost, low detection accuracy, absence of second-order punisher control, or insufficient group monitoring), enabling punishment can fail to increase efficiency—or *reduce* it due to wasted cost (Kurokawa, 2023; Cooney, 2025; Mihm & Toth, 2020).

- In large groups or settings with commitment types (i.e., some always defectors), absence of punishment virtually guarantees low efficiency. Targeted, involuntary punishment is necessary for high efficiency (Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023).

- Some models show strong non-linearities: small parameter changes (punishment level, cost, monitoring power) can tip the system from low to high efficiency or vice versa.

- The design dimensions most critical for prediction are `player_count`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, and monitoring/information (`show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`). Institutional design dimensions (e.g., `reward_exists`, collective funding, or centralized vs. peer sanctioning) are also central.

**Caution:**
- Do not assume punishment always increases efficiency; check whether cost and structure meets the model’s necessary thresholds.
- Use explicit formulas or phase diagrams when available (Prétôt et al., Greenwood et al., Vinayak, Cooney).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical Evidence:** The set is almost entirely theoretical or simulation-based, limiting direct calibration of treatment effect sizes with real behavioral data.
- **Efficiency Outcomes:** Many studies use behavioral or proxy outcomes (cooperation rate, frequency of punishers) rather than explicit efficiency or total payoff calculations.
- **Generalizability:** Results for repeated games, institutional settings, or group-structured models may not directly transfer to one-shot, small-group, or purely peer-punishment PGGs.
- **Interaction Effects:** Many models assume perfect implementation of game mechanisms and do not account for framing/motivation crowding, heterogeneities, or social context effects that may undermine simple predictions (Gold, 2020; Eriksson, 2019; Wittek, 2022).
- **Design Space Coverage:** Several design dimensions relevant to prediction (e.g., `default_contrib`, detailed reward mechanisms, fine-grained information cues) are underexamined within this literature set.
- **Ambiguity/Contingency:** Theoretical consensus is that punishment *may* increase efficiency, but only under proper parameter regimes—results are often non-linear or sensitive to unmodeled moderators (e.g., population composition, error rates, network clustering, presence of alternative incentives).
- **Crowding Out and Long-term Effects:** Some papers point to the possibility that punishment/incentives may erode prosocial motivation or dynamic efficiency in the long run (Povey, 2014; Gold, 2020).

---

**In summary:**  
This literature set provides strong but primarily theoretical guidance: punishment can increase PGG efficiency, but only if cost/efficacy/monitoring thresholds are met, and only if the mechanism is robust to second-order free riding and information errors. Prediction should focus on the most strongly evidenced dimensions and use threshold calculations from theory where available. Uncertainties remain regarding empirical robustness and effects outside idealized model settings.
