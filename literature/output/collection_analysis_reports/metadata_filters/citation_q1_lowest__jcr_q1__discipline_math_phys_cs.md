# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set of 103 studies is predominantly theoretical and simulation-based, with a secondary presence of lab experiments and observational studies. The majority of empirical results are based on simulations or dynamical models, with a small number of laboratory experiments directly manipulating punishment in games structurally similar to the public goods game (PGG). The literature focus is generally narrow for the downstream prediction task: most studies model public goods games or close variants with detailed consideration of the mechanisms and dynamics of punishment, reward, cooperation, and efficiency, but comparatively few provide direct empirical data on efficiency changes due to enabling peer punishment in PGGs with realistic experimental parameters.

The set is strongest in formal modeling of punishment and social dilemma mechanisms, and in theoretical and simulation results concerning efficiency and cooperation under punishment. Direct empirical demonstration of efficiency changes due to peer punishment—especially in laboratory PGGs with full reporting of both control and punishment-enabled group payoffs—is relatively scarce. Some empirical experimental studies exist, but they are often in closely related games (e.g., repeated prisoner’s dilemma, trust games, or resource extraction games) rather than canonical PGGs. There are also studies on adjacent mechanisms (reward, exclusion, partner choice, reputation, etc.) which provide contextual evidence.

Overall, the evidence base is broad for theoretical and mechanistic understanding but relatively narrow and uneven for empirical, payoff-focused prediction tasks directly calibrated to the efficiency effect of enabling peer punishment in standard or variant PGGs.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**  
- The literature is dominated by studies labeled `pgg=exact`, many of which directly study standard or spatial public goods games (PGGs), sometimes with institutional or design variants (e.g., spatial structure, probabilistic punishment, threshold-based rules).  
- A substantial number of papers use close variants (common pool resource games, feedback-evolving games, repeated PDs), which are `pgg=close` or `pgg=adjacent`.  
- There is a significant subset modeling dyadic social dilemmas, trust games, or resource sharing environments, whose generalizability to PGGs is context-dependent.

**Punishment or Sanctions:**  
- Many studies are `punishment=exact`, directly modeling peer punishment, pool punishment, or central exclusion mechanisms in PGGs or variants.  
- Several relevant studies are `punishment=close` or `punishment=adjacent`: modeling reward-based incentives, indirect punishment, exclusion, or related mechanisms (partner choice, costly signals, etc.) which are not standard peer punishment.  
- Some works include both explicit monetary or resource-based punishment and non-monetary punishment (e.g., blame/shame, exclusion, social norm enforcement).

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**  
- Papers with `payoff=exact` outcomes are fewer but provide direct evidence: some theoretical and empirical papers report group efficiency as group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum.  
- Many studies report `payoff=adjacent` outcomes (e.g., group payoff, total coins, net benefit, resource sustainability) that are closely related but sometimes not normalized for maximum achievable efficiency.  
- Many studies report only cooperation rates, strategy proportions, or other non-payoff behavioral outcomes; these are important for mechanism insight but less directly useful for efficiency prediction.

**Summary:**  
- Relevance to the downstream prediction task is strongest among papers with `pgg=exact`, `punishment=exact`, and `payoff=exact`.  
- The set contains high task-relevance for mechanism understanding, moderate coverage for adjacent or close variants, but relatively thin direct empirical coverage for the downstream prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Directly Inform Prediction):**  
- **Efficiency** (group payoff / max possible from full cooperation): explicitly measured in some theoretical and simulation-based works (e.g., Ohdaira, 2025; Gao & Li, 2023; Jia & Wang, 2024; Lu et al., 2024; Wang & Shen, 2024; Evans & Thomas, 2001).  
- **Total group payoff, welfare, surplus, or resource sustainability:** reported in a subset of studies, sometimes under dynamic or threshold conditions, and often used as a proxy for efficiency.
- **Net benefit** or cost-effectiveness comparisons (benefit from cooperation minus sanctioning costs) appear in some studies (e.g., Shuvo & Kabir, 2024).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Indirect or Adjacent):**  
- **Cooperation rate, contribution rate:** the most frequent outcomes in both theoretical and simulation papers. High relevance for mechanism insight, but do not account for cost of sanctioning or net efficiency.
- **Punishment frequency, assigned punishment, strategy proportions, norm compliance, prevalence of institutional support:** widely measured, providing insight into underlying processes but not directly mapping to group efficiency.
- **Stability, convergence, or phase diagrams:** used for mapping dynamic regimes (e.g., coexistence, full cooperation, cyclic dominance).
- **Indirect outcomes:** resource abundance, sustainability, network structure stability, or robustness to noise.

**Empirical Experimental Outcomes:**  
- **Group payoffs and efficiency:** rare but present, mostly in lab or field studies; more commonly, behavioral measures are prioritized.
- **Contribution changes following punishment introduction:** observed, but often without direct report of efficiency ratios.

**Distinction:**  
While there is rich behavioral outcome data, direct measurement of efficiency—especially normalized efficiency in the presence and absence of peer punishment—is rare and mostly theoretical or simulation-based.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Direct Payoff/Efficiency Findings (Empirical and Theory):**  
- **Enabling punishment often increases group efficiency relative to the control (no-punishment) baseline**—but the magnitude and even the direction depend on several key design dimensions (punishment cost, effectiveness, institutional support, etc.) [(Ohdaira, 2025); (Gao et al., 2020); (Jia & Wang, 2024); (Lu et al., 2024); (Wang & Shen, 2024); (Evans & Thomas, 2001)].
- **Threshold effects are common:** There exists a critical set of parameters (e.g., consensus/willingness to punish, punishment intensity, cost, etc.)—above which punishment yields high efficiency (often via full cooperation), but below which it may have no effect or even decrease efficiency due to wasted costs [(Gao & Li, 2023); (Gao et al., 2025); (Gao, Pan, & He, 2024)].
- **Punishment cost and implementation matter:** Efficiency gains depend on the relative cost of punishment to fine imposed, whether costs are shared, and whether more sophisticated dynamic rules (negative feedback, dynamic adaptation) are used [(Ohdaira, 2025); (Qian et al., 2022)].
- **Institutional context, targeting, and norm coordination are important moderators:** Institutionalized and well-targeted punishment (including pool punishment, exclusion, or grievance procedures) produce better efficiency gains than uncoordinated peer punishment [(Lu et al., 2024); (Wang & Shen, 2024); (Macleod, Lara, & Zehnder, 2025)].
- **Contextual factors can reverse the effect:** In poorly coordinated, high-cost, or normless environments, enabling punishment may decrease efficiency relative to control [(Macleod, Lara, & Zehnder, 2025); (McBride et al., 2016)]. Similarly, antisocial punishment, wealth visibility, and corruption can undermine or reverse efficiency gains from punishment [(Zhang et al., 2020); (Qian et al., 2023)].

**Adjacent and Mechanism Findings:**  
- **Reward-only or hybrid incentive schemes may be less effective than punishment or hybrid punishment/reward for maximizing efficiency**—especially when resources are renewable and incentives are optimally calibrated [(Lu et al., 2024); (Wang et al., 2024)].
- **Dynamic, feedback-based, or probabilistic punishment mechanisms** can expand the parameter space for stability and efficiency [(Qian et al., 2022); (Gao, Pan, & He, 2024)].
- **Spatial/network structure interacts with punishment:** Spatial clustering, network topology, partner choice, and heterogeneity affect the threshold and robustness of efficiency gains from punishment [(Gao et al., 2025); (Liu et al., 2022); (Chica & Santos, 2023)].
- **Behavioral measures (cooperation rates)** are typically higher under punishment but may not translate directly to efficiency if punishment is costly or misapplied.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the synthesized evidence, **the literature supports these main guidelines for predicting treatment efficiency (with peer punishment enabled) from design dimensions and control efficiency**:

1. **Punishment usually increases efficiency in settings where baseline (control) efficiency is low due to free-riding, provided the punishment is not prohibitively costly and is sufficiently targeted or institutionalized** [(Ohdaira, 2025); (Gao et al., 2020); (Jia & Wang, 2024); (Lu et al., 2024); (Evans & Thomas, 2001)].
2. **The magnitude of the efficiency gain is moderated by game and punishment design dimensions**: punishment cost, effectiveness, mode (peer vs. pool/institutional), consensus threshold, network structure, number of players, and presence of supporting institutions (e.g., grievance procedures) [(Gao & Li, 2023); (Gao et al., 2025); (Macleod, Lara, & Zehnder, 2025)].
3. **Punishment may not increase (and can even decrease) efficiency when**:
   - Punishment is too costly relative to its effect;
   - There is high antisocial or misapplied punishment;
   - Norms are uncoordinated or absent;
   - Corruption, bribery, or wealth visibility destabilize cooperation [(Macleod, Lara, & Zehnder, 2025); (Zhang et al., 2020); (Qian et al., 2023)].
4. **Efficiency gains from punishment are often largest when baseline control efficiency is low and punishment is well-calibrated and coordinated**—there may be diminishing or negative returns if control efficiency is already high due to other mechanisms (network reciprocity, voluntary participation, information structure) [(Gao et al., 2025)].
5. **Dynamic and threshold-based punishment, as well as feedback-based adaptation, can widen the efficiency-improving region** in parameter space; static or rigid punishment schemes risk excessive costs [(Qian et al., 2022); (Gao, Pan, & He, 2024)].

**Dimensionally, the best predictions will explicitly account for the key moderating design dimensions identified below. Behavioral outcomes (like cooperation rates) may be correlated with efficiency but should not be substituted for direct efficiency measures, especially when punishment is costly or misapplied.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions (with outcome implications):**
- **player_count:** Extensively considered; impacts thresholds for punishment effectiveness (larger groups often need higher punishment or better coordination) [(Gao et al., 2020); (Ohdaira, 2025)].
- **num_rounds:** Widely addressed; repeated games support greater punishment effectiveness (longer games allow for sustained threats) [(Evans & Thomas, 2001); (Macleod, Lara, & Zehnder, 2025)].
- **mpcr (marginal per-capita return):** Direct determinant of cooperation baseline and punishment thresholds [(Gao et al., 2025); (Wang & Shen, 2024)].
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Central—efficiency gains hinge on punishment cost relative to punishment effectiveness, and the mode/implementation (peer, institutional, probabilistic, dynamic, targeted, etc.) [(Gao & Li, 2023); (Qian et al., 2022); (Lu et al., 2024); (Macleod, Lara, & Zehnder, 2025)].
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Less common, but explicitly modeled in hybrid incentive studies; effects on efficiency less consistent than for punishment [(Lu et al., 2024); (Wang et al., 2024); (Chen & Chen, 2020)].
- **all_or_nothing (vs continuous contribution):** Modeled in both cases; interaction with punishment thresholds and cooperation stability noted [(Ohdaira, 2025); (Gao & Li, 2023)].
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Sometimes modeled—information and transparency can influence the strategic value and cost of punishment [(Evans & Thomas, 2001); (Macleod, Lara, & Zehnder, 2025); (Zhang et al., 2020)].

**Indirectly or Contextually Discussed:**
- **chat (communication):** When modeled, it majorly impacts norm emergence and punishment effectiveness, but is rarely directly manipulated [(Macleod, Lara, & Zehnder, 2025)].
- **default_contrib:** Rarely manipulated; framing effects on baseline cooperation may moderate punishment needs, but evidence is sparse.
- **punishment_tech:** Extensively varied (peer, pool, centralized, dynamic, probabilistic, exclusion, etc.), often with explicit mapping to efficiency outcomes.
- **reward_cost, reward_tech:** Present in some hybrid incentive studies with explicit results.

**Effectively Missing or Very Sparse:**
- **show_punishment_id:** Only occasionally modeled; the effect of anonymity vs. transparency on efficiency via punishment is discussed but not a major focus.
- **default_contribProp:** Framing effects (default in/out) are largely unstudied in this set re: payoff efficiency.
- **Fine gradient control over each display or framing variable.**

# 7) Important Limitations

**1. Scarcity of Direct Empirical Data:**  
- There is a striking lack of laboratory or field experiments directly reporting efficiency (normalized group payoff) with and without peer punishment in PGGs using all relevant design dimensions. Much of what is available is simulation or modeling evidence.

**2. Overreliance on Theoretical Models and Simulations:**  
- Most direct efficiency outcomes are derived in idealized or stylized settings, with strong game-theoretic assumptions. Behavioral complexity, heterogeneity, and psychological factors that affect real-world payoff outcomes may not be fully captured.

**3. Mechanism Generalizability:**  
- Many studies that show strong efficiency gains from punishment rely on optimal conditions (e.g., low-cost, high-effectiveness punishment, strong institution support, infinite or large populations, deterministic updating). These may not generalize to smaller, noisy, or miscoordinated group environments.

**4. Limited Coverage of Design Dimensions:**  
- Some critical prediction variables (chat, default contribution framing, transparency features) are sparsely addressed or not systematically varied with payoff reporting, limiting high-dimensional prediction.

**5. Efficiency vs. Behavioral Outcomes:**  
- Many papers report only cooperation rates or behavioral patterns, rather than group efficiency. Efficiency must often be inferred under the assumption that increased cooperation translates to increased efficiency, which is not always valid if punishment is costly.

**6. Contextual and Institutional Moderators:**  
- Studies show that institutional support, norm coordination, wealth visibility, and anti-social punishment can dramatically moderate or even reverse the efficiency impact of punishment. Few models integrate these moderators systematically.

**7. Lack of Cross-Validation:**  
- Comparing findings across game variants, payoff calculation models, and implementation details can be problematic, especially when “efficiency” is defined variably.

**8. Peer vs. Pool vs. Institutional Punishment:**  
- Much of the theory/simulation literature models idealized or institutionalized punishment rather than the decentralized, peer-to-peer punishment found in many empirical PGG experiments.

---

## **Summary**

- The literature base is rich in theoretical, mechanistic understanding of punishment’s role in cooperation and efficiency in PGG-like environments, but sparse in direct, high-powered experimental evidence linking peer punishment to normalized efficiency using all prediction dimensions.
- For prediction tasks, evidence supports that enabling punishment generally increases efficiency relative to control, conditional on reasonable punishment cost–effectiveness ratios, sufficient willingness to punish, and strong norm/institutional support.
- Key moderators include player count, number of rounds, mpcr, punishment cost/technology, and information structure; less is known about chat, default framing, and finer transparency features.
- Efficiency gains are not universal: they may be absent or even negative when punishment is costly, uncoordinated, or under adverse informational or institutional contexts.
- Prediction should rely most heavily on studies directly linking game design dimensions and control efficiency to efficiency changes under punishment, while remaining cautious about generalizing mechanism findings from stylized or adjacent domains.
