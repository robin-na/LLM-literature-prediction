# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists entirely of theoretical and review papers (no direct empirical or original experimental studies are present), with a strong emphasis on evolutionary models, simulation, and integrative reviews drawing on prior empirical work. The set of 48 papers is broad in its coverage of theoretical mechanisms and moderators relevant to public-goods-game-like (PGG) environments and includes key foundational and widely cited models regarding punishment, norm enforcement, and efficiency outcomes.

A significant subset of the papers is directly targeted at public goods games (or very close variants), with repeated attention to peer punishment, institutional punishment, and related sanctions. However, the empirical basis for quantitative prediction of efficiency effects is always referenced from prior studies, and papers vary in how directly they align with the prediction task focused on average efficiency as a function of game design and control efficiency.

Notably, several reviews repeatedly cite the same core set of experimental results (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000, 2002), meaning that while the coverage is broad, the empirical evidence base that directly targets payoff effects of punishment in PGGs is not as deep as the theoretical or interpretive literature. The remainder of the paper set includes adjacent models (e.g., indirect reciprocity, partner choice, social exchange variants) and broader sociological, psychological, or anthropological reviews, which use PGGs as case studies or analogies rather than focal empirical objects.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessing the literature set on the three relevance dimensions:

**a) pgg_or_variant**

- **Exact**: A substantial proportion of the core papers focus explicitly on public goods games or direct variants (e.g., Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Gintis, 2003; Boyd & Richerson, 1992; Parks et al., 2013).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Several papers model broader social dilemmas, indirect reciprocity, partner choice, or social exchange, applying some results to PGGs but not studying them directly.
- **Weak/None**: A minority discuss group cooperation abstractly or in animal/human societies without simulations or predictions directly tied to PGG structure.

**b) punishment_or_sanctions**

- **Exact**: Most core reviews and theoretical models focus specifically on peer punishment or sanctions in PGGs.
- **Close/Adjacent**: Some models treat institutional/cultural sanctions, rewards, ostracism, reputation systems, or more general forms of norm enforcement (some of which are not "punishment" per se).
- **Weak/None**: Very few papers ignore punishment completely; most at least contextually discuss its role.

**c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**

- **Exact**: A select set of theoretical/simulation papers make efficiency or direct group payoff a focal outcome (e.g., Fehr & Gintis, 2007; MACY, 1993; Gintis, 2003; Boyd & Richerson, 1992; Cai & Kock, 2009).
- **Close**: Many more discuss outcomes proxied by cooperation/contribution rates and infer payoff trends, sometimes with ambiguous translation to efficiency.
- **Adjacent/Weak**: A large portion (especially sociological/psychological reviews) focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes such as norm compliance, punishment frequency, and cooperation, and may either not report or not quantify efficiency or payoff changes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes** (directly aligned with prediction task):

- *Efficiency* (group payoff relative to maximum possible),
- *Total group earnings/welfare/surplus*,
- *Total coins generated*.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**

- *Contribution (cooperation) rates*, 
- *Punishment frequency*, 
- *Norm compliance*, 
- *Strategy prevalence*, 
- *Punishment assigned/received*, 
- *Antisocial punishment*, 
- *Fairness perceptions*.

A minority of the literature directly models or synthesizes group efficiency or aggregate payoffs as a function of game design and the presence/absence of punishment. The majority focus on behavioral dynamics (e.g., sustaining cooperation, preventing free-riding, norm stability) and make qualitative or indirect claims about their impact on welfare or group efficiency.

Several theoretical and review papers explicitly caution that *increases in cooperation rate do not always translate to gains in group efficiency*, mainly due to the resource costs of administering punishment, the possibility of antisocial punishment, or retaliation, which can diminish or reverse the efficiency gains from increased cooperation (Guala, 2012; Kurzban et al., 2015; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**a) General Effect of Punishment**

- The majority of theoretical models and widely cited reviews agree that *enabling punishment in PGGs robustly increases cooperation rates* and, under many conditions, *prevents or reverses the typical decline in cooperation seen without punishment* (Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Gintis, 2003; MACY, 1993; Parks et al., 2013).
- Where efficiency or payoff is measured, enabling punishment often leads to *marked increases in group efficiency, sometimes approaching the cooperative maximum*—but there are important caveats.

**b) Moderators and Contingencies**

- **Punishment cost and effectiveness**: The net effect of punishment on efficiency is crucially moderated by the *costliness of punishing* and the *punishment's impact on the target* (Gintis, 2003; Boyd & Richerson, 1992; Kurzban et al., 2015). If punishment is too expensive (resource-destroying) relative to its deterrence effect, group efficiency can stagnate or decline, even as cooperation rates rise.
- **Antisocial punishment, retaliation, and norm constraints**: Efficiency gains from punishment are undermined in the presence of *antisocial punishment* (punishing cooperators), counter-punishment, or weak norm constraints that allow destructive sanctioning (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Sylwester et al., 2013).
- **Game repetition (number of rounds)**: The efficiency gains from punishment are more likely to be realized in *long repeated interactions* (many rounds) as the effect of punishment compounds and the need for actual punishment diminishes over time as cooperation stabilizes (Guala, 2012; MACY, 1993).
- **Group size (player_count)**: Punishment more effectively sustains cooperation and efficiency in small-to-moderate-sized groups; positive effects often diminish with larger groups unless institutional-level mechanisms or coordinated punishment arise (Parks et al., 2013; Boyd & Richerson, 1992).
- **Communication (chat)** and information availability: Communication (e.g., chat, sharing round summaries, showing who punished) can facilitate coordination on norm enforcement, reduce costs of punishment, and boost efficiency (Guala, 2012; Parks et al., 2013). Lack of communication or information about others (absence of player IDs or summary stats) tends to make punishment less effective and more wasteful.
- **MPCR and enhancement factor**: The marginal per-capita return (MPCR) modulates both the temptation to free-ride and the returns to restoring cooperation via punishment. Higher MPCR makes cooperation easier to sustain and lowers the punishment intensity needed to achieve high efficiency (Cai & Kock, 2009; Chen & Perc, 2014).
- **Reward mechanisms**: The presence of reward options (alone or in combination with punishment) can also increase efficiency and, in some models, may be more cost-effective than punishment (Rand & Nowak, 2013; Parks et al., 2013; Hua & Liu, 2024).

**c) Disagreement and Ambiguity**

- A recurrent disagreement is on whether *enabling costly peer punishment typically increases efficiency in practical, finite-round lab PGGs*: while some reviews report large gains (Fehr & Gintis, 2007), others insist that the resource costs of punishment often offset or even exceed the welfare gains from increased cooperation (Guala, 2012; Kurzban et al., 2015; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- The success of punishment at increasing efficiency appears highly contingent on design details: low-cost, coordinated, or institutional punishment is often necessary for net gains; in the absence of these, peer punishment can reduce efficiency even as it increases cooperation (Guala, 2012; Rand & Nowak, 2013; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- There is general agreement that efficiency can be high with punishment and the right institutional context, but not in all circumstances.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For predicting treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency:**

- **If the control efficiency is low and punishment is enabled,** and:
    - Punishment is not prohibitively costly to the punisher,
    - Punishment opportunities are visible and allow effective targeting,
    - Antisocial punishment is rare or the institutional context suppresses it,
    - The game is repeated for many rounds,
    - Group size is moderate,
    - There is at least some opportunity for coordination (e.g., communication, summary info),
    
  **Then:** Substantial efficiency gains above the control are likely, potentially approaching full-cooperation levels (Fehr & Gintis, 2007; MACY, 1993; Gintis, 2003; Boyd & Richerson, 1992).

- **If punishment is costly, uncoordinated, or antisocial punishment is prevalent,** or if the number of rounds is short, the expected gains in efficiency are *attenuated or can even be negative*, despite potentially elevated cooperation rates (Guala, 2012; Kurzban et al., 2015; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).

- **Key dimensions that must be considered in prediction** (supported by the literature) are: *player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, punishment_tech/effectiveness, MPCR, communication or information features (chat, show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id), reward_exists, and the institutional context shaping norm enforcement.*

- *The existing literature supports modeling the effect of punishment as large, positive, and robust under favorable design conditions, but conditional and potentially null or negative under costly, short, uncoordinated, or norm-weak circumstances.*

- **Crucially, increases in contribution/cooperation rates cannot be assumed to directly translate into efficiency gains unless the cost structure and social context are taken into account.** Models that base predictions solely on behavioral shifts without accounting for punishment and coordination costs are likely to mis-estimate treatment efficiency (Guala, 2012; Rand & Nowak, 2013).

- *Reward options, if present, might produce more robust efficiency gains than punishment alone (Rand & Nowak, 2013; Parks et al., 2013; Hua & Liu, 2024).*

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions (frequently modeled/theorized with relevance to efficiency in PGG-like contexts):**

- `player_count` (group size): Extensively discussed; larger groups generally make cooperation and efficiency gains harder to sustain, unless punishment and other institutional mechanisms scale appropriately.
- `num_rounds`: Number of rounds is a key moderator; more rounds increase the likelihood that punishment can stabilize high efficiency.
- `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return): Core driver of the incentives for individual versus collective benefits; higher MPCR facilitates efficiency gains via punishment.
- `punishment_cost`: Widely discussed as a critical moderator; lower punishment cost increases the odds that punishment raises efficiency.
- `punishment_tech`/`punishment_magnitude`: Effectiveness of punishment; the size of penalty matters for deterrence and cost-benefit calculus.
- `all_or_nothing`: Sometimes addressed; discrete/binary contribution games may affect dynamics, but evidence is less direct.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Information structure is discussed as relevant to punishment targeting, coordination, and norm enforcement, directly or contextually.
- `chat`: Communication is flagged as an important moderator for coordination and can increase the efficiency benefit of punishment.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Reward mechanisms are increasingly modeled as alternatives or complements to punishment.

**Indirectly or Incompletely Informed Dimensions:**

- `default_contrib` (contribution framing): Rarely directly modeled, though framing and opt-in/opt-out effects are sometimes contextually discussed.
- Rich modeling of *contextual moderators* and *institutional features* not always perfectly mapped to PGG dimension labels (e.g., kinship, cultural context, external enforcement).

**Effectively Missing or Sparse:**

- No direct modeling or empirical analysis of `default_contrib` or detailed interface/framing effects.
- No systematic coverage of combinations/interactions across all 14 prediction dimensions; most analyses model smaller sets.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of direct empirical synthesis:** The literature set is nearly entirely theoretical or review-oriented, relying on simulation and cited (but not original) empirical work. It does not provide new meta-analytic, experimental, or quantitative data to directly parameterize prediction models.
- **Ambiguity on payoff outcomes:** Many papers focus on cooperation rate, norm compliance, or punishment behavior, with only a subset modeling or reporting on *efficiency* or *group payoff*—the outcome required for the prediction task.
- **Conflict across sources:** While core models predict robust efficiency gains from punishment, several influential reviews (Guala, 2012; Kurzban et al., 2015; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018) highlight that efficiency gains are conditional and sometimes negative in empirical lab data, especially under costly, uncoordinated, or norm-weak conditions.
- **Contingent moderation via design and context:** The predicted impact of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, group size, number of rounds, communication, and norms. Few studies offer quantitative calibration of these interactions for all the dimensions relevant to prediction.
- **Sparse modeling of interaction effects:** While many key dimensions are addressed, there are few models that fully cross all 14 prediction-relevant game design features, particularly for more subtle interface or information presentation variables (`default_contrib`, specific summary info).
- **Adjacency versus direct relevance:** A sizable subset of the literature, while informative about cooperation and punishment generally, applies to environments only loosely analogous to the prediction task (e.g., indirect reciprocity, evolutionary anthropology, or non-PGG social dilemmas).
