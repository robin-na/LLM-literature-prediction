# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists exclusively of **theoretical and simulation studies** (no empirical lab or field experiments), all published in biology, theoretical economics, and behavioral science venues. The set is **broad** in coverage of mechanisms, models, and variants of public goods games (PGGs), social dilemmas, and related collective action scenarios, but has **limited primary focus on the exact prediction task**: estimating the change in group efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation) when peer punishment is enabled, conditional on game design and control efficiency.

Most papers are **models**, either analytical or computational, covering:
- Exact or close variants of public goods games (including punishment and reward institutions, peer and institutional forms, and resource management analogs)
- Indirectly adjacent games (e.g., prisoner's dilemma, donation game, division of labour, partner choice, voluntary participation, reputation-based games)
- Mechanisms such as group selection, partner choice, indirect or reputation-based reciprocity, norm evolution, conformity, and extinction dynamics.

Outcomes measured are most frequently **cooperation rates, strategy frequencies, or norm establishment**. Direct reporting of group payoff, welfare, or efficiency is **less common but present** in multiple papers.

**Empirical data are missing from this set**; all findings are derived from theoretical reasoning or simulation.

**Overall**, the evidence base is theoretically rich and covers a diversity of mechanisms and structures relevant to the prediction task, but is **not directly empirical, and often does not prioritize efficiency as the main outcome**.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessing the literature on the three target-relevance dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant**:
  - **Exact relevance**: ~7/35 papers present models or analyses of canonical or slightly modified public goods games (`Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Powers et al., 2023; Odouard et al., 2023; Levy, 2022; Duong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Kristensen et al., 2025`).
  - **Close or adjacent relevance**: Most remaining papers use close variants, related collective action setups (e.g., thresholds, resource pool, partner choice, division of labour), or indirect reciprocity models. Standard dyadic games (prisoner's dilemma) are frequent, but only approximate PGG dynamics.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:
  - **Exact or close**: A substantial subset directly models explicit punishment or sanctioning (peer, institutional, centralized, or indirect forms); others analyze functionally similar mechanisms (partner choice, exclusion, selective extinction).
  - **Adjacent or weak**: Some only discuss punishment in review/conceptual context or analyze related mechanisms (e.g., ostracism, trust breaking, indirect reciprocity).

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
  - **Exact or close**: Fewer than half provide direct measures or predictions of efficiency, welfare, or group payoff. Several derive conditions for efficient equilibria or explicitly calculate group payoff as a function of design parameters (`Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Powers et al., 2023; Duong et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Cooney, 2025; Kristensen et al., 2025; Murase, 2025; Yuan et al., 2023; Kroumi, 2025; Peña et al., 2024`).
  - **Adjacent or weak**: Many report only behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates), sometimes discussing the implication for payoff/efficiency but not directly quantifying the payoff impact (`Odouard et al., 2023; Kido & Takezawa, 2024; Wen et al., 2025; Wang & Meng, 2023`).
  - **None**: Several provide only high-level theory or discuss norm/identity mechanisms (`Traulsen & Glynatsi, 2023; Bar-On & Lamm, 2023`).

**Summary**: There is a **moderate core** of papers highly relevant on all three dimensions, but the set overall is **weighted toward theory (not data), often emphasizing behavioral outcomes over direct efficiency outcomes, and is heterogeneous in modeling detail and transferability**.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff outcomes (directly relevant to efficiency)**:
  - **Group efficiency, total group payoff, welfare, surplus, total coins generated, or analogous measures** are primary outcomes in a significant minority of papers.
    - Examples: `Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Powers et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Cooney, 2025; Kurokawa, 2023; Murase, 2025; Kristensen et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2023; Kroumi, 2025; Fontanari & Santos, 2024; Peña et al., 2024`.
  - Some papers provide **explicit formulas** or phase diagrams for efficiency as a function of game parameters.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
  - **Cooperation rates, contribution rates, punishment/exclusion frequencies, norm compliance, frequencies of strategy types, stability of norms** dominate many papers.
    - Examples: `Odouard et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Kido & Takezawa, 2024; Wen et al., 2025; Wang & Meng, 2023; Graser et al., 2025; Lu & Wang, 2024; Morsky et al., 2024; Bruner & Smead, 2022; Pandula et al., 2024`.
  - Some discuss **oscillatory dynamics, evolutionary stability, or demographic fixation**, which are indirectly but not directly about efficiency.

- **Conceptual/qualitative/mechanistic outcomes**:
  - A subset discusses **norm evolution, motivational psychology, socio-ecological moderators, or identity formation** with no strict mapping to efficiency or payoff.

- **Explicit distinction**: Papers that report both **payoff and behavioral outcomes** frequently note that **increases in cooperation do not always translate to increases in efficiency**, particularly when punishment is costly or generates harmful externalities.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Punishment can increase efficiency, but this is highly contingent** on specific design parameters, initial conditions, and the institutional form of punishment:

- **Effectiveness of punishment**:
  - **Institutional punishment**: Strong support that well-implemented, cost-shared institutional punishment or sanctioning can stabilize high-efficiency equilibria in PGGs, but only when punishment is not too costly compared to fines, and when the group can coordinate sufficient punishment support (`Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Powers et al., 2023`).
  - **Peer/individual punishment**: More ambiguous; efficiency gains are possible but can be outweighed if punishment costs are high, punishment is misapplied, or retaliation occurs (`Cooney, 2025; Murase, 2025`; see also findings on cost/benefit ratios).

- **Cost to punisher vs. magnitude of punishment**:
  - Low punishment cost relative to magnitude of the fine enables efficiency gains; as cost rises, net efficiency gains diminish or may even reverse—punishment can then suppress cooperation or simply waste group resources (`Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Wang et al., 2024; Cooney, 2025`).

- **Group size and structure**:
  - Larger groups complicate punishment economics, often making threshold effects (critical mass of punishers needed) more severe; institutional mechanisms can offset coordination problems (`Powers et al., 2023; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025`).
  - In very large or spatially structured groups, efficiency gains depend on the evolution of hierarchy or localized sanctioning (`Powers et al., 2023; Yaman et al., 2023`).

- **Type and implementation of punishment**:
  - **Indirect or targeted sanctions** (exclusion, partner choice, selective extinction) can promote cooperation and sometimes efficiency, without the direct costliness of punishment (`Li et al., 2024; Graser et al., 2025; Kroumi, 2025`).
  - **Antisocial punishment/exclusion**, or punishment with poor targeting, undermines cooperation/efficiency (`Li et al., 2024; Kurokawa, 2023`).
  - **Norm internalization or conformity** can amplify the effect of punishment on cooperation, but the mapping to efficiency is indirect and only robust under certain cost structures (`Odouard et al., 2023; Kido & Takezawa, 2024`).

- **Critical moderators**:
  - **Detectability of defection**: When defection is hard to observe, costly punishment norms can be the most efficient solution; when defection is easy to monitor, punishment can actually reduce efficiency (`Murase, 2025`).
  - **Error rates, mutation, and cognitive sophistication**: Sophisticated reasoning or error-prone environments can backfire, undermining the efficiency benefits of punishment/reputation mechanisms (`Morsky et al., 2024`).
  - **Resource dynamics**: In public resource management, efficiency gains from punishment/reward depend on resource renewal rates; if growth is too low, even maximum cooperation cannot prevent inefficiency (`Wang et al., 2024`).

- **Bistability and initial conditions**: Several models exhibit **multiple stable states** (high or low efficiency), with the realized outcome depending on history, initial frequencies of punishers, or baseline efficiency (`Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Wang et al., 2024`).

- **Payoff/non-payoff distinctions**:
  - A recurring theme: **increases in cooperation rates do not always yield increases in efficiency** if the punishment is too costly or widely used. Some models find that *maximal cooperation can coincide with low efficiency* due to high sanctioning costs (`Cooney, 2025`).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For the task of predicting treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency given game design and control efficiency:**

- **If control efficiency is low (all-defectors)**, enabling punishment can—under the right cost/benefit and coordination conditions—**shift the system to high efficiency**. (But this requires favorably low punishment costs, sufficient group coordination, and often some mechanism for cost sharing: `Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Powers et al., 2023`). In many models, unless a threshold proportion of punishers/participants is achieved, efficiency remains low even with punishment enabled.

- **If control efficiency is already moderate/high**, enabling punishment may **increase, have little effect, or even decrease efficiency**, depending on whether punishment is used excessively or targeted well (`Cooney, 2025; Murase, 2025; Kurokawa, 2023`). In some models, misapplied or excessive punishment wastes resources.

- **Key design parameters to consider for prediction**:
  - **Player count**: Larger groups increase coordination challenges for effective punishment; institutional punishment is more effective in large groups with optimized hierarchy (`Powers et al., 2023`).
  - **Punishment cost/magnitude**: Lower costs and higher impact increase the likelihood of efficiency gains; high costs erode efficiency (`Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Wang et al., 2024`).
  - **Type of punishment**: Institutional/centralized with cost sharing outperforms peer punishment in scaling to high efficiency (`Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Yaman et al., 2023`).
  - **Resource/game structure**: When resources are renewable, the baseline growth rate can set hard limits on achievable efficiency even with perfect cooperation (`Wang et al., 2024`).
  - **Detection/communication**: When defection is hard to detect, punishment is more likely to yield efficiency gains (`Murase, 2025`); the presence of communication is not directly modeled in most papers, so effect is unclear.

- **Control efficiency baseline**: Several models emphasize that the relationship between control and treatment efficiency is *not monotonic*, and that **punishment only increases efficiency above control in favorable cost, implementation, and group structure regimes**.

- **Ambiguity/disagreement**: Some models find that peer punishment can actually **reduce efficiency** in large groups or with costly punishment, while others show dramatic efficiency gains only under very specific institutional conditions.

- **Contributions from behavioral outcomes**: Many papers measuring only cooperation rates find robust increases from punishment, but **explicitly caution that efficiency gains depend on the cost structure**, so predictions must not over-interpret increases in cooperation as guaranteed efficiency improvements.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions** (frequent, mechanistically central):
- `player_count` (group size): Frequently modeled and shown as important in threshold effects, scaling of punishment, institution formation, and evolutionary dynamics.
- `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return): Modeled as benefit/cost ratio or similar; crucial for stability of cooperation and emergence of efficient states.
- `punishment_cost/punishment_tech/punishment_magnitude`: Core to mechanism—extensively modeled as key moderators of efficiency impact.
- `all_or_nothing`: Binary vs. continuous contribution often modeled and shown to interact with punishment effectiveness.
- `num_rounds`: When repeated games are modeled, number and structure of rounds can moderate impact of punishment, but is not always foregrounded.
- `reward_exists`: Some models include co-present reward mechanisms and show interaction effects (`Wang et al., 2024`).

**Indirectly/contextually discussed** (less central, sometimes considered):
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Occasionally included in models of information visibility, reputation, or norm salience, but effects on efficiency are rarely quantified.
- `default_contrib`: Framing of default action is rarely modeled directly.
- `chat`: Social communication/interaction is often omitted; only a few models make assumptions about information sharing.
- `reward_cost/reward_tech/reward_magnitude`: Only directly addressed in models of hybrid/institutional incentive schemes.
  
**Sparse or effectively missing**:
- The **interaction and combined effects** of all 14 design dimensions are not explored in any one model; most models vary only a subset.
- Empirical validation of dimensional effects is absent.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **No empirical data**: The set provides **no direct quantitative estimates from real experiments or field data**. All results are theoretical or simulation-based, sometimes highly idealized.
- **Efficiency not universally reported**: Many models measure only cooperation rates or other behavioral outcomes, which may not translate directly to efficiency. Several models demonstrate that efficiency and cooperation can diverge when punishment is costly.
- **Parameter dependencies and non-monotonic effects**: Prediction is complicated by **nonlinear, threshold, or bistable dynamics**—small parameter changes can lead to large efficiency shifts, and initial conditions/history often matter (`Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Wang et al., 2024`).
- **Limited coverage of some design dimensions**: Key variables such as communication (`chat`), reward framing, or information structure are not comprehensively modeled or linked to efficiency outcomes.
- **Peer vs. institutional punishment**: Many papers that provide clear efficiency predictions focus on institutional rather than peer punishment; the direct analog for peer punishment may be less certain.
- **No quantification of interaction effects**: There is **no model in the set that quantifies how all design dimensions simultaneously moderate the efficiency gain from punishment**.
- **Ambiguity and disagreement**: Some models suggest that punishment will often not be efficiency-enhancing in large groups or unless carefully designed; others suggest possible large gains. **Mapping from control to punishment-enabled efficiency is highly context-dependent**.
- **Limited external validity**: Theoretical and agent-based results may not transfer cleanly to empirical or real-world settings without adjustment.

---

**In summary**, this literature set provides rich theoretical and mechanistic insight into how punishment and game design can moderate efficiency outcomes in public-goods-game-like environments. However, for precise prediction of treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency, the available evidence is incomplete and subject to important limitations. Predictions should be made cautiously, with explicit acknowledgment of parameter dependencies, the possibility of non-monotonic responses, and the non-equivalence of behavioral and payoff-based outcomes.
