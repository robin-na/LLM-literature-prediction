# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set includes 19 sources, primarily empirical laboratory experiments (n ≈ 10), several theory/modeling papers, and a smaller number of observational or conceptual/theoretical works. Overall, the set covers a relatively broad swath of collective action research, but direct, high-relevance empirical findings for efficiency effects of punishment in standard public goods games (PGGs) or close variants are concentrated in a subset of the papers. The source mix thus combines narrow, directly predictive evidence with a broader, largely adjacent or contextual literature. Most empirical papers focus on lab-based game experiments, typically manipulating standard PGG dimensions, with some cross-study overlap in design parameters (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost). Theoretical pieces offer general mechanisms and formal insights, including equilibrium predictions and comparative static analyses for punishment systems, while adjacent and conceptual literature contextualizes the role of punishment, reward, and reputation in human cooperation and its institutional underpinnings.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - **exact**: Several papers feature standard PGGs or very close analogs with direct manipulations of punishment (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Espín et al., 2022; Adams et al., 2022), or model them theoretically (Prétôt et al., 2024).
  - **close**: Some papers use common-pool resource (CPR) games, volunteer dilemmas, and similar repeated collective-action games, which share critical structural features with PGGs (van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Esmaeili et al., 2022; Molenmaker et al., 2022).
  - **adjacent/weak/none**: The remainder focus on related but distinct social dilemmas (e.g., ultimatum games, centipede games, effort allocation tasks, or field correlational studies) or are conceptual/theoretical without direct linkage to experimental PGGs.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - **exact**: Numerous studies manipulate the presence, cost, or structure of peer (and sometimes institutional) punishment (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Prétôt et al., 2024). Several theory papers model or discuss punishment as the central mechanism for norm enforcement.
  - **adjacent/none**: Some studies address related incentives (rewards, reputation, emotion-based responses) or punishment-like behavior in adjacent games, but not explicit sanctioning stages in PGGs.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - **exact/close**: Only a minority report true group efficiency or normalized group payoff as primary outcomes (Sparks et al., 2024; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Nunney et al., 2022 for centipede game).
  - **adjacent**: Many report contribution or cooperation rates, punishment assigned, or non-payoff behavioral responses (e.g., Adams et al., 2022; Esmaeili et al., 2022; Molenmaker et al., 2022).
  - **none**: A substantial number discuss norm compliance, social capital, leader attitudes, or punitive attitudes, but do not measure efficiency, payoff, or surplus.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (direct)**:  
  - Group earnings/welfare/surplus as a share of potential maximum (efficiency), explicitly reported in a subset of lab experiments with enabled punishment phases (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).  
  - Explicitly theoretical predictions about equilibrium efficiency under different punishment regimes (Prétôt et al., 2024).

- **Payoff-related outcomes (close/indirect)**:  
  - Total profit/resource size (CPR games), group ticket wins (centipede games).
  - Comparative or relative group payoffs in non-standard games (Nunney et al., 2022), or group surplus (Büyükboyaci et al., 2025) not always mapped to efficiency.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:  
  - Contribution or cooperation rates, frequency and severity of punishment, norm compliance, responses to chat or emotional signals, and punishment targeting patterns (e.g., Espín et al., 2022; Adams et al., 2022; Kido & Takezawa, 2024; Gummerum et al., 2022).
  - Willingness to cooperate, social capital, or perceived economic benefits (Skreli et al., 2024).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Empirical evidence (payoff/efficiency focus):**
  - Enabling costly peer punishment in standard repeated PGGs increases contribution rates and ultimately raises group efficiency compared to punishment-disabled controls, though there may be an initial reduction in efficiency due to punishment costs (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
  - The efficiency benefit of punishment is more robust and sustained over longer time horizons (many rounds), and in larger/densely connected groups. The negative early effect (costly punishment reduces earnings before cooperation is stabilized) tends to be offset in later rounds (Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
  - Costless forms of norm enforcement (e.g., disapproval points without economic impact) provide only a temporary or negligible efficiency gain (Sparks et al., 2024).
  - The structure of the punishment regime matters: strict immediate penalties may yield higher short-run efficiency, but graduated or endogenous (voted) sanctioning mechanisms improve or sustain longer-term efficiency, with differences narrowing over time (van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).
  - Theory papers provide explicit parameter boundaries: Peer punishment alone may be insufficient for efficiency unless the group overcomes second-order free-rider problems; institutionally funded collective punishment, if sufficiently strong and well-funded relative to cost, is predicted to yield high efficiency (Prétôt et al., 2024).
  - Contextual modifiers (e.g., chat/communication, emotional signals) can interplay with punishment and affect outcomes—though few studies jointly manipulate both (Adams et al., 2022; Nunney et al., 2022).

- **Empirical evidence (behavioral focus, non-payoff):**
  - Punishment propensities, targets, and frequency are highly sensitive to culture, gender, group heterogeneity, and emotional state (Espín et al., 2022; Gummerum et al., 2022).
  - Enabling chat or communication generally increases cooperative behavior, especially if the quality of discussion supports consensus (Adams et al., 2022), but does not guarantee higher efficiency if punishment is not present.
  - Rewards often have different dynamics: sometimes increasing cooperation, but not always increasing efficiency per se (Esmaeili et al., 2022; Molenmaker et al., 2022).

- **Mechanism/theoretical arguments:**
  - The effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency is contingent on parameters such as group size, MPCR, punishment cost/magnitude, and the institutional enforcement context.
  - There is potential for neutral or negative efficiency effects if punishment is poorly targeted (antisocial punishment), if costs outweigh cooperation gains, or in early rounds before cooperation is established.

- **Ambiguity/disagreement:**
  - Some adjacent studies (Büyükboyaci et al., 2025) show that punishment-like mechanisms may not always translate into higher efficiency, particularly if structure (direct, costly, targeted sanctioning) is missing or if punishment does not reliably change contribution behavior.
  - Conceptual and observational studies caution that group dynamics, emergent norms, or lack of collective support for punishment may undermine positive efficiency effects (Wittek, 2022; Takács, 2022).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct implications:**  
  - When group efficiency in the control (no-punishment) game is low-to-moderate, enabling costly peer punishment is empirically shown to increase, and often sustain, higher efficiency by raising and maintaining higher contribution rates—especially over many rounds and/or in larger, more connected groups, as long as the cost-benefit ratio of punishment is not excessive (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).
  - The efficiency gains may be delayed, with initial efficiency suppressed by punishment costs, but these are compensated as cooperation stabilizes.
  - The benefit of punishment is a function of game design dimensions: group size, MPCR, punishment cost/magnitude, number of rounds, network structure, and presence/quality of communication.
  - Theory (Prétôt et al., 2024) provides formulas for threshold investment in collective punishment needed for high-efficiency equilibria, cautioning that peer punishment alone may not be sufficient if second-order free-riding is rampant; institutional punishment is more reliable when well-designed.
  - Enabling costless or weak “symbolic” punishment does not suffice; only punishment with real economic consequences reliably sustains efficiency (Sparks et al., 2024).

- **Caveats:**  
  - In some parameter regimes, or if punishment is poorly structured (e.g., non-costly, difficult to target, or allows antisocial punishment), it may not increase efficiency and could reduce it due to excessive sanctioning or group fragmentation.
  - The positive efficiency effect is less clear or shallower in very short games, small groups, or if network or institutional conditions do not permit effective norm enforcement (van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).

- **Use of control efficiency:**  
  - If the design is similar to those extensively studied (e.g., 4-6 players, repeated rounds, moderate MPCR), and control efficiency is known, the predicted efficiency under punishment is typically higher, with the magnitude moderated by the above dimensions.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- `player_count`: Consistently manipulated and discussed (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Prétôt et al., 2024).
- `num_rounds`: Nearly all repeated PGG experiments specify and test rounds.
- `mpcr`: Explicitly specified and used in empirical and theoretical models.
- `punishment_cost`: Directly manipulated and crucial in both experiment and theory.
- `punishment_tech`: Specific structure (strict vs. graduated, peer vs. institutional) is tested in some experiments and specified in theory.

**Indirectly/Contextually Discussed:**  
- `chat`: Several studies include or exclude chat and analyze its effect on contributions (Adams et al., 2022).
- `all_or_nothing`: Variably implemented, but less often the focus of analysis.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Occasionally manipulated, usually in combination with punishment for modeling alternatives or exploring combined incentive regimes (Esmaeili et al., 2022).
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes reported in design descriptions, rarely a focus except when manipulating information structure or transparency.

**Effectively Missing:**  
- `default_contrib`: Contribution framing is rarely the primary experimental manipulation or directly linked to efficiency predictions.
- `show_punishment_id`: Only rarely used or directly analyzed.
- For some dimensions (e.g., `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `show_punishment_id`), content is sparse or only tangentially mentioned.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited Coverage of All Design Dimensions:**  
  - Only a subset of the 14 prediction dimensions is directly tested in relation to efficiency (notably, group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech).
  - Some dimensions (e.g., default contribution framing, punishment/reward identity display) are effectively missing or only described contextually.

- **Payoff vs. Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  - A significant portion of the literature operationalizes outcomes as cooperation/contribution rates or behavioral measures, making inference to efficiency indirect. Only a minority of studies report group efficiency as a primary outcome.

- **Generality of Evidence:**  
  - Most of the strong empirical evidence derives from repeated PGGs with relatively small, homogeneous lab groups (4-6 players). Generalization to substantially larger groups, field settings, or different institutional structures is limited.
  - Adjacent evidence (e.g., CPR games, centipede games, social capital surveys) provides indirect or at best close analogies. Care is needed when extrapolating quantitatively.

- **Ambiguity and Mechanism Complexity:**  
  - Several theory and conceptual papers highlight conditions where punishment may fail or even reduce efficiency, due to second-order free-rider problems, reputation traps, group dynamics, or the potential for antisocial punishment.
  - Quantitative predictions are possible where game structures closely match those experimentally tested or formally modeled; elsewhere, ambiguity and context sensitivity persist.

- **Empirical Gaps:**  
  - Direct comparative studies of all-or-nothing vs. continuous contribution games, or manipulations of punishment identity/reward/summary displays, are rare.
  - Little empirical data addresses the interaction between multiple dimensions (e.g., chat + punishment + group size) for efficiency in a factorial way.

**In summary:**  
This literature set offers direct, high-quality empirical evidence and precise theoretical models for the efficiency impact of peer punishment in repeated PGGs and close variants—conditional on a subset of game design dimensions (group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/structure). Other design dimensions are sparsely covered, and much evidence is behavioral or adjacent rather than payoff-focused. The literature supports an expectation of increased efficiency from enabling costly punishment in repeated, moderately sized PGGs when control efficiency is moderate or low. However, benefits depend on dimension-specific details and may not extend to poorly structured or weakly incentivized punishment regimes.
