# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of 22 sources, spanning both **theoretical** and **empirical studies**, with several designating their work as lab experiments, observational studies, or formal theory. The set is **moderately broad** in its coverage of collective action problems and public goods games (PGGs), but **direct, high-relevance empirical studies focusing on efficiency or related payoff outcomes in PGGs with and without punishment are limited**. Several theoretical papers model efficiency effects under parametric manipulation of punishment, while most empirical work either examines only behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation rates, punishment assignment, norm adherence) or studies adjacent problems (e.g., CPR experiments, real-world commons management, or field contexts with different incentive structures).

**Summary by type:**
- **Theory/modeling:** Several works give parametric predictions for efficiency as a function of design dimensions, especially in idealized evolutionary or replicator-dynamics settings.
- **Lab experiments:** Some exact or close-PGG studies examine punishment's effect on surplus/efficiency, but often provide only indirect or descriptive insight into efficiency outcomes.
- **Field/observational/adjacent:** Many papers contextually discuss PGG-like environments, sanctions, and norm enforcement but focus on broader, less controlled, or non-payoff outcomes.
  
# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**
- **Exact:** A minority of papers (e.g., Zhang & Cao, 2020; Engel, 2019; Zhu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2019) employ exact PGG or canonical variants in their analyses or experiments.
- **Close:** Several papers use closely related environments (e.g., step-level PGGs, CPR games, threshold games).
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Many focus on real-world or conceptual analogues (commons dilemmas, field interventions), often with less precise mapping to PGG parameters.

**Punishment or Sanctions:**
- **Exact/Close:** Key theory and experimental papers directly manipulate punishment conditions (peer or centralized) (e.g., Zhang & Cao, 2020; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).
- **Adjacent:** Some consider exclusion, compensation, or informal/institutional sanctions conceptually linked to punishment.
- **Weak/None:** Others operate with no explicit punishment, or focus solely on non-punitive cooperation mechanisms (e.g., role models, team identity).

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**
- **Exact:** Only a few papers model or experimentally report direct efficiency/group payoff outcomes as a function of punishment (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Liu et al., 2019; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Baker & Choi, 2018).
- **Adjacent/Close:** Some papers include descriptive or partial analysis of group earnings or welfare.
- **Mostly Non-Payoff:** Most papers focus on behavioral indicators (contribution, cooperation, norm compliance), with efficiency, surplus, or welfare either not measured or only contextually referenced.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes:**
- **Direct efficiency or group payoff:** Modeled or measured in a few key theoretical (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Liu et al., 2019) and some empirical (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021) works.
- **Related measures:** Occasionally, total earnings, welfare, or surplus are reported (Baker & Choi, 2018).
- **Mostly descriptive:** Some experiments report but do not analyze group earnings; others mention them in passing.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- **Cooperation/contribution rate:** Most commonly measured; adoption of cooperation or compliance as a primary dependent variable (e.g., Engel, 2019; Zhu et al., 2020; Steimanis et al., 2020).
- **Punishment behavior:** Frequency, type, target, and cost of sanctioning/punishing.
- **Norm enforcement, retaliation, compliance:** Discussed in several observational or theoretical papers.
- **Norm cascades, redundancy, information effects:** Addressed in field or sociological models, rarely connected to payoff.

**Distinction maintained:** Payoff outcomes are sparse and disproportionately present in theoretical work; behavioral outcomes dominate empirical/field literature.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Theoretical results indicate that punishment can drive the system from defection or cycling to full cooperation, thus maximizing efficiency, *if* the punishment 'fine' (cost imposed on defectors) is high enough relative to the MPCR and group size, and if mechanisms like 'insurance' or avoidance are absent or costly (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Liu et al., 2019).**
    - If punishment is weak, costly, or ineffective, or can be circumvented, efficiency gains may be small or null.
    - In some cases, if the cost of punishment is high and use is uncoordinated, net efficiency can be *lower* with punishment than without, due to waste from sanctioning (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021, equal endowment).

- **Empirical studies show *mixed* effects of punishment on efficiency (group payoff), depending on moderators:**
    - With equal endowments, peer punishment can reduce efficiency due to over-punishment and coordination failure; with salient inequality, punishment is more selectively applied, which can *increase* efficiency for some subgroups and, at minimum, not reduce it for the group (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).
    - Transparency or information structures can *backfire*, lowering cooperation and thus expected efficiency if individual punitive actions are made too salient (Engel, 2019). This suggests punishment's benefit for efficiency depends on feedback and information dimensions (e.g., show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id).

- **Punishment mechanism matters:** Peer punishment tends to promote higher cooperation than pool punishment in spatial PGG models (Zhu et al., 2020), but this is inferred from behavioral (not payoff) outcomes; in models of exclusion, robust efficiency effects may be stronger for exclusion than for punishment (Liu et al., 2019).

- **Punishment's net effect depends significantly on:**
    - **Punishment cost and fine-to-cost ratio.**
    - **Group structure (size, homogeneity/heterogeneity, endowment distribution).**
    - **Visibility of actions, information feedback, and normative context.**
    - **Availability of alternatives (e.g., compensation, insurance, role models, team identity).**
    - **Game repetition/number of rounds is modeled but not systematically isolated as a moderator.**

- **Mechanism arguments** (Baker & Choi, 2018; Jagers et al., 2020) posit that formal, accurate, and visible punishment mechanisms increase efficiency more than informal or reputational ones, but only under precise conditions (low error, manageable cost, public outcomes).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct prediction of treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency can be informed *only within a bounded subset* of settings**—most clearly, where:
    - The game is a standard (linear) PGG.
    - Punishment is enabled as peer or pool punishment, with known cost and effectiveness.
    - Control (no punishment) efficiency is moderate to low, and design dimensions regarding group size, punishment intensity, MPCR, and information feedback are matched to those modeled in exact-theory papers.

- **If punishment is sufficiently severe and not overly costly (relative to the social multiplier), efficiency is likely to increase sharply with punishment enabled** (Zhang & Cao, 2020; Liu et al., 2019).
    - Efficient deterrence requires the expected fine to surpass the incentive to defect, especially in small to moderate groups.
    - In large groups, or when punishment is weak, deterrence may fail, and efficiency returns to or remains at control levels.

- **Efficiency gains from punishment are attenuated or reversed if:**
    - Punishment is highly costly or poorly targeted, leading to waste (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).
    - Transparency is too high, leading to norm confusion or retaliation (Engel, 2019).
    - Feedback mechanisms, group structure, or extrinsic institutional conditions undermine norm coordination.

- **Indirect moderators:** Endowment heterogeneity and social norms can modulate efficiency gains, with punishment being more efficient in settings where heterogeneity creates salient coordination points for selective sanctioning.

- **Practical limitations for out-of-sample prediction:**
    - In absence of direct efficiency/outcome data, control efficiency plus well-calibrated design parameters (punishment cost, fine, group size, MPCR, feedback) are necessary but not sufficient.
    - Behavioral increases in cooperation under punishment do not always translate linearly into efficiency gains due to the endogenous cost of sanctioning.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**
- **player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Modeled in multiple theory papers; sometimes experimentally manipulated.
- **show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Examined empirically in studies on feedback/transparency (Engel, 2019; Qirko, 2020).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Occasionally addressed, but less systematically.
- **punishment_exists:** Central to prediction.

**Indirect/contextual coverage:**
- **chat:** Sometimes described or manipulated (e.g., team identity interventions), but not in direct comparison to payoff with or without punishment.
- **default_contrib:** Rarely, if ever, systematically varied or isolated in relation to payoff outcomes.
- **show_n_rounds:** Included in design, seldom treated as moderator of punishment's effect on efficiency.

**Mostly missing or underspecified:**
- **Reward dimensions** beyond mere existence are generally under-explored in relation to efficiency under punishment.
- **Interaction effects** (e.g., chat × punishment, default_contrib × punishment) are rarely (if ever) empirically isolated for payoff outcomes.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical evidence directly linking punishment to efficiency (not just contribution or cooperation rates) in experimental PGGs is limited.** Most measured outcomes are behavioral rather than payoff-based, and efficiency effects are often inferred rather than demonstrated.
- **Theoretical models are often set in infinite or well-mixed populations and may not generalize to finite, human-subject experiments or real-world applications.**
- **Adjacency and heterogeneity of experimental settings** (CPRs, field interventions, real-world commons, hybrid sanctioning mechanisms) limit the direct applicability of most findings to the prediction task.
- **Results frequently depend on unobserved contextual moderators** (e.g., endowment inequality, group identity, trust, prior norms) seldom coded in standard design dimensions.
- **Punishment effectiveness is highly sensitive to cost/benefit calibration and implementation details** (who can punish, targeting, anti-social punishment, feedback, and visibility).
- **A strong dissociation exists between behavioral indicators (cooperation, norm compliance) and net efficiency**; increased punishment can, paradoxically, reduce group surplus when overused or misapplied.
- **Outcome variable distinctions are often blurred or unreported; studies frequently report only descriptive statistics or qualitative findings on efficiency.**

---
**In summary:**  
The literature set provides important theoretical and some empirical context for predicting how enabling peer punishment affects efficiency in PGG-like environments, but **strong, parametric, cross-design evidence for payoff-based outcomes is sparse**. Predictions must integrate game design dimensions carefully with prior control efficiency, but remain highly conditional on punishment's perceived effectiveness, cost, institutional context, and group structure. The evidence is most applicable for canonical linear PGGs (small groups, enforceable punishment); generalization to varied or realistic settings is less certain and requires caution.
