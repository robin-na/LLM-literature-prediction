# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large (34 papers) and predominantly empirical, with most employing laboratory or field experiments; a few are observational or theoretical. The breadth of coverage is wide in terms of games (public goods games [PGGs], common-pool resource [CPR] games, networked games, market games, and others), cultures, and mechanisms (punishment, reward, communication, coalition formation, allocation power, etc.). However, the literature is more scattered with respect to the *prediction task*—many papers do not directly assess how enabling punishment affects group efficiency in PGGs versus a control.

Approximately a third of the papers provide *exact* or *close* empirical evidence on the effects of punishment on efficiency in PGGs or PGG-like environments (e.g., Chen, 2022; Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Karakostas et al., 2023). Others provide adjacent evidence through related games (e.g., iterated prisoner’s dilemmas, market games), theoretical mechanisms, or only measure behavioral—not payoff—outcomes. A sizeable portion focus only on baseline (no-punishment) games or on design dimensions (communication, coalition, framing) rather than on punishment effects.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *Exact*: Many papers use the standard repeated PGG (e.g., Chen, 2022; Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).  
- *Close*: Some use CPR games or networked variants that structurally resemble PGGs but with resource depletion/restoration (van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).
- *Adjacent/Weak*: Others use distinctly different social dilemmas (e.g., iterated PD, dictator games, market games).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *Exact*: Several analyze peer punishment or sanction mechanisms directly in PGGs (Chen, 2022; Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).
- *Close/Adjacent*: Related work examines institutional/automatic penalties, coalition allocation, carbon taxes, or emotion-driven punitive acts—often not peer-to-peer or not voluntary.
- *None*: Many papers focus on other interventions (e.g., communication, coalition) or do not manipulate punishment at all.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *Exact*: Some papers report *group efficiency*, *group profit*, or *total payoff* (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Karakostas et al., 2023).
- *Close*: Proxies such as investment levels, or welfare, that are strongly correlated but not always calculated as efficiency ratios.
- *Adjacent/None*: An important fraction measure only behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates, norm compliance, punishment frequency) and lack payoff reporting.

In summary, while the literature gives strong direct coverage for experimental PGGs with and without peer punishment, **some key variants and many design/dimension combinations—especially beyond player count, rounds, MPCR, and punishment cost—receive little or only indirect coverage.** Direct evidence on efficiency is present but with important contextual, technological, and institutional gaps.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes** (*efficiency, group payoff, welfare, surplus, total coins*):  
- *Directly measured* in several key PGG and CPR experiment papers (Chen, 2022; Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Karakostas et al., 2023).
- *Indirectly measured* through proxies (collective investment, resource stocks, total profit in market/corporate games).

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (*contribution rate, cooperation rate, punishment frequency, norm compliance*):  
- Widely reported, especially in studies focusing on mechanisms, social heuristics, cultural or personality moderators, or the psychological underpinnings of punitive acts.
- Even in some studies with experimental manipulation of punishment, efficiency or group payoff is *not* directly reported (e.g., Kingsley & Smith-Walter, 2024; Espín et al., 2022).

**Distinction maintained:**  
- While increasing cooperation/contribution *can* lead to higher efficiency, this is not always the case—punishment costs can destroy resources and lower total payoffs (Chen, 2022), or increased contributions can overshoot the efficiency optimum (Karakostas et al., 2023).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Punishment’s effect on efficiency:**  
- **Positive or more positive effects:**  
    - When punishment is peer-enabled, tangible, and costly, it tends to maintain or increase group efficiency in standard, homogeneous, repeated PGGs, *especially over longer time horizons* (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023). The efficiency gains materialize after an initial period wherein punishment is costly, as it discourages free riding and raises contributions that persist (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
    - In networked and larger groups, especially with dense connections, the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is stronger (Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
    - In adjacent settings, when punishment is *net-rewarding* (i.e., the act of punishment pays off for the group as a whole), it also boosts efficiency (Dasgupta & Musolesi, 2025).

- **Null, negative, or mixed findings:**  
    - In *heterogeneous* PGGs (different returns across members), enabling punishment does **not** increase group efficiency and can reduce average group payoffs due to the cost of punishment and the emergence of antisocial punishment (Chen, 2022).
    - In some field contexts and common-pool resource games, punishment interventions can reduce efficiency if they crowd out intrinsic motivation (Amirova et al., 2022), or when the expected benefit is low or coordination is not achieved.
    - In alternative mechanism games (e.g., allocation power or coalition stages), sanction-like mechanisms are only efficiency-improving under certain technologies (Karakostas et al., 2023). Sometimes they can even reduce efficiency by causing over-contribution.
    - When uncertainty or noise in monitoring is high, punishment mechanisms relying on reputation or accurate detection are less effective, and efficiency gains are diminished or absent (Gallo et al., 2022).
    - Some mechanisms serve as substitutes for peer punishment (coalition formation, communication, endogenous allocation), showing large efficiency gains *without costly punishment* (Falvey et al., 2025; Ball et al., 2025).

- **Reward versus punishment:**
    - When compared directly, *rewards* for contribution tend to outperform punishment in increasing group efficiency (Chen, 2022; Falvey et al., 2025).

**Moderators:**
- **Group size and network structure**: Positive punishment effects on efficiency are most pronounced in large/dense groups (Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
- **Game heterogeneity**: High-return versus low-return players may use punishment differently, undermining potential efficiency gains (Chen, 2022).
- **Length of game**: Long repeated games allow efficiency gains from punishment to accumulate and outweigh early costs.
- **Punishment cost and technology**: If costly enough, or structured as graduated (rather than strict), punishment can yield different efficiency dynamics (van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).
- **Monitoring and information**: Efficacy of punishment is undermined by noisy reputation or low transparency (Gallo et al., 2022).
- **Communication**: Can function as a substitute for punishment, or amplify/attenuate efficiency (Adams et al., 2022).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**When the control game efficiency is known, predictions for efficiency with punishment enabled, holding other design dimensions constant, should follow these synthesized rules:**

- **If the game is a standard, homogeneous, repeated PGG with peer-administered, tangible, and costly punishment:**
    - Expect efficiency to increase *relative to the control*, especially over longer time scales, larger groups, or denser social structures (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
    - Early rounds may show reduced efficiency due to punishment costs, but this is typically outweighed over time.

- **If the game has significant payoff heterogeneity among players:**
    - Do **not** expect efficiency gains from enabling punishment; efficiency can even decrease due to antisocial punishment and misaligned incentives (Chen, 2022).

- **If reward (not punishment) is available:**
    - Expect even greater gains in efficiency compared to punishment (Chen, 2022; Falvey et al., 2025).

- **If the game structure is a PGG-like variant (e.g., best-shot, weakest-link, CPR, or with market/competitive features):**
    - The efficiency effect of punishment is unpredictable and context-dependent. In some cases, sanction mechanisms can lower efficiency (Karakostas et al., 2023), while in others they are positive but only over time or when endogenously chosen (van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).

- **If monitoring or reputation is noisy:**
    - The positive effect of punishment on efficiency is substantially hampered; group welfare may remain low even if punitive acts increase (Gallo et al., 2022).

- **If the control game already has high efficiency (due to other mechanisms: coalition, communication, endogenous allocation):**
    - Further efficiency gains from adding punishment are likely to be small, and in some cases may even be negative if punishment leads to resource destruction or crowding out of intrinsic motivation.

*Explicitly, predictions from this literature are most reliable when the design matches the above-studied cases—predictions should be cautious with high heterogeneity, complex technologies, noisy monitoring, or minimal opportunity for repeated interaction.*

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed design dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Group size is manipulated and analyzed as a moderator of punishment’s effect on efficiency (Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Chen, 2022; Sparks et al., 2024).
- `num_rounds`: Longer repeated games amplify the positive effect of punishment on efficiency (Sparks et al., 2024); short or one-shot games may show no effect or negative efficiency.
- `mpcr`: Both level and heterogeneity of MPCR are critical; high heterogeneity undermines efficiency gains from punishment (Chen, 2022).
- `punishment_cost`: Directly manipulated, with nuanced findings—lower punishment cost does *not* always mean higher efficiency if it enables antisocial punishment (Chen, 2022).
- `punishment_tech`: Some variation (implementation of graduated vs. strict punishment, types of punishment), but detailed cross-paper comparisons are limited.

**Indirectly or contextually discussed:**
- `chat`: Communication is found to be an effective standalone intervention, sometimes substituting for punishment (Adams et al., 2022; Amirova et al., 2022).
- `all_or_nothing`: Most studies use continuous contribution, but some use all-or-nothing, affecting baseline efficiency and possible punishment dynamics (Ball et al., 2025).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Occasionally discussed—reward can strongly increase efficiency (Chen, 2022), but less often directly compared within the same experimental design.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Less frequently the subject of systematic variation, but transparency and monitoring are contextual factors.

**Effectively missing or sparse:**
- `default_contrib`: Framing of contributions is rarely highlighted.
- `show_punishment_id`: Hardly any evidence on whether punishers’ identities (anonymity/transparency) systematically affect efficiency.
- Other interaction effects among the lesser-studied dimensions (e.g., interaction of chat and punishment, framing effects) are sporadically addressed but not systematically.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Coverage gaps in design dimensions**: Several prediction dimensions (such as punishment/reward *technology* details, contributor framing, or the visibility of punishers/rewarders’ identities) are rarely manipulated or reported, making predictions less reliable when these are central.
- **Payoff vs. behavioral measures**: A significant portion of studies, even when experimentally manipulating punishment, report only impacts on behavior rather than on group efficiency or total payoff—which can differ in direction and magnitude.
- **Limited transferability to real-world, diverse, or noisy environments**: Effect sizes from standard lab PGGs with homogeneous players often fail to generalize to field settings, to games with payoff heterogeneity, or to contexts with noisy monitoring and complex social dynamics.
- **Ambiguity in mechanisms**: For heterogenous groups or alternative production technologies, the effect of punishment is ambiguous or even negative; design-feature interactions are insufficiently mapped.
- **Scarcity of systematic studies for critical interaction effects**: Very limited joint manipulation (e.g., high-cost vs. low-cost punishment in high vs. low MPCR, cross-level effects of communication and punishment).
- **Baseline efficiency dependence**: When the control game already has high efficiency due to other mechanisms (communication, coalition formation), the incremental effect of adding punishment is unclear or context-dependent.
- **Time dynamics**: The temporal arc (costs in early rounds, gains in later rounds) of punishment’s effect on efficiency is not always observable in short-duration/one-shot experiments.
- **Absence of theory–empirics integration**: Some theory/mechanism arguments are present (on motivation, crowding out, endogenous norm formation), but are not fully matched to empirical outcomes in the literature.

**In conclusion, this literature base is well-suited to support predictions of punishment’s effect on efficiency for standard, homogeneous PGGs with known control efficiency, repeated rounds, peer-administered costly punishment, and well-monitored actions. Prediction in games with significant player heterogeneity, alternative technologies, or less-standard design features requires caution, as punitive interventions may have neutral, negative, or even counterproductive efficiency effects in those domains.**
