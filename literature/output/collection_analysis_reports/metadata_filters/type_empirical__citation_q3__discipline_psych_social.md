# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is broad and empirically rich, with 127 studies spanning laboratory experiments, field experiments, and some observational studies. The bulk of relevant empirical evidence consists of controlled laboratory PGGs and close variants, including continuous and binary-action PGGs, one-shot and repeated interactions, and studies using both centralized and decentralized (peer) punishment. There is substantial coverage of game design dimensions (player counts, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/impact), types of punishment (centralized, democratic, peer), and sometimes the availability of reward mechanisms. Most studies focus on payoff-based outcomes, with a strong emphasis on contribution/cooperation behavior; many also measure efficiency or related group payoffs, though others emphasize behavioral motivations, perceptions, or emotion.

Theoretical contributions are minimal; almost all relevant evidence is empirical. The set is well-suited for the target prediction task, with high coverage of exact-relevance empirical findings, but includes a significant tail of papers that only discuss behavioral outcomes or are contextually adjacent to PGGs. There is especially dense coverage of the classic lab-type linear PGG, as well as a spread of studies varying punishment and reward institutions, costs/impact, and information structure.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Relevance label:** *exact* (majority), *close/adjacent* (substantial minority)
- **Summary:** The core literature includes extensive, *exact* evidence on standard public goods games and minor variants (e.g., threshold, common pool, optional participation, asymmetric endowments).
- Adjacent or close variants include step-level/threshold PGGs, common-pool resource games, trust games, and network/resource dilemmas.
- Broadly, there is sufficient *exact* coverage for standard PGGs, with supplementary adjacent evidence for related environments.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Relevance label:** *exact* (primary studies), *close*, *adjacent/weak* (for some contextual studies)
- **Summary:** Most directly relevant studies manipulate the enabling/disabling of punishment or sanctions (peer, leader/centralized, democratic, exogenous/mechanized), with cost and impact specifications reported. Some studies focus only on reward, and many further discuss the behavioral drivers of punishment, including legitimacy, emotional responses, and social learning.
- There are also adjacent studies examining informal sanctions, third-party punishment, and variants outside the PGG paradigm.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Relevance label:** *exact/close* for about half the studies; *adjacent/weak/none* in the remainder
- **Summary:** Many core PGG studies directly report efficiency (group payoff / maximum possible), surplus, or average earnings. Others only measure contribution/cooperation rates, frequencies of punishment, or perceptions, without direct payoff-based outcome reporting.  
- When only behavioral outcomes are measured, the connection to efficiency must be inferred and is less direct for the prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based outcomes (directly relevant):**
  - **Group efficiency:** Explicitly reported as a ratio to the theoretical maximum (e.g., Gurerk, 2013; Nockur et al., 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
  - **Group payoff/earnings, welfare, surplus, or total coins generated:** Consistently measured in many experimental PGGs.
  - **Profit inequality or equality measures:** Sometimes reported as secondary efficiency-related outcomes (Hauser et al., 2021).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - **Contribution/cooperation rates:** Universally reported, but not equivalent to efficiency when punishment is costly.
  - **Punishment frequency, amount, targeting, antisocial vs. prosocial punishment:** Common, especially where efficiency data are missing.
  - **Norm compliance, trust, perceived fairness, participant satisfaction:** Used in interpretation but not efficiency proxies.
  - **Motivational, emotional, or reputational outcomes:** (e.g., anger, legitimacy, trustworthiness, punitive sentiment).

**Distinction explicitly made:** Many studies report both cooperation and payoff; several papers (e.g., Gurerk et al., 2009; Nockur et al., 2021) show that increased cooperation under punishment does not necessarily translate into higher efficiency due to the cost structure of punishment.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Cross-Paper Synthesis

### **General Effect of Enabling Punishment on Efficiency**
- **Peer punishment often increases cooperation/contribution but may not increase, and sometimes reduces, efficiency** due to the costs of punishment outweighing gains in contribution (Gurerk et al., 2009; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Nockur et al., 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
- **Centralized or leader-administered punishment, especially when prosocial, is more likely to increase efficiency** relative to peer punishment, which suffers from over-punishment and coordination failures (Gurerk et al., 2009; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017).
- **Democratic or group-coordinated punishment reduces costly/antisocial punishment and improves or at least does not reduce efficiency compared to standard peer punishment**. Over longer games, its efficiency can equal or exceed control (Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Nockur et al., 2021).
- **The presence and type of punishment mechanism (peer, democratic, central) are critical moderators of the efficiency effect**.

### **Moderators Identified**
- **Punishment cost and impact (fee-to-fine ratio):**
  - **Lower cost/high impact punishment is more efficient**, but when costless, can lead to excessive, counterproductive punishment (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017).
  - **Higher punishment costs reduce overuse/antisocial punishment and can improve efficiency** (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Structure of the punishment institution:**
  - **Uncoordinated or rich punishment environments allow retaliation and can lead to feuds, nullifying efficiency gains** (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
  - **Coordination mechanisms (democracy/voting, group decisions) control excessive punishment** (Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Molleman et al., 2019).
- **Game length (number of rounds):**
  - In short games, punishment may reduce or not affect efficiency; in longer settings, efficiency tends to increase, especially with properly structured punishment (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
- **Information and transparency:**
  - **Visibility of endowments/incomes shifts punishment targeting and can improve equality & contributions** (Hauser et al., 2021).
  - **History/social learning availability increases the acceptance and efficiency of punishment institutions** (Gurerk, 2013).
- **Baseline/control efficiency:**
  - **When control efficiency is high, enabling punishment may reduce efficiency** (Nair et al., 2018; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
  - **When baseline efficiency is low (high temptation to defect), punishment has more scope for positive efficiency effects** (Nair et al., 2018; Aakre et al., 2016).
- **Decision structure/action space:**
  - With alternative (more harmful) defection options available, punishment may fail to improve and can even worsen efficiency (Mulder et al., 2006).
  - **Ability to enforce both participation and compliance is necessary for punishment to increase efficiency** (Aakre et al., 2016).

### **Conditional Patterns**
- **Punishment does not universally increase efficiency**; peer punishment often reduces it unless mitigated by institution design.
- **Democratic, centralized, or exogenous punishment mechanisms (with appropriate parameters) are generally more likely to improve efficiency**.
- **The marginal effect of enabling punishment is strongly modulated by the cost, mechanism, targeting, and opportunity for antisocial retaliation.**
- **Non-payoff increases in cooperation are not reliable indicators of efficiency increases when punishment is costly.**

# 5) Prediction Guidance

### **Mapping Literature to Prediction Task**
- **Use control efficiency as a baseline; the marginal effect of punishment depends on mechanism design and cost/impact ratios.**
  - *If peer punishment is introduced in a setting where control efficiency is already high, expect little or negative effect on efficiency, possibly due to over-punishment (Nair et al., 2018; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).*
  - *If the baseline efficiency is low, enabling punishment (especially centralized or well-calibrated peer punishment) is more likely to produce a net increase in efficiency (Gurerk, 2013; Qin & Wang, 2013).*
  - *Democratic or coordinated peer punishment can improve efficiency over uncoordinated peer punishment, especially as the game length increases (Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Nockur et al., 2021).*
- **Key design dimensions for prediction:**
  - *Punishment cost and magnitude (fee-to-fine ratio)*
  - *Punishment institution (peer, democratic, centralized, exogenous)*
  - *Game length (number of rounds)*
  - *MPCR and baseline efficiency*
  - *Social information and transparency (visibility of incomes, social learning/history)*
- **Efficiency gains from enabling punishment should be predicted as contingent on (a) the net effect of increased cooperation relative to the cost of punishment, and (b) the institution/mechanism/filter on punishment that guards against excessive antisocial punishment.**
  - *If punishment is coordinated, costly, and legitimacy is ensured, efficiency gains are more likely.*
  - *Uncoordinated or costless punishment is prone to excessive/antisocial use and often reduces efficiency.*

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (peer vs. centralized, voting/democratic, exogenous/probabilistic), sometimes `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `all_or_nothing`, `chat` (less often used), `show_other_summaries` (sometimes), `show_n_rounds` (in longer studies).

**Indirectly/contextually informed:**
- `default_contrib`: Framing effects (opt-in vs. opt-out), present only rarely.
- `show_punishment_id`: Identity effects are only occasionally manipulated, mainly in studies on retaliation or reputation.
- `show_other_summaries`: Sometimes discussed in the context of transparency or summary information.
- `reward_tech`, `reward_magnitude`: Less frequently the primary focus but present in some studies.

**Sparse or effectively missing:**
- Some combinations of these dimensions (e.g., chat plus peer punishment plus exogenous social learning) are rare or absent.
- Settings combining peer punishment, reward, and high communication are less represented.
- The literature is thinner on large group sizes (>10), heterogeneous networks, or very long rounds.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavioral-outcome bias:** Many studies maximize relevance for contribution/cooperation, but fewer report efficiency or payoff-based outcomes. Non-payoff results cannot be directly used for efficiency prediction when sanctions are costly.
- **Institution-specific effects:** Efficiency outcomes are highly sensitive to the punishment institution (peer, democratic, central, exogenous); direct extrapolation across institution types may be misleading.
- **Parameter uncertainty:** Only a subset of studies provide the full set of design parameters required for detailed mapping to prediction dimensions. Critical moderators such as motivation, legitimacy, group identity, and cultural context are less often systematically manipulated.
- **Contextual limitations:** The evidence base is strongest for small to moderate lab groups (player count < 10), continuous/Japanese PGG designs, and Western student populations; generalization to field, large groups, or different cultures/societies is only partially supported (and real-world frequencies/effects may be weaker).
- **Adjacency limits:** Studies on closely related games (trust, dictator, money-burning, common-pool, voluntary participation) provide valuable context but may not transfer directly to PGG efficiency outcomes.
- **Ambiguity on mechanism interactions:** The joint effects of punishment and reward, communication, and social learning/history are not fully explored in multifactorial designs, although single-factor findings are robust.
- **Ceiling/floor effects:** When control efficiency is already near the ceiling (high baseline), scope for improvement via punishment is very limited, and punishment may even reduce efficiency.
- **Retaliation and antisocial punishment:** The presence of retaliation cycles or antisocial punishment (including punishment of high contributors) is a major source of efficiency loss, especially in uncoordinated peer punishment environments.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature base is broad, empirical, and directly relevant to the efficiency-prediction task for repeated public-goods games with and without punishment, though the efficiency effect is highly sensitive to the punishment institution, cost structure, and baseline efficiency. Predictions should be strongly moderated by these dimensions, and by the nature of behavioral outcomes reported: increases in cooperation do not guarantee increases in efficiency when punishment is costly, and the structure and legitimacy of the punishment institution are key to predicting net efficiency effects. Key prediction dimensions are well informed for standard PGGs, but some combinations and environments (large groups, long games, complex institutions, field) are less covered. Limitations arise primarily from behavioral focus, context transfer, and institution-specific findings.
