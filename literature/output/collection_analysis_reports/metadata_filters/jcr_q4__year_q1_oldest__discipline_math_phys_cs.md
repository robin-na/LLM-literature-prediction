# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is dominated by theoretical and simulation modeling papers (9/13), with a minority of empirical lab studies (4/13). Most theory papers examine efficiency and group payoff outcomes using formal evolutionary or agent-based models, typically in stylized public-goods-like or repeated game settings. A few papers extend these models to include nuanced mechanisms such as metanorms (e.g., reward of punishers) or voluntary opt-out strategies. The experimental papers focus on behavioral responses such as fairness, proposal rates, and norm compliance, with only two reporting direct efficiency or payoff data as relevant to the prediction task.

On scope, the evidence base is moderately broad in terms of mechanisms (punishment, rewards, reciprocation, reporting, enforcement), but is somewhat narrow with regard to empirical studies using standard multi-player public goods games (PGG) with peer punishment and explicit reporting of efficiency effects. A significant number of papers cover adjacent games (ultimatum, prisoner's dilemma, principal-agent, repeated matching), and several analyze PGG-variants or abstract resource-sharing simulations. Thus, the evidence on real PGG lab treatments with controlled design parameters and outcome reporting analogous to the prediction task is incomplete, but the set offers strong theoretical coverage of punishment mechanisms.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessing the literature set using the three target-relevance dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant**:
    - *exact*: 3/13 papers (Deng et al., 2012; Kurokawa et al., 2010; Bowles & Gintis, 2004) model standard PGG or close variants.
    - *close/adjacent*: 7/13 papers use games with strong structural similarities (N-person Prisoner’s Dilemma, resource-sharing, repeated matching), making their insights moderately transferable but not fully exact.
    - *weak/none*: 3/13 papers have little or no direct PGG content, often focusing on dyadic games (ultimatum, principal-agent).

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:
    - *exact*: 7/13 papers directly model costly peer punishment or sanctioning.
    - *adjacent*: 5/13 papers cover broader or alternative sanctioning mechanisms (e.g., behavioral reciprocity, trigger strategies, adaptive learning, endogenous reporting) without explicit costly punishment mechanisms.
    - *weak/none*: 1/13 paper (Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006) treats punishment only in the context of general behavioral framing.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
    - *exact*: 6/13 papers report or theorize efficiency, total group payoff, or welfare as a primary outcome.
    - *adjacent*: 5/13 report only behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, cooperation rate, norm compliance) or limit analysis to conditions for possible cooperation.
    - *none*: 2/13 have only critical or conceptual discussion with no measurable outcomes.

Consequently, the literature provides several highly relevant theoretical models directly addressing PGGs with punishment and efficiency (e.g., Deng et al., 2012; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006), covers multiple adjacent variants with partial transferability, and contains limited direct empirical measurement in the precise format the downstream prediction task requires.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes:**
- *Efficiency/group payoff/total wealth*: The main theoretical papers (Deng et al., 2012; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006; Jaffe, 2004; Kurokawa et al., 2010) focus on average group payoffs, efficiency (relative to full cooperation), or aggregate wealth, often reporting these as primary results.
- *Surplus/total coins generated*: Used interchangeably with efficiency in simulations and models.
- Some empirical papers (Abbink et al., 2004) also report efficiency as total payoffs in the presence/absence of punishment.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- *Contribution rate/cooperation rate*: Many studies (especially empirical) measure rates of cooperation, frequency of norm compliance, or honesty/slack in reporting (Davis & Holt, 1999; Chen, 2012), but often do not compute aggregate group payoff or efficiency.
- *Punishment and reward frequency*: Used as both an independent and dependent variable for interpreting the underlying mechanism, not as a payoff outcome.
- *Strategy dynamics*: Some theory papers investigate the evolutionary success of certain strategies or equilibria, which is a step removed from actual efficiency outcomes.

Most importantly, **only a subset of the literature provides direct evidence on efficiency or average group payoff differences between punishment-enabled and control treatments**—the rest provide insights into mechanisms or behavioral effects but are not directly predictive for efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Positive Effects of Punishment on Efficiency:**
- Several theory papers (Deng et al., 2012; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kendal et al., 2006) predict that enabling costly peer (or concerted) punishment in PGG (or close variants) robustly increases group efficiency relative to controls with punishment disabled, especially as group size increases and punishment mechanisms are strong enough to deter defection.
    - *Mechanism*: Both rare but severe punishment (Deng et al.) and metanorm-enforced punishment (Kendal et al.) are especially potent in stabilizing high efficiency.

**Negative or Ambiguous Efficiency Effects:**
- Two models (Jaffe, 2004; Abbink et al., 2004) find that while punishment increases norm compliance or reduces selfishness, the costs of punishment (direct loss of resources through sanctions) can outweigh the gains from increased cooperation, resulting in lower or unchanged efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline.
    - Jaffe’s (2004) model and Abbink et al.'s (2004) ultimatum experiments show that unless norm changes produce strong synergistic surplus, costly punishment can be net efficiency reducing.
- The Davis & Holt (1999), Chen (2012), and Castro & Toro (2008) studies show behavioral cooperation can rise in the presence of punishment or alternative sanctioning mechanisms, but they do not provide efficiency measures, meaning the translation to group payoff is unclear.
- Some theoretical arguments (Jones, 1999) indicate that the game structure (duration, discounting) is crucial: punishment only sustains efficiency over longer horizons or sufficient continuity; otherwise, it is ineffective.

**Moderators and Conditional Effects:**
- *Group size*: The positive effect of punishment on efficiency tends to be stronger in larger groups, where concerted or community-enforced punishment mechanisms reduce costs and increase the probability of a cooperative equilibrium (Deng et al., 2012; Annen, 2011).
- *Punishment severity/cost*: Severe, less frequent punishment is more efficient, as high punishment costs can negate efficiency gains (Deng et al., 2012; Bowles & Gintis, 2004).
- *Metanorms/reward mechanisms*: Combining punishment with rewards or with enforcement of punishment (through additional metanorms) yields higher and more robust efficiency gains (Kendal et al., 2006).
- *Framing and reporting structure*: When punishment and experience-sharing are exogenous or transparent, efficiency is higher than when truthful reporting must be endogenously incentivized (Annen, 2011).
- *Game structure*: If voluntary participation is possible (loner strategy; Castro & Toro, 2008), high efficiency can occur without punishment.

**Preserved Ambiguity:**
- There is disagreement across the literature: While "classic" theory (Bowles & Gintis, Deng et al., Kendal et al.) predicts robust efficiency gains from punishment, agent-based models (Jaffe, 2004) and behavioral experiments (Abbink et al., 2004) illustrate scenarios in which costly punishment reduces efficiency relative to the control.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based on the paper set, the literature suggests the following for predicting efficiency in PGG-like environments with peer punishment (given design parameters and baseline/control efficiency):

1. **Positive average effect is likely but not guaranteed**: Most theoretical models suggest enabling peer punishment will increase efficiency over control, provided punishment is sufficiently severe (relative to cost), metanorms may be in place, and/or group size is large.

2. **Magnitude depends on punishment structure and group size**:
    - Severe, rare, or concerted punishment mechanisms are more likely to yield large efficiency gains, especially in larger groups (Deng et al., 2012).
    - If punishment is mild, frequent, or costly, efficiency gains may be modest or negative (Jaffe, 2004; Abbink et al., 2004).

3. **Baseline efficiency is moderating**:
    - If control efficiency is already high due to other mechanisms (e.g., voluntary participation, opt-out, or high baseline cooperation), the marginal effect of adding punishment may be small or negative (Castro & Toro, 2008; Jaffe, 2004).

4. **Combined mechanisms amplify effects**:
    - If reward and punishment (or metanorm support for punishers) are present together, efficiency effects of adding punishment are likely to be more positive and robust (Kendal et al., 2006; Chen, 2012 for behavior).

5. **Caveats—design and context are critical**:
    - The effectiveness of punishment depends on dimensions such as player count, punishment cost/severity, group information flow, and cultural framing (Annen, 2011; Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006). Generalization across settings should be cautious.

6. **When using design dimensions for prediction**, the best-supported effects are for:
    - Player count (larger groups → stronger positive effect),
    - Punishment cost and structure (lower cost, higher impact → stronger effect),
    - Existence of reward/metanorms,
    - Baseline efficiency (control payoff).

7. **Predict with explicit uncertainty**: Given the documented negative or ambiguous findings when cost of punishment is high (Jaffe, 2004; Abbink et al., 2004), predictions should retain uncertainty, especially for empirical settings closely matching these studies.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Multiple theories model varying group size, showing increased punishment effectiveness and efficiency gains in larger groups (Deng et al., 2012; Annen, 2011; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kurokawa et al., 2010).
- `num_rounds`: Modeled in most repeated-game theory papers, with evidence that longer games (or higher continuation probabilities) make punishment more effective (Jones, 1999; Kurokawa et al., 2010).
- `mpcr`: Explicitly present in several theory papers as a key parameter (Deng et al., 2012; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Kurokawa et al., 2010), influencing both baseline efficiency and the effect of punishment.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Widely analyzed in theory and agent-based models (Deng et al., 2012; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Jaffe, 2004; Abbink et al., 2004), determining tradeoffs between deterring defection and resource wastage.
- `all_or_nothing`: Featured explicitly in several models, moderating the effect of sanctioning (Deng et al., 2012; Kurokawa et al., 2010; Annen, 2011).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Directly discussed when analyzing metanorms and combined incentive schemes (Kendal et al., 2006; Chen, 2012).

**Indirect/Contextually Discussed:**
- `default_contrib`: Some models assume or vary altruism baselines, but not as a design feature.
- `chat`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`: Discussed mainly as informational/enforcement mechanisms (Annen, 2011 discusses experience sharing and summary reporting; some empirical papers allow chat but do not systematically analyze its differential impact).

**Effectively Missing:**
- Direct evidence on the effect of `chat`, visibility of punishment (`show_punishment_id`), default framing (`default_contrib`), and reward technology on efficiency is sparse or absent.
- Little statistical or empirical evidence exists for the combined interaction effects of multiple design dimensions (e.g., how punishment cost interacts with group information flow or chat).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical evidence with exact PGG and efficiency outcomes is limited**: Very few lab studies in this set directly measure average efficiency in multi-player PGGs with controlled variations in peer punishment, limiting generalizability.
- **Reliance on theoretical/simulation models**: The strongest positive findings are from simulation or formal models, which rest on assumptions about rationality, game structure, and equilibrium selection that may not hold in actual human groups.
- **Conflicting empirical and simulation evidence**: Some agent-based models and empirical studies (Jaffe, 2004; Abbink et al., 2004) show harm to efficiency from punishment, contradicting classic theory models, especially when punishment is costly or deterrence is incomplete.
- **Indirect transfer from adjacent games**: Several papers use games such as N-person Prisoner's Dilemma or principal-agent/ultimatum structures. While informative, these are not exact analogs of the prediction setting and may misestimate efficiency impacts due to structural differences.
- **Sparse dimension-level evidence**: Only a small subset of the 14 design dimensions are consistently modeled and/or reported across the evidence base; several—particularly those having to do with information structure and communication—are underexplored for efficiency outcomes.
- **Context and framing effects**: At least one paper (Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006) cautions that outcome effects may be highly dependent on context, framing, and implicit cues not captured by formal design dimensions.
- **Missing joint variation**: No paper investigates the joint effect of all or most of the 14 dimensions, limiting the ability to model cross-dimensional interactions.
- **No out-of-sample validation of predictions**: Almost all model-based claims are theoretical, with little empirical validation under varied experimental control for generalization.

In summary, while the literature as a whole provides a strong theoretical basis for expecting positive efficiency effects from peer punishment in PGG-like games under certain parameter regimes, prediction should remain cautious—particularly where baseline efficiency is already high, punishment is costly, or empirical findings suggest negative or muted effects. With partial dimension coverage, the use of this base for quantitative efficiency prediction requires explicit uncertainty and acknowledgment of these limitations.
