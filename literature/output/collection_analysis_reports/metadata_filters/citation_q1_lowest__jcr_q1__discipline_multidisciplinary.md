# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set comprises a broad and diverse collection of 82 papers, including both empirical (primarily laboratory experiments) and theoretical/modeling studies. There is a strong representation of classic and modern public goods games (PGG) with variations, as well as adjacent social dilemmas (e.g., threshold PGGs, division-of-labor games, trust games, and contest games). The evidence base includes several studies reporting direct efficiency or payoff outcomes (the key metric for prediction), though a large portion focus on cooperation/contribution or behavior rather than group efficiency per se. Notably, a minority of papers provide control-and-treatment (punishment-enabled vs. disabled) efficiency directly in PGG settings. There is considerable attention to game design features, structural moderators (group size, punishment parameters, information, composition), and interventions (punishment institution design, facilitation, rewards, communication, reputation, and third-party enforcement). The mix spans narrow, high-relevance studies and broader, mechanism-oriented or adjacent research on related dilemmas.

# 2) Task Relevance

### a. `pgg_or_variant`
- **exact**: Many papers use standard or slightly modified repeated public goods games (Harrell, 2019; Molenmaker et al., 2023; Bühren et al., 2023; Botta et al., 2024; Zefferman, 2023; Nakagawa et al., 2022; Clark & Dickinson, 2020, etc.).
- **close/adjacent**: Notable subpopulations address close structural variants (threshold PGGs, linear PGGs with contest/competition, division-of-labor, trust games). Some model institutional or environmental variations.
- **none/weak**: A segment of the set addresses only tangentially relevant games or mechanisms, or control scenarios with no sanctions.

### b. `punishment_or_sanctions`
- **exact**: Numerous studies explicitly compare conditions with and without peer punishment, report punishment as a core manipulated variable, or dissect its design and side effects (Harrell, 2019; Molenmaker et al., 2023; Bühren et al., 2023; Zefferman, 2023; Botta et al., 2024).
- **adjacent/weak**: Several instead focus on indirect sanctions (ostracism, reputation), non-monetary consequences, or punishment in closely-related but non-PGG settings (e.g., Prisoner’s Dilemma, trust games).
- **none**: A subset addresses PGGs or other social dilemmas with no punishment dimension.

### c. `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
- **exact**: About a dozen papers report efficiency or total/group payoff as a main outcome and analyze its response to punishment (Harrell, 2019; Molenmaker et al., 2023; Bühren et al., 2023; Zefferman, 2023; Botta et al., 2024; Heine & Strobel, 2020; DeCaro et al., 2024; Garrido et al., 2025; Mohlin et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2022; Nirjhor & Nakamaru, 2023).
- **close/adjacent**: Many focus on behavioral proxies (contribution, cooperation frequency) and only secondarily report payoff; others address efficiency in adjacent or non-PGG settings.
- **none/weak**: A significant fraction never report group payoff, surplus, or efficiency in any form.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (`efficiency`, group total payoff, surplus, accumulated wealth, etc.): Explicitly addressed in a moderate subset, particularly in studies focused on net welfare, efficiency ratios, or accumulated income under different punishment regimes. Some theory papers provide formulas or simulation-based estimates for efficiency.
- **Behavioral outcomes** (contribution rate, cooperation frequency, norm compliance, punishment frequency, convergence): More common; most studies report these as primary outcomes, with efficiency often only inferred when contributions are high.
- **Other outcomes**: Emotional, attitudinal, or reputational effects; mechanisms of norm formation; perception of punishment; belief updating; neural signals.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### General Relationship between Punishment and Efficiency

- **Enabling peer punishment often increases contributions**, but the **effect on efficiency is highly variable** and strongly moderated by the **cost of punishment, structure of the group, and design details** (Harrell, 2019; Molenmaker et al., 2023; Bühren et al., 2023; Zefferman, 2023; Botta et al., 2024).
    - **Efficiency gain is not guaranteed**: If punishment is costly or ineffective, or if there is substantial antisocial/ineffective punishment, efficiency can stagnate or even decline despite higher contributions (Molenmaker et al., 2023; Heine & Strobel, 2020; Ezeigbo, 2017).
    - **Leader/centralized punishment tends to be more efficient** than distributed/peer punishment because of lower total punishment costs and less counter-punishment (Harrell, 2019; Botta et al., 2024; Zefferman, 2023).

### Moderators and Key Mechanisms

- **Punishment cost and effectiveness** are fundamental: Efficiency gains require **punishment to be cheap-to-administer and effective at discouraging free-riding** (Bühren et al., 2023; Zefferman, 2023; Mohlin et al., 2023; Botta et al., 2024; Nirjhor & Nakamaru, 2023; Zhou et al., 2022). **Costly or weak punishment can backfire**, leading to lower welfare (Ezeigbo, 2017; Heine & Strobel, 2020).
- **Group characteristics** matter: In **homogeneous/uniform groups**, punishment is more effective and efficiency is more likely to increase; in **heterogeneous/pluriform groups**, punishment can become discriminatory or antisocial and may lower efficiency (Molenmaker et al., 2023; Heine & Strobel, 2020).
- **Baseline (control) efficiency** is a critical baseline: **Where control efficiency is low due to persistent free-riding, effective punishment can close the gap to full efficiency** (Botta et al., 2024; Zefferman, 2023), **if institutions are appropriately designed**. **Where control efficiency is already high, additional punishment may merely waste resources** (Lie-Panis et al., 2024).
- **Institutional design** (coordination, facilitation, and the structure of punishment opportunities) can dramatically alter the effect of punishment: Coordinated or facilitated punishment increases efficiency; uncoordinated or abused systems (e.g., regulatory abuse, profitable punishment) may **reduce** it (Harrell, 2019; DeCaro et al., 2024; Alam & Rai, 2025).
- **Information and monitoring**: Rich, low-cost group-member information can substitute for, or undermine, punishment; **perfect monitoring enables maximal efficiency with stable, minimal punishment “threats”** (Botta et al., 2024; Berger & De Silva, 2021).
- **Adjunct institutions**: The inclusion of rewards or dynamic, flexible institutions can outperform punishment-only setups and maximize efficiency (Garrido et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2022; Jia & Wang, 2025).
- **Environmental, cultural, or psychological context**: Sample composition, group trust, cognitive/emotional manipulations, and cultural context can mediate effects but are less systematically investigated.
- **Special cases**: Some settings with competition, profitable punishment, or severe antisocial punishment see **punishment reducing efficiency relative to control** (Heine & Strobel, 2020; Alam & Rai, 2025; Ezeigbo, 2017).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct prediction of treatment efficiency from design, control efficiency, and punishment dimensions** is best supported when the study:
    - Reports efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome.
    - Compares no-punishment and punishment-enabled conditions under otherwise-matched design dimensions.
    - Clearly states punishment cost, magnitude, monitoring effectiveness, group size, and institution (peer vs. centralized).
- **Enabling peer punishment increases efficiency relative to control** especially when:
    - The **punishment is low-cost and high-impact**.
    - Group structure is **uniform/homogeneous**.
    - Punishment is **well-coordinated and not abused**.
    - **Baseline efficiency is low** (i.e., there is room for improvement).
    - **Monitoring is sufficient** for reliable detection of defectors.
- **Efficiency gains are reduced (or negative) when**:
    - **Punishment is costly relative to its deterrence impact**.
    - **Punishment is misapplied or antisocial** (especially in heterogeneous groups or competitive environments).
    - **Punishment inflicts more cost (including counter-punishment) than the benefit of higher cooperation**.
    - **Profitable punishment** introduces *perverse incentives* and lowers welfare (Alam & Rai, 2025).
- **In close or adjacent game types**, efficiency gains may not materialize without careful institutional calibration—sometimes only *mixed* institutions (punishment + reward) or centralization yield optimal outcomes (Garrido et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2022).
- **Control (no-punishment) efficiency is an important predictor**—in very high-control-efficiency cases, marginal efficiency gain from punishment is lower or even negative (Lie-Panis et al., 2024).

## Dimension-Specific Guidance:
- **Player count/group size**: Larger groups make punishment harder to coordinate, and, unless punishment/monitoring is centralized or highly efficient, efficiency gains diminish (Zefferman, 2023; Botta et al., 2024).
- **Punishment cost/effectiveness**: High punishment cost-to-impact ratios harm efficiency (Ezeigbo, 2017; Bühren et al., 2023).
- **Monitoring/Information**: Higher monitoring/observability makes effective punishment systems more likely to increase efficiency (Botta et al., 2024; Berger & De Silva, 2021).
- **Institutional design (structure of punishment)**: Centralized or leader-based punishment is more efficient than distributed, due to lower redundancy and waste (Harrell, 2019).
- **Group structure/composition**: Pluriform or heterogeneous groups are at risk of discriminatory/antisocial punishment, which harms efficiency (Molenmaker et al., 2023).
- **Facilitated/coordinated enforcement**: Facilitation/guided group discussion improves institutional efficiency when introducing punishment (DeCaro et al., 2024).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed** (parameters systematically analyzed and directly linked to efficiency or similar payoffs):
- `player_count`: Strong, direct evidence on how group size moderates punishment effectiveness (Harrell, 2019; Zefferman, 2023; Botta et al., 2024; Nirjhor & Nakamaru, 2023).
- `num_rounds`: Well specified in experimental and theoretical work, affecting the long-run sustainability of efficiency gains.
- `mpcr`: Regularly varied. Lower MPCR increases potential gain from punishment; efficiency gains more likely when MPCR is low (Zefferman, 2023).
- `punishment_cost` / `punishment_tech` (magnitude): Central to most theoretical and many empirical findings—efficiency gains require punishment to be cost-effective.
- `punishment_exists`: The core experimental or modeling manipulation; all relevant findings concern enabling/disabling punishment.

**Indirectly Informed**:
- `chat`: Sometimes manipulated; evidence suggests that chat/communication moderates the effect of punishment on efficiency via facilitation and coordination (DeCaro et al., 2024).
- `all_or_nothing`: Studied as both continuous and binary contributions; can modulate the impact of punishment on efficiency.
- `show_other_summaries`: Group feedback is shown to increase group convergence and possibly efficiency (Nielsen & Pfattheicher, 2024).
- `show_punishment_id`: Less frequently analyzed; its effect is mostly on antisocial/counter-punishment rather than directly on efficiency.
- `reward_exists`: Some studies (especially in adjacent settings) show that reward can have additive or even greater efficiency boosts but at sustainability costs (Garrido et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2022).

**Contextually Discussed (not systematically manipulated, but mentioned or controlled for)**:
- `default_contrib`: Sometimes manipulated for framing effects, with limited focus on efficiency.
- `show_n_rounds`: Standard in many experiments, rarely isolated as a moderator of punishment’s effect on efficiency.
- `reward_cost` / `reward_tech`: Infrequently analyzed as primary focus; more relevant to mixed-incentive or reward-only designs.
  
**Effectively Missing**:
- Very few studies provide detailed, head-to-head analysis of all 14 dimensions. Certain configuration features (e.g., `show_punishment_id`, `reward_cost/tech`) are rarely central to reported efficiency outcomes.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Many studies measure cooperation/contribution rather than payoff or efficiency**; predictions based on these may be unreliable if punishment is costly.
- **Generalizability**: Many empirical studies use small groups, fixed lab settings, homogeneous samples, and standard punishment tech/cost; extrapolation to larger, heterogeneous, real-world settings risks error.
- **Design coverage**: Key moderators like `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `show_punishment_id`, and details of punishment institution structure are not uniformly varied or even reported across the literature.
- **Measurement challenges**: Efficiency is defined consistently only in a subset of papers. Many provide only behavioral proxies or partial/aggregated payoffs.
- **Evidence gaps**: Experimental control/treatment comparisons for efficiency are sparse relative to the number of studies focusing on other outcomes.
- **Potential for “negative” results**: Several studies show that punishment can reduce efficiency, especially with poorly designed institutions, costly punishment, profitable or discriminatory punishment, or in heterogeneous groups. This underscores the risk of overgeneralizing from averages.
- **Theoretical bias and heterogeneity**: Competing models (with variable assumptions about agent types, conditional cooperation, and strategy sets) yield divergent predictions, especially in the absence of empirical validation.
- **Limited coverage of long-term, ecological, and “real-world” PGG variants**: Many designs are idealized or restricted in duration, feedback, and participant diversity.
- **Lack of cross-dimensional interaction analysis**: Most studies test only a few dimensions at a time; interactions (e.g., how group size and punishment cost jointly affect efficiency) are not systematically mapped.

---

In summary, the literature set offers strong support for the premise that enabling peer punishment can increase efficiency beyond baseline, **provided several institutional and design thresholds are met**. However, **the effect is highly sensitive to implementation details** (especially punishment cost/effectiveness, coordination, and group structure), and **even small design differences can reverse the efficiency outcome**. For quantitative prediction, **studies reporting direct efficiency impacts as a function of experimental design and control efficiency should be prioritized**, but **substantial uncertainty remains when moving beyond well-studied regimes**.
