# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is exclusively comprised of theory papers (philosophy, modeling, and evolutionary analysis), with no empirical or experimental studies directly conducted by the authors themselves. However, several theory papers reference or meta-analyze existing experimental work. The breadth of the literature is moderate—while several foundational and adjacent social dilemma paradigms (e.g., trust game, networked cooperation, repeated games, evolutionary models) are covered, only a handful engage specifically and systematically with public goods games (PGGs) featuring peer punishment and the resulting impact on group efficiency or payoff. Some papers provide detailed model-based predictions regarding institutional forms and efficiency, while others address related behavioral, evolutionary, or conceptual issues with less direct relevance to the efficiency prediction task.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**  
- *Exact:* Five papers (Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Vlerick, 2016; Lim & Capraro, 2022) are either PGG-focused or explicitly analyze public goods dilemmas, often with punishment.
- *Close/Adjacent:* Several others use games structurally similar to PGGs (networked trust games, repeated sharing games, evolutionary dilemmas) or discuss PGGs as examples, but usually shift quickly to more general or adjacent paradigms.
- *Weak/None:* Some address only evolutionary or psychological contexts around altruism and cooperation, not PGGs or formal social dilemmas.

**Punishment or Sanctions:**  
- *Exact/Close:* Most of the relevant papers focus on, or centrally discuss, punishment or sanctions as mechanisms for sustaining cooperation or efficiency—even in the adjacent literatures where the core game is not a PGG (e.g., trust or sharing games).
- *Adjacent/Weak:* A few conceptually discuss punishment (as a human universal, a byproduct of social evolution, or a model vulnerability) without formal models or outcomes.
- *None:* Some do not include punishment or sanctions at all.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**  
- *Exact:* Four to five theory papers explicitly model group efficiency, welfare, total payoff, or closely related ratio-to-optimum outcomes (Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Rosas, 2008 [first]; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Vlerick, 2016; Lim & Capraro, 2022; Xu et al., 2014).
- *Close/Adjacent:* A few additional papers treat group fitness or average payoff as a central outcome, even if in an adjacent paradigm (trust networks, stag hunt, etc.) or discuss resource preservation as an efficiency analog.
- *Weak/None:* Many others measure only behavior (e.g., cooperation rates) or evolutionary stability, not group payoff or efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes:**  
- The following payoff outcomes are observed or modeled in depth:
    - Efficiency: group total payoff as a proportion of the maximum possible (Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Vlerick, 2016; Lim & Capraro, 2022; Xu et al., 2014)
    - Total group payoff or average fitness (O'Connor, 2016)
    - Welfare, resource preservation, or surplus (often analogized or used in models)

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
- Most of the remainder focus on:
    - Contribution rate, norm compliance, or cooperation rates
    - Evolutionary prevalence and persistence of altruism (Woodcock & Heath, 2002; Rosas, 2008 [second]; Forber & Smead, 2016)
    - Strategy convergence or prevalence of emotional motivation or strong reciprocity (Clavien, 2012; Vromen, 2017)
    - Application of social norms, moral reasoning, or commitment devices (Vlerick, 2020; Handfield & Thrasher, 2019)

**Distinction:**  
Only the first set (efficiency/payoff) aligns with the downstream prediction task; the latter outcomes are indirect unless explicitly modeled as leading to a quantified improvement in group payoff.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment Increases Efficiency Under Many, But Not All, Conditions:**  
    - Multiple theory papers with supporting meta-analytic or comparative evidence agree: when punishment is institutionally feasible and adopted (especially via collective or locally-scaled governance), it usually boosts group efficiency compared to no-punishment baselines (Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Vlerick, 2016; Lim & Capraro, 2022; Xu et al., 2014).
    - *However*, this is not universal. Costly direct punishment can lead to "efficiency loss" due to over-punishment or resource waste (Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012).

- **Temporal and Structural Moderators:**  
    - The net effect of punishment on efficiency depends strongly on time horizon: in short games, punishment may be net inefficient due to high costs upfront, but in longer repeated interactions with stable groups, the cost of punishment declines over time as cooperation stabilizes, and efficiency can ultimately surpass control levels (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
    - The structure of enforcement (individual vs. collective, local vs. global goods) moderates adoption and effectiveness: misaligned governance and poor information quality diminish punishment's benefits (Vasconcelos et al., 2022).

- **Punishment Design Factors Matter Critically:**  
    - Punishment *method* (direct cost vs. exclusion, reputation, or rating-based sanctions) impacts efficiency. Exclusion and indirect/reputation-based punishment mechanisms are frequently more efficient and stable, limiting the resource cost of sanctioning (Rosas, 2008; Xu et al., 2014).
    - Punishment effectiveness and cost (cost-to-impact ratio) affect whether punishment leads to efficiency gains or losses. Overly costly or weak punishment can reduce group payoff (Lim & Capraro, 2022).

- **Network and Information Structure are Key Moderators:**  
    - Structured networks plus punishment deliver greater efficiency than well-mixed populations with punishment alone; network details determine threshold costs for achieving full cooperation (Lim & Capraro, 2022; Xu et al., 2014).

- **Learning, Memory, and Observability Also Matter:**  
    - Longer memory, local information, and explicit summary feedback can increase the effectiveness—and thus efficiency—of punishment institutions (Vasconcelos et al., 2022).

- **Behavioral Pathways Are Not Always Efficiency-Shaped:**  
    - Some papers (e.g., those on emotional mechanisms or evolutionary altruism) suggest that mechanisms like guilt, moral learning, and reciprocity can enhance cooperation, which *may* indirectly improve efficiency, but the relationship is not always modeled in payoff terms.

**Conflicts/Ambiguity:**  
- Not all models agree on the universal positivity of punishment; potential efficiency loss due to overuse or miscalibration is highlighted (Rosas, 2008; Frey & Rusch, 2012).
- The relative performance of different punishment/reward systems is variable; dynamic punishments or exclusion seem most robust but require specific implementation contexts.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Punishment Effects Depend on Multiple Moderators:**  
    - Strong support exists for including punishment presence, punishment method/technology, punishment cost, group size, time horizon, and information availability as key predictors of treatment efficiency.
    - Simply enabling peer punishment often, but not always, increases efficiency, particularly when baseline (control) efficiency is low and the design features favor sustained cooperation (repeated rounds, stable partners, information feedback, effective but not wasteful punishment) (Vasconcelos et al., 2022; Frey & Rusch, 2012).

- **Use of Control (No-Punishment) Efficiency:**  
    - Control-game efficiency is informative: if the baseline is already near optimum, the potential efficiency gains from punishment may be moot or negative (due to sanctioning costs). If baseline efficiency is low, the potential gain is greater, contingent on favorable design moderators.

- **Game Design Dimensions as Predictors:**  
    - Theoretical and meta-analytic evidence suggests strong prediction value from: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech/method, show_other_summaries/information feedback, and network structure.
    - Indirect/moderate value is attached to all_or_nothing framing, default_contrib framing, and, in some models, the observability and transparency of punishment/reward identity.
    - Network structure and learning environment details (not always explicitly coded in game dimensions, but sometimes associated with show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, and chat) may need further operationalization for precise prediction.

- **Behavioral Outcomes and Efficiency:**  
    - Cooperation rate, compliance, or norm adherence are not direct predictors of efficiency unless translated (via model or empirical mapping) into group payoffs. Caution is advised in using purely behavioral outcomes.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- player_count (scale effects and governance alignment)
- num_rounds (time horizon/learning, efficiency evolution)
- punishment_cost, punishment_tech (cost/impact mechanism, method type)
- mpcr (relative returns to cooperation)
- all_or_nothing (sometimes, especially in network/trust paradigms)
- show_other_summaries (information, feedback, memory; indirect but supported in models)

**Indirectly or Contextually Discussed:**
- chat (social learning, not explicitly modeled but discussed in information terms)
- show_n_rounds (in planning, memory, and learning context)
- show_punishment_id (sometimes referenced as identity or reputation, not always isolated as a formal dimension)
- reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech (a few mention comparison of reward vs. punishment efficacy, rarely central)
- default_contrib (framing aspects only occasionally noted)

**Effectively Missing/Dormant:**
- Several papers do not model or mention: show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id, reward dimensions, default_contrib, or chat, except in passing or as contextual factors with minimal prediction guidance.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **No Direct Empirical Quantification:**  
    - All evidence is theoretical or simulation-based, sometimes referencing meta-analyses or empirical regularities but not providing direct experimental estimates or regression coefficients for the effect of punishment across parameter spaces.

- **Generalizability Is Sometimes Unclear:**  
    - Several models operate in "adjacent" paradigms (trust games, networked sharing, evolutionary games) which, although structurally similar, may not perfectly transfer to standard PGGs.

- **Payoff–Behavior Mapping Often Assumed, Not Shown:**  
    - Many papers discuss cooperation stabilization or behavioral persistence without always demonstrating that higher cooperation translates quantitatively to higher efficiency (payoff) in their models.

- **Sparse Coverage of Certain Dimensions:**  
    - Information dimensions (show_punishment_id, chat, default_contrib, etc.) are underrepresented as formalized predictors—more commonly invoked as context or design features than separately manipulated variables.

- **Uncertainty in Boundary Conditions:**  
    - Some findings provide only qualitative (not quantitative) or conditional guidance (e.g., "if punishment is not too costly...", "if local institutions are adopted..."), leaving uncertainty about thresholds or marginal effects.

- **Predominance of Theoretical Over Empirical Synthesis:**  
    - The absence of high-powered empirical synthesis or parameter sensitivity means that effects established as "robust" in models may break down in real-world experimental applications due to individual heterogeneity, unforeseen interaction effects, or design idiosyncrasies.

**In summary:**  
The paper set provides strong, multi-model theoretical support that (a) enabling peer punishment often increases group efficiency in PGGs, (b) the degree of this increase depends on several key design dimensions (notably time horizon, group stability, punishment method/cost, and information structure), and (c) baseline (control) efficiency is informative for the potential gain from punishment. However, the evidence is exclusively theoretical or meta-analytic, lacking direct empirical quantification, and leaves several design dimensions, moderators, and exact effect sizes only partially addressed or contextually discussed. Care should be taken in extrapolating point predictions or applying results to sparsely covered dimensions.
