# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set provides an extensive, high-quality pool of primarily **empirical experimental** evidence, complemented by a robust body of theory/simulation papers. The core of the evidence directly addresses repeated public goods games (PGGs) and close variants, manipulating punishment and/or sanction mechanisms, and reporting both **group efficiency** (payoff-based outcomes) and a wide array of behavioral measures. The paper set encompasses a wide range of design dimensions (group size, rounds, punishment cost/tech, MPCR, communication, anonymity, network structure, etc.), institutional variations (peer vs. central punishment, voluntary vs. imposed, exogenous vs. endogenous, targeted vs. random), and context moderators (heterogeneity, group composition, culture, prior experience). Many studies provide **exact or close matches** to the prediction task: the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency, controlling for the same game with punishment disabled. There is also coverage of adjacent and weakly relevant research (single-shot games, partner choice, reputation, various non-punitive mechanisms), but these are clearly distinguished in relevance labels.

**Empirical orientation:** The majority of directly relevant evidence is from laboratory experiments—often using the canonical linear PGG/VCM design. Field, framed-field, and lab-in-the-field studies are included and provide ecological/contextual findings, especially on heterogeneity and norm effects. Theory and simulation papers elaborate on the analytic underpinnings, boundary conditions, and evolutionary stability of punishment-based cooperation.

**Breadth & depth:** The evidence is broad—covering PGGs, close CPR games, trust/investment games, team and network production, and multi-stage/threshold/club/contest variations. It is also deep: many papers vary or report all key prediction dimensions or provide parametric analyses on punishment cost/tech, group structure, heterogeneity, and institutional features.

# 2) Task Relevance

### Relevance Coding

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *Exact*: Canonical repeated linear PGGs with controlled manipulation of punishment.  
- *Close*: Variants/CPR games with similar social dilemma structure, group production, and efficiency measures.  
- *Adjacent*: Investment, trust, contest, or threshold games—mechanistically similar but not strictly matching the PGG.  
- *Weak/None*: Single-interaction, non-cooperation or non-payoff studies.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *Exact*: Controlled, costly peer or central punishment/sanctioning implemented as a treatment; specification of cost and technology; deduced impact on efficiency.
- *Close*: Exclusion/ostracism/voting as alternative sanction, automatic institutional punishment, or endogenous/third-party sanction mechanisms.
- *Adjacent*: Reputation, reward, partner switching, gossip, information provision, or norm communication as indirect/adjacent sanction-like mechanisms.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *Exact*: Group payoff, total earnings, welfare, surplus, or explicit efficiency ratio to social optimum across control/treatment.
- *Close*: Step-level provision rates, success rates in public good or CPR games as clear proxies for efficiency.
- *Adjacent*: Only contribution rates, cooperation, or behavioral proxies; no payoff-based reporting.

### Summary

- For *pgg_or_variant* and *punishment_or_sanctions*, the core evidence is **exact** or **close**, facilitating direct prediction calibration.
- For *efficiency or related payoff*, most experimental papers report true efficiency/earnings; the few reporting only behavioral outcomes or proxies are marked as *adjacent*.
- **The breadth and frequency of exact/close relevance on all three dimensions is exceptional compared to most topic literatures.**

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related (Efficient):**  
- Group average payoff, total earnings, group profit as percentage of optimum, welfare/surplus, ratio of actual to fully cooperative outcomes (canonical efficiency).
- Step-level provision rates (threshold PGGs or CPRs) when mapped to group success and surplus.
- Specific reporting of efficiency values for both control (no-punishment) and treatment (punishment-enabled) conditions in many studies.

**Non-payoff/Behavioral:**  
- Contribution rate, cooperation rate, rate of norm compliance, frequency and intensity of punishment, targeting, retaliation/antisocial punishment.
- Proxies: frequency of full cooperation, punishment usage frequency, norm enforcement, exclusion, partner choice, coalition formation, norm perceptions, beliefs, and emotions.

**Distinguishing point:** Many studies explicitly contrast behavioral (non-payoff) and efficiency (payoff) outcomes, cautioning that behaviorally sustained high cooperation may not always map to higher efficiency due to punishment costs or misdirected sanctions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### 1. **Punishment Increases Efficiency—But Only Under Key Conditions**
- In canonical repeated PGGs and close CPR variants, **enabling peer or mild central punishment typically increases group efficiency** compared to a no-punishment baseline, particularly when (a) punishment is effective (high impact/cost), (b) is not overly frequent, and (c) is targeted at low contributors/defectors (Fehr et al., 2002; Sefton et al., 2007; Gürerk et al., 2018; Kamei, 2024; Arechar et al., 2018).
- **The realized efficiency gain is moderated by punishment cost, impact ratio, and group heterogeneity.** When punishment is cheap and effective, the gain is large; when it is costly or mis-targeted (antisocial punishment, retaliation feuds), efficiency can decline.

### 2. **Punishment Cost, Technology, and Network Structure Are Critical**
- The **cost-to-impact ratio is a strong moderator**: higher impact per unit cost yields higher net efficiency from punishment (Sefton et al., 2007; Gürerk, 2018; Nikiforakis et al., 2010).
- **Network structure and punishment coverage matter**: complete/symmetric networks or centralized enforcement support higher efficiency; incomplete/asymmetric networks can reduce or eliminate efficiency gains from punishment (Leibbrandt et al., 2015; Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Fatas et al., 2020).
- **Group size and repeated interaction**: Larger group size can reduce cooperation unless punishment is highly effective or centrally coordinated (Levine & Modica, 2016; Sui et al., 2017).

### 3. **Punishment Can Fail to Improve Efficiency**
- When **punishment is frequent, overly costly, misdirected (antisocial), or allows for retaliation/feuds**, costs can outweigh the cooperation gain, reducing efficiency below the no-punishment baseline (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Luini, 2009; Bruhin et al., 2020; Nikiforakis & Engelmann, 2011).
- **Group heterogeneity** (in endowments, productivity, or group composition) often undermines punishment effectiveness and can introduce efficiency losses if norms are ambiguous, information incomplete, or punishment is misapplied (Kingsley, 2016; Reuben & Riedl, 2009; Mantilla et al., 2021).
- **In some field or high-baseline environments**, adding punishment can decrease efficiency by creating coordination failures or raising direct costs where intrinsic cooperation or informal norms already sustain high efficiency (Javaid & Falk, 2015).

### 4. **Exclusion, Ostracism, and Alternative Sanctioning Can Substitute for Peer Punishment**
- **Costless or redistributive sanctions** (e.g., ostracism, exclusion, feedback) can sometimes increase efficiency as much or more than costly peer punishment, especially when group size is moderate and norm clarity is high (Maier-Rigaud et al., 2010; Dugar, 2013).
- **Centralized or institutional punishment can outperform decentralized/peer punishment** if group coordination is a challenge or antisocial punishment is a risk (Ostrom, 2006; Casari & Plott, 2003).

### 5. **Communication, Reputation, and Social Learning Moderate the Effect**
- **Communication (chat), endogenous institution formation, and social history** often amplify the efficiency gains from punishment by reducing antisocial punishment and supporting mutual expectations of compliance (Bochet et al., 2006; Gürerk, 2013).
- In contrast, in some settings, **communication alone can match or exceed efficiency gains from punishment** without incurring direct punishment costs.

### 6. **Control Efficiency (Efficiency without Punishment) Is a Consistent Baseline, But Not the Sole Determinant**
- The *higher the baseline control efficiency*, the *smaller* the potential absolute gain from enabling punishment (diminishing returns).
- However, **high control efficiency does not preclude punishment from reducing efficiency** if added punishment costs outweigh gains, or if coordination is undermined (Botelho et al., 2022; Javaid & Falk, 2015).

### 7. **Cultural/Contextual Moderators and Individual Factors**
- **Cultural context, group composition (e.g., in-group/out-group, ethnicity), and subject pool** (students vs. general population) can robustly moderate both the frequency of antisocial punishment and the effect of punishment on efficiency (Bruhin et al., 2020; Mantilla et al., 2021; Drouvelis et al., 2021; Bortolotti et al., 2015).
- **Emotional context (anger/happiness) and cognitive manipulation** can switch the sign of punishment's effect on efficiency (Lee & Min, 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **When predicting treatment efficiency in repeated linear PGG-like environments:** **If punishment is peer-enabled, normatively targeted, not too costly, and control efficiency is below maximum, predict a positive increase in efficiency relative to control.** Use direct quantitative evidence from similar parameterizations where available (e.g., 4-5 players, 10-20 rounds, MPCR 0.4–0.5, punishment cost/impact 1:3) (Fehr et al., 2002; Arechar et al., 2018; Sefton et al., 2007).
- **Quantitative impact:** The best-studied parameter regimes typically show efficiency increasing from 40–60% of optimum (control) to 70–90+% (punishment-enabled) in lab PGGs with well-calibrated punishment. Deviations are common when antisocial punishment is prevalent, sanctioning is costly or weak, or network structure is limiting.
- **Key dimension moderators:**  
  - **Punishment cost**: Higher cost weakens the efficiency gain, and may turn it negative if not offset by stronger norm enforcement.
  - **Punishment technology**: Higher impact per unit cost strengthens efficiency gain; non-monetary/costless punishment can yield even greater net gains.
  - **Network/information structure:** Complete networks, high transparency, and visibility of actions/punishments increase the effectiveness and efficiency effect; incomplete networks or hidden actions/punishers reduce or reverse the gains.
  - **Group size, heterogeneity, and partner stability:** Efficiency gains shrink as group size increases, or as heterogeneity increases (unless punishment is clearly norm-targeted).
  - **Communication/chat:** When enabled, chat often produces larger efficiency gains than punishment alone, and chat + punishment is generally not additive—the effect plateaus at the higher of the two.
  - **Control efficiency:** When control efficiency is near maximum, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency due to additional deadweight costs, especially if punishment is used regardless of actual free-riding.
  - **Cultural/social context:** The presence of antisocial punishment or weak norm salience can reduce or reverse the efficiency effect of punishment.
- **Special attention:** Do **not** infer efficiency gains from non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates) without direct evidence that payoff increases outweigh sanction costs. Direct payoff reporting is essential.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

The **most empirically-supported dimensions** (directly/indirectly informed):
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return), `punishment_cost`, and `punishment_tech` (cost-to-impact), with numerous parameterizations explicitly tested.
- `all_or_nothing` (binary vs. continuous contribution)—both forms well represented.
- `chat` (communication)—present in many; its effect as a moderator is well-documented.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`—explicit in several studies toggling punishment and reward separately and jointly.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds` (feedback about others' actions and round structure)—often specified and tested for moderating effects.
- `show_punishment_id` (punishment anonymity/visibility)—varied in key studies as a moderator of efficiency effects.
- **Variants on punishment institution/architecture**: endogenous institution formation, third-party punishment, exclusion/ostracism, group/norm voting, antisocial punishment, and hybrid institutions.

**Dimensions only contextually or sparsely addressed (missing/rare):**
- `default_contrib` (opt-in/opt-out contribution framing)—seldom explicit.
- Fine variations on `punishment_tech` beyond cost-to-impact (e.g., minimum group size for punishment, consensus/collective rules) are represented in some papers, but not all settings.
- **Rare or missing:** Direct tests of multi-level, dynamic, or real-world settings where repeated interaction and punishment institution co-evolve outside lab contexts.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Boundary conditions:** The net benefit of punishment for efficiency is **not universal**—it depends critically on the institutional design, cost-effectiveness, and the behavioral ecology (prevalence of antisocial punishment, cultural norm strength, baseline compliance).
- **Over-representation of student samples and laboratory settings** relative to diverse global environments and field/real-world contexts, potentially limiting external validity.
- Many studies report **conditional average effects**, but the range of observed group outcomes can be wide (group-level heterogeneity, outliers, instability).
- **Control (no-punishment) efficiency is often set artificially low in lab PGGs**; in naturally high-efficiency settings, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency if punishment is overused.
- **Design dimension interactions are not always fully factorially mapped**—e.g., how network asymmetry, cost structure, and group composition jointly affect the marginal efficiency of punishment.
- A subset of studies uses adjacent or non-PGG designs (PD, trust/investment games, CPR games), which can limit transferability for precise quantitative prediction.
- **Many close/adjacent and theory papers focus on cooperation rates rather than efficiency or payoff**, which can lead to misinference if used uncritically.
- **Long-term dynamics and removal of punishment mechanisms**: Efficiency gains may disappear or reverse if punishment is removed partway (legacy and learning effects are sometimes negative).
- **Potential collinearity/confounding between communication and punishment:** When both are enabled, it is difficult to parse the unique marginal effect of each.

---

Overall, the literature provides a strong empirical and theoretical basis for predicting the direction and—when careful dimension-matching is possible—the approximate magnitude of peer punishment’s effect on PGG efficiency in canonical lab designs. Caution is warranted when generalizing to new populations, highly heterogeneous environments, or games with unconventional punishment technologies or institutional contexts, and behavioral outcomes must be clearly distinguished from efficiency effects.
