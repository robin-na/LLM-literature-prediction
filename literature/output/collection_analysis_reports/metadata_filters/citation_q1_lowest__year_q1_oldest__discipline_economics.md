# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of 20 studies, including a mix of experimental/empirical lab experiments (e.g., Fehr et al., 2010; Fischer & Nicklisch, 2007; McEvoy, 2012; Klempt, 2012; Bruttel & Eisenkopf, 2012) and a substantial proportion of theory/modeling papers (e.g., Janus & Lim, 2009; Matsushima, 2012; Evans & Thomas, 2001). The empirical papers are mainly laboratory experiments with small to medium group sizes, tightly controlled parameters, and short horizons. Theoretical papers cover a range of social dilemma games, including repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma and variants, with proofs on the sustainment of cooperation and efficiency under different punishment and incentive structures. Some studies do not report new primary data but contextualize or mention public goods games with punishment (e.g., Nikiforakis, 2010), and a few are only tangentially related or focus on game-theoretic analogues or social context outside the main prediction task. 

Overall, the evidence base is **relatively broad in its theoretical coverage of punishment and efficiency in social dilemmas**, but is **narrow in empirical coverage of standard public goods game (PGG) environments manipulating punishment and directly reporting efficiency or group payoff outcomes**.

# 2) Task Relevance

### pgg_or_variant:
- **Exact**: Only a few papers (Fehr et al., 2010; Fischer & Nicklisch, 2007) study PGGs or very close variants directly.
- **Close/Adjacent**: Many theory papers focus on repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma or other social dilemmas with clear parallels to PGGs (e.g., Janus & Lim, 2009; Matsushima, 2012; Evans & Thomas, 2001), but are not strictly PGGs.
- **None/Weak**: Several papers have only tangential or no relevance (e.g., Vyrastekova et al., 2011; Nikiforakis, 2010).

### punishment_or_sanctions:
- **Exact**: Fehr et al. (2010), McEvoy (2012), and multiple theory papers model explicit punishment or sanctioning.
- **Adjacent/Weak**: Some studies consider contract mechanisms or exclusion as analogues to punishment (Bruttel & Eisenkopf, 2012; Ishida, 2009). Others address reward, third-party sanctioning, or monitoring (Kusakawa et al., 2012).
- **None**: Some entries (e.g., Rosenbaum et al., 2012) do not include punishment as a variable.

### efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:
- **Exact/Close**: Several theoretical papers make efficiency or group payoff the main outcome (Fehr et al., 2010; Janus & Lim, 2009; Evans & Thomas, 2001; Heller & Sieberg, 2008).
- **Adjacent/Weak**: Some report on contribution or cooperation rates, not efficiency (McEvoy, 2012; Klempt, 2012; Kusakawa et al., 2012).
- **None**: A subset of studies report only behavioral measures, or do not analyze outcomes (Nikiforakis, 2010; Vyrastekova et al., 2011).

**In summary:** Only a small core of the literature is **directly and exactly relevant to the prediction task** of estimating efficiency changes in PGGs with/without peer punishment. Most of the literature provides extrapolative or theoretical relevance via adjacent social dilemmas and theoretical mechanism arguments.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**:
    - *Directly measured*: Efficiency (group payoff/relative to cooperative optimum), total earnings, welfare, or surplus (Fehr et al., 2010; Janus & Lim, 2009; Matsushima, 2012; Bruttel & Eisenkopf, 2012; Heller & Sieberg, 2008).
    - *Indirect/adjacent*: Occasional reporting of average group or coalition earnings, or predictions about attainable payoff profiles (Aramendia, 2006; Ishida, 2009).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
    - Contribution rates, cooperation rates, punishment frequencies, norm compliance, retaliation, and motives for punishment are commonly reported (McEvoy, 2012; Klempt, 2012; Kusakawa et al., 2012).
    - In several theory papers, outcomes are limited to equilibrium characterizations or conditions for cooperation, not quantitative payoffs (Jones, 1999; Annen, 2011).
- **Outcomes not measured or reported**:
    - Some papers discuss only theoretical concepts or analogies without empirical measurement (Wittman, 2005; Aruka & Akiyama, 2011, 2009).

**Summary**: Direct payoff-based outcomes, critically required for the target prediction task, are distinctly less frequently measured than contribution rates and other behavioral variables.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Empirical evidence (PGG and close variants):**
    - *Short PGGs*: Enabling peer punishment typically **reduces efficiency** due to the direct cost of punishing outweighing the gain from increased cooperation; average group payoffs decrease (Fehr et al., 2010). This is most robust in standard 10-round or shorter lab settings.
    - *Longer PGGs*: Over many rounds (e.g., 50+), and especially when punishment is paired with reputation or endogenous institution choice, **punishment can increase efficiency** to or above control levels (Fehr et al., 2010; Annen, 2011 [theory]).
    - *Coalition/voluntary punishment*: Voluntary, uncommitted punishment mechanisms do **not reliably increase contributions or efficiency**. Only when credible punishment is available (with pre-commitment or coalition enforcement) do cooperation and (likely) efficiency increase (McEvoy, 2012).
    - *Voting and contracts*: Institutional rules (e.g., contracts, voting rules) affect efficiency, but outcomes depend on design (Fischer & Nicklisch, 2007; Bruttel & Eisenkopf, 2012). These mechanisms may substitute or interact with punishment.

- **Theoretical results (mostly adjacent to PGGs):**
    - *Punishment enables efficient equilibria* in repeated social dilemmas, given sufficient punishment severity, patience, and transparency (Janus & Lim, 2009; Matsushima, 2012; Evans & Thomas, 2001).
    - *Effect depends on design*: Efficiency gains require credible and severe (but not necessarily draconian) punishment that is enforceable, with group size and endogeneity of information being critical factors (Aramendia, 2006; Annen, 2011).
    - *Punishment cost moderates effect*: If the cost of punishing is too high, or if there are deadweight losses, efficiency may decline when punishment is enabled (Fehr et al., 2010; Heller & Sieberg, 2008; Wittman, 2005).

- **Dissent, ambiguity, and moderators:**
    - Experimental and theoretical evidence agree that the **effect of punishment on efficiency is highly conditional**. Efficiency appears to drop in short games with costly punishment but may rise in long or institution-rich settings, or with low punishment cost.
    - The presence of additional mechanisms (reputation, contracts, voting, or rewards) and specifics of the sanctioning technology (who can punish, how much, at what cost, and with what transparency) are significant moderators.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Synthesizing across the paper set, **the most evidence-backed guidance for predicting efficiency with punishment enabled** in PGG-like settings is as follows:

- **Short time horizon (≤10 rounds):** Expect efficiency to **decrease** when enabling punishment, compared to control, due to direct costs of punishment generally outweighing the cooperation gains (Fehr et al., 2010).
- **Long time horizon (many rounds):** Efficiency may **increase**, especially if punishment is combined with reputation or the game includes endogenous institution choice (Fehr et al., 2010; Janus & Lim, 2009; Annen, 2011).
- **Effect of design dimensions:**
    - *Player count*: Larger groups require different punishment/information structures to sustain efficiency (Annen, 2011); some theoretical results are robust to group size with exogenous experience sharing.
    - *Punishment cost*: Lower punishment cost increases the likelihood of efficiency gains; high-cost punishment tends to reduce efficiency (Fehr et al., 2010; Heller & Sieberg, 2008).
    - *Punishment technology*: Credibility, severity, and enforceability of punishment strongly condition the effect—for mild/voluntary/uncommitted punishment, efficiency gains are unlikely (McEvoy, 2012; Evans & Thomas, 2001).
    - *Information transparency*: Reputation mechanisms and summary feedback can convert the efficiency impact of punishment from negative to positive under the right conditions (Fehr et al., 2010).
    - *Presence of reward*: While theoretical support exists for reward as a substitute or complement (Janus & Lim, 2009), empirical findings in this set are sparse.
    - *Chat/communication*: Rarely isolates as a moderator; more work needed.
- **General prediction logic**: Use the observed efficiency in control (no-punishment) as a baseline, and adjust based on game design—predicted efficiency with punishment should **decrease** in typical short, costly-punishment lab games, and **possibly increase** in longer, feedback-rich, or institutionally flexible designs.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed by evidence:**
- `player_count`: Directly included and discussed; effects of group size highlighted especially in theory (Annen, 2011; Fehr et al., 2010).
- `num_rounds`: Consistently identified as a key moderator in both empirical and theoretical findings (Fehr et al., 2010; Matsushima, 2012).
- `punishment_cost`: Direct empirical and theoretical attention given to effects; high relevance (Fehr et al., 2010; Heller & Sieberg, 2008).
- `mpcr` (Marginal per-capita return): Frequently included, often as part of the payoff structure determining incentives; discussed in relation to sustaining cooperation (Fehr et al., 2010; Janus & Lim, 2009).
- `all_or_nothing`: Included in several studies, though its interaction with punishment and efficiency is less frequently isolated.
- `punishment_tech`: The modality (who can punish, how severe, with what triggers) is a recurring focus.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Information availability and institutional transparency are sometimes discussed, especially for reputation and endogenous choice effects.

**Indirectly or contextually informed:**
- `chat`: Discussed in some experiments but not as a principal moderator for efficiency.
- `default_contrib`: Framing (opt-in/opt-out) occasionally referenced but not systematically analyzed regarding punishment effects.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: These dimensions are occasionally discussed in theory as alternatives or complements to punishment, but empirical evidence is limited in this set.

**Effectively missing:**
- Several dimensions, notably *chat*, *default_contrib*, *reward_tech*, and *reward_cost*, are not systematically varied or analyzed in relation to efficiency effects of punishment in this evidence set.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical scarcity for core setting**: Only a small subset of papers experimentally measure the direct effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in standard PGGs. Most other evidence is theoretical or uses adjacent games or outcome variables.
- **Payoff vs. behavioral outcomes**: Many reported outcomes are contribution rates, cooperation frequencies, or behavioral responses. **Direct efficiency measurements are less common**; translation from behavioral change to efficiency is often assumed, not shown.
- **Generality and extrapolation from adjacent games**: Theoretical results mainly use repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma or other social dilemmas, which have structural similarities to but are not identical with PGGs. Their direct quantitative applicability is therefore limited and must be interpreted with caution.
- **Limited manipulation of full design space**: Not all 14 relevant design dimensions are varied or systematically analyzed, creating significant evidence blind spots for certain contexts (e.g., effects of communication, default contribution frame, or reward mechanisms alongside punishment).
- **Ambiguity and conditionality**: Where findings are most directly relevant (e.g., Fehr et al., 2010), **the direction and magnitude of the efficiency effect of punishment are highly conditional** on specific game design parameters (number of rounds, punishment cost, feedback). Generalizing across all possible PGG variants is methodologically unsafe.
- **Absence of large-N, field, or high-external validity studies**: The empirical evidence is mainly based on laboratory studies with small groups and simplified settings; scalability and field applicability are untested.
- **Sparse data on interaction effects**: Few, if any, studies systematically examine interactions between key design variables (e.g., whether low punishment cost effects depend on player count or the presence of reward).

**Summary:** The literature provides valuable theoretical and some empirical guidance on how design dimensions, especially time horizon and punishment cost, moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like tasks. However, **significant caution is warranted** when making detailed predictions for untested combinations of design variables or for environments departing from the constrained lab setting.
