# 1) Evidence Base

The supplied paper set offers a broad, multi-disciplinary literature base, with a strong mix of both empirical (lab, field, and observational studies) and theoretical (evolutionary, computational, mechanistic) papers. The coverage includes numerous direct tests of public goods games (PGGs) and their close variants, as well as adjacent designs (e.g., common-pool resource games, step-level games, dyadic donation and social dilemma games). Several high-impact lab experiments directly manipulate punishment presence/cost in classic PGGs (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017), while other papers investigate the effect of punishment using simulations or models with explicit efficiency or group payoff outcomes (e.g., Adami et al., 2016; Sasaki & Uchida, 2013). Some papers focus on the interaction of punishment with other mechanisms such as reward, exclusion, institutional design, information, network structure, or group composition. The set also includes a substantial fraction of studies where the primary outcomes are behavioral (cooperation/contribution rates, punishment frequency) rather than payoff-based, and a significant number of papers discuss psychological, neural, or social mechanisms underlying punishment but without measuring group efficiency.

Overall, the evidence base is broad and rich for many core design dimensions, with multiple papers offering direct or strong indirect evidence on the effect of punishment on efficiency. However, for some dimensions (e.g., communication/chat, visibility of punishment identity, detailed information displays), coverage is limited. Certain contextual moderators (such as corruption, anti-social/institutional features, group norms) are explored in depth by a small subset of papers.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### Empirical and Theoretical Convergence:
- **Enabling peer punishment in standard PGGs typically increases efficiency**, often dramatically (e.g., nearly reaching the social optimum) when punishment is sufficiently costly to deter defection but not so costly as to undermine group payoffs (Gächter et al., 2017; Adami et al., 2016; Sasaki & Uchida, 2013).
- **Social exclusion and ostracism** (a form of sanctioning) can be *more robust and efficient* than costly punishment alone, especially when exclusion is low-cost and effective (Sasaki & Uchida, 2013; Sääksvuori, 2014).
- **Institutional features** (centralized punishment, election of sanction rules) and *alignment with local norms* strongly moderate the punishment effect. Institutional corruption or opportunities for bribery can reverse or undermine the positive effect of punishment on efficiency (Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Vollan et al., 2013).
- **Cost-effectiveness of punishment** is a crucial moderator: if the fee-to-fine ratio (punishment cost per impact) is unfavorable, punishment may fail to increase efficiency or may even lower it (Rand & Nowak, 2013; Okada et al., 2015; Vukov et al., 2013 [adjacent]).
- **Group size and structure**: Punishment’s positive effect on efficiency is *stronger in small groups* and in settings with low mobility/repeated interactions; effectiveness diminishes in large groups without supporting institutional mechanisms (Powers & Lehmann, 2017; Wubs et al., 2016; Roos et al., 2014).
- **Contextual dependence**: The magnitude (or even the sign) of efficiency change upon enabling punishment can reverse depending on bribery, corruption, group norms, opportunity for antisocial punishment, or whether reputation and information mechanisms are available (Milinski, 2016; Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Rand & Nowak, 2013).

### Dissent, Ambiguity, Negative or Mixed Effects:
- **Costly punishment can sometimes reduce net efficiency,** especially due to misuse (antisocial punishment, non-cooperative norms, retaliation; e.g., Rand & Nowak, 2013; de Melo & Piaggio, 2015).
- **Anti-social or misaligned reward/punishment mechanisms** (e.g., anti-social rewarding, poorly designed institutions) can erode the expected gains or even make punishment detrimental (dos Santos, 2015; Vollan et al., 2013).
- **Behavioral mechanisms** (contribution rates, norm compliance) do not always translate to increased efficiency when punishment is used; costs can offset gains from improved cooperation.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

## Direct Evidence
- **Standard PGGs with moderate group size (4–6 players), moderate rounds (10–30), linear MPCR, and efficient peer punishment (cost/impact < 0.5):** Enabling punishment is expected to substantially increase group efficiency relative to baseline/control, often near the social optimum, unless baseline cooperation is already extremely high (Gächter et al., 2017; Adami et al., 2016).
- **Punishment cost/effectiveness**: Lower cost and/or greater effectiveness of punishment (higher fine per unit cost) yield greater efficiency gains (Sasaki & Uchida, 2013; Okada et al., 2015).
- **Institutional vs peer punishment**: Centralized/institutional punishment can outperform or underperform peer punishment depending on the presence of corruption opportunities, method of rule selection, and alignment with local norms (Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Vollan et al., 2013).
- **Social exclusion (ostracism)**: If available and effective, can increase efficiency robustly, even where costly punishment does not (Sasaki & Uchida, 2013; Sääksvuori, 2014).
- **Absence of punishment**: Efficiency tends to decay over repeated rounds, often substantially below social optimum (Burton-Chellew et al., 2015).

## Indirect/contextual Guidance
- **Group size**, **network structure**, and **opportunity for communication** can all moderate punishment effects. Punishment is more effective at increasing efficiency in small groups, stable networks, or with opportunities for repeated interaction; less so in large, fluid, or anonymous settings unless specific institutional supports are in place (Powers & Lehmann, 2017; Roos et al., 2014).
- **Transparency, visibility, and reputation systems** can amplify efficiency gains from punishment by reducing misuse and increasing legitimacy (Milinski, 2016).
- **Risk of antisocial punishment or misapplication**: High levels of antisocial punishment or lack of consensus norms can reduce or negate efficiency benefits (Rand & Nowak, 2013; de Melo & Piaggio, 2015).
- **Reward mechanisms**: In games allowing both punishment and reward, reward generally enhances cooperation more efficiently (less destroyal of value), though not always. Presence/adaptability of reward structures should be noted (Rand & Nowak, 2013; dos Santos, 2015).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed (core empirical/theoretical focus in papers):**
    - `player_count`: Extensive, with empirical and theoretical analyses on group size and its modulating effect on punishment efficiency.
    - `num_rounds`: Almost ubiquitously reported and manipulated.
    - `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return): Commonly varied, directly discussed as a determinant of efficiency and threshold for cooperation under punishment.
    - `punishment_exists`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Central to almost all relevant papers.
    - `all_or_nothing`: Reported (continuous vs binary contributions) in most empirical designs.
    - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Studied, especially in theory papers exploring combined systems or anti-social rewards.
- **Indirectly informed:**
    - `show_other_summaries`: Sometimes discussed in connection to information transmission, especially in institutional or transparency designs.
    - `show_n_rounds`: Echoed in round structure manipulations and in studies of repeated vs one-shot games.
    - `default_contrib`: Infrequently addressed; only contextually related where design choice affects contribution salience.
    - `chat`: Explored in some empirical studies, especially around communication/coordination, but less commonly in direct efficiency measurement.
    - `show_punishment_id`: Discussed in a minority of studies—mainly those focusing on reputation/social consequences.

- **Only contextually discussed or effectively missing:**
    - Specific reporting or systematic variation of `show_punishment_id` and detailed info displays.
    - The framing or default option for contributions (`default_contrib`) is rarely systematically varied.
    - Some design features (e.g., the exact form of multipliers or risk structure in threshold and all-or-nothing variations) are given contextually, not systematically analyzed across multiple studies.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Not all papers measure efficiency/group payoff directly**; many rely on behavioral proxies. Where inferences are necessary, the relationship to efficiency should be treated as uncertain and possibly design-dependent.
- **Real-world complexity underrepresented**: Most findings are from simplified, highly controlled lab environments with standard PGG rules; real-world or field contexts may show weaker, more variable, or even reversed efficiency effects due to corruption, conflicting norms, or institutional ambiguity.
- **Sparse evidence for some design dimensions** (e.g., visibility of punishment, explicit contribution defaults, detailed feedback structures, communication). Prediction for designs manipulating these features must rely on theory or limited empirical findings.
- **Ambiguity and conflicting results exist**, especially concerning the effect of punishment in large groups, institutional settings with corruption opportunities, and environments where antisocial punishment is possible.
- **Lack of comprehensive coverage for interactive effects**: Only a few papers systematically explore interactions between design dimensions (e.g., punishment × group size × communication).
- **Few quantitative meta-analyses**: Most synthesis is qualitative or mechanistic; quantitative prediction (e.g., precise efficiency estimates) must be extrapolated with caution.
- **Generalizability** to settings with non-standard population structure, extreme parameter values, or evolutionary/long-run settings is limited.

---

# Summary

The literature provides strong and converging evidence that enabling efficient, well-designed peer punishment in standard PGGs with small to moderate groups, moderate rounds, and standard parameters tends to increase efficiency substantially, often restoring it toward the social optimum. The effect is robust across provision and maintenance variants, but is sharply moderated by punishment cost-effectiveness, institutional features (including opportunities for corruption or exclusion), and the alignment of formal sanctions with local norms. When punishment is costly or susceptible to antisocial use, efficiency gains can be greatly reduced or reversed. The prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design features is best supported when mapping to lab-like public goods games with direct payoff measurement; extrapolation to more complex or real-world settings should account for institutional, social, and contextual moderators using theoretical guidance where empirical data are sparse.
