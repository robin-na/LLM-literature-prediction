# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base consists of a large and relatively broad set of experimental studies (primarily laboratory experiments, with several field experiments and some framed or artefactual field experiments) focusing on public goods games (PGGs) and close variants. Most papers are empirical, with only a few containing primarily theoretical or conceptual arguments. The bulk of the literature evaluates interventions including punishment (peer, centralized, exogenous, endogenous, probabilistic, redistributive, ostracism, etc.), often cross-cut with other institutions such as reward, communication, information feedback, heterogeneity, and group composition.

The literature provides a rich empirical basis for evaluating the impact of punishment on efficiency—understood as the ratio of realized group payoff to the full-cooperation benchmark—across a wide variety of experimental design dimensions. The evidence is especially strong and detailed in standard linear PGGs, but also covers common-pool resource (CPR) variants, asymmetric roles, and dynamic or networked environments.

# 2) Task Relevance

Relevance is assessed on three target dimensions:

**a) pgg_or_variant:**
- The majority of papers are labeled `exact` or `close`, focusing directly on standard linear PGGs or very close CPR/institutional analogs (e.g., resource extraction, threshold games). A substantial minority are `adjacent`—e.g., repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, trust games, allocation/market games—which, while sharing social dilemma properties, are less structurally similar.
- Overall, the evidence set is highly relevant for PGG-like environments, with good coverage of canonical lab PGGs.

**b) punishment_or_sanctions:**
- Nearly all studies include a punishment or sanction treatment, with many labeled `exact` (classic costly punishment, peer or centralized), and many close or adjacent (redistributive punishment, ostracism, ex post exclusion, threshold enforcement, taxation as sanction, group allocation mechanisms).
- Some studies focus solely on reward, communication, or other non-punitive mechanisms—these are labeled `none` or `adjacent` and are not directly informative for punishment effects.

**c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
- A significant share of papers report efficiency or a directly related group payoff measure as their primary outcome (`exact`), or as a close/adjacent outcome (e.g., welfare, surplus, group earnings).
- However, many studies—especially those focused on behavioral mechanisms—report only contributions, cooperation rates, or punishment behavior, making their relevance to our outcome only `adjacent` or `weak`.
- Overall, direct efficiency evidence is abundant, but for some design dimensions, the literature is richer in behavioral proxies than in explicit efficiency metrics.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related (efficiency, group payoff, earnings, welfare, surplus):**
- Many studies report group efficiency as a primary outcome, giving the realized fraction of maximum possible group payoff.
- Group payoff is often reported in experimental tables, sometimes alongside individual payoffs and inequality/variance metrics.
- Surplus, net profit, welfare, and similar outcome variables are also used, all mapping closely to efficiency under the group definition.

**Non-payoff behavioral (contribution rate, cooperation, punishment assigned):**
- Virtually all studies report average group contribution or cooperation rates, as well as rates/amounts of punishment or reward assigned, targeting, and antisocial punishment.
- Measures of norm compliance, conditional cooperation, and institutional preference are common.
- Some studies focus only on these behavioral outcomes and do not report efficiency, thus limiting their direct relevance for our prediction task.

**Distinction:**
- Importantly, several papers note explicitly that increased contributions from punishment do not always increase efficiency, particularly when punishment costs are high or punishment is mistargeted or misapplied.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## a) Punishment Often Increases Contributions, But Efficiency Effects Are Conditional

- **In standard linear PGGs:**
    - Punishment consistently increases cooperation/contribution rates relative to no-punishment controls (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Page et al., 2013; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).
    - However, it frequently reduces or fails to increase efficiency compared to controls, due to the direct and indirect costs of punishment (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Colombier et al., 2011; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016).
    - Efficiency gains are contingent on: punishment technology (cost/effect ratio), anti-social punishment prevalence, targeting accuracy, group composition, legitimacy, observability, emotional and informational environment (Page et al., 2013; Mantilla et al., 2021; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Lee & Min, 2021; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).

- **Centralized punishment and formal sanctioning:**
    - When punishment is centralized, group efficiency is more likely (Castillo et al., 2021; Angelovski et al., 2018; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019), but details like sanctioner incentive (fixed vs. variable payoff) and legitimacy affect the magnitude and even direction of the effect.
    - Democratic or majority-voting mechanisms tend to yield higher efficiency than arbitrary or dictator punishment, largely by reducing anti-social punishment (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).

- **Punishment in variants and real-world environments:**
    - CPR and threshold games often show positive efficiency effects from well-targeted, externally imposed punishment (Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Ntuli et al., 2023), but not always from peer punishment—especially where anti-social punishment or heterogeneity in ability/capacity exists (Vollan et al., 2019; De Geest & Kingsley, 2019; Robbett, 2016).

- **Redistributive/Costless/Reward-Linked Punishment:**
    - Design variation such as redistributing fines as rewards, or using contribution-contingent payoff allocation, greatly increases the efficiency effect of punishment, by eliminating waste or disincentivizing anti-social punishment (Page et al., 2013; Karakostas et al., 2023).

## b) Moderators and Boundary Conditions

Several key design dimensions and contextual factors moderate the punishment-efficiency relationship:

- **Player count**: Large groups tend to experience greater legitimacy/identifiability challenges (Zheng & Nie, 2013) and potentially declining positive effects of punishment.
- **Number of rounds**: Longer games may increase opportunity for learning or reputation effects, which can enhance (Fatas et al., 2020) or undermine (Lowen & Schmitt, 2013) punishment's positive efficiency impact.
- **Chat/communication**: Strongly moderates efficiency, often substituting for punishment or augmenting its effectiveness, especially in fostering shared standards and reducing misdirected punishment (Kamei, 2019; Koch et al., 2021).
- **Punishment cost and technology**: High costs tend to reduce or reverse efficiency gains; higher effectiveness/predictability/redistribution increases positive effects (Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Kingsley & Brown, 2016; Wang & Qin, 2015; Khadjavi et al., 2017).
- **Information and observability**: Incomplete or noisy information about contributions/endowments/past actions reduces targeting accuracy and thus the efficiency effect (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021, 2019; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Gallo et al., 2022).
- **Norms and agreements**: The presence of a clear, ambitious, or widely agreed-upon group norm or explicit agreement sharpens the positive efficiency effect of punishment (Dannenberg, 2016), while ambiguous or minimum-only norms can crowd out the effect.
- **Group composition and heterogeneity**: Mixed groups (e.g., by ethnicity or ability) can reduce effectiveness of punishment via weakened norm enforcement or in-group favoritism (Mantilla et al., 2021; Waichman, 2020; Angelovski et al., 2018).

## c) Non-payoff outcomes do not guarantee efficiency change

- Many studies caution against inferring efficiency effects from behavioral measures (contributions, compliance), especially under typical punishment regimes where the cost of punishment is high or its application is poorly targeted.
- In several studies, contributions increase but efficiency gains are canceled out by punishment costs or increased anti-social punishment, or even result in lower net group payoffs (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Colombier et al., 2011; Peng, 2022; Vollan et al., 2019).

## d) Endogenous mechanisms and voluntary provision of punishment

- When institutions for punishment are endogenously chosen (by vote), efficiency gains from punishment are amplified, but only when the institutional structure supports pro-social punishment and minimizes abuse or anti-social punishment (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019).
- The cost or barrier to acquiring punishment rights is a critical moderator: even small access costs for punishers can entirely erase efficiency gains by most group members opting out, leading to worse outcomes than control (Ramalingam et al., 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the evidence:

- **Do not assume a uniform efficiency gain from enabling peer punishment:** The direction and size of the effect depend on the interaction of multiple design dimensions and context.
- **Use control (no-punishment) efficiency as a baseline:** The efficiency of the control condition informs the available “headroom” for improvement, but the sign and size of the treatment effect are conditional.
- **Interrogate the design details:**
    - If punishment is costly, peer-administered, unregulated, or prone to anti-social use: expect little or no efficiency gain, or a possible loss, relative to the control.
    - If punishment is low-cost, highly effective, well-targeted (via transparent contributions/endowments), democratically instituted, or its cost is mitigated (e.g., through redistribution or reward linkage): expect a positive efficiency effect, size increasing with control group inefficiency.
    - Centralized, deterministic, or externally imposed punishment mechanisms tend to produce more reliable efficiency gains, provided costs are not excessive.
    - If large group size, significant heterogeneity, noise in monitoring, or known in-group bias: expect weaker or negative effects on efficiency.
    - If communication or clear pre-play agreement on a high standard is present along with punishment, expect a positive or strongly positive effect on efficiency as compared to control (Koch et al., 2021; Dannenberg, 2016).
- **Punishment technology and feedback matter:** If punishment is exogenous, immediate, saliently linked to norm violation, and large relative to gain from deviation, expect strong positive or at least non-negative marginal effects on efficiency (Wang & Qin, 2015; Khadjavi et al., 2017).
- **Consider institutional selection and legitimacy:** If institutions are chosen by group members and perceived as legitimate, even imperfect punishment can facilitate efficiency gains (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Zheng & Nie, 2013).
- **Reward or redistribution alternatives can outperform punishment:** If a reward or redistributive mechanism is available, it may achieve similar or larger efficiency gains with lower social cost (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Page et al., 2013).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**
- `player_count` (frequently varied and systematically studied)
- `num_rounds` (short and long games, presence of end-game effects)
- `mpcr` (marginal per capita return, a universal moderator)
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech` (extensively analyzed; key drivers of efficiency effect)
- `chat`/`communication` (strong moderator)
- `all_or_nothing` (studied in both continuous and binary/threshold games)
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds` (less frequently but systematically included in several studies)
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (reward institutions directly compared to punishment in a number of studies, especially for relative efficiency effects)
- `default_contrib` (occasionally varied; baseline framing sometimes matters)
- `punishment_exists` (the focal treatment in nearly all papers)

**Indirectly informed or contextually discussed:**
- `show_punishment_id` / observability of punisher identity (less common, studied primarily as a legitimacy/moderation factor)
- `show_other_summaries` (reporting of others’ outcomes/decisions and its effects)
- `default_contrib` (framing of opt-in/out contributions is occasionally discussed but not as a central moderator)

**Effectively missing/sparse:**
- Simultaneous experimental manipulation of multiple dimensions (e.g., chat and punishment, varying both cost and technology within the same design) is rare, limiting interaction effect estimation.
- Some environments, such as those with explicit dynamic or network structures, sequential organization, or field/real-world contexts, are underrepresented and typically less granular in their reporting of all 14 dimensions.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Context dependence:** Even in standard lab PGGs, context, subject population, and institutional framing can substantially moderate the efficiency effect of punishment (e.g., ethnic composition, emotional induction, group reputation, cultural background).
- **Strong non-linear interactions:** Many studies document non-linear or threshold effects (e.g., in group size, punishment cost, norm clarity), cautioning against additive or main effect models for prediction.
- **Indirect inference required for some design dimensions:** Several dimensions (e.g., punishment observability, endowment visibility, default contribution framing) are only variably reported, and their interactions with other design features are not universally present across studies.
- **Prevalence of non-payoff behavioral outcomes:** For a significant subset of studies, behavioral changes are measured but not their net payoff or welfare consequences, limiting the ability to draw direct efficiency predictions.
- **Boundary conditions:** Punishment's efficiency effects may reverse (become negative) when anti-social punishment is common, punishment is costly or widely used, group composition is heterogeneous, monitoring/targeting is poor, or when the environment is characterized by high baseline efficiency.
- **Field/real-world generalizability:** Even high-quality field experiments indicate that positive lab effects may not generalize to real-world settings due to differences in legitimacy, norms, or enforcement (Noussair et al., 2015; de Melo & Piaggio, 2015).
- **Sparse evidence for joint manipulation of all design features:** Most studies vary only a subset of the 14 key design dimensions; generalization to novel combinations requires caution.

---

In summary: The literature offers abundant empirical evidence (primarily from lab PGG studies) for the prediction task, with nuanced and often conditional findings regarding the effect of enabling peer punishment on group efficiency. The overall pattern is that punishment can enhance efficiency, but only when designed and implemented so that targeting is reliable, costs are low relative to gains, and anti-social punishment is constrained. In many realistic or complex environments, especially with ambiguous norms, heterogeneity, or weak monitoring, punishment may fail to improve or can even reduce efficiency relative to controls. Predictive models should be highly sensitive to these moderators, anchoring treatment predictions to control efficiency while conditioning strongly on the states of specific design dimensions.
