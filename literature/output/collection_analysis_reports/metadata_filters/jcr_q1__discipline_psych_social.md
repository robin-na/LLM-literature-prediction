# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set represents an exceptionally broad and diverse collection of academic work, including a substantial number of both empirical and theoretical papers: 367 sources, primarily lab experiments, field experiments, simulations, and reviews. Empirical studies dominate, with many classical and modern laboratory public goods games (PGGs) featuring a range of game design dimensions and sanctioning mechanisms (peer punishment, exclusion/ostracism, centralized punishment, third-party punishment, reward, reputation, and more).

Within the set, a substantial subset of empirical studies report group payoff or efficiency (the target outcome for prediction), though many others report only behavioral outcomes such as cooperation or contribution rates, punishment/reward frequencies, norm compliance, or psychological variables. Some high-quality meta-analyses and simulation/theory papers address mechanisms and moderators, but they do not always supply direct quantitative evidence for PGG efficiency.

From the perspective of the prediction task—estimating the effect of peer punishment on group efficiency—the evidence base is broad and deep, with many canonical PGG studies that manipulate punishment, control efficiency, and various design features. However, there is also considerable heterogeneity: some studies use adjacent games (CPR, PD, trust, dictator, team production), extend into field/ethnographic settings, or focus only on non-payoff outcomes. Not all prediction dimensions are equally or systematically covered.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. PGG or variant**
- **Relevance: exact (majority), close (significant minority), adjacent/weak/none (residual)**
- Many studies are exact matches to standard linear or binary PGGs; others use closely related social dilemmas (CPR, threshold games, team production) which are considered "close" variants.
- Some studies use adjacent but not equivalent designs (dictator games, networked games, evolutionary models), or report on real-life, field, or survey cooperation contexts (only "adjacent" or "weak" relevance).
- Overall, the evidence base is **strongly relevant** to PGG prediction, with many "exact" and "close" matches, and moderate-to-minor inclusion of less directly relevant environments.

**b. Punishment or sanctions**
- **Relevance: exact (many), close (many), adjacent/weak/none (several)**
- There is rich coverage of peer punishment (classic "punishment enabled" PGGs), and reasonable coverage of centralized, third-party, exclusion, and asymmetric punishment; however, other forms (restorative, indirect, "anti-social" punishment) and variously costed, public/private, and ambiguous interventions are also present.
- Some papers refer only to reward, reputation, commitment devices, or indirect norm enforcement (adjacent), while a nontrivial minority do not manipulate or analyze punishment.
- Overall, the paper set has **good to excellent** coverage of direct and indirect sanctioning, but evidence is not always systematic across mechanistic varieties.

**c. Efficiency or related payoff outcome**
- **Relevance: exact (substantial but not universal), close (numerous), adjacent/weak (prevalent), none (many)**
- A substantial subset measure efficiency as defined by total group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum; many more report group payoff, welfare, or surplus (close).
- However, much of the literature reports only on contribution rates, norm compliance, or psychological moderators, not on efficiency/payoff (adjacent or weak relevance to prediction).
- In sum, ample direct outcome data is present, but much evidence is still only **indirect, incomplete, or behavioral** rather than payoff-based.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**: group efficiency (total earnings as ratio of optimum), group payoff, welfare/surplus, total coins/dollars generated. These are typically available in most high-quality empirical PGG studies and some meta-analyses.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**: contribution/cooperation rates, punishment/reward frequency/types, norm compliance, psychological variables (anger, fairness, trust, legitimacy), ethanolographic/survey norms, sanctions/support ratings.
- A significant number of studies with high behavioral relevance do **not** report group efficiency directly; they must be interpreted cautiously when inferring prediction relevance.
- Reviews, theory, and mechanism papers often reference payoff effects in passing but provide only behavioral or qualitative guidance.
- **Clear distinction**: True treatment efficiency data is both critical and limited relative to the broader behavioral literature.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the most directly relevant empirical and theoretical studies, clear themes and areas of ambiguity emerge:

- **Peer punishment generally promotes higher cooperation and group efficiency in standard repeated PGGs, particularly when baseline efficiency is low (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Gintis et al., 2003; Gächter et al., 2017; Barclay, 2004; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Krügel & Maaser, 2025).**
    - The magnitude of the effect varies but is often sizable (e.g., +40% efficiency over no-punishment, though context is crucial).
    - Efficiency gains often emerge slowly, after early rounds of possibly excessive punishment costs, and are maximized when punishers are prosocial and/or punishment is normatively constrained.

- **Centralized or democratic punishment often achieves equal or greater efficiency than peer punishment, while incurring lower punishment costs (Nockur et al., 2021; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Eichenseer, 2023; Castillo & Hamman, 2021).**
    - Centralized/democratic punishment is less prone to over-punishment, coordination failure, and antisocial punishment.

- **Punishment can fail to improve, or even decrease, group efficiency if:**
    - Punishment costs are high relative to its deterrent power (Nockur et al., 2021; Kurzban et al., 2015).
    - Antisocial or misdirected (e.g., retaliatory) punishment is frequent (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Handfield et al., 2016).
    - Control efficiency is already high (Nair et al., 2018; Lubell & Scholz, 2001): punishment often adds deadweight cost without large gains.
    - Monitoring is weak or noisy: punishment is either weakly targeted or misapplied (Duell et al., 2024; Eisenkopf & Walter, 2022; ABREU et al., 1991).

- **Design dimensions strongly moderating the effect include:**
    - **Number of rounds (num_rounds):** Longer games allow punishment to have time to deter, reduce punishment costs as cooperation stabilizes, and thus greater efficiency improvements (Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Kroupa, 2014; Krügel & Maaser, 2025).
    - **MPCR:** Lower MPCR (i.e., more "difficult" games) generally see larger absolute gains from punishment, but at some threshold, punishment may only break even or fail (Farjam et al., 2015).
    - **Information and transparency (show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, transparency of endowments):** More transparency enhances targeting, reduces antisocial punishment, increases positive effect on efficiency (Hauser et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2025).
    - **Sanctioning institution type (punishment_tech, punishment_cost):** Lower cost, more credible, and more coordinated punishment systems (incl. exclusion/ostracism, "collective punishment with commitment") yield higher efficiency (Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Duell et al., 2024; Masclet, 2003).
    - **Presence of chat or communication:** Can dramatically increase efficiency, sometimes even outperforming punishment (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; OSTRUM et al., 1992).
    - **Reward mechanics:** Rewards can substitute or complement punishment, often with less deadweight loss (Stoop et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2022; Rand & Nowak, 2013).

- **Social context and group composition also moderate the effect:**
    - Peer punishment effectiveness is higher in high-trust societies and smaller groups (Balliet & Van Lange, 2013; Barclay, 2004).
    - Heterogeneous or ethnically diverse groups can either improve or degrade the efficiency of punishment, depending on local norms and traditions.

- **Mechanism papers reinforce and nuance these patterns:**
    - Punishment is most efficiency-enhancing when it is credible, sufficiently costly to deter but not excessive, and normatively or institutionally constrained (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Ye et al., 2011; Kroupa, 2014; Farjam et al., 2015).
    - In environments with high control efficiency, punishment often reduces efficiency (Nair et al., 2018).
    - Exclusion/ostracism and coordinated, norm-based punishment are particularly effective at reducing free-riding and can raise efficiency dramatically (Masclet, 2003; Sääksvuori, 2014).
    - In field-like or CPR settings, punishment must be combined with communication or endogenous institution choice for efficiency to be reliably improved (OSTROM et al., 1992; Chávez et al., 2018).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should this literature inform prediction of treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency as a function of design dimensions and control efficiency?**

- **If control efficiency is low** (group fails to approach the cooperative optimum in the absence of sanctions), then adding peer punishment, especially with moderate costs and high impact, is strongly predictive of a substantial efficiency gain, provided other conditions are favorable (empirical: Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Gächter et al., 2017; Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
- **If control efficiency is high** (cooperation is already sustained by other means—e.g., communication), punishment may add little or may even decrease efficiency due to excess cost (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; Nair et al., 2018; Lubell & Scholz, 2001).
- **Key moderators**:
    - **Sanctioning institution**: Peer punishment often increases contributions but can reduce efficiency via excessive or antisocial punishment (Nockur et al., 2021); centralized/democratic/collective punishment or ostracism leads to more reliably positive or neutral efficiency effects.
    - **Number of rounds**: In short games, punishment may only destroy value; in longer games, deadweight losses decline as cooperation becomes the norm.
    - **MPCR**: In "hard" games (low MPCR), only strong, well-designed punishment moves efficiency up; in "easy" games (high MPCR), punishment may not be required or could backfire.
    - **Information/transparency**: Making contributions, endowments, or punishment visible greatly increases the positive effect of punishment on efficiency, especially in unequal environments.
    - **Cost/effectiveness of punishment**: Strong, targeted punishment (high ratio of impact to cost) is more likely to yield efficiency gains; high-cost, low-effect punishment yields at best neutral, at worst negative efficiency change.
    - **Possibility of antisocial punishment or retaliation**: High rates of antisocial punishment (e.g., punishing high contributors, or leader corruption in IPGG/bribery games) undermine or reverse the efficiency gain of punishment.
    - **Role of chat or communication**: Communication alone may be as or more effective than punishment; combining both is especially powerful, but if chat is present, incremental gains from punishment are small.
    - **Group structure and heterogeneity**: Small, homogeneous groups benefit more; large, diverse, or low-trust groups may require institutionally coordinated punishment for efficiency gains.
    - **Correlation with non-payoff outcomes**: Caution is warranted—raising contribution rates or norm compliance does not always raise efficiency if punishment is excessive/uncoordinated.

- **General Prediction Strategy**:
    - Use **control efficiency** as a strong baseline: estimate the treatment effect as a (positive or negative) multiplier or increment, depending on moderators above.
    - Adjust upward if:
        - Punishment is centralized, democratic, or ostracism-based;
        - Chat/communication is absent or enabled alongside punishment;
        - Number of rounds is high;
        - MPCR is moderate/low and baseline cooperation is low;
        - Contribution/endowment information is transparent.
    - Adjust downward or neutralize if:
        - Punishment is peer-based and uncoordinated;
        - Antisocial or retaliatory punishment is possible;
        - Game is short, MPCR is high, or control efficiency is already high;
        - Punishment cost exceeds deterrence benefit;
        - Monitoring is noisy or weak.
    - When only non-payoff behavioral evidence is available, infer efficiency impact with caution and preference for matching studies that report both contribution and payoff.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions (strong empirical or meta-analytic coverage):**
- `player_count` (group size): effects best established for small to medium groups (n=3–12), with diminishing returns or coordination breakdown in large groups.
- `num_rounds`: Longer repeated games consistently show greater efficiency gains from punishment.
- `mpcr`: Strongly moderated; low MPCR environments show greater relative jumps from punishment.
- `punishment_cost` / `punishment_tech`: Direct evidence that lower cost, higher impact, or normatively constrained punishment is more efficiency-enhancing (Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Barclay, 2004).
- `all_or_nothing`: Both continuous and binary PGGs are covered, with similar qualitative patterns.
- `chat`: Communication is a strong positive moderator and sometimes a substitute for punishment.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Information visibility is well covered as a moderator.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Reward mechanisms and their interaction with punishment are represented, but not in all studies.
- `show_punishment_id`: Some evidence shows public punishment enhances deterrence and reduces antisocial punishment, but can also lead to retaliation (transparency cut both ways).

**Indirectly informed or contextually discussed (some evidence, but less systematic):**
- `default_contrib` (framing): Framing effects (opt-in/opt-out) are explored in some papers, but less so than core strategic variables.
- `punishment_magnitude`: Though universally present in design, not always varied experimentally or reported separately from `punishment_cost`.
- `show_other_summaries`: Variably manipulated; when available, tends to enhance targeted punishment effects.

**Effectively missing or very sparsely discussed:**
- Implementation-level UI/UX features, very large group sizes, interaction with specific subject pools (except cross-cultural studies), and micro-detailed institutional design features (beyond centralized/peer/collective distinctions) are infrequently discussed in terms of their direct effect on efficiency.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Payoff-based efficiency evidence is less frequent, and direct mapping to design dimensions is sometimes ambiguous; many studies report only behavioral outcomes, not efficiency.**
- **Ambiguity and disagreement exist:** Some robust evidence for negative or mixed effects of punishment on efficiency, especially in short games, with high antisocial punishment, or when control efficiency is already high. The effect is not universally positive (Nockur et al., 2021; Handfield et al., 2016; Nair et al., 2018).
- **Limited experimental variation on several dimensions:** Many influential studies use canonical designs without systematic variation of, e.g., group size, MPCR, or cost/impact ratios; generalization beyond standard lab setups should be done with caution.
- **External validity is contested:** Some meta-analyses and field/ethno papers question how representative lab PGG findings are for real-world institutions, which often feature less costly, more coordinated punishment and more complex social context (Guala, 2012; Balliet et al., 2011; Spadaro et al., 2022).
- **Sparse coverage for "edge" design dimensions:** Features like reputation mechanisms, partner choice, dynamic networks, or hybrid sanctioning institutions are sometimes tested but not consistently mapped to efficiency outcomes.
- **Potential publication bias and cross-study heterogeneity:** Even meta-analyses report substantial cross-societal, cultural, and experimental protocol variation; results may not generalize out-of-sample.
- **Indirect evidence reliance:** In the absence of direct efficiency outcomes, behavioral proxies may misestimate actual group welfare, especially if punishment costs are high, or if punishment is misapplied.
- **Measurement and implementation differences:** Methods of reporting efficiency (absolute, relative, cumulative, per round), anonymization, payoff salience, and cultural framing differ across studies.
- **Rarely are all 14 prediction dimensions varied together or fully crossed; interactions between dimensions often speculative or based on mechanism/theory only, not direct empirical comparison.**

---

In summary, the literature robustly supports the efficiency-enhancing effect of peer punishment in classic, repeated PGGs **when control efficiency is low, punishment is well-designed, and key moderators are favorable**—but **context, institution type, cost, and social structure strongly moderate this effect**, and punishment can reduce efficiency or have no effect when these are unfavorable or when control efficiency is already high. Prediction should always be grounded in the closest available empirical analogs and nuanced for each design dimension, with caution about over-generalizing from behavioral outcomes to efficiency, or from standard lab environments to complex real-world settings.
