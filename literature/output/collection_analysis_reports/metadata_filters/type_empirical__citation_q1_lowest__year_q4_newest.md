# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises a large and diverse collection of mainly laboratory-based empirical studies, virtually all using controlled experimental designs (mostly lab experiments, with a few field/lab-in-the-field experiments) testing public goods games (PGG) and close variants. Most studies are empirical; a very small number of purely theoretical or modeling papers are present, and some reviews/meta-analyses appear with secondary synthesis. Overall, the evidence base is broad in coverage of treatments and moderators: it includes standard repeated and one-shot linear PGGs, common-pool resource games, threshold public goods, institutional and endogenous institution designs, and social dilemma variants (e.g., trust games, min-effort games). 

A significant number of papers focus directly on the effect of introducing punishment or sanctioning—peer, formal, leader-based, or institutionalized—and many of these measure payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group earnings/payoff, welfare). There is good representation of between-condition comparisons (punishment-enabled vs. control/no-punishment) and of key moderators (punishment cost and efficacy, heterogeneity, information, network structure, gender, norms, and more). Some studies include reward, ostracism, or communication arms for comparison.

However, despite the breadth, two caveats are critical:  
- Not all papers address efficiency directly, with a substantial subset focusing on behavioral outcomes (e.g., contributions, punishment frequency) rather than group payoff/efficiency.
- Coverage of the full range of game design dimensions is uneven—some, like `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, and `punishment_cost`, are extensively studied; others (e.g., `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id`, `reward_tech`) receive little or no direct attention.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- The majority of high-quality, experimental papers are **exactly relevant**, focusing directly on standard linear or threshold public goods games (PGG), including both homogeneous and heterogeneous variants, as well as common-pool resource (CPR) games that are structurally close.
- A second tier of studies use close PGG variants: trust games, public bads, team investment games, stag-hunt, and minimum-effort/coordination games—these are **adjacent** or **close** in most cases.
- Some papers reference public goods games only as context; these are labeled **weak** or **none** relevance for the structural match.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- Many studies provide **exact** relevance: they manipulate the presence, cost, type, or institutional structure of punishment (peer, leader, formal, ostracism, collective, higher-order, counter-punishment).
- Studies that focus on reward, communication, or allocation but not punishment are labeled **none** for punishment relevance.
- A moderate number examine **adjacent** or **close** interventions—e.g., group-level sanctioning, antisocial punishment, non-monetary disapproval, automatic penalties, or indirect reputation effects.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- Many high-value studies directly measure **efficiency or group payoff** (**exact**)—either through average earnings, welfare, or closely related metrics.
- A substantial subset focus primarily or entirely on **contribution rates, punishment frequency**, or other **non-payoff behavioral** outcomes—these are **adjacent** or **close**.
- Some papers report neither payoff nor efficiency, focusing on attitudes, beliefs, or emotion—**none**.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (relevant to prediction):**
- **Efficiency/group payoff**: The main outcome of interest—defined as total group earnings relative to the full-cooperation social optimum.
- **Welfare, earnings, surplus, total tokens/coins generated**
  
**Non-payoff or behavioral outcomes (supporting only indirect inference):**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**: Often used as a proxy but not equivalent to efficiency, especially when punishment is costly.
- **Punishment frequency, assignment, or severity**
- **Norm compliance, compliance with rules**
- **Trust, reciprocity, network formation, willingness to cooperate or punish**
- **Anti-social punishment and retaliation**
- **Voting on institutions, institutional choice**

Papers that report only cooperation/contribution, punishment behavior, beliefs, or attitudinal outcomes cannot be the primary basis for efficiency predictions, though they can illuminate mechanisms and moderators.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical Consensuses
- **Enabling punishment often increases contributions/cooperation, but efficiency gains depend critically on the cost and structure of punishment, group composition, and contextual moderators.**
- **With standard costly peer punishment**: In canonical linear PGGs with moderate cost and effectiveness (e.g., 1:3 cost-to-impact), punishment raises cooperation and, in many cases, increases efficiency relative to control—provided that anti-social punishment is limited, baseline efficiency is low, and coordination is achieved (e.g., Kamei, 2024; Sparks et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Kamei et al., 2023).
- **Punishment can fail to increase efficiency** (and may reduce it) if:
    - **Costs of punishment exceed the gains from increased contributions** (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022; Deng et al., 2025).
    - **Antisocial punishment or discriminatory punishment is prevalent**, often in heterogeneous or pluriform groups (Chen, 2022; Molenmaker et al., 2023).
    - **Punishment is weak, poorly targeted, or institutionally inefficient** (Nhim et al., 2023; Peng & Fan, 2023; Schaefer, 2023).
- **Centralized/formal punishment and ostracism**: When formal or leader-based punishment is available (as in Kamei, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Jarungrattanapong, 2022), or when ostracism is allowed, **efficiency gains tend to be larger and more robust**, especially in open/migratory settings or when punishment is deterrent and well-coordinated (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Duell et al., 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025).
- **Institutional design is key**: Graduated or dynamically chosen punishment institutions tend to produce higher or more sustainable efficiency, especially in long-run or endogenously chosen settings (van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).

## Moderators and Boundary Conditions
- **Heterogeneity and group structure** can undermine punishment’s positive effect on efficiency; in some heterogeneous PGGs, punishment fails to improve payoff or even reduces it (Chen, 2022; Molenmaker et al., 2023).
- **Network structure** of punishment (e.g., limited punishment links) can lead to higher cooperation and efficiency than complete punishment networks, likely due to reduced bystander effects (Peng & Fan, 2023; Bühren et al., 2025).
- **Transparency** (visibility of endowments or contributions) is necessary for punishment to improve efficiency in unequal groups (Chen et al., 2025).
- **Cognitive ability and information**: In some designs, high cognitive ability or accurate monitoring increases both the effectiveness of punishment and achieved efficiency (Ye et al., 2023; Eisenkopf & Walter, 2022).
- **Magnitude and cost of punishment**: High effectiveness (large penalty per unit cost) supports efficiency gains; high-cost, low-effect punishment tends to destroy surplus (Kamei, 2024; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2025).
- **Presence of communication/chat**: Sometimes substitutes for punishment, but in most settings, absence of communication means higher reliance on formal punishment for efficiency gains (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
- **Leadership and selection/voting**: Leader-based punishment is more effective when leaders are democratically selected, frame their role as responsibility, and especially if female (Jiang & Wang, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024).
- **Reward mechanisms**: Often produce similar or higher efficiency gains than punishment, especially in heterogeneous groups or when reward is majority-vote based (Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022).
- **Experience, norm uncertainty, and group learning**: Efficiency gains from punishment are largest when groups have experienced prior regulatory failure and receive facilitation to coordinate on enforcement norms (DeCaro et al., 2024).

## Findings where punishment decreases or does not improve efficiency:
- **High frequency or indiscriminate application of punishment**, especially when coordinated targeting is missing (Botelho et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).
- **Voluntary, avoidable, or very costly punishment institutions** often fail to improve efficiency (Del Ponte et al., 2025; Schaefer, 2023; Nhim et al., 2023).
- **Counterproductive anti-social punishment or group discrimination** erodes gains (Molenmaker et al., 2023; Chen, 2022).
- **High cost/low impact punishment mechanisms** (punishment ratios below ~1:2) or open-ended, non-credible punishment tend to reduce or fail to change efficiency (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **If the design dimensions (group size, number of rounds, MPCR, absence of chat, standard peer/leader punishment with moderate cost and 1:3 or better cost-to-impact ratio, and no major sources of heterogeneity) match those in canonical lab PGGs, empirical evidence supports a strong expectation that enabling punishment will raise efficiency compared to control—with a magnitude that depends on punishment cost/effectiveness and coordination (Kamei, 2024; Sparks et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Joseph et al., 2025).**
- Efficiency improvements are unlikely if punishment is costly but ineffective, or if group conditions favor indiscriminate or retaliatory punishment. In these conditions, control efficiency becomes a more reliable (but not perfect) ceiling for achievable treatment efficiency (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Chen, 2022).
- **Contextual and design moderators are crucial:** Prediction accuracy depends on incorporating:
    - `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, and `punishment_tech` (strong direct evidence)
    - `player_count` and `num_rounds` (extensive support)
    - `show_other_summaries`, `chat`, `reward_exists` (known to moderate, with evidence, but effect sizes less certain)
- **Indirect or partial evidence** (from studies using only contribution as an outcome) may overstate efficiency gains from punishment—especially when the cost of punishment is not accounted for.
- Any prediction algorithm should condition on known control efficiency, but apply modifiers based on institutional design and key dimensions (above) that empirically moderate the sign and size of the punishment effect.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (with robust empirical evidence):**
- `player_count`
- `num_rounds`
- `mpcr` (Marginal Per Capita Return)
- `punishment_cost`
- `punishment_tech` (technology: peer, leader, formal, ostracism, counter-punishment, etc.)

**Indirectly informed or frequently discussed:**
- `chat` (communication): Substitutes for punishment in some designs, robust evidence for chat’s positive effect on contributions.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Information/feedback structures, especially transparency in contributions or endowments, are important moderators of punishment's effectiveness.
- `all_or_nothing`, `default_contrib`: Some coverage exists—binary (all-or-nothing) games, opt-in vs. opt-out framing.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Several studies include reward conditions—typically showing that combined or majority-vote rewards are more effective or at least as effective as punishment for efficiency.
- `show_punishment_id`: Some evidence from studies manipulating anonymity; impact on retaliation and anti-social punishment.

**Only contextually addressed or largely missing:**
- `default_contrib` (framing): Sparse; a few studies mention framing effects.
- `show_punishment_id`: Generally less directly manipulated, but privacy/anonymity discussed as contributors to antisocial punishment.
- `reward_tech`, `reward_cost` (technology/cost of reward), especially for non-majority-vote or unconventional reward systems: rarely manipulated systematically.
- Some settings combine multiple less-studied dimensions (e.g., migration, network/graph structure); these are less well parametrized.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Not all studies report efficiency or group payoff directly**; those that don't should be used only for mechanism or context, not as the basis for quantitative prediction.
- **Heterogeneity of settings and mixed findings**: Efficiency effects of punishment are context-sensitive and can be negative (especially when punishment is expensive, anti-social, or when reward is a better alternative)—predicate predictions on design and context, not on headline averages.
- **Sparse evidence on certain prediction dimensions**: As above, dimensions like `reward_tech`, `default_contrib`, and `show_punishment_id` are underrepresented, limiting confidence in predictions outside the well-tested parameter regime.
- **Field and large-group evidence limited**: Most studies use small, lab-based groups; generalizability to field or organizational interventions may be limited.
- **Ambiguity in "close" and "adjacent" variants**: Results from trust games, allocation games, or team investment games do not always map directly to PGGs; use such evidence for qualitative, not quantitative, guidance.
- **Control efficiency is an incomplete predictor** when key moderators (e.g., institution design, transparency, group heterogeneity) are not incorporated.
- **Potential publication bias** towards positive findings—failure-to-improve or negative efficiency effects may be underreported.
- **Behavioral and cultural moderators** (e.g., norm strength, in-group/out-group bias, anti-social punishment, cognitive load, or gender composition) can all strongly condition outcomes; these are not always controlled for or reported.

---

**In summary**:  
The available evidence robustly shows that the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments is moderate to strongly positive in well-designed, small-group, lab settings with standard punishment technology, and negative or negligible in cases of high punishment cost, anti-social/discriminatory punishment, or poor institutional design. The literature reliably points to key design dimensions (`player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`) as critical moderators. Prediction models should be strongly conditioned on both control efficiency and these design dimensions, with adjustment for institutional and contextual moderators indicated by the literature. Efficiency gains from punishment are not universal or automatic; in the presence of high punishment cost, anti-social punishment, or group heterogeneity, the marginal effect of punishment on efficiency may be zero or negative, and reward or alternative institutions may outperform punishment for boosting efficiency.
