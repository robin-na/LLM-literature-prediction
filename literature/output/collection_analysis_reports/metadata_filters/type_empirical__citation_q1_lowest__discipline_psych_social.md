# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large (n=142) and primarily empirical, overwhelmingly based on experimental lab studies focused on economic games. A strong subset directly implements or varies public goods game (PGG) structures with and without peer or centralized punishment. There is a solid, but smaller, representation of adjacent games (common-pool resource, team production, trust and ultimatum games, minimum effort, etc.), as well as field experiments and observational studies (with much less frequent direct efficiency outcome reporting). Theory and meta-analysis are sparsely represented and generally play a contextual or mechanism-explanatory role rather than providing quantitative predictions. Overall, the paper set provides a broad basis for examining the downstream prediction task, though the highest relevance and evidence density is for standard repeated linear PGGs with peer punishment.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- _Exact_: A large core of papers (notably Zhang et al., 2024; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Sparks et al., 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Castillo & Hamman, 2021, and others) employ standard or canonical PGGs.  
- _Close/Adjacent_: Many others use closely related designs (CPR games, team production, coordination, trust, and ultimatum games). Adjacency is sometimes strong in payoff structure but differs in group structure, interaction symmetry, or institutional features.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- _Exact_: The core PGG studies and several variants (e.g., Ye et al., 2023; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Hou et al., 2019) directly manipulate costly peer or centralized punishment as the treatment variable.  
- _Close/Adjacent_: Additional studies address third-party punishment, disapproval, exclusion, or indirect and formal/informal sanctions mechanisms. Some field/observational studies discuss sanctioning only theoretically or as cultural context.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- _Exact_: A meaningful subset of the best-aligned studies directly measure group efficiency or average group payoff as a central outcome (Zhang et al., 2024; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Joseph et al., 2025; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Duell et al., 2024, etc.).  
- _Close/Adjacent_: Many others focus on contribution rates or norm compliance but discuss or allow inference regarding efficiency. Some adjacent and field studies either do not report efficiency or only discuss group-level behavioral shifts ambiguously related to payoffs.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related (Relevant for Prediction Task):**
  - *Group efficiency*: Ratio of actual to maximal group payoff under full cooperation (reported in numerous lab experiments).
  - *Total/average group payoff or earnings*: Directly measured in multiple PGGs and some adjacent games.
  - *Related resource outcomes*: Surplus, resource sustainability, or group success in CPR or threshold games.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Not Substitutes for Efficiency):**
  - *Contribution/cooperation rates*: Most commonly measured; often used as a proxy for cooperation, but does not always correlate with net efficiency, especially when punishment costs are high.
  - *Punishment frequency/amount*: Very frequently reported.
  - *Norm compliance and social perceptions*: Common in both experimental and field/observational studies.
  - *Excluded outcomes*: Gossip, disapproval, participation rates, or selection/emotional responses without payoff quantification.

> **Distinction**: Several studies noted that increasing contribution does not always increase efficiency because costs of administering punishment may more than offset gains.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of Empirical Findings:**

- **Positive Effect on Efficiency (Majority Trend):**  
  - _Enabling punishment in canonical repeated PGGs with reasonably calibrated punishment cost-to-impact ratios (often 1:3 or 1:2) and moderate MPCR (0.4–0.5) almost always increases efficiency_ (Zhang et al., 2024; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Joseph et al., 2025; Sparks et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2025; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Duell et al., 2024), especially over longer rounds or when punishment is sufficiently severe and well targeted.

- **Magnitude Moderators:**
  - *Punishment cost* and *effectiveness* (impact per unit): High cost or low impact reduces the net efficiency benefit; low-cost punishment (or third-party, institutional, or collective mechanisms) is more likely to improve efficiency.
  - *Punishment design*: Institutional/centralized or collective punishment often yields larger, more robust efficiency gains than decentralized, unstructured peer punishment, especially when commitment or group consent is built in (Duell et al., 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
  - *Game length*: Efficiency gains often grow across rounds, as cooperation stabilizes and net punishment costs decrease (Sparks et al., 2024; Suleiman & Samid, 2021).

- **Contextual & Moderator Effects:**
  - *Transparency/monitoring*: Effect of punishment on efficiency is higher when contributions/endowments are transparent (Chen et al., 2025); when monitoring is noisy or incomplete, punishment is less effective (Eisenkopf & Walter, 2022; Alt et al., 2023).
  - *Group / network structure*: Denser sanction networks (standard PGG) favor positive efficiency effects, but efficiency gains do not translate to sparse or generalized exchange networks (Kanitsar, 2021). Open group structures or migration can either enhance or undermine punishment’s efficiency effect depending on design (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
  - *Cognition/culture/psychology*: Group cognitive ability, cultural background, and prevalence of prosocial norm-keepers or antisocial punishers can moderate or even reverse efficiency gains (Ye et al., 2023; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Angelsen & Naime, 2024).

- **Negative or Null Effects:**
  - *Punishment reduces efficiency when costs are too high, when punishment is applied excessively, or when antisocial punishment is common (Botelho et al., 2022; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024; Herne et al., 2022).*
  - Peer punishment in certain environments—even when it boosts contributions—yields net negative or negligible efficiency due to punishment costs (Botelho et al., 2022).
  - Formal or collective punishment designs that are not well targeted can fail to improve, or may reduce, efficiency (Calabuig et al., 2024).
  - In polycentric/subgroup-based PGG variants, costly subgroup sanctions did not improve or even reduced group efficiency (Milinski & Marotzke, 2022).

**Theoretical or Mechanism Arguments:**

- Many studies note that for punishment to improve efficiency, it must deter defection at a lower social cost than the value of increased cooperation. Institutional design (who punishes, when, and with what information) heavily affects this calculation.
- Several meta-analyses and reviews (e.g., Jin et al., 2025) reaffirm strong effects on cooperation, but warn that costliness often erodes net efficiency effect—clear theoretical guidance, though direct empirical links are sometimes missing.

**Payoff vs. Behavioral Outcomes:**

- Increased cooperation does not guarantee increased efficiency when punishment is frequent, misapplied, or too costly (explicit in Botelho et al., 2022; Herne et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).
- Some studies show that when antisocial punishment is prevalent, the net effect of punishment on efficiency can be null or negative (Angelsen & Naime, 2024).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct Mapping Feasibility:**  
  Studies in exact-relevance, lab-based, repeated linear PGGs with reported efficiency/payoff and clear design documentation support direct prediction of average efficiency increase when punishment is enabled, especially when baseline (control) efficiency without punishment and specific game parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/impact, no-chat, etc.) are provided.
  
- **Key Moderators to Include:**
  - *Punishment cost and effectiveness* (punishment_cost, punishment_tech): Lower cost/high impact punishment is much more likely to improve efficiency.
  - *Feedback/transparency* (show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds, showPunishmentId): Necessary for punishment to deter; hidden endowments or contributions often nullify efficiency gains.
  - *Game length (num_rounds)*: Positive efficiency effects of punishment are more consistent and accumulate in longer games.
  - *Group structure (player_count, group density, open vs. closed)*: Classic PGGs (small, fixed groups) are best supported; evidence is mixed in larger, open, or networked groups.
  - *Institutional form*: Centralized or collective punishment, especially with consent or commitment features, tends to produce stronger efficiency gains.

- **Control Efficiency as Baseline:**  
  The control game’s baseline efficiency (no punishment) is highly predictive of the starting point. Prediction should expect an efficiency increase when punishment is enabled (in standard PGGs under well-specified cost/impact regimes), but the *size* of increase depends crucially on punishment cost, implementation design, and the presence of moderating features (as noted above).

- **Cautions:**
  - In settings with very high punishment costs, prevalent antisocial punishment, or highly noisy/opaque feedback, enabling punishment can result in lower or unchanged efficiency.
  - Evidence suggests that when punishment is ineffective, misdirected, or its design encourages antisocial application, efficiency is not improved (sometimes reduced).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Best-Informed (Directly and Empirically Supported):**  
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `chat`, `all_or_nothing`
- `show_other_summaries` and `show_n_rounds` (when contributions or endowments are transparent)
- `reward_exists` (for reward/punishment comparative studies)
- Several papers allow direct calibration of how these features moderate the effect of punishment on efficiency.

**Indirectly or Contextually Mentioned:**  
- `default_contrib` (framing), `chat` (as communication/no communication), `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `show_punishment_id`
- Institution selection/leader selection features (e.g., democratic vs. imposed leader) are discussed in the context of punishment effectiveness (Benard & Barclay, 2020; Castillo & Hamman, 2021).
- Design elements like migration, openness, or group formation are well described in contextualized studies.

**Sparse or Effectively Missing:**  
- `default_contrib` (very sparsely addressed in direct experimental comparison)
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (except in comparative reward vs punishment papers)
- `show_punishment_id` (identity of punisher) is rarely manipulated directly; often described only as anonymous or not.
- Many real-world and field studies lack full mapping to these dimensions.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Incomplete Dimension Coverage:**  
  Several prediction-specific dimensions (especially default contribution framing, detailed feedback mechanisms, reward technologies, or identity disclosure) are only partially addressed across studies, and rarely systematically varied within single studies.
- **Efficiency Measurement Gaps:**  
  Many studies still rely on non-payoff outcomes (contribution rates, norm compliance, punishment behavior) and either do not measure, or do not report, payoff-based efficiency—requiring inference or caution in transferring findings to predictions about efficiency.
- **Contextual/Population Dependency:**  
  Several papers show that effectiveness of punishment is contingent on cultural context, group composition (norm-keepers, reciprocators, antisocial punishers), and psychological attributes (e.g., cognitive ability, fairness norms), which are not typically parameterized in game design dimensions.
- **Negative/Null and Boundary Findings:**  
  Not all studies find positive efficiency effects for punishment—cost, antisocial application, and lack of transparency or network structure can eliminate or reverse the effect. Studies disagree in some cases, or show positive effects only in limited, tightly controlled settings.
- **Limited Real-World Generalizability:**  
  Most evidence is from canonical laboratory experiments among WEIRD populations. Transfer to field, large-scale, or open-group settings, or to environments with richer communication or changing group structure, is limited by available evidence.
- **Overreliance on Key PGG Paradigms:**  
  The clearest evidence is for standard, small-group, repeated linear PGGs with well-understood punishment mechanisms. Prediction in more complex or less standard variants requires greater extrapolation and caution.
- **Behavioral–Payoff Disconnect:**  
  Behavioral improvements (higher contributions, more norm compliance) are not always realized as efficiency gains, especially when punishment is overused, poorly targeted, or too costly.

---

## **In summary**:  
The literature strongly supports the prediction that, in standard repeated PGGs with moderate group size and moderately costly, effective punishment, enabling punishment increases efficiency relative to control. However, the strength, direction, and robustness of this effect are sensitive to (a) the cost and targeting of punishment, (b) game transparency and feedback, (c) institutional design, and (d) group psychological/cultural characteristics. Where efficiency increases, the effect scales with factors such as the number of rounds, size/effectiveness of punishment, and group openness. Control efficiency is a good baseline, but parameter-specific and context-specific adjustments, reflecting the above moderators, are needed for accurate prediction. Limitations include gaps in dimension coverage and sometimes a disconnect between increased cooperation and realized efficiency, especially in settings prone to excessive or misdirected punishment.
