# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is extensive (255 papers), spanning empirical experimental lab and field studies as well as a broad range of formal theoretical models. For the downstream prediction task—forecasting the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game (PGG) environments, using detailed game design and control efficiency—this literature base is both rich and heterogeneous.

The empirical core is comprised of many highly relevant laboratory experiments following the canonical linear PGG/VCM design, with clear manipulations of punishment, reward, group composition, information, and institutional choice. Multiple high-citation studies (e.g., Fehr & Gächter 2000/2002; Masclet et al. 2003; Nikiforakis 2008; Sutter et al. 2010) focus directly on PGGs with explicit efficiency (payoff/welfare) outcomes, providing quantitative treatment-control contrasts. Field experiments and lab-in-the-field studies are present but less frequent, offering valuable insights on external validity. Theory papers examine punishments’ mechanisms and parameter sensitivities; many explicitly model efficiency outcomes.

Some experiments and models treat adjacent or weakly related environments: two-player Prisoner’s Dilemmas, trust games, contest games, and coordination problems, as well as common-pool resource (CPR) dilemmas and indirect reciprocity settings. These contribute to understanding general mechanisms (punishment, reward, monitoring, group structure), but only some can be directly mapped to PGG efficiency.

In sum, for predicting efficiency impacts of punishment across different PGG designs and control baselines, the empirical evidence on standard lab PGGs is deep and high-quality; coverage is sparser and more contextual for field, naturalistic, or adjacent-domain studies.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**: Most of the heavily-cited/most-informative empirical and theory papers are *exactly* focused on PGG or canonical variants (e.g., Fehr & Gächter 2000; Sutter et al. 2010; Egas & Riedl 2008; Masclet et al. 2003), often with all key design variables (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment parameters) fully specified. Several field/CPR studies are *close* or *adjacent*. Many theoretical models synthesize PGGs, indirect reciprocity, and related collective action games.
- **punishment_or_sanctions**: Many papers provide *exact* manipulations/enabling/disabling of peer punishment (and sometimes reward) technology. Several designs involve *centralized* or *delegated* punishment or exclusion (ostracism), offering rich evidence on mechanism variants. Some studies address *adjacent* mechanisms (exclusion, partner choice, reputation, monitoring, deposit/commitment systems).
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: A substantial number of lab papers explicitly report efficiency (payoff as a % of optimum), group earnings, or welfare as primary outcomes (*exact*). Many others focus on contribution levels or punishment frequency (behavioral outcomes—*adjacent*), and some offer only indirect or no payoff data (*weak* or *none*).

In sum, for the primary prediction task (impact of punishment on efficiency in PGGs), relevance is *exact* to *close* for ca. 20-30 core papers, *adjacent* for several dozen, and *weak/none* elsewhere. The strongest empirical and theoretical foundation is for repeated, small-group, continuous-contribution PGGs in laboratory settings.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based outcomes (directly predictive):**
    - Group efficiency (payoff relative to cooperative optimum), average earnings, welfare, surplus, total coins generated. Many core PGG papers (e.g., Fehr & Gächter 2000/2002, Masclet et al. 2003, Sutter et al. 2010, Barclay 2004, Andreoni & Gee 2012) report these explicitly and make treatment-control (no punishment vs. punishment) efficiency contrasts.
    - Some studies on exclusion and institutional choice (Gürerk et al. 2006; Putterman et al. 2011; Hamman et al. 2011) also measure group payoff outcomes.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (cannot substitute for efficiency):**
    - Contribution rates, cooperation rates, norm compliance, punishment frequency, prosocial punishment, antisocial punishment, retaliation, trust, willingness to punish or reward, etc. Many studies focus on contributions as the primary outcome and infer efficiency only indirectly.
    - Some studies examine punishment as norm enforcement, reputation, third-party effects, or psychological motives without measuring payoffs.

A critical caveat: *An increase in contribution rate does not always translate to higher efficiency, especially when punishment is costly or misdirected. Papers that only report behavioral outcomes must not be treated as direct evidence on efficiency.*

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesized Cross-paper Findings:**

*Empirical studies* (esp. canonical lab PGGs):

- **Punishment often increases efficiency relative to no-punishment baselines when:**
    - Groups are small (<8), repeated interaction is present, punishment is not prohibitively costly, antisocial punishment is rare, and monitoring is accurate and timely ([Fehr & Gächter 2000]; [Masclet et al. 2003]; [Barclay 2004]; [Gürerk et al. 2006]; [Sutter et al. 2010]; [Masclet 2003]; [O'Gorman et al. 2009]; [Andreoni & Gee 2012]; [Putterman et al. 2011]).
    - The positive effect is often *substantial* (10-40 percentage points in late rounds; sometimes approaching the social optimum).

- **Punishment can have *no effect* or *reduce* efficiency when:**
    - Antisocial or misdirected punishment is common ([Herrmann et al. 2008]; [Gächter & Herrmann 2011]; [Fehr, Hoff, & Kshetramade 2008]), leading to large payoff losses (notably in certain cultures/field contexts, e.g., Russia, rural India).
    - Monitoring is noisy or punishment is weak ([Ambrus & Greiner 2012]; [Grechenig et al. 2010]; [Tenbrunsel & Messick 1999]); punishment costs offset gains from increased cooperation.
    - Social trust is high, and externally imposed punishment is resented, leading to crowding out ([Ostrom et al. 1992]; [Janssen et al. 2010]; [Vollan 2008]; [Janssen et al. 2010]).
    - Counter-punishment (retaliation), feuding, or revenge is possible ([Nikiforakis 2008]; [Hopfensitz & Reuben 2009]; [Fehr & Fischbacher 2004]); costs spiral, reducing or nullifying efficiency improvements.

- **Moderators and mechanism insights:**
    - *Punishment cost and effectiveness*: Low cost/high impact punishment is more likely to increase efficiency, but can also fuel feuds ([Egas & Riedl 2008]; [Ambrus & Greiner 2012]).
    - *Punishment targeting*: Efficiency gains are highest when punishment is restricted to low contributors; allowing indiscriminate punishment often reduces efficiency ([Ertan et al. 2009]; [Herrmann et al. 2008]).
    - *Communication and reputation*: The combination of punishment and communication (especially promises) can vastly improve efficiency; communication alone is often as effective as punishment ([Bochet & Putterman 2009]; [Ostrom et al. 1992]; [Cardenas 2004]).
    - *Endogenous choice*: When players can endogenously choose the institution, punishment is rarely chosen unless its efficiency is high, and the act of institutional choice boosts efficiency above exogenous assignment ([Sutter et al. 2010]; [Putterman et al. 2011]).
    - *Cultural, social, and group composition moderators*: Culture, institution legitimacy, group identity, and heterogeneity in returns matter greatly ([Herrmann et al. 2008]; [Barclay 2004]; [Alexander & Christia 2011]; [Gächter et al. 2010]).
    - *Group size*: Efficiency gains from punishment decline in larger groups; exclusion mechanisms (ostracism) lose power in large groups ([Kerr et al. 2009]; [Gürerk et al. 2006]).

*Theory papers:*
- Formal models corroborate and extend empirical findings: punishment can enable stable, efficient equilibria in repeated, small-group, information-rich environments ([Fehr & Schmidt 1999]; [KANDORI 1992]; [Sigmund et al. 2010]; [Fehr & Gintis 2007]; [Kandori 1992]); but efficiency is highly sensitive to punishment cost, reputation/information accuracy, possibility of counter-punishment, and whether second-order free-riding is controlled ([Perc 2012]; [Sigmund et al. 2010]; [Ohtsuki et al. 2009]).
- Institutional and centralized punishment can be highly efficient—if designed to minimize cost and maximize targeting.
- Weak, noisy, or antisocial punishment mechanisms can generate inefficient equilibria or even collapse cooperation.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based on the reviewed literature, predictions about treatment efficiency (efficiency with punishment enabled) from design dimensions and control efficiency should be made as follows:

- **Baseline expectation:** In repeated, small-group, laboratory PGGs with accurate monitoring, anonymous but focused punishment (targeting only low contributors), and moderate to high MPCR, enabling punishment is very likely (but not guaranteed) to increase efficiency compared to the control. Efficiency gains may range 10–40 percentage points, particularly in late rounds, but are always limited by the cost of punishment itself.

- **Magnitude of effect:** The efficiency gain is:
    - *Larger* when baseline (control) efficiency is low (due to declining contributions).
    - *Smaller* (or negative) if: punishment is costly, misdirected, feuding/counter-punishment is frequent, monitoring is noisy, or cultural context favors antisocial punishment.

- **Key moderators for prediction (supported by directly payoff-relevant evidence):**
    - *Punishment cost and impact (punishment_cost, punishment_tech)*: Lower cost and higher impact increases the likelihood and size of efficiency gains but can also spur more punishment activity—risking cost spirals or feuds.
    - *Information structure (monitoring, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id)*: Accurate, timely feedback on contributions and punishment is crucial; noise makes punishment less efficient or even welfare-negative.
    - *Group composition/culture*: Homogeneous, prosocial groups are more likely to see efficient punishment. In societies/groups with frequent antisocial punishment, efficiency gains are unlikely or negative.
    - *Presence of communication/reputation (chat)*: When combined with punishment, communication and/or reputation mechanisms (punishment observability, indirect reciprocity) synergistically boost efficiency and reduce the need for costly enforcement.
    - *Group size (player_count)*: Efficiency gains from peer punishment attenuate as group size increases; exclusion works well for small groups only.
    - *Institutional regime*: Centralized or delegated punishment can produce higher efficiency than peer-to-peer punishment, especially if delegation minimizes targeting errors and cost ([Andreoni & Gee 2012]; [Kosfeld et al. 2009]).

- **Control efficiency as anchor**: The expected efficiency with punishment generally will not fall below the control unless
    - Antisocial punishment is prevalent,
    - Monitoring is highly imperfect,
    - Punishment is poorly targeted (allowed against high contributors),
    - Or feuding/counter-punishment is possible and frequent.

- **Contextual caveats**: Prediction accuracy drops in real-world, field, or highly context-dependent settings. Institutional legitimacy, cultural norms, enforcement credibility, and participant buy-in (endogenous choice) are essential for positive efficiency effects ([Vollan 2008]; [Ostrom et al. 1992]; [Herrmann et al. 2008]).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Strongly and directly informed dimensions (with payoff/efficiency outcomes):**
- `player_count` (group size): Many studies use 3–5 players per group; efficiency gains from punishment are robust in small groups, less so as group size increases.
- `num_rounds`: Most lab PGGs use 6–20 rounds; longer games allow contribution/efficiency to recover over time with punishment, but feuding can arise in longer treatments.
- `punishment_cost`/`punishment_tech`: Cost-to-impact ratio is central: lower cost and higher impact produce larger efficiency improvements—up to a limit.
- `mpcr`: Both empirical and theory papers find that higher MPCR can make punishment less necessary, but when MPCR is low, punishment has greater relative effect.
- `all_or_nothing`: Both continuous and binary (all-or-nothing) contribution games are well represented.
- `chat` (communication): Strong effect when combined with punishment; sometimes communication alone achieves the same level of efficiency.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Feedback about contributions/past punishments is critical for effective, targeted punishment and efficient enforcement.

**Indirectly informed:**
- `punishment_tech` (impact/magnitude)—distinct from cost—is covered in cost-to-impact findings.
- `show_punishment_id`: Less frequently manipulated, but evidence suggests that non-anonymous, reputationally visible punishment can be much more efficient or, if observable, can trigger parochial/antisocial punishment under some social contexts.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Compared directly with punishment in some studies; reward-only sometimes produces higher efficiency.
- `default_contrib`: Few studies directly manipulate default contribution frames, but some report on opt-in vs. opt-out framing’s effect on baseline cooperation.

**Only contextually discussed or very sparse:**
- `show_other_summaries`, aspects of feedback detail and information structure, though central in theory, are less often directly varied in lab studies.
- `chat` is well studied as all-or-none but rarely parameterized for degrees of communication or other chat features.
- Some combinations (e.g., reward and punishment together) have fewer studies.
- Very little coverage of designs with *both* peer and institutional punishment, or punishment combined with complex information regimes.

**Effectively missing:**
- Nuanced manipulations of default contribution (`default_contrib`), certain exotic parameter regimes (large player counts >16), and some types of feedback structure.
- Very sparse evidence on naturally occurring PGGs or punishment in real-world, large-scale collective action outside the lab.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Non-equivalence of non-payoff and efficiency outcomes**: Many studies—especially outside the core lab PGG literature—measure only contributions, punishment frequency, or psychological variables. These *cannot* be substituted for efficiency outcomes in prediction without strong caveats. Increased cooperation rate does *not* guarantee higher efficiency, especially if punishment is destructive or feuding occurs.
- **Context and culture**: Effects of punishment on efficiency are highly context-sensitive. Results from canonical lab games may not generalize to field or high-variance cultural contexts ([Herrmann et al. 2008]; [Vollan 2008]; [Ostrom et al. 1992]).
- **Punishment design and targeting**: Poorly designed punishment (high cost, weak impact, misdirected, or enabling counter-punishment) can destroy efficiency. Many negative-outcome studies warn that punishment must be both efficient and well-targeted to produce gains.
- **Complex moderating interactions**: The impact of punishment strongly depends on joint values of multiple design variables (e.g., cost, monitoring, communication, group size, culture, reward availability), making linear prediction risky.
- **Endogeneity and self-selection**: Many of the most efficiency-enhancing punishment institutions are endogenously chosen (voting, opt-in)—effects can be smaller or negative when institutions are imposed or lack legitimacy.
- **Incomplete design coverage**: Some design dimensions are missing or underexplored, especially for large groups, rare/complex feedback structures, and hybrid institutions (combined punishment-reward, mixed peer/institutional regimes).
- **Temporal profile**: Efficiency gains often materialize only in later rounds; short games or analyses focusing on early rounds may underestimate benefits.
- **Publication bias and lab-field gap**: The majority of efficiency-positive evidence comes from lab studies; field evidence is more ambiguous and context-bound.

---

**In summary:** Prediction of efficiency impacts from enabling punishment in PGG-like environments is best grounded in repeated, small-group, laboratory PGG studies with specified cost/impact/rule regimes and accurate monitoring. For other contexts, especially where antisocial punishment, noise, or legitimacy issues occur, efficiency gains are less robust and predictions should be cautious. Accurate mapping from game design and control efficiency to treatment efficiency requires careful attention to punishment cost-effectiveness, information structure, targeting, cultural context, and the interaction of multiple design factors—many of which are well-informed in the empirical and theoretical literature, but some of which remain partially or poorly covered.
