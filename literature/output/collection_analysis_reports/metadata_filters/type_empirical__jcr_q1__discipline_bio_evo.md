# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base consists primarily of empirical, laboratory, and field-experimental studies directly investigating public goods games (PGGs), their close variants (common pool resources, collective-risk dilemmas), and a spectrum of punishment and reward implementations, with a minority of papers addressing adjacent game designs or motivational/mechanistic questions. The balance is weighted toward **empirical experimental studies**, with minimal theory-only contributions and very few purely observational or modeling papers.

Within this set, about 20 papers are of **exact relevance** to all three central aspects: standard PGGs, the experimental manipulation of punishment versus control conditions, and group efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes. Numerous additional papers study CPRs or other collective action games with punishment and efficiency outcomes; some focus on institutional or population-level moderators (e.g., culture, social norms, group composition, external versus self-imposed punishment). A substantial number of studies focus instead on behavioral or psychological mechanisms of punishment, or on adjacent outcomes such as cooperation rates, norm-following, or emotional underpinnings.

Overall, the **paper set is broad**, encompassing laboratory, field, and cross-cultural experiments, but in terms of the **core prediction task**—estimation of efficiency change under punishment given game design—there exists a robust empirical basis, albeit with significant heterogeneity and contextual caveats. There are enough directly relevant studies to support synthesis, but also enough diversity to highlight limitations and sources of ambiguity.

---

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) PGG or Variant Relevance:**  
- There is **strong coverage** of standard PGGs: Most directly relevant papers use linear or all-or-nothing PGGs with canonical group sizes and round structures (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; O'Gorman et al., 2009; Egas & Riedl, 2008).
- **Close variants** include CPR experiments, threshold public goods, and collective-risk games, which share many structural features but introduce real-world resource dynamics or probabilistic loss.  
- Some papers use adjacent or non-PGG designs (Ultimatum Games, Dictator Games, Prisoner's Dilemmas); while these inform mechanisms, they are **not directly usable for efficiency prediction in PGGs**.

**b) Punishment or Sanctions Relevance:**  
- **Peer punishment** (punishment by group members) and **external/institutional punishment** are the core manipulation in many studies.
- Coverage includes both **costly and non-costly punishment**, one- or multi-punisher regimes, second- and third-order punishment, ostracism, and rule-based sanctioning.
- Studies also address related mechanisms: bribery/corruption, antisocial punishment, and the interplay of punishment with institutional or community features (e.g., self-imposed vs. imposed rules).

**c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome Relevance:**  
- A **subset of studies** measures efficiency directly as the ratio of achieved group payoff to maximum possible under full cooperation (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019).
- Some report **group earnings or welfare** (Nhim et al., 2023; Vollan, 2008; Muthukrishna et al., 2017)—readily translatable to efficiency.
- A notable fraction focus **only on behavioral outcomes** (contribution rate, cooperation frequency) without direct efficiency reporting.
- Several studies showing *increased cooperation* with punishment also note that **net payoffs do not necessarily increase** due to punishment costs (e.g., Egas & Riedl, 2008; Sääksvuori et al., 2011).

**Summary of Relevance Labels:**  
- For most main papers:  
  - `pgg_or_variant`: **exact** or **close**
  - `punishment_or_sanctions`: **exact**
  - `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`: **exact**, **close**, or *occasionally* **adjacent** (when only behavioral outcomes reported)

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related / Efficiency Outcomes:**
  - Several studies **explicitly measure efficiency** (ratio of payoff to social optimum) (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Vollan, 2008).
  - Other studies use **group payoff, surplus, total earnings, or mean profit** as their primary outcome—these are *directly relevant* for prediction (e.g., O'Gorman et al., 2009; Nhim et al., 2023).
  - Some papers provide **only behavioral proxies** or infer efficiency from reported changes in contributions or extraction.

- **Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
  - **Contribution rate, cooperation frequency, punishment assigned, norm compliance** (dominant outcomes in many studies; crucial for understanding mechanism but *not directly* the efficiency outcome for prediction).
  - **Punishment frequency/type (prosocial, antisocial)**, emotional/motivational data, and psychological/neural correlates feature heavily in mechanism-focused papers.
  - Some studies examine **resource extraction or conservation**; in CPR settings, reduced extraction often implies higher efficiency, but this is an inference unless directly calculated.

- **Ambiguity/Overlap:**
  - Papers sometimes claim efficiency changes based on contribution rate or assumption (e.g., increased cooperation **may** imply increased efficiency), but, repeatedly, cost of punishment or unintended consequences (e.g., antisocial punishment, crowding-out) **can offset the cooperation gains**, resulting in ambiguous or even negative net efficiency effects.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### 4.1 General Effect of Punishment

- **Enabling peer punishment in standard lab PGGs frequently** leads to **increased cooperation** (Gächter et al., 2017; O'Gorman et al., 2009) but not always to increased efficiency:  
    - **If punishment is used frequently and is costly**, the cost to punishers can **offset or outweigh gains** from higher contributions, sometimes resulting in lower net group payoff (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Traulsen et al., 2012; Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021).
    - **When punishment is relatively cheap and/or coordination mechanisms prevent redundant punishment**, efficiency gains are more likely (O'Gorman et al., 2009).
    - **High initial cooperation (no-punishment baseline already efficient)**: adding punishment often provides **little or negative** additional efficiency (e.g., Egas & Riedl, 2008).
- **The “punishment technology” (cost-to-impact ratio) is a primary moderator of effects**:  
    - High impact/low cost punishment can stabilize cooperation, but may still not increase efficiency if punishment is over-used (Egas & Riedl, 2008).  
    - More efficient systems (e.g., volunteer’s dilemma setups, singles punisher or cost-heterogeneous regimes) can reduce redundant punishment and net efficiency loss (O'Gorman et al., 2009; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2013).
- **Institutional Contexts (e.g., field/CPR or group competition):**
    - In real-world resource or field contexts, external punishment usually **increases efficiency if broadly accepted and social trust is low**, but **can crowd out voluntary cooperation and reduce efficiency** if imposed on high-trust or self-organizing groups (Vollan, 2008; Nhim et al., 2023).
    - **Intergroup competition** is a key moderator: with competition, punishment is more likely to enhance efficiency (Sääksvuori et al., 2011), while in isolated groups it may not.
    - **Bribery/corruption opportunity** in punishment institutions **reverses efficiency gains**—punishment only increases efficiency when the punitive power is not subverted (Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Spadaro et al., 2023).

### 4.2 Indirect and Moderating Effects

- Efficiency effects **vary across cultures** and institutional backgrounds (Gächter et al., 2010; Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011). Cultural context, buy-in, history of cooperation, and social trust are **key moderators**, sometimes overwhelming game-theoretic predictions derived solely from design dimensions.
- Negative side effects: **Antisocial punishment** (punishing cooperators), especially when cultural acceptance is low, can offset or reverse expected efficiency gains (Gächter & Herrmann, 2009; de Melo & Piaggio, 2015).
- **Alternative or complementary sanctioning mechanisms** (e.g., ostracism, social exclusion) can increase payoffs and cooperation with fewer direct costs (Sääksvuori, 2014).
- **Reward mechanisms and reputation transfer**—in designs where payoff consequences for positive reputation exist—are often as, or more, effective in increasing efficiency with less risk of crowding-out or antisocial dynamics (Semmann et al., 2004; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

### 5.1 Core Guidance for Predicting Efficiency with Peer Punishment Enabled:

- **Do not assume enabling punishment will always increase efficiency.**
- **Carefully account for the punishment technology (cost/impact), as well as baseline control efficiency.**
    - *If control efficiency is already high*, marginal gains from punishment are limited, and risk of over-punishment or antisocial punishment may reduce efficiency.
    - *If control efficiency is low (cooperation decaying over rounds)*, well-designed punishment often increases payoff, but only if punishment costs do not swamp contribution gains.
    - **Efficiency increases are most robust** in designs where:  
        - Punishment is **efficient** (high impact, low cost, e.g., 1:3 ratio),  
        - System discourages redundant/retaliatory punishment (e.g., single punisher, coordination cues),  
        - Antisocial punishment is rare or mechanisms limit it,  
        - Punishment is not vulnerable to corruption or is institutionally legitimate,
        - The group is not composed of highly prosocial/norm-driven members already.
    - **Efficiency decreases or ambiguous results** are most likely when:
        - Punishment is costly and/or misapplied,
        - Antisocial punishment is common, especially in low-trust or culturally unsupportive environments,
        - Institutional punishment is imposed without consensus/support,
        - Bribery, corruption, or loopholes subvert enforcement,
        - Contributions (and thus potential for gain) are already near optimal in control.

### 5.2 Control Efficiency as a Predictor:

- **Baseline (control) efficiency is a critical moderator:** The lower the control efficiency, the greater the potential for punishment to increase efficiency—but only if the structural and cultural preconditions for *effective* punishment are met (Gächter et al., 2017; O'Gorman et al., 2009).
- **Additive effects** should be discounted by expected punishment usage costs; in some CPR field studies, efficiency gains from reduced extraction can be nearly cancelled by enforcement costs (Vollan, 2008; Nhim et al., 2023).

### 5.3 Mapping Design Dimensions to Prediction

- **Punishment cost, magnitude, and assignment regime:** Directly determine likelihood and efficiency cost of punishment; predictive models should weight these heavily (Egas & Riedl, 2008; Traulsen et al., 2012).
- **Player count, rounds:** Magnitude of opportunity for learning, decay, or retaliation; longer games with larger groups may enable more complex punishment dynamics.
- **Chat, observability, summary displays:** Absence of communication/reputation generally increases reliance on—and costliness of—punishment; chat and summary access can substitute for (or amplify) punishment effects, but this is primarily behavioral unless efficiency is reported.
- **Institutional context, group support, and trust:** Effects of punishment rules are highly contingent on group support and cultural/institutional trust. Efficiency gains in low-trust or new institutions; possible efficiency loss (“crowding out”) in high-trust or cohesive groups (Vollan, 2008; Castillo et al., 2011).
- **All-or-nothing vs continuous contributions, MPCR, default framing:** Direct mapping to overall gain/loss potential and sensitivity to strategic incentives.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count` (directly manipulated or reported in almost all relevant experiments)
- `num_rounds` (likewise reported; essential for decay/learning and sustained punishment effects)
- `mpcr` (marginal per capita return; explicit in most PGGs, regulates incentive for cooperation)
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (cost per point and impact; major in nearly all PGG+punishment experiments)
- `all_or_nothing` (binary/step-level versus continuous contributions; reported in most studies)
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds` (round visibility and feedback; included in many designs)

**Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
- `chat`/communication (often manipulated; effects usually on contribution behavior, sometimes efficiency)
- `default_contrib` (opt-in/out framing; occasionally discussed, rarely primary)
- `show_punishment_id` (punisher identity visibility; assessed mainly as mechanism but not as primary moderation of efficiency)

**Less Directly Covered/Effectively Missing:**
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (although some studies include reward or reputation treatments, systematic variation is sparse compared to punishment)
- Game-specific technicalities (e.g., assignment of punishment rights, second-order punishment, ostracism vs monetary penalty) are highly variable and rarely mapped across full dimension sets.

# 7) Important Limitations

### 7.1 Gaps and Ambiguities

- **Behavioral outcomes ≠ efficiency:** Many studies conflate increased cooperation with improved efficiency without accounting for the cost of enforcement. Where both are reported, efficiency effects are commonly smaller, ambiguous, or negative compared to behavioral effects.
- **Cultural and institutional moderators are incompletely specified:** Efficiency effects are highly context-dependent (culture, preexisting trust, group support for rules), but many prediction dimensions do not encode these factors.
- **Antisocial punishment and crowding-out** are real risks, especially in some cultural or real-world environments, and can reverse expected efficiency gains; these outcomes are difficult to predict from standard game dimensions alone.
- **Real-world institutional complexity and adaptability:** Many field experiments reveal that externally imposed punishment can backfire or fail when not supported by local norms, and features like bribery/corruption (Muthukrishna et al., 2017), imperfect monitoring, or 'opting out' are insufficiently represented in most lab PGGs.
- **Sparse direct evidence for some design dimensions:** While punishment cost/effectiveness, group size, and MPCR are well studied, dimensions like chat, observability, framing, and reward/punishment identity are less systematically varied or linked to efficiency outcomes.

### 7.2 Cautions for Prediction

- Extrapolation from lab to field—and from payoff proxies to true efficiency—is fraught with risk.
- Control efficiency is a strong anchor for prediction, but only when moderators (punishment tech, cost, group context) match closely.
- **Predictions outside exact experimental contexts** (e.g., large groups, real-world resources, split incentives, complex mixed-sanction institutions) require caution, as contextual moderators dominate.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature provides strong, directly relevant empirical evidence for the effects of punishment on group efficiency in PGG-like games, with clear dependence on punishment technology, institutional context, baseline cooperation, and group characteristics. Efficient and well-coordinated punishment can substantially boost efficiency when baseline cooperation is low and punishment costs are modest. However, punishment also frequently fails to improve—and can reduce—efficiency when costs are high, coordination is absent, or cultural/institutional support is lacking. For accurate prediction, models must integrate control efficiency with design-specific penalty costs, assignment mechanisms, and context cues. Many prediction dimensions are well covered, but effects of some (chat, observability, framing, rewarding) are less firmly established for efficiency outcomes. Ambiguity, context dependence, and occasional contradictory results warrant careful quantification of uncertainty in efficiency predictions under punishment-enabled game designs.
