# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set contains 20 primarily empirical, laboratory-based experimental studies, with a narrow focus on human economic games related to cooperation, but with a broader span in terms of game types (public goods, public bads, dictator, prisoner's dilemma, trust, and novel custom games). Most papers use repeated or one-shot group games, with a minority addressing field or observational data, animal analogues, or neural modulation. There is a substantial mix in how directly the papers address the prediction task: only a minority focus on public goods games (PGG) with explicit punishment and group efficiency outcomes. Several studies explore closely related mechanisms (social information, norms, reputation, neural basis of cooperation) but do not report, or only infrequently report, direct efficiency or payoff-related outcomes. Theory papers are absent, and mechanism arguments within empirical work are limited to interpretation of results.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact:** A moderate subset of studies (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Nhim et al., 2023; Micheli et al., 2021; Burton-Chellew & D’Amico, 2021) use standard linear PGGs or well-defined variants.
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some use public bads, common-pool resources, multi-player prisoner's dilemma, mini ultimatum games, or dictator/trust/investment games (e.g., Del Ponte et al., 2025; Gallier et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2019).
- **Weak/None:** A few papers focus only on single-player tasks, animal behavior, or LLMs and are at best contextually informative (e.g., Johnson & Obradovich, 2025; Cisarovsky et al., 2012).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact:** Several core papers directly compare games with and without costly punishment mechanisms (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Nhim et al., 2023; Del Ponte et al., 2025).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some address less direct forms of sanctions (costless disapproval, peer evaluation, destruction, reputation systems), or only discuss punishment as background.
- **Weak/None:** A significant fraction discuss games without punishment, or manipulate other mechanisms such as rewards or information, not sanctions per se.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact:** Only a few studies report group efficiency or clear payoff-based outcomes as primary dependent variables (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Nhim et al., 2023; Del Ponte et al., 2025; dos Santos et al., 2014).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Several report only contribution rates, norm compliance, trust, or neural/psychological/behavioral measures, which require caution when extrapolating to efficiency.
- **Weak/None:** Many do not report group payoff, welfare, surplus, or similar.

Thus, **empirical coverage for the core prediction task—estimating the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games as a function of design dimensions and control efficiency—is limited but not absent**. Only a handful of papers meet the criteria for direct, high-relevance evidence.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related Outcomes (directly predictive):**
- *Group efficiency and payoff:* Reported in Sparks et al. (2024), Nhim et al. (2023), Del Ponte et al. (2025), dos Santos et al. (2014).
- *Welfare, surplus, total coins:* Sometimes reported as part of group earnings; more often, these metrics are implied by the group payoff measures when reported.

**Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes (not sufficient for payoff/efficiency prediction, but informative):**
- *Contribution rate, cooperation rate, norm compliance:* Frequently primary outcomes in most PGG or CPR studies.
- *Punishment frequency/decision, antisocial punishment, trust, emotion expressed, neural activation, normative judgments, social preferences, and learning from success/common behaviors*: Common, especially in adjacent designs or cognitive/neural studies.

**Distinctions:**  
Many studies measure and interpret effects on contribution or compliance as proxies for efficiency, but true efficiency impacts can diverge due to the costs of punishment and specific design structuring (Nhim et al., 2023; dos Santos et al., 2014). Several studies do not report payoff at all, and among those that do, only a subset distinguish between contribution increases and net group efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Presence of costly punishment mechanisms can sustain higher contributions and ultimately increase group efficiency in repeated PGG environments, but the efficiency gains are often realized only in the longer run as cooperation stabilizes and punishment costs subside (Sparks et al., 2024).**
- **Costless forms of social disapproval or reputation mechanisms alone are insufficient to sustain cooperation or improve efficiency in the long run compared to control (Sparks et al., 2024; dos Santos et al., 2014).**
- **The *structure* and *cost* of the punishment institution are critical:** In some designs (notably strict enforcement or costly-peer-punishment regimes), efficiency does not increase (and may decrease) compared to control because the cost of punishment can offset gains in contribution (Nhim et al., 2023). In contrast, centralized or automated punishment with minimal cost can increase efficiency.
- **Reward mechanisms (when present) can have equivalent or stronger effects than punishment on contribution rates; however, effects on efficiency specifically are less often reported and may differ due to cost structures (Micheli et al., 2021).**
- **In decentralized, voluntary pledge-based or opt-in games, peer or automatic punishment mechanisms often fail to improve efficiency unless ambitious participation can be ensured; participants may game the system by making unambitious pledges or opting out (Del Ponte et al., 2025).**
- **Cognitive and informational context matters:** The efficiency-enhancing effect of punishment can be undermined or eliminated by superfluous information, cognitive load, or environmental disturbance, which can alter player attention and increase antisocial punishment (dos Santos et al., 2014). 
- **Social information about successful behaviors can reduce cooperation (and thus likely efficiency) even without explicit punishment (Burton-Chellew & D’Amico, 2021).**
- **Many factors affecting punishment behavior (e.g., neural or psychological, intent sensitivity) can modulate how sanctions are used, but actual efficiency effects are only inferred, not directly observed, in these studies.**

# 5) Prediction Guidance

The current literature supports the following guidance for predicting average efficiency in PGG-like environments when peer punishment is enabled, given design dimensions and control efficiency:

1. **When the game closely matches standard repeated linear PGG designs** (e.g., 3–5 players, 10+ rounds, continuous contributions, no side incentives, peer punishment with real material cost and direct effect on payoffs), **enabling costly peer punishment reliably increases group efficiency** compared to the no-punishment baseline, especially for medium to long games (Sparks et al., 2024). The increase can be robust and recovery from initial efficiency losses (due to costly punishment) may occur across rounds.

2. **However, the efficiency gains are strongly moderated by the costliness and type of punishment**:
   - If punishment is costly and heavily used, the group may achieve higher contributions but not higher efficiency, as punishment costs offset the gains (Nhim et al., 2023).
   - Centralized or low-cost punishment (including automated fines, probabilistic enforcement) may improve net efficiency if costs are proportionally low and enforcement is effective (Micheli et al., 2021—but note outcome is contribution rate, not direct efficiency).
   - In purely voluntary, opt-in regimes, punishment is often strategically avoided and does not improve efficiency relative to control (Del Ponte et al., 2025).

3. **Interaction with other design parameters** is apparent:
   - Longer games allow for the longer-term efficiency benefits of punishment to unfold (Sparks et al., 2024).
   - Higher marginal per capita return (MPCR) increases the potential for efficiency gains, but the benefit from punishment also depends on baseline efficiency without punishment (improvement may be greatest when the baseline is low).
   - Cognitive burden, informational noise, and environmental factors can reduce or reverse the efficiency benefits through increased antisocial punishment or reduced prosocial focus (dos Santos et al., 2014).

4. **Contribution rate improvements do not guarantee efficiency improvements.** Cost structures, frequency of antisocial punishment, and implementation details (voluntary vs. mandatory, peer vs. centralized) must be considered.

5. **Control efficiency is predictive but not determinative:** When control efficiency is already high (without punishment), the potential efficiency gain from enabling punishment is reduced and may even be negative if punishment is overused or misdirected (Nhim et al., 2023; Sparks et al., 2024).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- `player_count`: Commonly varied (mostly 3–5); evidence suggests punishment effects are robust in these group sizes.
- `num_rounds`: Longer games (e.g., ≥10 rounds) demonstrate the evolving impact of punishment on efficiency; short games may not allow delayed efficiency gains to appear.
- `mpcr`: Varying MPCR is common; its interaction with punishment is sometimes discussed.
- `punishment_cost`: Directly manipulated in several studies (Sparks et al., 2024; Nhim et al., 2023).
- `all_or_nothing`, `chat`: Occasionally manipulated; chat is usually absent in punishment-focused designs to avoid confounds.
- `default_contrib`: Sometimes present as opt-in/opt-out framing; less commonly a causal focus.
- `punishment_tech` (mechanism details): Occasionally addressed (peer vs. centralized, automatic vs. manual).
- `reward_exists`: Discussed when compared to punishment (Micheli et al., 2021).
- `show_n_rounds`: Usually reported; less often manipulated as a causal variable.

**Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**  
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes backgrounded but rarely treated as main factors.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Occasionally assessed (Micheli et al., 2021), mainly for comparison rather than prediction.
  
**Effectively Missing or Weakly Informed:**  
- Dimensions such as `show_punishment_id` (identity transparency in punishment) and detailed technical aspects of implementation (e.g., interface effects, dynamic parameter adjustment) are rarely manipulated or analyzed causally.
- Impact of chat, information presentation, and real world field variation (e.g., ethnicity, cultural context) is mostly observational, not experimental.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Payoff/efficiency outcomes are infrequently the primary focus;** most studies report on contribution rates, norm compliance, or other behavioral outcomes, so direct evidence for efficiency prediction is patchy.
- **Several high-relevance design dimensions are underexplored** (e.g., identity transparency of punishment, information visibility, reward mechanism comparison) as causal variables.
- **Empirical findings may be highly context-dependent:** Efficiency benefits from punishment often depend critically on the specific cost structure, baseline contribution rates, cognitive environment, and enforceability—limits external validity.
- **Ambiguity exists when generalizing from special institutional contexts** (tax vs. peer punishment, automated fines, voluntary opt-in) to standard PGGs.
- **Rare attention is given to non-lab, larger scale, or culturally diverse settings,** limiting prediction to typical laboratory environments.
- **Adjacency and conceptual variants are common:** Many papers study adjacent games, indirect measures, or animal behavior, providing only peripheral insights for efficiency prediction.
- **Potential for disagreement and ambiguity in findings:** Some papers (e.g., Nhim et al., 2023) directly conflict about whether punishment will increase or decrease efficiency, dependent on costs and institutional structure.
- **Control efficiency is treated variably:** Not all studies report or control for the baseline efficiency, undermining reliable calibration for prediction models.

---

**In summary**: The literature set offers **moderate, not comprehensive, support** for predicting the impact of enabling peer punishment on group efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. Strongest guidance is available for standard, repeated, small-group PGGs under laboratory conditions, especially where punishment is costly but not prohibitively so. The effect depends heavily on institutional details and baseline efficiency, and findings outside these core designs are indirect or only contextually informative. Caution is warranted when extrapolating beyond these conditions, and key prediction-relevant game dimensions remain underexplored.
