# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises a mix of empirical (mostly laboratory experiments, with a field experiment and some survey/hypothetical studies) and theoretical articles. About half the studies are empirical lab experiments in PGGs or closely related social dilemmas; a few employ field designs or scenario/survey approaches. Several papers contribute evolutionary or formal theoretical models of cooperation and punishment, while a minority focus on games or mechanisms only loosely related to public goods games.

The evidence base is relatively broad in scope for the question of punishment and cooperation in social dilemmas but narrower and patchier for the specific task of **predicting the change in group efficiency** (total payoff/optimal payoff) when **peer punishment** is enabled, conditional on game design dimensions and control efficiency. A subset of the papers provide exact or close empirical evidence about efficiency effects in PGGs with peer and institutional punishment; others measure related behavioral or theoretical outcomes, and several discuss only adjacent game structures or mechanisms. There are also a few theory papers that provide conceptual context but no direct empirical evidence.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *Exact* relevance: Several papers (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Castillo et al., 2021; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Zhang & Pei, 2022) use standard or near-standard linear public goods games, both in experiment and theory.
- *Close* relevance: Others (e.g., Becchetti et al., 2018; Castro & Toro, 2008; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020; Voelkl, 2015) use adjacent social dilemma setups (all-or-nothing PGGs, common-pool resource games, trust/bargaining/minigames).
- *Adjacent/Weak*: A handful of studies (Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014; Madeo & Mocenni, 2021; Güth et al., 2007) use games not closely aligned with the canonical PGG structure.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *Exact*: Peer punishment and/or institutional punishment, including endogenous and exogenously assigned punishment, are present in most key empirical and theory papers (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Castillo et al., 2021; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010).
- *Close/Adjacent*: Some papers focus on related sanctioning (redistribution, tax/subsidy, leader punishment) or feedback mechanisms as analogs to punishment (Becchetti et al., 2018; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020).
- *Weak/None*: Several focus on social feedback (not monetary), or on preference for punishment, not its effect.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *Exact*: Group efficiency (total payoff / optimal payoff) is directly measured or modeled in a subset of studies (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Castillo et al., 2021; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Lee & Iwasa, 2014).
- *Close/Adjacent*: Many papers measure behavioral outcomes (contribution, cooperation, honesty, trust) and infer possible efficiency implications but do not report efficiency explicitly.
- *None*: Some studies focus solely on the psychological drivers or frequency of punishment, not group outcomes.

**Summary:**  
- Task-relevant empirical and theoretical evidence exists, but only a minority of papers offer *direct, exact* evidence on treatment efficiency effects of enabling punishment in standard PGGs.
- Many other papers discuss adjacent mechanisms or behavioral outcomes, providing indirect support or caveats.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Efficiency/Payoff Outcomes (Direct Measures):**
  - *Total/group payoff*, *group efficiency*, *welfare*, and *average earnings* are directly measured or modeled in a limited subset of studies (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Castillo et al., 2021; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015; Abbink et al., 2004).
- **Behavioral Outcomes (Non-Payoff):**
  - *Contribution/cooperation rates* (Campos-Vazquez & Mejia, 2016; Noussair et al., 2015; Becchetti et al., 2018), *punishment frequency* (Windmann et al., 2021; Davis & Holt, 1999), *norm compliance*, and related measures dominate the empirical literature.
  - *Personality and attitudes toward punishment* (Roberts et al., 2013; Windmann et al., 2021; Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014).
  - *Trustworthiness, honesty, slack* (Güth et al., 2007; Chen, 2012).
- **Proxies/Adjacencies:**
  - Where efficiency is not measured, increased cooperation/contribution is sometimes taken to imply higher efficiency, but this is not always valid (see Main Findings).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment’s Effect on Efficiency Is Mixed, Highly Sensitive to Game Structure:**
  - In *peer punishment* PGGs, punishment often increases cooperation but **reduces efficiency** because the cost of punishing can outweigh the benefit of increased contributions (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Zhang & Pei, 2022; Abbink et al., 2004).
  - *Institutional/centralized punishment* (a single manager, externally administered) shows a more robust **increase in both contribution and efficiency** (Castillo et al., 2021; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).
  - *Theoretical models* (Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Voelkl, 2015; Lee & Iwasa, 2014) generally indicate that when punishment is strong enough (cost-effective, credible, or graduated), and the initial scope for defection is not overwhelming, efficiency can be increased or even approach optimal levels.
- **Punishment Cost and Effectiveness Matter:**
  - Higher *cost-to-impact* ratios for punishment usually reduce net efficiency gains from enabling punishment—especially in peer punishment regimes (Kocher & Matzat, 2016). More effective or "graduated" forms of punishment and combinations with reward are generally predicted to be better for efficiency (Lee & Iwasa, 2014). Excessively harsh, non-graduated punishment is often inefficient.
- **Peer vs. Centralized Punishment:**
  - *Peer punishment* is especially prone to antisocial use, second-order free-riding, or retaliatory dynamics, which can further suppress group payoff (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Zhang & Pei, 2022).
  - *Centralized/institutional punishment* is less subject to these issues and more likely to deliver efficiency gains, even when the cost structure varies (Castillo et al., 2021).
- **Context, Framing, and Behavioral Mechanisms:**
  - The efficiency effect does not always generalize from lab to field or across framing (Noussair et al., 2015; Becchetti et al., 2018). In some field/framed settings, adding sanctions does not increase cooperation let alone efficiency.
  - The *effectiveness of punishment depends on background conditions*: with high baseline cooperation (or in the presence of voluntary participation mechanisms), punishment may be less needed (Castro & Toro, 2008).
  - *Identity of who can punish, feedback mechanisms (public vs. private),* and *visibility* may moderate outcomes (Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct Guidance:**  
- For *standard peer punishment* in PGGs: Enabling punishment typically **reduces efficiency compared to control**, especially at typical punishment costs, because punishment use is frequent enough to generate costs that are not compensated by the uptick in cooperation. This is empirically found (Kocher & Matzat, 2016) and supported by theoretical review (Zhang & Pei, 2022). *If* punishment is rarely used or highly effective, losses can be minimized and (in theory) efficiency could rise, especially with close-to-optimal baseline cooperation.
- For *centralized/institutional punishment*: There is strong empirical and theoretical support that enabling punishment **increases efficiency versus control** (Castillo et al., 2021; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015), especially when the punishment is sufficiently effective.
- *Reward mechanisms* typically *increase cooperation and efficiency more reliably* than punishment (Kocher & Matzat, 2016), unless the reward level is too low.
- When predicting the efficiency effect of punishment:
  - **Peer punishment**: Expect efficiency to *decrease* relative to control unless the punishment cost is low and/or baseline cooperation is high.
  - **Centralized punishment**: Expect efficiency to *increase* relative to control, relatively insensitive to details of manager selection.
  - The actual efficiency change depends strongly on the **punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and baseline (control) efficiency**.
  - Non-payoff behavioral improvements (higher contribution, more norm compliance) do *not* guarantee efficiency gains; in peer setups, costs can easily overwhelm the benefits.
- Guidance is less reliable for field settings, minigames, or for design dimensions that were not systematically manipulated in the literature.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `all_or_nothing`, `mpcr`: Frequently manipulated or specified in both empirical and theory studies; several models/experiments explicitly test effects across these.
- `punishment_cost` & `punishment_tech`: Directly manipulated or parameterized in multiple studies; shown to moderate efficiency effects of punishment.
- `chat`: Sometimes manipulated (Kocher & Matzat, 2016), but typically absent in efficiency studies.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Examined in a minority of studies (Kocher & Matzat, 2016) with explicit comparison to punishment.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Addressed mainly in theoretical or adjacent studies on feedback/public information (Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020).
  
**Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
- `default_contrib`: Framing of contribution choice is not systematically manipulated, but framing effects are described contextually.
- `punishment_magnitude`: Sometimes mentioned in passing (as “effectiveness”), but less often a focus than punishment cost.
  
**Effectively Missing:**
- Little to no direct evidence on effects of: `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`—except in studies on feedback/social reputation (Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020), which investigate visibility of actions but do not combine it directly with monetary punishment.
- No studies systematically cross all 14 specified design dimensions.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse Direct Evidence for Treatment Efficiency:** Only a subset of the literature directly reports (or models) the change in group efficiency/payoff when peer punishment is enabled, holding the control condition and design dimensions constant.
- **Overreliance on Behavioral Proxies:** Many studies conflate or use contribution/cooperation rate as a proxy for efficiency, but increased cooperation does *not* necessarily translate into increased efficiency (especially for peer punishment).
- **Variation in Punishment Regimes:** Centralized/institutional punishment and peer punishment differ sharply; findings cannot be freely generalized across them.
- **Heterogeneity in Experimental Designs:** Differences across studies in group size, round number, MPCRs, punishment cost structures, and reward modalities complicate cross-study synthesis.
- **Absence of Quantitative Predictive Models:** Few papers provide parameterized, quantitative predictions for efficiency change as a function of design dimensions and control efficiency; most evidence is qualitative or at best comparative.
- **Limited Field Generalizability:** Positive efficiency effects in lab PGGs sometimes fail to appear in field/hybrid or contextually different environments.
- **Feedback and Identity Mechanisms Underexplored:** Most work on visibility, identity, or social feedback (showing summaries, rounds, punishment IDs) concerns non-monetary sanctions and does not jointly manipulate these with monetary punishment.

**In summary:**  
The literature provides partial but not fully systematic support for predicting treatment efficiency effects of enabling punishment, strongest where peer and centralized punishment effects are directly compared in lab PGGs. Findings about efficiency must not be conflated with results about cooperation/contribution rates. Caution is needed in extending parameter estimates outside laboratory contexts and in settings where multiple design dimensions interact. Peer punishment is most often associated with reduced efficiency versus control, except in rare cases of very high control cooperation or highly effective punishment; centralized punishment has a more positive effect on efficiency but also depends on implementation details. Prediction should be based on the structure and cost-effectiveness of the punishment regime, baseline efficiency, and should consider whether the design matches those tested in the available literature.
