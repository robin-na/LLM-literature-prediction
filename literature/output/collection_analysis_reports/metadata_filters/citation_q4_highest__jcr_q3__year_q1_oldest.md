# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base comprises 72 papers, including a diverse mix of empirical laboratory experiments, empirical observational studies, and a substantial body of theoretical and simulation models. The set is **broad** in scope, encompassing work that ranges from standard public goods games (PGGs) with and without punishment, to adjacent social-dilemma settings (e.g., trust games, prisoner's dilemmas, indirect reciprocity frameworks, evolutionary models). The **density of evidence is highest for standard PGGs with peer or institutional punishment**, especially in lab environments—many studies directly manipulate punishment as a treatment and report contribution or efficiency outcomes. However, significant portions of the literature are **theoretical**, providing mechanistic explanations or predictions for the roles of punishment, reward, reputation, norms, and information. 

Some empirical papers provide **direct payoff-based outcomes (group earnings, efficiency)**, while others focus on **non-payoff behavioral outcomes** like contribution rates or punishment frequency. Several theoretical and modeling papers address efficiency or related payoff concepts explicitly, but others focus on evolutionary stability or cooperation prevalence instead. 

# 2) Task Relevance

### a) PGG or Variant
- **Exact relevance**: Many core experimental and theoretical studies are on standard linear PGGs (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Nikiforakis, 2010; Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
- **Close to adjacent relevance**: A substantial number of papers use close variants—public goods with altered incentives, trust games, prisoner's dilemma, indirect reciprocity, reputation games, or evolutionary models that only partially match formal PGG specifics.
- **Some papers are only weakly relevant**: Where neither the game structure nor group interaction closely matches the PGG.

### b) Punishment or Sanctions
- **Exact**: There is strong representation of exact punishment manipulations—where peer or institutional punishment can be enabled/disabled experimentally or modeled (e.g., Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
- **Close to adjacent**: Some papers only discuss punishment indirectly (e.g., via social exclusion, reputation loss, or third-party enforcement).
- **None/weak**: Papers centered on trust, reward, or reputation systems without explicit punishment as a design feature.

### c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome
- **Exact**: Several core experiments and theoretical papers measure or model efficiency as the main outcome (group earnings compared to the cooperative maximum).
- **Close/adjacent**: Many report **contribution rates or cooperation**, inferring efficiency changes but not directly measuring total payoffs (e.g., contribution increases as a proxy for efficiency).
- **Weak/none**: Others focus exclusively on non-payoff behaviors or attitudes (e.g., punishment frequency, trust, norm compliance).

**Overall**: There is **strong direct relevance for all three dimensions** in the central cluster of experimental/theoretical PGG punishment papers, but significant adjacent or weak relevance elsewhere in the set.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

### Payoff-Related Outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, welfare, total earnings)
- **Directly measured**: 
  - Many lab experiments report group efficiency explicitly or calculate it from earnings (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Tyran & Feld, 2006; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
  - Theoretical models often use explicit payoff calculations to derive equilibrium group efficiency (e.g., Gintis, 2000; Cressman et al., 2012).
- **Indirect or inferred**: 
  - In cases where only contributions are reported, efficiency can be inferred if the costs of punishment are accounted for, but this is sometimes missing.
- **Absent or weak**: 
  - Many adjacent or theoretical papers focus on the stability of cooperation or strategy frequencies, not payoffs.

### Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes
- **Frequently reported**:
  - Contribution rates, cooperation rates, frequency or magnitude of punishment, anti-social punishment, retaliation, norm enforcement.
  - The occurrence/rate of these behaviors is often discussed even when efficiency is the target concept.
- **Interpreted cautiously in relation to efficiency**:
  - Many studies caution that higher contributions do not always lead to higher efficiency due to the costs of punishment (e.g., Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).

**Distinction is crucial**: Some papers show that efficiency can decline even as cooperation rises, due to costly punishment or counter-punishment cycles.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Enabling punishment typically increases contributions**: Multiple empirical and theoretical studies show a robust increase in cooperation/contributions when peer or institutional punishment is enabled (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Gintis, 2003; Aoyagi & Fréchette, 2009).
- **Efficiency may increase, but is highly contingent on design details**:
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness** are critical: Too cheap and/or too effective punishment can lead to excessive use, undercutting efficiency despite high contributions (Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
    - **Counterpunishment/retaliation** can reverse efficiency gains: Multi-stage or unconstrained punishment can lead to cycles that reduce both contributions and efficiency (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Janssen & Bushman, 2008).
    - **Information environment/feedback** shapes effects: The format and content of feedback strongly moderate whether punishment increases efficiency (Nikiforakis, 2010). Feedback emphasizing contributions rather than earnings supports higher efficiency.
    - **Punishment type** (pro-social vs. anti-social): Presence of anti-social punishment can eliminate efficiency gains (Rand et al., 2010).
    - **Endogeneity and perception**: Punishment regimes implemented by group vote (endogenous) are more effective at raising efficiency than exogenously imposed ones, especially when mild (Tyran & Feld, 2006).
    - **Network structure and status**: In asymmetric networks, the efficacy of punishment for efficiency is moderated by player status (Eckel et al., 2010).
    - **Monitoring/observability**: Efficiency gains depend on the ability to identify free riders; noisy or uninformative feedback, or limited monitoring, diminishes the benefit of punishment (Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007; Carpenter, 2007).
    - **Group size**: Increasing group size or the inability to observe/punish many group members can moderate or nullify efficiency effects (Carpenter, 2007).

- **Costs of punishment matter**: If punishment is too frequently used or is directed at cooperators (anti-social punishment), the group can be worse off than the control (e.g., Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Rand et al., 2010).
- **Reward mechanisms**: Reward can increase contributions and efficiency but is vulnerable to second-order free-riding (Sasaki & Unemi, 2011; Hauert, 2010); the architecture of the incentives matters.
- **Institutional (legal) punishment**: Even mild, non-deterrent legal punishment, when combined with social punishment, can substantially increase efficiency by raising marginal incentives and reducing enforcement costs (Kube & Traxler, 2011).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Prediction of treatment efficiency from design and control efficiency should:**
    - **Use direct empirical evidence where available**: For standard lab PGGs with clearly specified dimensions (player count, MPCR, punishment cost, information, etc.), empirical studies provide the strongest guidance.
    - **Adjust for punishment design and cost**: Predict higher efficiency when punishment is costly enough to deter overuse but cheap enough to be credible, provided anti-social punishment/counterpunishment are constrained.
    - **Incorporate feedback/monitoring structure**: Expect higher gains in efficiency from punishment when contribution feedback is available and monitoring is broad; expect little or negative gain if information is poor or punishment is unobserved/unguarded.
    - **Account for endogeneity/endogenous law**: When punishment regimes are chosen by subjects, expect higher efficiency than for imposed regimes at similar cost/effectiveness.
    - **Check for anti-social punishment**: If game design allows or incentivizes anti-social punishment, predicted efficiency gains should be heavily discounted and may vanish.
    - **Integrate status/network effects if relevant**: For games with notable status asymmetries or centralized actors, the impact of punishment depends on the status of central figures.
    - **Use caution in inferring from contribution rates alone**: Especially in settings where punishment costs are high, efficiency may stagnate or fall even as contributions rise.
    - **Leverage simulation/theoretical models generically when empirical analogues are absent**, but interpret with caution due to potential measurement mismatch.

- **Only a subset of the 14 design dimensions are robustly supported for direct prediction from this literature**.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed**:
- `player_count` (group size): Heavily studied; effect sometimes moderated by ability to monitor/punish within larger groups.
- `num_rounds`: Repeated games are standard; effect of repetition on contribution decay and efficiency is well documented.
- `mpcr`: Central to payoff incentives; effects of varying MPCR are frequently studied and modeled.
- `punishment_cost`: A major moderator; well established negative relationship with punishment frequency and with net efficiency benefit.
- `punishment_tech` (punishment effectiveness/mechanics): Multiple papers reference difference between peer, institutional, single/multiple stages.
- `show_other_summaries` (feedback): Explicitly shown to affect treatment efficiency (Nikiforakis, 2010).
- `show_punishment_id` (anonymity): Theoretical and some empirical support for anonymity moderating retaliation and thus efficiency (Janssen & Bushman, 2008; Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007).
- `all_or_nothing`: Modeled, but less often tested empirically.

**Indirectly Informed**:
- `chat`: Some studies on communication, but rarely as a primary moderator of efficiency with punishment.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Reward mechanisms are discussed primarily in contrast to punishment (Hauert, 2010), less often manipulated directly alongside punishment in the same study.
- `default_contrib`: Option framing is rare in the PGG-punishment literature.
- `show_n_rounds`: Information on game horizon appears in a few studies, with some evidence it affects end-game behavior.

**Contextually Discussed or Effectively Missing**:
- Precise manipulation of `default_contrib` is rare or absent.
- Simultaneous manipulation of multiple feedback and identification options is uncommon.
- Effects of reward and punishment together are theorized (Cressman et al., 2012), but not empirically tested across multiple parameterizations.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse empirical evidence for some dimensions**: Some design features (default contribution, joint reward and punishment, opt-in/opt-out framing, group composition) are rarely or never manipulated directly alongside punishment in the same study.
- **Measurement inconsistency**: Many papers use contribution rate as a proxy for efficiency or do not report group payoff, making direct translation to efficiency outcomes problematic. Some observed increases in contribution do not translate into increased efficiency due to the cost of sanctions.
- **Retaliation, anti-social punishment, and counterpunishment**: Not always experimentally manipulated; their presence often only inferred or modeled, despite being critical moderators (and sometimes devastating for efficiency).
- **Generality vs. specificity**: Theoretical models generalize beyond lab implementations and may ignore lab idiosyncrasies; conversely, lab results may not transfer to field environments or more complex social dilemmas.
- **Winner’s curse and parameter sensitivity**: Efficiency effects of punishment are often non-monotonic and sensitive to precise parameterizations of cost, group size, and punishment effectiveness.
- **Incomplete information on information**: Feedback structures (`show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`), although shown to be critical, are not always precisely described, limiting extrapolation.
- **Lack of diversity in reward mechanisms**: While theoretical attention has been given to rewards, the joint and comparative effects with punishment are rarely explored with payoff outcomes in empirical papers.

**Summary**: The literature provides robust, multi-method evidence for the conditions under which punishment increases, reduces, or does not affect efficiency in public goods games, conditional on key design dimensions. However, dimensions such as default contribution framing, detailed feedback content/visibility, combined reward/punishment schemes, and real-world complexity are weakly represented or only modeled. Ambiguities and countervailing results (e.g., anti-social punishment reducing efficiency; excessive punishment costs outweighing cooperation gains) must be preserved in any prediction, and efficiency should not be inferred from cooperation rates without careful consideration of punishment costs.
