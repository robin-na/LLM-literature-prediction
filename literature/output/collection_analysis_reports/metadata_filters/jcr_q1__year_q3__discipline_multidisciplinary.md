# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is large and relatively diverse, containing both empirical (lab experiments, field experiments) and theoretical works. The empirical evidence base is strongest for standard public goods games (PGGs), especially repeated games with peer punishment. Many studies provide high-specificity laboratory evidence, complemented by a substantial number of theoretical and computational modeling papers exploring mechanisms. There is also a substantial number of adjacent studies (e.g., threshold PGGs, common-pool resource games, contests, prisoners' dilemmas with punishment options), some of which measure efficiency/payoff and others which focus on behavioral or mechanism-level outcomes.

The literature is most comprehensive regarding standard linear PGGs with or without punishment and the effect of design features such as group size, rounds, MPCR (marginal per-capita return), and punishment cost. There is a relatively broad base of papers considering variants (e.g., threshold PGGs, networked games, leader- or institution-based punishment, spatial and local interactions). Reward mechanisms are sometimes considered as a comparator or in combination with punishment. Some dimensions, such as chat, default contribution framing, or observability of sanctioning, receive less direct empirical attention.

Overall, the evidence base provides a robust platform for theorizing and predicting the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency in PGG-like environments, but coverage is uneven across all 14 design dimensions and varies in outcome specificity (behavioral vs. payoff/efficiency).

# 2) Task Relevance

**Relevance dimensions** (using labels: exact, close, adjacent, weak, none):

- **PGG or Variant:** There is a substantial number of studies with exact PGGs or close variants (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Ozono et al., 2020; Harrell, 2019; Duong & Han, 2021; Salahshour, 2021; Hintze et al., 2020). Some papers use closely related settings (threshold PGGs, ROSCA, resource extraction, contests)—these are labeled close or adjacent depending on structural similarity.
- **Punishment or Sanctions:** Direct manipulation or examination of punishment is frequently present (exact), especially peer or institutional punishment, although some papers test other forms (third-party, exclusion, contest punishment), or discuss payoff analogs (entrance costs, sanctions as exclusion).
- **Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:** A minority of empirical papers report group efficiency, group payoff, or similar directly; many more theoretical works model efficiency or welfare. However, many high-quality behavioral studies measure only contributions, cooperation rates, or punishment frequencies (adjacent or weak for this dimension).

In sum, the literature is *strongly relevant* for the core prediction task in terms of game structure and punishment, but only *modestly or unevenly relevant* regarding direct measurement and prediction of efficiency outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Direct Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes**: Some studies provide direct measures of efficiency as defined (group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum)—e.g., Kamijo et al. (2020), Ozono et al. (2020), Harrell (2019), Hintze et al. (2020), Heine & Strobel (2020), Lohse & Waichman (2020). Theoretical studies frequently model efficiency or average group payoff.
- **Related Payoff Outcomes**: Several papers report average group payoff, welfare, surplus, or achievement (threshold games) as close proxies, often convertible to efficiency with additional assumptions.
- **Behavioral Outcomes**: A majority of lab studies, including many referencing punishment, emphasize contribution/cooperation rates, compliance, or punishment frequency rather than efficiency or group payoff—this includes studies focusing on behavioral mechanisms, emotions, and motivations.
- **Distributional Outcomes**: Some work highlights the effect of punishment on outcome inequality (e.g., Tsvetkova et al., 2020).
- **Absence of Payoff Outcomes**: Many adjacent or weakly relevant studies provide only behavioral evidence, which cannot be uncritically extrapolated to efficiency, especially where punishment is costly or the effect of increased contribution is ambiguous for group welfare.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Effect of Enabling Peer Punishment on Efficiency:**
- Enabling peer punishment **often increases contributions** but does **not always increase efficiency or group payoff**, and may at times decrease it due to the cost of administering punishment (Kamijo et al., 2020; Hintze et al., 2020; Heine & Strobel, 2020; Lohse & Waichman, 2020).
    - In standard linear PGGs with moderately high MPCR and moderate-cost, moderate-impact punishment, the **efficiency gain from peer punishment is usually positive or small** (Harrell, 2019; Hintze et al., 2020), but **becomes null or negative** where punishment is costly or coordination on prosocial targets is poor.

- **Reward mechanisms** often outperform or more reliably increase efficiency (Kamijo et al., 2020; Ozono et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2018; Góis et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2021).
    - Where **reward is available**, it can compensate for inefficiency, especially when the public good is itself inefficient (Kamijo et al., 2020).
- **Design Features Moderating Punishment Effect:**
    - **Game Inefficiency or Local/Global Incentives:** When contributing to the public good is locally inefficient (MPCR < 1 or negative local returns), punishment can fail to increase—and may decrease—efficiency (Kamijo et al., 2020; Ozono et al., 2020).
    - **Punishment Cost and Effectiveness:** Lower punishment cost and higher impact promote cooperation and efficiency, but very low cost can trigger antisocial punishment (Salahshour, 2021; Hintze et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021).
        - There is a sweet spot: *too costly* → little effect; *too cheap* → antisocial punishment dominates, reducing efficiency.
    - **Structure of Punishment (peer vs. leader/institutional):** Centralized/leader punishment can increase efficiency more than peer punishment due to lower redundancy and coordination cost (Harrell, 2019; Duong & Han, 2021).
    - **Observability and Norm Constraint:** Institutional or social mechanisms that limit antisocial punishment or allow conditional strategies (i.e., observability, reputational transparency) tend to promote efficiency gains from punishment (García & Traulsen, 2019; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- **Contextual Moderators:**
    - **Anti-social or counter-punishment** (punishment targeting high contributors, retaliation) can destroy efficiency gains (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Li et al., 2021).
    - **Social value orientation or competitive norms**: Where players are competitive rather than prosocial or individualist, punishment can worsen inefficiency (Honjo & Kubo, 2020).
    - **Institutional corruption, poor monitoring, or weak alignment with group welfare**: Institutional punishment can lose efficiency benefits if it is corruptible or not transparent (Lee et al., 2019).
- **Specific Variants and Delimited Findings:**
    - In contest or competitive environments, punishment can increase inefficiency via over-contribution (Heine & Strobel, 2020).
    - In threshold public goods and collective-risk dilemmas, punishment can help sustain high achievement (proxy for efficiency), especially at high risk, but the effect is complex and often balanced by punishment costs (Góis et al., 2019; Lohse & Waichman, 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Baseline (control) efficiency, group size, MPCR, punishment cost/effectiveness, and structure of punishment institution** are the *best-informed predictors* for the outcome of enabling peer punishment.
- **If control efficiency is low but the public good is locally efficient (MPCR moderately high):**
    - Adding peer punishment is *likely* to increase efficiency modestly, provided punishment cost is not excessive and antisocial punishment is minimized.
    - However, gains may be *partially or fully offset* by punishment costs. **Predicted efficiency gain should be dampened compared to behavioral increases in contribution** (Harrell, 2019; Hintze et al., 2020; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- **If control efficiency is already high, or the public good is locally or globally inefficient:**
    - **Expect little or no efficiency gain (possibly even a negative effect) from enabling punishment.**
    - **Reward-based interventions** or institutionally constrained punishment may be superior for efficiency (Kamijo et al., 2020; Ozono et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2018).
- **Design specifics:**
    - *Lower punishment cost* and *higher impact per cost* favor efficiency, but only up to the point where antisocial punishment risk does not rise.
    - *Centralized/leader punishment* or mechanisms supporting coordinated, prosocial targeting improve efficiency relative to peer punishment, which suffers more from redundant and retaliatory punishment (Harrell, 2019; Duong & Han, 2021).
- **Game structure:**
    - **Variants with threshold production, contests, or possible individual solutions**: Enabling punishment may amplify inefficiency or have ambiguous effects, particularly if it triggers within-group competition or feuding (Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Heine & Strobel, 2020).
- **Key caveat:** *Increased contribution* should **not** be equated with *increased efficiency* unless punishment costs are small relative to contribution gains.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- **player_count, num_rounds:** Frequently manipulated/recorded in lab and theory studies; group size effects are generally well-documented.
- **mpcr:** Central in all PGGs and most theoretical studies as a moderator of both baseline efficiency and the effect of punishment.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Detailed analysis of cost, cost-to-impact ratio, and technological structure (peer, leader, institution) in several highly relevant studies.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Reward often studied as comparator or supplement to punishment.
- **all_or_nothing (vs. continuous):** Less frequently a focal moderator, but several studies alternate between binary and continuous versions.

**Indirectly Informed:**
- **show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id, show_n_rounds:** Information structure is sometimes addressed, especially regarding feedback and observability, but evidence is scattered.
- **default_contrib:** Framing effects less prominently isolated; only contextually discussed.

**Only Contextually Discussed or Missing:**
- **chat:** Discussed as (not) present; rarely a focal variable for efficiency outcomes, but more for behavioral cooperation.
- **punishment magnitude* (sometimes reported as cost-to-fine ratio, but not always separately manipulated).
- **reward magnitude:** Likewise seldom separate from reward cost.
- **Dimension interaction:** Most studies do not provide rigorous interaction effects for all design features due to feasible experimental limits.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavioral vs. Efficiency Outcomes:** A large fraction of the literature focuses primarily on behavioral measures (contribution, cooperation, punishment frequency) and only secondarily, if at all, on efficiency/payoffs. This limits quantitative prediction reliability for efficiency effects; one cannot assume that increased contribution translates to increased efficiency when punishment is costly.
- **Context and Scope:** Many findings are derived from small-group lab studies with stylized settings; generalizing to larger groups, complex real-world PGGs, or highly networked environments should be done with caution.
- **Incomplete Coverage of Prediction Dimensions:** Some design features (e.g., chat, default contribution, observational structure) are not systematically tested for their interaction with punishment or their impact on efficiency.
- **Variant Generalizability:** Results from contests, threshold games, ROSCAs, or other adjacent designs may not generalize to linear PGGs, especially for efficiency outcomes, due to structural differences (e.g., externality of punishment, presence of outside options).
- **Heterogeneity and Conflict:** There is **clear evidence of context-dependence and occasional contradiction** (e.g., punishment reduces efficiency via costly feuds, antisocial punishment, or in locally inefficient PGGs; other times, punishment increases efficiency with careful institution design).
- **Limited Cross-Dimensional Data:** While individual effects of MPCR, group size, punishment cost, etc., are somewhat documented, **higher-order interactions among dimensions** (e.g., how the effect of group size depends on MPCR and punishment cost jointly) are not generally available in the literature.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature provides robust and nuanced evidence for predicting the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. Key prediction moderators are MPCR, punishment cost/effectiveness, institutional structure of punishment, and baseline efficiency. However, efficiency (payoff-based) outcomes do not always track cooperation/contribution rates, and costs of punishment frequently offset efficiency gains. The evidence base is weaker for design features like chat, framing, and all interaction effects across 14 design dimensions. Prediction should take a cautious, qualified approach, emphasizing context, design, and observed baseline efficiency.
