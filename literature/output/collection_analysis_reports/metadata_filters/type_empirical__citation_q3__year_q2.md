# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large and comprehensive for laboratory economic experiments, with a predominant focus on **empirical, experimental, and especially lab-based public goods games (PGGs) and variants**. The majority of papers are **empirical**, with a very small minority that are theory-based or observational. Across the set, there is heavy coverage of standard and variant PGGs, punishment and sanctioning mechanisms (both costly and costless, peer and centralized, endogenous and exogenous), and a wide array of **game design dimensions**.

**Payoff-related outcomes** (efficiency, group payoff) are directly measured and analyzed in many core studies, although a substantial subset of the literature focuses instead on **non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (contribution rates, cooperation, frequency of punishment, norm compliance etc.). There is also considerable examination of moderators and mechanisms—including network structure, information conditions, legitimacy, authority structure, group heterogeneity, and procedural framing—that affect the relationship between punishment and efficiency.

Overall, for the task of predicting the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency (relative to a control with punishment disabled), this evidence base is both **broad and deep** in coverage of standard PGGs, most relevant dimensions, and a spectrum of institutional and contextual variations.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**
- *exact*: The **core of the paper set** focuses on standard PGGs or direct close variants (e.g., continuous or binary contributions, linear or threshold payoffs). Topical coverage of PGG and CPR (common-pool resource) games is extensive.
- *close*: There is also coverage of threshold public goods, snowdrift games, team production/team reward games, and multi-firm collusion games that are structurally akin to PGGs.
- *adjacent/weak*: A smaller subliterature covers pairwise PDs, dictator games, and third-party punishment games—not directly usable for quantitative prediction, but useful for mechanism understanding.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**
- *exact*: Costly **peer punishment**, centralized punishment, institution-enabled punishment, exogenous and probabilistic punishment, and costless (moral or social) sanctions are all **directly studied**. Some papers also examine variations (second- and third-order punishment, restriction to legitimate punishers, antisocial punishment, networked punishment structure).
- *close/adjacent*: Many studies examine reward, exclusion, communication as alternatives/substitutes/complements to punishment, or reputation, exit/threat, or mediation as sanction-like mechanisms.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
- *exact*: A core subset provides **direct, quantitative efficiency or group payoff outcomes** with and without punishment, often as a ratio to the maximum possible.
- *close*: Many others report average group earnings or welfare, which are convertible to efficiency measures in standard designs.
- *adjacent/weak*: A notable number only report behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, punishment frequency) and must not be conflated with efficiency effects; some do not report any efficiency or payoff at all.

**Summary:** The paper set is **highly relevant** for the prediction task along all three target dimensions, with some sections (esp. for non-payoff outcomes or in adjacent game classes) requiring caution and explicit acknowledgment of relevance limitations.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (directly relevant):**
- **Efficiency/group payoff**: Defined as total group earnings as a fraction of the full cooperation optimum; reported or directly calculable in many studies (e.g., Dickinson et al., 2015; Kamijo et al., 2014; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Average earnings/welfare/surplus**: Used as proxies for efficiency, particularly when the experimental design is standard and mapping is clear.
- **Related outcome**: Some studies report profit/loss or total coins generated.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (indirectly relevant or proxies):**
- **Contribution or cooperation rates**: Frequently used, but must be clearly distinguished from efficiency—higher contributions do not always yield higher group payoff if the cost of punishment is high or antisocial punishment is common (Simpson et al., 2017).
- **Punishment frequency/volume**: Used to discuss mechanisms; the effect on efficiency depends on cost and targeting.
- **Norm compliance, trust, willingness to punish, retaliation**: Mechanistically informative, but not directly efficiency outcomes.

**Notably, some** *behaviorally focused* studies (e.g., Cheung, 2014; Skatova & Ferguson, 2013) do not report payoff-based outcomes at all, and thus are less directly informative for downstream efficiency predictions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical Findings

### Punishment Generally Increases Cooperation, but Efficiency Gains Are Conditional

- **Costly peer punishment** *often increases cooperation rates*, but efficiency gains are not guaranteed and critically depend on the cost/technology of punishment, group structure, and the prevalence of antisocial/retributive punishment (Simpson et al., 2017; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- The **efficiency effect of punishment is highly context- and design-dependent**. Key moderating factors include:
    - **Punishment cost-to-impact ratio**: Lower-cost/more-effective punishment generally yields higher efficiency (Qin & Wang, 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014), whereas high-cost or misapplied punishment can negate or even reverse efficiency gains (Simpson et al., 2017; Boosey & Isaac, 2016).
    - **Network structure and symmetry**: Incomplete or asymmetric punishment networks undermine efficiency gains, mostly due to under- or mis-targeted punishment and retaliation (Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
    - **Institutional design**: Centralized or leader-based punishment, especially when the leader is prosocial, tends to yield higher efficiency with lower punishment costs (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017). Endogenous institution formation (by vote) often augments both cooperation and efficiency (Dickinson et al., 2015; Gürerk, 2013).
    - **Feedback and information**: Full, accurate feedback on contributions and punishment supports legitimate/prosocial punishment and higher efficiency (Faillo et al., 2013; Nicklisch et al., 2016). Noisy or incomplete information leads to misapplied punishment and reduced efficiency.
    - **Endowment heterogeneity**: Group heterogeneity (in endowments or returns) may undermine the positive effect of punishment, especially if information about endowments is absent during punishment (Kingsley, 2016; Kube et al., 2015).
    - **Retaliation/antisocial punishment**: When antisocial punishment or cycles of retaliation prevail, efficiency gains are lower or negative (Simpson et al., 2017; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - **Non-material/costless punishment**: Costless punishment (moral disapproval, reputation, approval/disapproval points) does not harm efficiency and may improve it, especially relative to costly punishment (Simpson et al., 2017; Dugar, 2013).

- **Reward vs. punishment**: In some designs, rewards (material or costless) and communication can achieve similar or greater efficiency gains than punishment, particularly in homogeneous groups (Gangadharan et al., 2017; Dilar et al., 2015).

- **Punishment effectiveness varies with baseline efficiency and group characteristics**: Where baseline (control) efficiency is high, further efficiency gains from punishment are limited or negative, especially if the marginal benefit of increased cooperation does not offset punishment costs (Jiang et al., 2013).

### Specific Game Design Moderators

- **Player Count**: Some evidence that effectiveness of punishment (especially targeted) increases in larger groups (Roux & Thöni, 2015), though complexity, monitoring, and antisocial punishment can pose risks in larger n.
- **Number of Rounds**: Longer games allow time for punishment to raise efficiency over time as costly enforcement declines due to stabilized high cooperation (Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Chat/communication**: When combined with punishment, communication further boosts efficiency; by itself, chat can be highly effective, especially in homogeneous settings (Gangadharan et al., 2017), and can sometimes substitute for punishment.
- **Punishment legitimacy and targeting rules**: Restricting punishment rights to high contributors (legitimate punishment) and providing full feedback reliably improve efficiency over baseline or unrestricted peer punishment (Faillo et al., 2013).
- **Endogenous rule choice**: Allowing players to vote for or choose punishment institutions increases acceptance and efficiency (Dickinson et al., 2015; Gürerk, 2013).
- **Leader/centralized punishment**: Prosocial leader as sole punisher can outperform peer punishment (Harrell & Simpson, 2016); designated punishers can reduce wasted punishment and boost efficiency (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017).
- **Punishment resource abundance**: Efficiency can be undermined if punishment resources are scarce and improperly targeted (Ozono et al., 2017).

### Design Features That Can Reduce or Reverse Efficiency Gains

- **Harmed minority or payoff heterogeneity**: Presence of a group member harmed by the public good neutralizes or reverses the usual positive effect of punishment on efficiency (Dekel et al., 2017).
- **High error rate, misapplied sanctions**: Judicial error, especially type I errors (punishing cooperators), directly reduces efficiency (Markussen et al., 2016).
- **Imperfect monitoring, incomplete punishment networks**: Partial monitoring can backfire and lower efficiency, while full monitoring paired with punishment can restore efficiency (De Geest et al., 2017).
- **Multi-stage/open retaliation environments**: Allowing repeated punishment rounds or explicit opportunities for counter-punishment can undermine efficiency due to feuds (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Kamei & Putterman, 2015).
- **Weak, immaterial, or non-binding sanctions**: Symbolic, costless punishment tools (emoticons, smilies) are inconsistent or ineffective at raising efficiency (Peeters & Vorsatz, 2013).
- **Subject pool differences**: Efficiency gains from punishment are often absent or reversed in general population samples compared to student samples, stemming from antisocial punishment and low responsiveness (Bortolotti et al., 2015).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Rule:**
- *Enabling costly peer punishment in standard PGGs typically raises cooperation rates, but the resulting effect on efficiency is conditional and non-monotonic—punishment can increase, have no effect, or decrease efficiency, depending on design details and the baseline (control) efficiency.*

### When Efficiency Gains are Likely

- **Standard linear PGG with homogeneous endowments, full information, moderate-to-low punishment costs (high punishment effectiveness), and symmetric punishment networks:**
    - Expect a moderate to substantial **increase in efficiency** when enabling peer punishment, assuming baseline efficiency is low or moderate (Dickinson et al., 2015; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017).
    - Gains are highest with longer games (see num_rounds), partner matching, and legitimacy constraints on punishment (Faillo et al., 2013).
    - Gains improve further with endogenous institution choice or when moral/social (costless) judgments are used instead of material punishment (Simpson et al., 2017; Dugar, 2013).

### When Efficiency Gains are Small or Absent

- **High-cost or low-punishment-effectiveness environment**; existence of substantial antisocial or misapplied punishment; asymmetrical or incomplete punishment network; high error rates; or in heterogeneous/harmed-minority groups.
    - Efficiency effects of punishment range from *neutral* to *negative* (Simpson et al., 2017; Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Kingsley, 2016; Dekel et al., 2017).

- **If the control game is already near-maximally efficient**, punishment yields little or no further increase and may even reduce efficiency due to unnecessary punishment costs (Jiang et al., 2013; Grimalda et al., 2016).

- **When group composition or the subject pool is nonstandard**, e.g., representative adult samples, expect lower or negative efficiency effects compared to student samples due to more frequent antisocial punishment (Bortolotti et al., 2015).

### When Enabling Punishment Can Reduce Efficiency

- **Costless or non-targeted punishment** can lead to over-punishment, retaliation, or 'cold glow' effects, driving efficiency below baseline (Simpson et al., 2017; Ouss & Peysakhovich, 2015).
- **Environments with opportunity for antisocial punishment** or cycles of retaliation (especially with multiple punishment stages/known punisher identity) can experience net negative effects on efficiency (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).

➤ **Dimensions that are consistently critical for prediction:**
- **mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, player_count, num_rounds, network structure, information/feedback features, endowment and payoff heterogeneity.**
- **Control efficiency** (no punishment) is a useful baseline, but must be interpreted conditionally; it is *not* a guaranteed lower bound when punishment can lead to over-punishment or retaliation (Boosey & Isaac, 2016).

➤ **When using the literature to guide predictions:**
- Use studies **with direct, quantitative efficiency measurements** and similar design dimensions as the primary reference points.
- Explicitly factor in the design dimensions and moderators mapped above; avoid inferring efficiency change from contribution rate alone unless punishment is non-costly or material cost is negligible.
- If the empirical basis is thin for certain combinations of design dimensions (e.g., incomplete network + high heterogeneity + few rounds), assign higher uncertainty and allow for the possibility of negative, null, or even perverse effects.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count, num_rounds:** Nearly all relevant studies specify group size and number of rounds, often varying these dimensions.
- **chat:** Several studies manipulate or report communication/chat as a critical moderator of efficiency effects.
- **mpcr:** Marginal per-capita return is specified, varied, and shown to moderate both baseline efficiency and the impact of punishment.
- **all_or_nothing, default_contrib:** Action space (e.g., binary vs. continuous, default framing) is generally reported; some studies specifically test action space effects.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Nearly all key studies report and sometimes manipulate punishment cost and the cost-to-impact (fee-to-fine) ratio; central for mapping to efficiency.
- **punishment_exists, punishment_tech:** The presence, type, and effectiveness of punishment are universally reported in direct-relevance studies.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Less commonly manipulated but included in some studies (especially reward vs. punishment comparisons).
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Feedback, transparency, and informational design are major emphasis areas—full feedback and transparency often moderate punishment’s effect on efficiency.

**Indirectly / Contextually Discussed or Missing:**
- **default_contrib:** Only a minority of studies manipulate default framing; most use opt-in (default keep).
- **all_or_nothing:** Most use continuous contributions, but some systematic studies of binary vs. continuous designs exist.
- **Network structure (who can punish whom):** Directly addressed in some studies (Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
- **Endowment and benefit heterogeneity:** Key modulator for certain studies; not always specified or manipulated in all.
- **show_punishment_id (identity transparency):** Manipulated in some studies, often as part of higher-order punishment or retaliation mechanism research.
- **Chat and communication structure:** Studied but sometimes as a binary feature (present/absent) rather than along a nuanced spectrum.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Context and Institutional Scope:** Most findings are from controlled lab settings with student samples; generalizability to field or culturally diverse environments, or to populations with different norms (e.g., general population, professional or real-world groups), is less certain (Bortolotti et al., 2015; Grimalda et al., 2016).
- **Overreliance on Studied Parameter Ranges:** Some design dimensions (e.g., extreme values of punishment cost/tech, very large or very small groups, very long or short time horizons) are underexplored; extrapolation should be approached cautiously.
- **Lack of Direct Payoff Outcomes in Some Subfields:** Several published studies focus exclusively on behavioral outcomes or beliefs, limiting their utility for payoff-based prediction unless mapping to efficiency is reliable and linear.
- **Ambiguity from Contradictory Findings:** When conditions for punishment effectiveness are not met (asymmetry, heterogeneity, incomplete information, antisocial punishment), findings conflict; no single summary applies in all cases.
- **Complex Interactions Among Moderators:** Efficiency effects often depend on *interactions* among design features (e.g., legitimacy × feedback, endogeneity × network structure), making predictions in unstudied combinations less certain.
- **Sparse Evidence for Some Dimensions:** Some dimensions (fine-grained feedback structures, very high-order punishment, complex network topologies, endogenous partner selection) have limited empirical study.
  
**Bottom line:** While there is strong and broad literature support for modeling and predicting the effect of peer punishment on efficiency across common PGG designs, the true effect is governed by a complex, interacting set of game parameters and context-specific moderators. Predictive modeling should incorporate these moderators explicitly, avoid overgeneralizing from behavioral to payoff outcomes, and acknowledge greater uncertainty in under-studied regions of the design space.
