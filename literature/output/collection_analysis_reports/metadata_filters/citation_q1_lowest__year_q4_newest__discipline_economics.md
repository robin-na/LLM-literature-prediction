# Evidence Base

The paper set is substantial, comprising 117 sources, and predominantly consists of empirical experimental (lab) studies, with a sizable subset of theoretical contributions and a smaller number of adjacent or field experiments. The empirical base is broad in terms of experiment variations, with a strong emphasis on standard public goods games (PGGs) and close variants. Many studies manipulate the presence and structure of punishment, and a considerable fraction measure payoff-relevant outcomes, including group efficiency, earnings, or surplus. However, a significant share of papers examine only non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates, norm compliance, or punishment frequency), or study adjacent environments such as trust games, CPR games, and team production tasks. Theoretical contributions are present and are mostly well-grounded in the economic theory of cooperation and institutional design. Overall, the evidence base is strong and highly relevant for the prediction task, albeit with variation in the directness and detail of what each dimension is able to inform.

# Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**: The majority of the literature is directly about standard PGGs or close variants (`exact` or `close`). A further, smaller set is `adjacent`, focusing on related social dilemmas (trust games, CPR, market externalities). A minority are more distant.
- **punishment_or_sanctions**: Many papers are `exact` in testing peer or institutional punishment as a main treatment. Some examine endogenous institution formation, ostracism, or centralized punishment (`close`). Adjacent studies consider reward, withdrawal, or less-standard forms (tax/regulatory, social exclusion).
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: About half the papers (`exact` or `close`) report group efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or comparable outcomes. The remainder focus on contribution rates and compliance or are `adjacent` and do not measure payoff-based outcomes.

In summary, the core of the set is highly relevant to the task, with a meaningful minority only able to support prediction indirectly.

# Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**: Group efficiency, group payoff, average earnings, surplus, and total coins/resources generated are the primary dependent variables for prediction. These are central in papers such as Jiang & Wang (2024), Chen (2022), JARUNGRATTANAPONG (2022), Ye et al. (2023), Peng & Fan (2023), Kamei (2024), Joseph et al. (2025), Krügel & Maaser (2025), Botelho et al. (2022), Kim et al. (2025), Cobo-Reyes et al. (2022), Huang et al. (2024, theory), and others.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**: Many papers measure contribution or cooperation rates, as well as punishment and compliance frequencies, but do not report efficiency (e.g., Coutts (2024), Alt et al. (2023), Hoeft & Mill (2024), Hopp & Süss (2024)).
- **Mixed outcome reporting**: Some report both payoffs and contributions but focus analysis on only one.
- **Theory papers**: These typically provide equilibrium, welfare, or efficiency arguments without empirical data.

It is crucial to note that only papers reporting group payoff or efficiency outcomes can directly inform the prediction task. Papers focusing solely on contributions provide only indirect guidance.

# Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Directionality and Mechanisms

- **Punishment often increases contributions, but efficiency gains depend on cost and targeting.** Several studies (e.g., Jiang & Wang, 2024; Kamei, 2024; JARUNGRATTANAPONG, 2022; Joseph et al., 2025) find that enabling punishment robustly increases group efficiency relative to no-punishment controls, especially under canonical lab conditions with moderate or deterrent punishment costs and well-targeted sanctioning.
- **Costly punishment can offset or even outweigh benefits.** Multiple studies (Chen, 2022; Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Calabuig et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2025; Nhim et al., 2023) document that when punishment is costly and/or frequently used, the direct losses from punishment expenditures can exceed the gains from higher cooperation, resulting in net lower efficiency compared to the control. This is further exemplified when anti-social punishment is prevalent or punishment is institutionalized in a costly, rigid way.
- **Reward mechanisms may outperform punishment.** Some studies (Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022) demonstrate that enabling reward (especially majority-vote or centralized rewards) increases efficiency more than punishment, particularly in heterogeneous groups.
- **Punishment effectiveness depends on severity, targeting, and institutional structure.** The positive effect of punishment on efficiency is strongest (and sometimes only present) when punishment is highly deterrent but used sparingly (Kamei, 2024; Huang et al., 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025), when it is targeted at low contributors (Huang et al., 2024, theory), or when the punishment institution is centralized or complete (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Bühren et al., 2025).
- **Moderators include MPCR, network structure, representation, group heterogeneity, and communication.** The efficiency effects of punishment vary with group size (player_count), marginal per-capita return (mpcr), punishment cost and magnitude, completeness of punishment network (Peng & Fan, 2023; Bühren et al., 2025), information structure, decision structure (self-representation vs. representation; Kim et al., 2025), open vs. closed group membership (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022), and cognitive ability (Ye et al., 2023).
- **Under certain designs, punishment reduces efficiency.** Costly, frequent, or anti-social punishment can result in net efficiency losses (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Calabuig et al., 2024; Del Ponte et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2025; Herne et al., 2022).
- **Theoretical and simulation results** reinforce these empirical findings: targeted, minimal punishment can produce full cooperation and maximal efficiency if properly calibrated (Huang et al., 2024; Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Camera & Gioffré, 2025), but poorly targeted or excessively costly punishment is less effective or even detrimental.

# Prediction Guidance

**How to use this literature for predicting PGG efficiency with punishment enabled, given game design and control efficiency:**

- **If control efficiency is low and design parameters match canonical PGGs** (moderate MPCR, moderate group size, no extreme heterogeneity, punishment is deterrent, cost is not excessive):
    - *Empirical evidence supports a robust uplift in efficiency when punishment is enabled*, especially if punishment is well-targeted, institutionalized, or only slightly costly (Jiang & Wang, 2024; Kamei, 2024; JARUNGRATTANAPONG, 2022; Joseph et al., 2025; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; theoretical support: Huang et al., 2024).
    - *Predicted efficiency* should be substantially higher than control, approaching or achieving the social optimum if punishment is minimal but sufficient.
- **If punishment is very costly, anti-social, or used frequently:**
    - *Several studies report net decreases in efficiency* due to resource destruction (Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Calabuig et al., 2024; Del Ponte et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2025; Herne et al., 2022), even as contributions rise. Prediction should factor in possible net losses relative to the control, especially in the presence of antisocial punishment or heterogeneous returns (Chen, 2022; Peng & Fan, 2023; Nhim et al., 2023).
- **If the punishment institution is complete, centralized, deterrent, and costless or nearly so:**
    - *Near-maximal efficiency can be expected* (Kamei, 2024; Bühren et al., 2025; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2024 theory).
- **Other moderators (from design dimensions):**
    - **Representation** (Kim et al., 2025): Efficiency gains from punishment are lower or negative under representative decision structures than self-representation.
    - **Punishment Network Structure** (Peng & Fan, 2023; Bühren et al., 2025): Incomplete or fragmented punishment networks can suppress the efficiency gains expected from enabling punishment.
    - **Group Heterogeneity** (Peng & Fan, 2023; Chen, 2022): Heterogeneous MPCR or endowments can reduce or reverse efficiency gains from punishment due to anti-social punishment or misalignment of incentives.
    - **Cognitive and cultural moderators**: Cognitive ability, cultural norms, the decision process (democracy vs. exogenous institution), and prior play history moderate the magnitude and reliability of effects.
- **Reward alternatives:** If efficient, cost-effective reward mechanisms exist, they may outperform punishment for increasing efficiency, especially in heterogeneous settings (Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022).
- **If baseline/control efficiency is already high:** The marginal benefit of adding punishment may be limited, as group norms or alternative mechanisms (communication, coalition, etc.) may already sustain cooperation.

# Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed (strong empirical/theoretical coverage):**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (incl. cost-effectiveness ratio), `reward_exists`, `punishment_exists`, `reward_cost`, `all_or_nothing` (continuous vs. discrete), `chat` (no/yes), `show_n_rounds`

**Indirectly Informed or Only in Context:**
- `default_contrib` / `default_contrib_prop` (few papers directly manipulate default framing, but theory suggests potential framing effects)
- `show_other_summaries` and `show_punishment_id` (information feedback and identity display are variably present; some studies manipulate transparency, but efficiency effects are less directly reported)
- `punishment_magnitude`, `reward_magnitude` (very rarely varied independently; most studies report one punishment cost-impact ratio)
- `reward_tech` (relevant in reward/punishment comparison studies)

**Effectively Missing:**
- Some studies contextualize or describe design choices but lack variation or systematic analysis on certain dimensions (e.g., default contribution, show_punishment_id).

**Findings on dimensions:**
- **player_count, num_rounds**: Larger groups and repeated play can either strengthen or weaken punishment's effects; smaller groups may sustain efficiency without punishment in some contexts.
- **mpcr**: Efficiency gains from punishment are higher when MPCR is moderate-to-high.
- **punishment_cost/tech**: Lower punishment cost relative to impact increases likelihood that punishment raises efficiency. If cost is high, efficiency gains are less likely.
- **all_or_nothing**: Most findings are from continuous-contribution games; all-or-nothing can increase unpredictability and amplify inefficiency from punishment.
- **chat, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Communication and transparency generally improve cooperation and can amplify or substitute for punishment; less direct evidence on their interaction with efficiency.
- **reward_exists**: Where compared, reward can be as effective or more effective than punishment in some settings, especially under heterogeneity.

# Important Limitations

- **Context specificity:** Many robust findings hold within specific parameter combinations (cost/impact of punishment, group composition, institutional structure) and may not generalize. Heterogeneity in MPCR, group size, or punishment network can overturn standard results.
- **Ambiguous net effects:** While the behavioral response to punishment (higher contributions) is common, the net effect on efficiency can be positive, neutral, or negative depending on costs, targeting, and frequency—requiring caution in extrapolation.
- **Absence of complete design dimension variation:** Not all 14 prediction variables are systematically analyzed across studies; some combine design features or lack coverage of framing/feedback/identity dimensions.
- **Behavioral-outcome bias in some papers:** A minority of contributions measure only non-payoff outcomes (contributions, compliance, norm adherence), which do not permit precise prediction of treatment efficiency changes.
- **Conflict and cultural/psychological moderators:** Several studies highlight contexts (social identity, cultural norms, prior exposure, cognitive ability) that sharply moderate the effectiveness and efficiency of punishment, yet these factors are not directly captured in standard design variables.
- **Adjacency limits:** Outcomes from adjacent games (trust games, CPR, beauty contest, coordination, team production) provide mechanism insight but have more limited transferability to PGG efficiency predictions.
- **Downstream ambiguity:** Some settings (e.g., voluntary/pledge-based public goods, non-targeted or group-wide punishment, multilevel or decentralized games) yield either null or negative effects and illustrate that adding punishment does not uniformly increase efficiency—even when contributions rise.

# Summary

The literature analyzed here offers robust, direct empirical and theoretical evidence on the effect of enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. The best-supported generalization is that peer or centralized punishment increases group efficiency relative to no-punishment controls when the punishment is not overly costly, is well-targeted, and is institutionalized to maximize deterrence per unit cost. However, high punishment costs, anti-social use, fragmented institutions, or group heterogeneity (in MPCR or endowments) can eliminate or reverse these efficiency gains. The net efficiency effect is thus contingent and can be reliably predicted only with close attention to game design dimensions and the control (no-punishment) efficiency. The literature is particularly informative for standard lab PGGs with well-documented parameters but leaves important design dimensions—such as default contribution framing, identity transparency, and information feedback—less fully explored. Finally, findings from non-payoff or adjacent studies, while directionally supportive, should be interpreted as qualitative guidance rather than quantitative prediction of efficiency outcomes when punishment is enabled.
