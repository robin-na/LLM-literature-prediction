# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is extensive (145 papers), comprising both **empirical (lab and field experiments, observational studies)** and **theoretical (formal models and simulations)** research. The evidence base is **broad in design coverage** but varies in directness to the prediction task. There is a strong representation of **standard public goods games (PGG), their close variants, and adjacent social dilemma contexts**. A substantial portion of the papers focus on **direct, empirically measured impacts of peer punishment on group efficiency**—the primary outcome of interest. Others investigate mechanisms, moderators, or non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation rates, punishment frequency) without always reporting efficiency per se. A subset of the literature includes **field experiments and heterogeneity in context, sanction technology, reward options, communication, and population structure**.

Papers range from detailed experimental reports (with direct control vs. punishment efficiency data), through theoretical/simulation work mapping efficiency as a function of design dimensions, to meta-analytical syntheses, but also include numerous papers that are only **tangentially relevant**—either measuring non-payoff outcomes, focusing on adjacent games, or omitting punishment or PGG elements.

---

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) pgg_or_variant:**  
- ***exact:*** The set contains many papers with exact PGG designs or very close variants (repeated, one-shot, with/without communication, various group sizes, etc.), offering robust evidence for the target context.
- ***close/adjacent:*** Some studies use Prisoner’s Dilemma, threshold games, or coordination/contest games, providing useful but less directly transferable findings.
- ***none/weak:*** A minority use unrelated paradigms.

**b) punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- ***exact:*** Numerous papers directly test peer punishment, both decentralized (peer) and centralized, using standard fee/fine ratios.
- ***close/adjacent:*** Some investigate reward, ostracism/exclusion, or indirect sanctions (reputation/partner choice), which may substitute or moderate the effect of punishment.
- ***none/weak:*** Some papers offer only context (e.g., baseline cooperation in PGGs without sanctions).

**c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- ***exact:*** Many empirical and theoretical papers directly measure or model efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or closely related metrics.
- ***close:*** Others report group earnings, surplus, or similar aggregate payoffs.
- ***adjacent/weak:*** Several report only behavioral outcomes (cooperation rate, norm enforcement, trust), or focus on mechanisms without quantifying payoff/efficiency effects. Some discuss efficiency but do not empirically report it.

**Summary:**  
The literature set is **strongly relevant** to the prediction task, with a solid core of papers directly addressing all three task-relevance axes. However, some dimensions (e.g., reward-only variants, pure partner choice games, third-party punishment) should be interpreted more cautiously when generalizing to peer-punishment-enabled PGGs.

---

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (exact/close relevance):**
  - *Efficiency* (ratio of observed group payoff to the fully cooperative benchmark)—directly measured in many experiments.
  - *Group/individual earnings, wealth, surplus,* or *total coins generated*—often reported in experimental and simulation studies.
  - *Welfare/efficiency* calculated in theoretical models or via evolutionary simulation (mean group payoff across parameter sweeps).
  - *Group achievement*—fraction of rounds/groups reaching collective goals (in threshold games, often a strong proxy for efficiency).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (adjacent/weak relevance):**
  - *Contribution rate*, *cooperation frequency*, *punishment frequency*, *norm compliance*, *reputation changes*.
  - Psychological responses—trust, emotional state, perception of fairness, punitive motivation.

**Distinction:**  
While cooperation, contribution, and norm compliance are often correlated with efficiency, several studies demonstrate that *increases in cooperation do not always produce gains in efficiency*, especially when costs of punishment are high or antisocial punishment is present.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Patterns:**
- **Punishment can increase efficiency**: In *standard, repeated PGGs with moderate or low-cost, effective punishment*, enabling peer punishment typically increases efficiency compared to control, especially when baseline efficiency is low (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016; Markussen et al., 2014; Dal Bó & Dal Bó, 2014).
- **Punishment sometimes reduces efficiency**: When punishment is moderately or highly costly (fee-to-fine is low, e.g., 1:3 or 1:2), or when antisocial punishment is prevalent, or in presence of inequality or corruption opportunities, introducing punishment can *reduce* efficiency compared to control (Wu et al., 2016; Gächter et al., 2017; Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Kurzban et al., 2015; Barrett, 2016).
- **Moderators and mechanisms:**
    - *Communication/norm-signaling* amplifies the efficiency gains from punishment; their absence can render punishment less effective or even negative in net effect (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Grosset al., 2016).
    - *Emotional context and venting* modulate efficiency: angry or unregulated punishers often impose higher costs, reducing group efficiency; cooling-off mechanisms can mediate this (Dickinson & Masclet, 2015).
    - *Centralization of punishment power* and *democratic adoption* (voting, voluntary power transfer) enhance efficiency effects (Markussen et al., 2014; Gross et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014).
    - *Second-order punishment* and *reward mechanisms* (especially when carefully designed) can help establish and sustain efficient punishment equilibria, solving the second-order free-rider problem (Sasaki et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2015).
    - *Antisocial punishment*, *counter-punishment*, and *corruption opportunities* undermine or reverse efficiency effects, sometimes leading to efficiency below control (Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Gächter et al., 2017).
    - *Game parameters*: **lower punishment costs**, **high punishment impact**, **smaller groups**, **repeated/iterated games**, and **higher MPCR** conditions favor positive efficiency effects; costly, ineffective punishment, large group size, or one-shot designs often yield weaker or negative effects.
- **Competing mechanisms:**
    - **Ostracism/exclusion** (peer or pool) is often more effective and less costly in restoring efficiency than direct costly punishment (Sasaki & Uchida, 2013; Liu et al., 2017; Feinberg et al., 2014).
    - **Gossip, reputation, and reward** mechanisms can substitute for or complement punishment; in some contexts, they are *more efficient* than peer punishment (Wu et al., 2016; Milinski, 2016).

**Theoretical/Simulation Results:**
- **Efficiency effects are highly parameter-dependent**: Formal models and simulations underscore that design features (punishment/reward cost, impact, antisocial opportunities, network structure) produce sharp transitions in group efficiency as dimensions change (Perc et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2015; Wolitzky, 2013).
- **Conditional, adaptive, or hybrid policies**—using reward when cooperation is low and punishment once established—are most efficient overall (Chen et al., 2015).
- **Spatial structure** usually amplifies the benefits of punishment, especially when clustering allows cooperators and punishers to support each other; in well-mixed groups, the effect is weaker (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013, 2017).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

The literature offers **robust, multi-source support** for predicting the direction and, under some conditions, the size of efficiency changes when **peer punishment is enabled in PGG or its close variants**, conditional on both **game design dimensions** and **control (no-punishment) efficiency**:

1. **Baseline control efficiency is a key predictor:**
   - If control efficiency is low (i.e., group payoffs well below the social optimum), enabling peer punishment (with moderate cost and effective implementation) will, **on average, substantially increase efficiency**, sometimes approaching the cooperative maximum—especially with repeated rounds and smaller groups (Gächter et al., 2017; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016).
   - If control efficiency is already high (due to communication, reputation, or selection), the marginal gain from punishment may be limited or negligible, and unnecessary punishment may reduce efficiency due to costs (Markussen et al., 2014; Wu et al., 2016).

2. **Game design dimensions critically moderate the effect:**  
   - **Punishment cost/effectiveness (punishment_cost, punishment_tech):** Lower costs and higher impact ratios predict larger efficiency gains; too costly or ineffective punishment can reduce efficiency (Barrett, 2016; Kurzban et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2016).
   - **Group size (player_count):** Smaller groups are more likely to sustain efficient punishment equilibria. Larger groups face greater coordination and monitoring challenges, and punishment tends to be less efficient (Markussen et al., 2014; Wolitzky, 2013).
   - **Number of rounds (num_rounds):** Repeated games support higher efficiency with punishment. One-shot or few-shot games rarely see efficiency gains due to insufficient opportunity for reputation effects, learning, or deterrence (Kurzban et al., 2015; Hilbe et al., 2014).
   - **Punisher visibility/identity (show_punishment_id), communication (chat), norm-signaling (show_other_summaries):** Enabling these features amplifies efficiency gains (Andrighetto et al., 2013), whereas anonymity or lack of feedback dampens or reverses them.
   - **Presence of rewards (reward_exists/tech/cost):** Rewards complement or substitute for punishment, sometimes achieving higher efficiency at lower cost (Wu et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2015; Sasaki et al., 2015).
   - **Antisocial punishment/corruption opportunities:** If present, these sharply reduce or reverse expected efficiency gains (Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Gächter et al., 2017).

3. **Implementation of punishment matters:**
   - **Democratic choice (voting), centralized punishment, or voluntary power transfer mechanisms** lead to higher efficiency than imposed or exogenously allocated punishment power (Markussen et al., 2014; Gross et al., 2016).
   - **Emotional regulation/venting mechanisms** (cooling-off period, mood manipulation) influence efficiency via their effects on punishment severity and frequency (Dickinson & Masclet, 2015; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016).

4. **Significant exceptions and caution:**
   - In games with **high antisocial punishment**, high inequality, or corruption/bribery, punishment may decrease efficiency compared to control (Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Gächter et al., 2017).
   - **Partner choice and exclusion** are in some cases more efficient than direct peer punishment (Liu et al., 2017; Feinberg et al., 2014).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

Of the 14 **prediction dimensions**:

**Directly informed (frequently manipulated/analyzed, with efficiency measured):**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `punishmentExists` (on/off), `reward_exists` (in some studies)
- Several studies also specify or manipulate `all_or_nothing` (discrete vs. continuous contribution) and `default_contrib`.

**Indirectly informed (contextually discussed, sometimes measured behaviorally):**
- `chat` (enabling/disabling communication)
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds` (feedback, round structure clarity)
- `show_punishment_id` (visibility of punisher)
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `rewardMagnitude` (used in hybrid or alternative treatments)

**Sparse/missing (rarely considered as direct moderators of efficiency):**
- `default_contrib` (contribution framing), with some exceptions.
- `all_or_nothing` (when not central to the design).
- `show_punishment_id` (punisher identifiability is often held constant or unreported).
- Many studies do not jointly manipulate all dimensions, so coverage for multidimensional prediction is incomplete; interaction effects are often unreported outside simulation studies.

**In summary**:  
- The **efficiency effects of punishment are best mapped for the major structural parameters** (group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, presence of chat/norm-signaling, centralization/choice of punishment regime, and reward presence).
- **Less is known about nuanced or layered effects** of less commonly manipulated dimensions, or their higher-order interactions.

---

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Many studies report only behavioral outcomes**: While nearly all relevant studies suggest a link between cooperation/contribution and efficiency, not all explicitly measure efficiency or net payoff. Behavioral increases (e.g., in contributions) do not always translate into higher payoffs, especially if punishment costs are high or misapplied.
- **Antisocial punishment and counter-punishment are under-measured**: Some studies find substantial heterogeneity in who punishes, with antisocial punishment eroding efficiency gains; but detailed mapping of these effects to prediction dimensions is sparse.
- **Coverage of edge-case design dimensions is incomplete**: Notably, evidence on the effects of default contribution, visibility of rounds, or nuanced information feedback (beyond chat or summary statistics) on efficiency with punishment enabled is not robust.
- **Transfer from theoretical/simulation to real-world settings is sensitive to assumptions**: Simulation and evolutionary results often rest on simplified learning rules or homogeneous populations. Their generalizability to heterogeneous, real-world, or high-stakes settings is debated.
- **Rarely are all 14 dimensions varied jointly and systematically within the same study**: This limits certainty regarding higher-order or interaction effects and may hinder accurate prediction for complex or "out-of-sample" game designs.
- **Corruption, social exclusion, and hybrid reward-punishment mechanisms**: While some papers highlight the importance of these factors, empirical evidence is more limited and context-sensitive. The comparative efficiency of these alternative sanctions versus peer punishment is not always directly measured.
- **Population/cultural effects**: Substantial cross-societal differences are observed in outcomes like punishment effectiveness and antisocial punishment, but are not always explainable by design dimensions alone.

---

**In sum**:  
The paper set provides a **rich, high-confidence empirical and theoretical foundation** for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency in PGG-like environments across key design dimensions, especially when using **baseline (control) efficiency** as a reference. However, **precision is greatest in “standard” designs** (small groups, iterated, moderate-cost punishment, no rampant antisocial punishment), and **caution is warranted** for atypical or complex designs, environments with high antisocial punishment or corruption, or where key interaction effects among design dimensions may occur. For unmeasured or infrequently studied dimensions, or for complex interactions, predictions should be treated as provisional and uncertainty explicitly acknowledged.
