# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set comprises 96 papers encompassing both empirical (lab experiments, field experiments, observational studies) and theoretical (analytical, simulation-based) research. The evidence base is relatively broad in theoretical and conceptual coverage but becomes narrower when focusing specifically on *predicting average efficiency effects* of **enabling punishment in public goods games (PGGs) and close variants**. Among the paper set, a minority directly report efficiency (group payoff relative to the social optimum) as the primary outcome; most others focus on punishment behavior, cooperation/contribution rates, or norm enforcement, with efficiency as an indirect or adjacent outcome.

Crucially, a subset of papers provides **highly relevant and direct empirical evidence** for the prediction task (e.g., Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Gächter et al., 2017; Cason & Gangadharan, 2013; Freytag et al., 2014), with detailed efficiency or group payoff results across no-punishment and punishment-enabled treatments under different game designs. Several robust simulation/theoretical studies also model the efficiency impact of punishment under varied parameters (e.g., Boyd et al., 2014; Farjam et al., 2015). However, much of the literature explores behavioral, psychological, or motivational mechanisms, social context, and institutional structure rather than reporting payoff outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact**: A substantial portion of studies use the standard PGG or extremely close linear variants, often with well-specified game parameters (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Gächter et al., 2017; Barclay & Raihani, 2016).  
- **Close/Adjacent**: Some studies model related social dilemma structures (pollution, volunteer’s dilemma, threshold/step-level or provision/maintenance PGGs, etc.)—useful but possibly differing in institutional or dynamic features (Freytag et al., 2014; Cason & Gangadharan, 2013; Sääksvuori, 2014).
- **Weak/None**: A significant number operate further afield (trust games, dictator games, market studies, real-world social contexts), offering primarily contextual or motivational insights with only indirect relevance to PGG efficiency.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact**: Multiple key studies manipulate punishment as a treatment in the PGG, focusing on classic peer or centralized punishment institutions with clear cost/impact parameters.
- **Close/Adjacent**: Some investigate ostracism (social exclusion), anti-corruption mechanisms, milestone penalties, or punishment framed as exclusion, which function as sanctions but may differ in psychological/cost structure.
- **Weak/None**: Many papers discuss reward, gossip/reputation, or moral disapproval in lieu of true material punishment.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact**: Direct reporting of efficiency or total group payoff under punishment/no-punishment is present in a minority (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; Harrell & Simpson, 2016).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Many others report mean earnings, group profit, or changes in related monetary outcomes, which can often be mapped to efficiency with caution.
- **Weak/None**: The majority measure only behavioral indicators (contribution, cooperation, punishment rates) or perception/trust, without payoff-based outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (directly relevant to efficiency):**
  - *Efficiency (group payoff as a proportion of optimum)*: Harrell & Simpson (2016); Gächter et al. (2017); Cason & Gangadharan (2013); Freytag et al. (2014); Asgharpourmasouleh et al. (2017); Barker & Barclay (2016); Farjam et al. (2015).
  - *Earnings, welfare, surplus, coins generated*: Treated as closely related proxies in several papers (Barclay & Raihani, 2016; Feinberg et al., 2014; Baldassarri, 2015).
  - *Mean group profit/group outcome ratios*: Used as the operationalization of efficiency in some settings.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - *Contribution/cooperation rates*: Extensively reported (Balliet & Van Lange, 2013; Parks et al., 2013).
  - *Punishment/reward frequency, antisocial punishment*: Many studies focus exclusively on how often or who punishes (Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016; Pfattheicher et al., 2017).
  - *Trust, reputation, willingness to cooperate*: Frequently analyzed as mediators or moderators.
  - *Norm compliance, exclusion, group solidarity*: Explored both empirically and theoretically.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the most relevant papers:

**General pattern:**  
- **Enabling punishment almost always increases cooperation and contributions**—but the effect on *efficiency* is mixed and highly design-dependent.
- **Efficiency** (payoff as a proportion of optimum) improves with punishment only when punishment mechanisms are well-calibrated (low cost, high effectiveness, structure to avoid over-punishment), group sizes are small or moderate, and/or repeated play enables reciprocity and norm establishment (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Gächter et al., 2017; Boyd et al., 2014).

**Contexts yielding efficiency gains:**  
- **Centralized or coordinated punishment** (especially by prosocial leaders) yields the highest efficiency, minimizing waste due to costly peer-punishment (Harrell & Simpson, 2016).
- **Peer punishment** can restore efficiency close to social optimum if the punishment tech is effective and *costs are not excessive* (Gächter et al., 2017).
- **Ostracism (exclusion-based sanctions)** and reputation systems also increase efficiency when available, sometimes with less cost than monetary punishment (Feinberg et al., 2014; Diekmann et al., 2014).
- **Game designs with chat (communication), reputation, and longer time horizons** amplify the positive effects of punishment by making norms salient and reducing misapplied punishment (Kroupa, 2014).

**Contexts yielding ambiguous or negative efficiency effects:**  
- When **punishment is costly and frequent (typical peer-punishment lab PGG)**, **punishment costs can outweigh gains from cooperation**, reducing group payoff (Kurzban et al., 2015; Kroupa, 2014; Handfield et al., 2016).
- **Antisocial punishment** (punishing cooperators or justified actions) and counter-punishment further erode efficiency, especially in the absence of communication or when motivation is retributive rather than deterrent (Pfattheicher et al., 2017; Handfield et al., 2016).
- **Large groups & low relatedness**: Theoretical work indicates that efficiency gains from punishment are unlikely to be sustained unless a minority of highly visible punishers can induce cooperation (Boyd et al., 2014).

**Design moderators identified:**  
- **Player count**: Peer punishment is less effective, sometimes counterproductive, as group size grows (Boyd et al., 2014; Parks et al., 2013).
- **Punishment cost/tech**: Lower cost and higher impact (“fee-to-fine” ratio) improve the prospects for efficiency gains.
- **Num rounds**: Repetition helps punishment mechanisms “pay off” by spreading costs over time and deterring future defection (Kurzban et al., 2015; Barclay & Raihani, 2016).
- **Chat, show punishment ID, show other summaries**: Visibility, reputation, and feedback strengthen norm establishment and reduce unnecessary/antisocial punishment; absence of these worsens outcomes (Kroupa, 2014; Diekmann et al., 2014).

**Special cases & exceptions:**  
- **Corruption/bribery potential**: If punishment authorities/leaders can be bribed, punishment can reduce, not increase, efficiency (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
- **Threshold/milestone PGGs**: The structure of payoff functions (milestone penalties, step-level returns) changes whether punishment is beneficial (Freytag et al., 2014).
- **Negative MPCR (taking games) or local competition**: Enabling punishment/money burning under these structures can drastically decrease efficiency (Farjam et al., 2015; Barker & Barclay, 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **If the control (no-punishment) game has *low efficiency* (rapid decay of cooperation, low earnings), enabling peer punishment is likely to increase average efficiency—especially if the design features small to moderate group size, moderate MPCR, and reasonable punishment cost-to-impact ratio.** Predictions should assume an upward adjustment in treatment efficiency, with the magnitude depending on the *punishment institution*: greatest for centralized or coordinated punishment, intermediate (and more variable) for peer punishment (Gächter et al., 2017; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Asgharpourmasouleh et al., 2017).

- **If the control game already shows *high efficiency* (cooperation is stable via communication, reputation, or exclusion), punishment may confer little additional benefit, and—if punishment is overused or misapplied—may even reduce efficiency due to direct costs or retaliatory dynamics** (Kroupa, 2014; Kurzban et al., 2015).

- **The following design dimensions should be used to moderate expected treatment efficiency:**
  - **player_count:** Larger groups diminish punishment effectiveness (Boyd et al., 2014).
  - **num_rounds:** More rounds support efficiency gains, as learning and norm establishment accumulate (Kurzban et al., 2015).
  - **chat/communication:** Presence increases the likelihood that punishment is efficient (Kroupa, 2014).
  - **mpcr:** Higher returns per contribution support higher potential gains from increased cooperation, especially under well-designed punishment (Farjam et al., 2015).
  - **punishment_cost/punishment_tech:** Lower punishment cost and appropriate impact optimize the efficiency effect of punishment.
  - **reward_exists:** If both punishment and reward are available, the marginal efficiency gain from adding punishment is reduced, and reward may be more efficient (Rand & Nowak, 2013; Parks et al., 2013).
  - **show_punishment_id/reputation features:** Design features increasing punishment transparency may reduce antisocial punishment and foster efficiency (Kroupa, 2014; Diekmann et al., 2014).

- **When indirect or adjacent evidence is used, predictions should be hedged and treated as suggestive rather than quantitative.** Most theoretical and behavioral studies can provide only qualitative directionality unless mapped to directly comparable empirical results.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**  
- **player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Frequently manipulated or specified; core parameters for mapping to prediction.
- **chat, show_punishment_id, show_other_summaries:** Examined for their impact on norm enforcement, transparency, and efficiency.
- **all_or_nothing, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Sometimes varied; reward features often discussed as alternatives or complements to punishment.

**Indirectly Informed:**  
- **default_contrib**: Contribution framing occasionally discussed (opt-in vs. opt-out), less often as a key manipulated variable.
- **show_n_rounds**: Sometimes present as a design note but less commonly investigated as an independent moderator of efficiency.
- **show_other_summaries**: Often present in reporting feedback/information structures but rarely isolated in analyses.

**Contextually Discussed/Effectively Missing:**  
- **default_contrib, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries:** Rarely the primary focus of an analysis; their impacts are less consistently mapped to efficiency predictions.
- **reward_cost, reward_tech:** Often mentioned, but quantitative efficiency effects of reward vs. punishment are less often directly compared outside theoretical syntheses.
- **show_punishment_id:** Sometimes manipulated, especially in studies of reputation, but often not reported as a separate moderator.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited direct efficiency data:** Relatively few studies report group efficiency (payoff as a proportion of optimum) in both control and punishment treatments across a range of design dimensions. Most provide behavioral or adjacent outcomes, requiring caution in quantitative mapping for prediction.
- **Experimental vs. real-world settings:** Lab PGGs may exaggerate punishment costs and frequency due to anonymity, lack of communication, and suppression of real-world moderating forces (Kroupa, 2014).
- **Sparse coverage of certain design dimensions:** Some dimensions (default_contrib, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, reward specifics) are underreported or only discussed in passing.
- **Generality of adjacent/indirect evidence:** Many papers investigating related economic games, punishment motives, psychological or sociological mechanisms contribute only contextually or at the level of theoretical expectations, not empirical efficiency outcomes.
- **Ambiguity and mixed findings:** Even among the most directly relevant studies, there is disagreement: some show clear positive efficiency effects of punishment, others show net negative or weak effects depending on punishment cost, antisocial punishment, or group size.
- **Neglected factors:** Cultural, societal, and network moderators—often central to real-world application—are sometimes included (trust, social structure) but rarely linked quantitatively to efficiency predictions.
- **Complexity of interactions:** The efficiency effect of punishment is shaped by higher-order interactions among multiple design features (e.g., punishment institution × group size × communication × payoff structure), and much of the literature does not systematically explore these interactions.

---

**Summary:**  
While the literature set includes several high-quality, directly relevant experimental and theoretical sources for predicting average efficiency changes when punishment is enabled in PGGs, much of the evidence remains behavioral, qualitative, or context-dependent. The core findings—enabling punishment tends to increase cooperation but raises or lowers efficiency depending on game structure and design—are robust but must be moderated by player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, communication, and baseline efficiency. Prediction models should therefore emphasize these dimensions and be cautious where evidence is only indirect or design parameters are unclear.
