# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is extensive (217 papers), including both empirical (lab and field experiments, observational studies) and theoretical (analytical models, simulations) work. A substantial subset features experimental laboratory studies of public goods games (PGG) and their close variants, but about half the set investigates adjacent games (e.g., threshold PGG, trust games, CPR, PD) or punishments outside standard PGGs. Many high-relevance studies directly manipulate punishment and report group efficiency or payoff-based outcomes, but a significant portion focus on cooperation rates or behavioral outcomes, not payoffs. Theoretical contributions supplement empirical work, providing mechanistic insights, but simulation and model assumptions vary widely. Overall, the evidence base is broad, highly varied in context and methodology, and not uniformly focused on the central prediction task.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. pgg_or_variant**:  
- *Exact*: Many papers cover standard linear or threshold PGGs or indistinguishable variants (including spatial/networked PGGs, public bads, or resource games).  
- *Close*: A sizeable number use adjacent environments (trust games, CPR, multi-level PGGs, etc.), which typically retain key incentive structures.  
- *Adjacent*: Numerous studies use iterated Prisoner's Dilemma or similar two-player games, which inform some mechanisms but differ in group interaction and payoff technologies.  
- *Weak/None*: Some studies analyze norm enforcement, reputation, or punishment outside game contexts; these are only tangentially relevant.

**b. punishment_or_sanctions**:  
- *Exact*: Many experiments and a good number of models explicitly manipulate the presence and parameters of punishment/sanctions (cost, severity, tech, etc.), whether peer or institutional.  
- *Close*: Studies sometimes use alternative sanctions (e.g., exclusion, third-party punishment, minimum contribution taxes), which are functionally similar.  
- *Adjacent*: Reputation, gossip, indirect reciprocity, or partner choice studies may capture punitive/ostracism effects without explicit costly punishment.  
- *Weak/None*: Some papers focus on non-punitive interventions (reward, leadership, communication, etc.).

**c. efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- *Exact*: A minority of empirical and theoretical studies report efficiency as the ratio of observed group payoff to the social optimum (full cooperation); some use total group payoff, welfare, surplus, or clearly analogous quantities.  
- *Close/Adjacent*: Many studies primarily report contribution rates, cooperation rates, or frequencies of behavioral strategies, with only indirect or unreported links to actual payoffs or efficiency (often requiring assumptions for mapping).  
- *Weak/None*: Several papers have purely behavioral or attitudinal outcomes, and some only discuss mechanisms or conceptual issues.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes** (i.e., efficiency, group payoff/welfare/surplus/earnings/coins):  
- Direct experimental efficiency reporting is present in a limited subset of high-quality empirical and theoretical PGG studies (e.g., Jiang & Wang 2024; Krügel & Maaser 2025; Kamei et al. 2023; DeCaro et al. 2024; Nhim et al. 2023; Ohdaira 2025; Otten et al. 2024; Duell et al. 2024).  
- Many theoretical simulation/modeling studies report average payoffs or welfare as proxies for efficiency but often in non-PGG or stylized variants.  
- Several institutional and policy studies in adjacent environments (e.g., CPR, trust, or division-of-labour games) report group payoff or efficiency.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
- The majority of included experimental and theoretical papers report contribution rates, cooperation frequencies, frequency of punishment, antisocial/prosocial punishment rates, norm compliance, or strategy prevalence.  
- Some focus on mechanisms (emotions, beliefs, social identity), without connecting to payoffs.
- Many studies equate increased cooperation with presumed efficiency gains, but do not demonstrate this directly.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## General Direction and Heterogeneity

- **Punishment often—but not always—increases efficiency:**  
  Enabling (costly) punishment in repeated, standard PGGs tends to increase group efficiency (i.e., move average payoffs closer to the full-cooperation benchmark) under many "textbook" lab conditions (small groups, moderate cost/effectiveness of punishment, transparent contributions), but not universally. In certain settings, especially with high-punishment costs or the presence of antisocial punishment, efficiency gains are small, absent, or negative.

- **Design details and context are critical moderators:**  
  The direction and magnitude of punishment's effect on efficiency—holding control efficiency constant—depend strongly on several design dimensions, including:
  - **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** Lower punishment costs and higher effectiveness promote greater efficiency gains (Zefferman 2023; Bühren et al. 2023).
  - **Monitoring/feedback technology:** Punishment increases efficiency only when individual contribution information is available; group-level feedback alone often nullifies the effect (Eisenkopf & Walter 2022).
  - **Group composition and heterogeneity:** Discriminatory punishment (against perceived outgroups or heterogeneous members) undermines efficiency; homogeneous groups benefit more (Molenmaker et al. 2023).
  - **Transparency and information:** Public endowment or action visibility is often required for punishment to raise efficiency (Chen et al. 2025). Secrecy can suppress the positive effect.
  - **Institutional structure:** Peer punishment, third-party punishment, pool punishment, and exclusion mechanisms differ in efficiency effects; e.g., exclusion (Jia & Wang 2024) or institutionally guided punishment (Kamei et al. 2023) can yield high efficiency gains.
  - **Reward mechanisms:** The coexistence of punishment and reward can interact (sometimes positively, sometimes not) to affect efficiency (Lu et al. 2024; Garrido et al. 2025).
  - **Costly side effects:** The costliness of punishment frequently erodes some or all of the gain from higher cooperation rates (Nhim et al. 2023; Milinski & Marotzke 2022).
  - **Social preferences and leadership:** Leadership type, prosociality, and selection method can mediate punishment's effectiveness (Jiang & Wang 2024; Chen et al. 2025).

- **Dependency on baseline (control) efficiency:**  
  - If baseline cooperation (control/no-punishment efficiency) is already high due to other design features (communication, trust, voluntary participation, etc.), enabling punishment may yield minimal or even negative marginal efficiency due to punishment costs (Lie-Panis et al. 2024).
  - When control efficiency is low and free riding frequent, punishment's effect on efficiency is typically larger.

## Special findings and exceptions

- **Antisocial/discriminatory punishment:** Punishment targeting cooperators or outgroups can decrease efficiency and even depress cooperation (Molenmaker et al. 2023; Angelsen & Naime 2024).
- **Institutional design and consensus mechanisms:** Group consensus, hierarchy, and institutional learning/facilitation can sharply moderate impacts (Krügel & Maaser 2025; DeCaro et al. 2024; Powers et al. 2023).
- **Punishment avoidance and evasion:** If avoidance or evasion of punishment is possible (opting out, unambitious pledges), punishment is less or not effective at all (Del Ponte et al. 2025).
- **Over-severe or profitable punishment:** Punishment mechanisms that are too harsh or that incentivize punishers (profitable punishment) can crowd out voluntary cooperation, reduce efficiency, or increase inequality (Hernandez et al. 2022; Alam & Rai 2025; Chiba-Okabe & Plotkin 2024; Nirjhor & Nakamaru 2023; Goto & Matsui 2025).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct implications:**
- If the game design is a standard, repeated PGG (small group, clear feedback, fixed MPCR, peer or institutional punishment, moderate cost/effect), and control efficiency is moderate to low, enabling punishment will almost always increase efficiency, sometimes substantially.
- Magnitude of gain depends mainly on: punishment cost/effectiveness, monitoring (individual vs. group feedback), transparency, group homogeneity, and prevalence of antisocial/erroneous punishment.
- If punishment is cheap or intermediate in cost and well-targeted at defectors, expect substantial increases in efficiency—up to or near full cooperation.
- If punishment is costly, or group structure or monitoring is such that punishment is misapplied/discriminatory, gains may be small or absent.
- Peer punishment is most effective in homogeneous, well-monitored, transparent settings; institutional (pool, exclusion) or hybrid (reward-punishment) systems often outperform peer-only punishment where coordination, consensus, or large groups are required.
- If control efficiency is already high (e.g., with communication, trust, or reputational mechanisms), enabling punishment often yields only a small additional efficiency gain, or even a net reduction due to the direct cost of sanctions.
- For adjacent or stylized games (CPR, trust, multi-level/group-structured or sequential games), the same moderators apply qualitatively, but with less certain quantitative effect sizes.

**When evidence is based on non-payoff outcomes:**  
- If only cooperation/contribution rates are reported (not payoffs), infer efficiency changes only if the cost of punishment is low or negligible; otherwise, increases in cooperation may not translate to higher net efficiency.

**Key caveats:**  
- In settings with possible antisocial, discriminatory, or profit-motivated punishment, or where strategy noise/avoidance is high, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency and may even decrease it.
- Institutional interventions (e.g., minimum contributions, exclusion, central punishment) can outperform pure peer punishment for efficiency, but only if structured to minimize abuse and minimize administrative cost.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**  
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`: Most relevant studies specify and analyze these, showing that small groups/short games are typical, but effects sometimes vary with size and repetition (Powers et al. 2023).
- `mpcr`: Controlled and analyzed in many experiments and models; a higher MPCR generally strengthens punishment’s effect.
- `punishment_cost`/`punishment_tech`: Extensively analyzed; cost/effectiveness, targeting, and implementation (peer vs. pool vs. exclusion) are repeatedly found to be critical moderators.
- `chat`/`show_other_summaries`: Communication (chat) is shown to increase control (no-punishment) efficiency; feedback about others’ actions is essential for punishment to improve efficiency.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Less often manipulated, but several studies address their coexistence with punishment and effects on efficiency (Lu et al. 2024; Garrido et al. 2025).
- `all_or_nothing`/`default_contrib`: Utilized in some models; effects of discretization and framing are more rarely isolated.
- `show_n_rounds`/`show_punishment_id`: Occasionally manipulated (transparency effects); rarely treated as primary moderators.
- `show_other_summaries`: Disaggregated feedback (who did what) is shown to matter for effective sanctioning (Eisenkopf & Walter 2022; Nielsen & Pfattheicher 2024).

**Indirectly/contextually discussed:**  
- Features like group composition (homogeneity/heterogeneity, community structure—sometimes not explicit in the dimensions), norm uncertainty and uncertainty about intentions, social identity, and psychological mechanisms are found to moderate punishment effects but are not always directly parameterized.

**Sparse or missing in the literature for prediction:**  
- The separate effect of `default_contrib` (opt-in/opt-out framing), `reward_tech` or distinction among types of `punishment_tech` (beyond peer/pool/third-party/exclusion), as consistent moderators across studies.
- `show_punishment_id` is underexplored as an efficiency moderator.
- Many design dimensions are not systematically varied jointly with detailed efficiency measurements.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Reporting bias toward behavioral outcomes:** A large portion of the literature (especially older and theoretical papers) reports on cooperation rates or behavioral patterns, not actual group payoffs or efficiency, making results less useful for the exact efficiency prediction task.
- **Contextual and sample limitations:** Most strong efficiency evidence is from relatively small, homogeneous lab studies with undergraduate participants under highly controlled conditions. These may not generalize to more diverse, high-conflict, or field environments.
- **Incomplete coverage of design space:** Not all design dimensions (from the 14 listed) are systematically manipulated or reported; indirect inferences may be required and interactions among dimensions are under-explored.
- **Ambiguity and inconsistency in adjacent/enriched games:** When moving beyond standard linear PGGs, the effects of punishment on efficiency become more sensitive to contextual factors and institutional details, leading to divergent outcomes (e.g., strict enforcement may reduce efficiency relative to a minimum contribution regime—Nhim et al. 2023).
- **Limits of transfer from non-payoff to payoff measures:** In many studies, efficiency effects are only inferred from cooperation or contribution rates; this inference is valid only when punishment costs are modest or negligible.
- **Sensitivity to antisocial/discriminatory punishment:** In heterogeneous or culturally diverse groups, or where monitoring is imperfect, efficiency gains may be suppressed or reversed by harmful side effects of punishment (Molenmaker et al. 2023; Angelsen & Naime 2024).
- **Institutional and historical path dependence:** Effects may depend on group history, prior regulatory failures, or norm learning—factors often not captured by the prediction dimensions.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature supports the prediction that enabling peer or institutional punishment frequently increases efficiency relative to no-punishment controls in public-goods-game-like environments—but only under specific, well-understood design parameters: low to moderate punishment cost, effective monitoring/feedback, clear targeting, and minimal scope for antisocial/discriminatory punishment. Control efficiency is an informative predictor of treatment efficiency only when these moderators are accounted for. The evidence is strongest for standard lab PGGs and becomes weaker or more contingent for adjacent designs or field-like environments, for which careful context-matching and design-parameter mapping are critical.
