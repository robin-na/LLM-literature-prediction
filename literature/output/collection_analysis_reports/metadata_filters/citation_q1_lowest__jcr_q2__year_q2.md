# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set includes a mix of **theoretical and empirical (mainly experimental)** studies, spanning economics, psychology, physics, and computational modeling. A minority are large-scale field experiments; most are lab experiments or formal models. The set covers **some canonical public goods games (PGG) and numerous adjacent designs** (e.g., repeated helping games, bargaining, resource sharing, duopolies). 

A subset of papers directly examine **explicit peer punishment in the PGG or close variants**, while others explore alternative mechanisms (hierarchy, exit, surveillance, endogenous sanctions, reputation). There is moderate diversity in social dilemma structures, participant pools, and implementation of punishment.

Importantly, only **a limited number of papers report direct payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, surplus)** in control and punishment-enabled PGGs; several others report closely related or adjacent outcomes, or focus on underlying mechanisms without direct payoff analysis. Thus, the evidence base is **broad in topic but relatively narrow and uneven with respect to the task of predicting treatment efficiency under peer punishment in explicit PGG settings**.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessed across three target-relevance dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant**:
  - **Exact:** Several papers directly investigate classic or spatial public goods games (Wu et al., 2014; Otto & Bolle, 2016; Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017; Zhang et al., 2014).
  - **Close/Adjacent:** Many probe adjacent environments: repeated helping games, resource sharing, bargaining, Cournot duopoly, and indirect reciprocity (Camera & Gioffré, 2017; Mariano & Correia, 2015; Feinberg & Kets, 2014; Gaudeul et al., 2017; etc.).
  - **Weak/None:** Some studies are only contextually related (Bügelmayer & Spiess, 2014; Sääksvuori & Ramalingam, 2015).

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:
  - **Exact:** Wu et al. (2014), Hugh-Jones & Perroni (2017), Nasrallah & Cheaib (2016), Camera & Gioffré (2017), dos Santos et al. (2014), Mariano & Correia (2015) directly model or implement explicit sanctions/punishment.
  - **Close/Adjacent:** Several examine punishment-inspired mechanisms (exit, disapproval voting, conditional dissociation, hierarchical control, reputation-based sanctions).
  - **Weak/None:** Many models do not include any form of punishment (e.g., Yan, 2017; Ohdaira, 2014; Song & Zhong, 2015).

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
  - **Exact or Close:** Wu et al. (2014), Otto & Bolle (2016), Hugh-Jones & Perroni (2017), Zhang et al. (2014), Camera & Gioffré (2017), Mariano & Correia (2015), Ognedal (2016), Nasrallah & Cheaib (2016), dos Santos et al. (2014).
  - **Adjacent/Weak:** Several provide only behavioral or strategic outcomes, such as contribution rates or punishment frequency, rather than group payoff or efficiency.
  - **None:** Some do not report or analyze any payoff-based outcomes.

Overall, **the literature set has moderate direct relevance to the prediction task—high for specific dimensions and experimental designs, but uneven coverage and limited direct empirical comparisons between control and punishment conditions within standard PGG settings.**

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (directly relevant):**
  - **Efficiency:** Ratio of observed group earnings to possible maximum if all cooperate (Wu et al., 2014; Otto & Bolle, 2016; Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017; Camera & Gioffré, 2017; Mariano & Correia, 2015).
  - **Total/group payoff, welfare, surplus:** Used variably across papers; sometimes as “average group income” or “mean final payoff.”
  - These are reported in direct connection to punishment in a subset of studies (Wu et al., 2014; Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017; Mariano & Correia, 2015). Several theory papers provide explicit equilibrium payoffs or efficiency conditions as functions of punishment or institutional parameters (Camera & Gioffré, 2017; Nasrallah & Cheaib, 2016; Ognedal, 2016).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (adjacent/indirectly relevant):**
  - **Contribution/cooperation rates:** How much players contribute or how often they cooperate (reported in most PGG variants).
  - **Punishment frequency/assignment:** How often punishment is used, who is targeted.
  - **Norm compliance, exit rates, antisocial punishment, reciprocity, emotional responses, deception.**
  - These outcomes inform about the mechanisms by which punishment might shape payoff, but **are not payoff or efficiency themselves**.

- **Link between behavioral and payoff outcomes:**
  - Some studies clarify when increased cooperation does (or does not) translate into higher efficiency, highlighting the role of punishment cost, targeting efficacy, and side effects (Wu et al., 2014; Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017; dos Santos et al., 2014).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesizing across the literature:**

- **Peer punishment can substantially increase efficiency—especially when punishment is well-targeted, low-cost to the punisher, and effective at deterring free-riders (Wu et al., 2014; Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017; Mariano & Correia, 2015).** This is empirically and theoretically supported, particularly for games with low baseline (control) efficiency.

- **The efficiency gain from punishment is not universal:**
  - If punishment is easy to abuse, poorly targeted, too frequent (including antisocial punishment), or if punishment cost is high or poorly structured, the outcome can be neutral or even detrimental to efficiency (Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017; D'Exelle & Riedl, 2013; dos Santos et al., 2014; Qu et al., 2016).
  - Coordination requirements for punishment moderate outcomes: **higher coordination thresholds (harder to punish) generally yield more positive efficiency effects** by channeling punishment to true free-riders.
  - **Environmental and cognitive factors** (e.g., cognitive load, salience, social distance) mediate whether punishment boosts efficiency (dos Santos et al., 2014; Johnson, 2016).

- **Alternative mechanisms:** 
  - **Hierarchy/central authority can substitute for punishment, yielding even higher efficiency in some repeated PGG settings** (Otto & Bolle, 2016).
  - **Internalized sanctions** (moral emotions, reciprocity, ostracism, exit) can partially substitute, but their efficiency effect is less robust and highly contingent on group composition and trust (Gaudeul et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2014).

- **Structural moderators:**
  - **Game parameters (mpcr, group size, number of rounds, punishment technology, punishment cost)** strongly affect the size and often even the sign of the treatment effect.
  - **Information structures** (who sees what about others, how outcomes or history are reported) can either facilitate efficient punishment or enable counterproductive dynamics (D'Exelle & Riedl, 2013).

- **Empirical range:** Differences between control and punishment conditions (when measured) can be large (low to high efficiency), but the effect is sometimes null or negative under adverse design features or context.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based on the reviewed literature, **the most reliable prediction is that enabling peer punishment in a PGG-like game—when punishment is reasonably well-structured, non-arbitrary, and not excessively costly—will tend to increase efficiency, especially when control efficiency is low**. 

- **Magnitude and direction of the change** depend critically on:
  - **Punishment cost**: Lower costs to punishers (with fixed punishment effectiveness) yield greater efficiency gains (Wu et al., 2014).
  - **Punishment technology**: Higher coordination requirements (e.g., group rather than individual must agree to punish) improve targeting and efficiency (Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017).
  - **MPCR**: Efficiency gains from punishment are larger at lower baseline returns to cooperation (Wu et al., 2014).
  - **Cognitive and social context**: If players are under cognitive load or social distance is high, the efficiency benefit of punishment may disappear (dos Santos et al., 2014; Johnson, 2016).
  - **Possibility of antisocial or arbitrary punishment**: When prevalent, this can offset or overwhelm any efficiency gain.

**Predictors informed directly by the literature**: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, all_or_nothing, chat (contextual moderator), show_other_summaries (moderates evidence and targeting), reward_exists (sometimes explored as an alternative).

**Less support, mostly indirect or context-based**: default_contrib, reward_cost, reward_tech, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id (information structure is sometimes discussed, but not always manipulated or reported).

**When predicting treatment efficiency**:
- If **control efficiency is already high** (i.e., group is already cooperating), enabling punishment may yield only modest or no gains.
- If **control efficiency is low**, punishment is more likely to produce a dramatic positive shift—provided it is well-designed and the environment supports effective deterrence rather than retaliation or arbitrariness.

**Special caution**: Enabling punishment **does not guarantee higher efficiency**; poor punishment design can reduce efficiency. Predictive models should incorporate available design details, and conservative estimates are warranted if punishment cost is high, targeting is poor, or antisocial punishment is likely.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed by empirical or theoretical results:**
  - **player_count:** Almost universally varied; group size often moderates effect magnitude.
  - **num_rounds:** Repetition is key to supporting peer punishment and sustaining cooperation.
  - **mpcr:** Central in PGG models; lower mpcr increases risk of collapse without punishment, amplifies treatment effect.
  - **punishment_cost / punishment_tech:** Strong, clear effects on the relative efficiency treatment effect; efficiency gains drop when cost is high or technology enables arbitrariness/abuse.
  - **all_or_nothing:** Sometimes manipulated; makes cooperation dynamics more distinct.
  - **show_other_summaries / show_n_rounds:** Information structure is shown to moderate effects in several studies.
  - **chat:** Explored as a contextual moderator in some lab studies.

- **Indirectly or contextually discussed:**
  - **default_contrib:** Sometimes referenced as “framing” or initial conditions but not systematically studied.
  - **reward_exists / reward_cost / reward_tech:** Occasionally included (Mariano & Correia, 2015), often as an alternative to punishment, but results not robust across settings.
  - **show_punishment_id:** Public vs. private punishment sometimes discussed as affecting deterrence and retaliation (D'Exelle & Riedl, 2013), but not a consistent independent variable.

- **Effectively missing or weakly addressed:** Direct mapping for several interaction terms or more nuanced dimensions (e.g., real-time feedback, heterogeneity, external enforcement) is sparse or implicit.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited direct experimental evidence in standard PGGs for both control and punishment conditions with payoff outcomes.** Most studies relevant for the prediction task are either theoretical or report only behavioral outcomes.

- **Moderate variability in operationalization of punishment and efficiency**: E.g., punishment via deduction points, exit, ostracism, reputation, or formal hierarchy—sometimes only one is manipulable at a time; effects may not translate cleanly across mechanisms.

- **Contextual dependence and external moderators**: Cognitive load, social distance, heterogeneity, and information structures often matter as much or more than base game design—these moderators are inconsistently reported/documented.

- **Adjacency of many studies:** Many robust findings relate to adjacent games (e.g., resource sharing, repeated helping, bargaining) and must be cautiously mapped to PGG context.

- **Punishment often proxies for a bundle of effects:** Including targeting ability, deterrence, coordination problems, and strategic retaliation. Without clear implementation details in the predictor dimensions, prediction may be unreliable.

- **Nonlinearities and threshold effects:** Several studies emphasize that treatment effects are not always monotonic; e.g., efficiency may peak at intermediate values of punishment severity/coordination/parameters, not just increase linearly.

In summary, **while the literature supports a conditional positive prediction for punishment-enabled efficiency in PGG-like environments, the prediction is highly dependent on implementation details that are only partially illuminated by this paper set. Ambiguity, disagreement, and context-sensitivity remain significant.**
