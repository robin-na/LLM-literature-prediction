# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 22 sources with a majority anchored in empirical laboratory experiments, complemented by several theory/modeling contributions. Most of the empirical papers use repeated linear or threshold public goods games (PGG) or direct variants, with some addressing related paradigms (e.g., common-pool resource games, volunteer’s dilemma). Theoretical papers span analytical models of PGGs, punishment technology, and mechanism design. The breadth of the evidence base is reasonably strong for standard PGGs, especially for the effect of enabling (peer or centralized) punishment mechanisms on group outcomes. However, coverage is narrower for more specialized design dimensions (e.g., chat, default contribution framing, reward mechanisms), and some papers focus on adjacent but not exact game structures or outcome measures.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Multiple empirical and theoretical papers test or model exact PGGs or very close variants, particularly linear, repeated PGGs with 4–10 players, known rounds, and standard MPCRs (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
- **Close or adjacent relevance:** Several sources study games closely related to PGGs (generalized exchange, CPR, volunteer’s dilemma, threshold public goods, and collective action frameworks), offering transferable but not fully equivalently situated findings (Kanitsar, 2021; Di Guida et al., 2021; Bolle, 2021).
- **Weak or none:** Some theory or empirical work is only contextually related, focusing on social norm theory or indirect reciprocity.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Many studies directly analyze the effect of introducing punishment—either peer or centralized—on group outcomes (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
- **Close or adjacent:** Some investigate softer or structurally different interventions (e.g., exclusion as punishment, contribution-based grouping, or social feedback rather than monetary sanctions).
- **Weak/none:** Several adjacent studies discuss punishment in the context of broader mechanism design or as a conceptual background.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact:** Several key empirical and theoretical sources report efficiency (group payoff/total earnings relative to cooperative optimum) as a primary outcome (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Close/adjacent:** In some, only contribution rates or related behavior are measured, or efficiency implications are inferred rather than reported.
- **Weak/none:** Behavioral or psychological outcomes without direct payoff metrics are common in some studies.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (direct relevance):**  
  - **Efficiency:** Several laboratory studies and theoretical models explicitly report group-level efficiency or aggregate payoffs with and without punishment (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
  - **Earnings, welfare, surplus:** Some papers equate efficiency with welfare, total net profit, or social surplus.
- **Behavioral outcomes (indirect or adjacent relevance):**  
  - **Contribution rate, cooperation rate:** Frequently measured, these are not efficiency but are closely linked—many studies interpret higher contributions as proxies for higher efficiency, though not all investigate or calculate net group payoffs.
  - **Punishment frequency, amount spent on punishment:** Assess escalation and targeting but do not report payoff impact directly.
  - **Norm compliance, exclusion/replacement rates, feedback:** Sometimes the focus is on processes or mechanisms rather than outcomes.
- **Distinguishing notes:**  
  - Some studies report only behavioral outcomes and do **not** provide direct efficiency data (Windmann et al., 2021; Grund et al., 2020).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Punishment Increases Efficiency (on average):**  
- There is **converging evidence** that enabling punishment (either peer or centralized) generally **increases group efficiency** or total payoffs relative to control (no-punishment) PGGs, though the magnitude varies considerably across groups and contexts (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).

**Magnitude and Moderators of Efficiency Gains:**  
- **Punishment's effectiveness** is highly dependent on the **cost-effect ratio**—if punishment is costless or highly effective (high fine per unit of cost), efficiency gains are greater (Kanitsar, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
- **Type of punishment mechanism:** Centralized punishment effectiveness is robust across cost/benefit regimes; peer punishment's net effect is stronger when the fraction of "strong reciprocators" is high (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Network structure:** Efficiency gains from peer punishment are observed in standard PGGs but may be absent in generalized exchange settings (Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Institutional legitimacy:** In centralized punishment settings, manager selection method (vote/random) does not affect efficiency, but the prosociality of the manager does (Castillo et al., 2021).
- **Societal/cultural context:** Cross-country and group heterogeneity are substantial; more "norm-keepers" (who punish both high and low contributors) correlate with lower efficiency (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Game parameters:** Group size, MPCR, and the structure and clarity of punishment/reward tech modulate outcomes (Botta et al., 2021; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021).

**Important Conditionalities and Limits:**  
- **Antisocial or misapplied punishment** (punishing contributors) can erode efficiency, but is relatively rare unless "norm-keepers" are prevalent (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Punishment may have no efficiency effect** if it is too costly (Kanitsar, 2021), or if the game/network structure is not amenable (Bolle, 2021; Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Anticipation and deterrence:** Efficiency gains occur not just through deterring free riders, but by raising contributions from cooperators in anticipation of punishment (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Partial or threshold games:** For optional participation or threshold public good games, effectiveness of punishment may hinge on whether the punisher can reach key thresholds or stabilize cooperation (Botta et al., 2021; Bolle, 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Strongest Guidance:**  
  - Where the game matches a standard repeated linear PGG with moderate group size (e.g., 4–10), **enabling punishment should increase average efficiency relative to the control,** unless punishment is exceptionally costly to apply or the network structure is sparse (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021).
  - The **control game's efficiency** (without punishment) serves as a baseline; activation of punishment reliably shifts outcomes upward, but the effect size depends on the prevalence of strong reciprocators, punishment cost/effectiveness ratio, and absence of strong norm-keeping punishing both high and low contributors.

- **Dimension-Specific Moderators:**  
  - **Punishment cost/effectiveness:** Low cost and/or high effectiveness (high fine per cost) raise efficiency gains (Kanitsar, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
  - **Centralization:** Centralized punishment is robustly effective, regardless of manager selection (Castillo et al., 2021); peer punishment's net efficiency gain is more context-dependent (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
  - **Network structure:** Dense sanctioning (typical PGG) supports efficiency gains; sparse networks may not (Kanitsar, 2021).
  - **Presence of rewards:** Addressed only marginally; some theory (Skarzhinskaya & Tsurikov, 2021) suggests selective incentives (e.g., reward or contract) can substitute for punishment, but empirical support is scarce.

- **Less Informed or Missing Dimensions:**  
  - Chat, default_contrib, identity revelation, reward cost/tech—few, if any, sources directly analyze these.
  - Effects are often reported as averages; **variance across groups** (within the same treatment) can be high, meaning point predictions should allow for uncertainty linked to group composition.

- **Contextual caveats:**  
  - **Antisocial punishment** (when prevalent) or cost-ineffective punishment can bring little/no efficiency gain.
  - In some PGG variants or related games, punishment can have no effect or even a negative effect if it fails to stabilize cooperation efficiently.
  - Results from games with exclusion, feedback, or endogenous matching are only partially transferable.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**  
- **player_count**: Explicitly varied (usually 4–10); efficiency findings are robust across moderate group sizes (common across main empirical papers).
- **num_rounds**: Standard (often 6–10), usually fixed and known.
- **mpcr**: Direct relevance; higher MPCR facilitates cooperation and raises achievable efficiency.
- **punishment_cost**: Central moderator in many theory/empirical analyses.
- **punishment_tech**: Some theory papers model it; reflects fine-to-fee or effectivity ratio.

**Indirectly/contextually informed:**  
- **all_or_nothing**: Some studies on all-or-nothing (threshold) games, but findings are qualitatively aligned with continuous PGGs when punishment is used (minor exceptions noted).
- **chat/communication**: Present in some designs but outcomes rarely parsed by chat on/off; little evidence on interaction effect.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries**: Common but not systematically varied or linked to efficiency in punishment treatments.

**Missing or sparse:**  
- **default_contrib**: Rarely addressed or manipulated.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Only sparse theoretical or adjacent empirical consideration (theory on reward/contract mechanisms; little direct empirical analysis).
- **show_punishment_id**: Not systematically studied in relation to efficiency outcomes in this set.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Outcome measurement heterogeneity:** Not all papers use efficiency or aggregate payoff; some report only behavioral outcomes, which may overstate or understate true efficiency effects.
- **Variance and group heterogeneity:** Substantial cross-group variation—composition effects and societal/cultural context matter, but are hard to quantify with only design dimensions (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Sparse dimension coverage:** For several prediction variables (chat, default contribution, reward parameters, identity revelation), empirical support is limited or absent.
- **Game structure boundaries:** Prediction guidance is weaker when games differ structurally from standard PGGs (e.g., generalized exchange, CPRs, threshold or binary-contribution games).
- **Empirical vs. theoretical split:** Some claims are grounded primarily in theory; empirical validation may be partial or missing, especially for novel variants.
- **Limited real-world external validity:** Laboratory PGGs use simplified payoffs, anonymized interactions, and moderate group sizes; real-world applications may see different effects.
- **Assumed ceteris paribus:** Empirical studies often hold several parameters constant; extrapolation across wider parameter ranges (very high/low MPCR, large groups, large numbers of rounds) involves additional uncertainty.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature base provides robust evidence that, in standard repeated linear PGGs with moderate group sizes, enabling punishment (especially if reasonably cost-effective) raises group efficiency over baseline. The size and reliability of this effect are moderated by punishment cost-effectiveness, group composition (strong reciprocators vs. norm-keepers), and the structure of punishment (peer vs. centralized, dense vs. sparse networks). Some key prediction dimensions are directly informed, others are not, and several potentially moderating factors are insufficiently studied within these 22 papers. When the prediction target precisely matches standard PGG features, the literature supports a positive efficiency increment. For edge-case games or incomplete design specification, prediction requires caution and inclusion of greater uncertainty.
