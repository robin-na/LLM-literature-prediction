# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set is broad and robust for the specific downstream prediction task. It consists predominantly of **empirical, experimental papers**—primarily lab experiments, with some field experiments and observational data—focused on public goods games (PGG) and closely related collective action dilemmas. The evidence is heavily weighted toward **actual payoff-based outcomes**, including efficiency, group earnings, total welfare, and surplus creation, under various institutional, social, and game-theoretic design dimensions.

Both **standard linear PGGs** and a range of **variants** (e.g., common-pool resource games, threshold games, trust/investment games, competition/contest environments, and voting/institution formation) are represented. The **majority of included studies directly manipulate and measure the effects of peer punishment (sanctions) and, in many cases, also compare or combine these with reward options, communication, exclusion/ostracism, endogenous voting, centralized/delegated punishment, and alternative information structures**.

Overall, the literature is **rich in empirical variation on design parameters relevant to the prediction task**. Theoretical discussions, mechanism analyses, and adjacent studies (e.g., trust games, dictator games, contest games, behavioral measures) supplement the evidence but are less central. Papers purely about behavioral outcomes without payoff/efficiency measures are present but not predominant for the core task.

# 2) Task Relevance

**1. pgg_or_variant**:  
- **Relevance:** *exact* for a large portion of papers, especially empirical lab studies on standard PGGs and voluntary contributions mechanisms.  
- **Close** to *adjacent* for variants like common-pool resource (CPR) games, group contests, trust games, or step-level public good games where the structure and incentives are very similar, though settings may include extraction rather than contribution, or focus more on competition or asymmetric information.

**2. punishment_or_sanctions**:  
- **Relevance:** *exact* for most included experiments, which directly manipulate peer punishment, sanction options, exclusion mechanisms, or centralized punishment technologies.  
- *Close* or *adjacent* relevance where the punitive mechanism is exclusion/ostracism, non-monetary sanctions, delegated punishment, or where punishment is via institution formation, side payments, or voting.

**3. efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- **Relevance:** *exact* for most central studies, which report group efficiency, earnings, or welfare using definitions closely matching the prediction task.  
- *Close* or *adjacent* in some studies that only report contribution rates, cooperation rates, or related behavior, or where payoff measures are proxies rather than direct group efficiency.  
- Several studies are behavioral only (*weak* or *none* for the efficiency dimension).

**Summary:** The literature is highly relevant and empirically rich along all three axes, with many *exact* matches on PGG design and payoff effect of punishment, though for fine-grained or boundary-case game design dimensions, some evidence is only *close* or *adjacent*. Studies based on trust/dictator/contest games or non-payoff outcomes should be weighted as supporting context, not direct evidence.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes** (central for prediction):  
- **Efficiency (group payoff vs. social optimum)**: directly measured in many canonical PGG studies (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Sefton et al., 2007; Ambrus & Greiner, 2012)
- **Total group earnings/welfare/surplus**
- **Net earnings after punishment/reward costs**: Several papers explicitly distinguish this from gross payoffs to clarify efficiency effects (e.g., Casari & Luini, 2009)
- **Provision rates and net returns** for threshold/step-level or contest games (used as proxies for efficiency)
- **Effect sizes**: Quantitative comparisons between no-punishment (control) and punishment treatments

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (not efficiency, but sometimes closely tracked):  
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**: Often closely correlated with—but not equivalent to—group efficiency
- **Punishment frequencies, targeting, and expenditure**
- **Norm compliance, anti-social punishment, retaliation/counter-punishment**
- **Voting behavior**, exclusion/ostracism decisions, group sorting/aggregation

**Explicit distinction:**
- MANY studies find **punishment raises contributions more than it raises efficiency**, as the costs of punishment may offset or exceed the benefits from extra cooperation.
- Several studies report *increased cooperation but decreased efficiency* due to overuse or mis-targeting of punishment, antisocial punishment, or retaliation/counter-punishment (e.g., Nikiforakis, 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## **Cross-Study Synthesis of Punishment Effects on Efficiency**

**General Patterns:**
- **Enabling punishment in PGGs often increases contribution rates robustly** (Fehr & Gächter, 2000).
- **Efficiency (payoff-based outcome) usually increases, BUT this is contingent on punishment not being overused, misdirected, or countered by retaliation**.

**When does punishment increase efficiency?**
- When the **cost to punishers is moderate or low relative to the behavioral deterrence achieved** (Sefton et al., 2007; Ambrus & Greiner, 2012).
- When **punishment is targeted at low contributors/free riders and not anti-socially at cooperators** (Ertan et al., 2009; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).
- When **punishment is combined with effective feedback, communication, or voting to refine its use** (Putterman et al., 2011; Sutter et al., 2010).
- **Endogenous institution choice (voting) or centralization/delegation** can further boost efficiency gains by reducing misuse and aligning expectations (Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Kosfeld et al., 2009).
- **Reputation mechanisms, longer games, and social context** (e.g., low trust) moderate punishment impact—long time horizons, full feedback, and reputation tracking all tend to increase the positive effect on efficiency (Fehr et al., 2010; Noussair & Tucker, 2005).

**When does punishment fail to improve—or even reduce—efficiency?**
- When **punishment is cheap but not very effective, leading to excessive punishment cycles** (Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Casari & Luini, 2009).
- When **counter-punishment/retaliation is possible and frequent**, causing wasteful feuding (Nikiforakis, 2008; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Nikiforakis & Engelmann, 2011).
- When **antisocial or misdirected punishment is prevalent** (punishing high contributors or non-defectors), sometimes driven by cultural or social norms (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Fehr et al., 2008).
- In **short games or one-shot settings**, where the cost of punishment is not offset by sufficient behavioral change (Fehr et al., 2010; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).
- **Heterogeneity or normative conflict** (i.e., division of group into parties with conflicting interests or returns) can foster feuds and reduce net efficiency despite higher cooperation (Nikiforakis et al., 2012; Tan, 2008).
- In **environments with high trust or strong existing prosocial norms**, imposed punishment can even crowd out cooperation and reduce efficiency ("crowding-out": Vollan, 2008; Tyran & Feld, 2006).

**Other critical moderators:**
- **Form of punishment institution**: Consensual or reputation-based punishment increases efficiency, while unfettered, unstructured peer punishment can reduce it (Casari & Luini, 2009; Ertan et al., 2009).
- **Punishment effectiveness/power (punishment_tech)**: High-leverage punishment increases efficiency gains more than low-leverage (Sutter et al., 2010; Ambrus & Greiner, 2012).
- **Combination with rewards** or communication: Rewards alone are less effective, but combined with punishment or as an alternative, may further boost or replace efficiency gains at lower cost (Sefton et al., 2007; Kube & Traxler, 2011).
- **Network structure**: The network topology of who can punish whom decisively alters efficiency consequences of punishment (Carpenter et al., 2012).
- **Information/feedback structure ("show_other_summaries")**: Showing contributions rather than earnings supports higher efficiency with punishment enabled, and the effect is strongly moderated by feedback content (Nikiforakis, 2010).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For the task: "Given game design and control efficiency, predict average efficiency when punishment is enabled."**

**Directly Supported Dimensions & Guidance:**
- **player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, chat, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id, show_n_rounds**: Broad empirical coverage for most of these dimensions (see next section), allowing for relatively precise adjustment of treatment efficiency predictions using literature-based moderators.
  - ***Smaller groups (3–5 players) and longer games (15+ rounds) tend to see more sustained efficiency gains from punishment, especially when random rematching is avoided.***
  - *All-or-nothing contributions generally behave similarly, but some parameterizations (threshold games) may show non-linear effects of punishment on efficiency (Guillen et al., 2007).*
  - **High marginal per-capita return (MPCR) increases the effectiveness of punishment for efficiency; very low MPCR can reduce it, as the social multiplier for punishing free riders is weak (Kosfeld et al., 2009; Carpenter et al., 2009).**
  - **Low-cost, high-impact (high punishment_tech/punishment effectiveness per unit cost) mechanisms reliably produce larger efficiency gains; high-cost, low-impact punishment risks overuse and net loss of efficiency (Anderson & Putterman, 2006).**
  - **Enabling chat or face-to-face communication increases both the level and efficiency of cooperation—even more than punishment alone. If both are enabled, expect super-additive efficiency (Bochet et al., 2006).**
  - **Feedback design is critical: feedback on contributions supports higher efficiency with punishment than feedback on earnings or both (Nikiforakis, 2010).**
  - **Exclusion/ostracism as a form of punishment (punishment tech = exclusion) generally yields large, sustained efficiency gains if the cost to punishers is not excessive (Cinyabuguma et al., 2005; Maier-Rigaud et al., 2010).**
  - **Institutional choice (endogenous voting) amplifies the positive effect, especially with strong group support for the punishment regime (Tyran & Feld, 2006; Vollan, 2008).**

**Cautions and Moderators:**
- *If the control efficiency (no punishment) is **already high** (due to context, communication, or strong social norms), the marginal benefit of punishment may be zero or negative (Kocher et al., 2012; Bochet et al., 2006).*
- *If antisocial punishment or retaliation are likely (either due to social context or feedback design), punishment may reduce efficiency even if contributions go up (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Nikiforakis et al., 2008).*
- *Cultural, social, or institutional context (not always encoded in the 14 dimensions) can override design-based predictions—e.g., in Russia and some field settings, punishment reduces efficiency (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Castillo et al., 2011).*
- *Counter-punishment/counter-sanctions, if structurally possible, sharply reduce or reverse efficiency gains from punishment relative to basic treatments (Nikiforakis, 2008; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).*
- *Endogenous institution formation/voting and centralization of punishment are often necessary for maximal efficiency gains (Sutter et al., 2010; Andreoni & Gee, 2012).*
- **Combined punishment and reward systems** (where design allows) may out-perform punishment alone for efficiency, but rewards alone are generally less sustaining (Sefton et al., 2007).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Well-Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count, num_rounds**: Robustly varied experimentally, strong empirical guidance (3–5 most common, up to 16 tested in some cases).
- **mpcr**: Varies across studies (often 0.4–0.7), with efficiency impacts well studied.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Cost-impact ratio a central moderator with detailed empirical results (including convex cost functions, exclusion, probabilistic/personalized punishment).
- **all_or_nothing**: Both all-or-nothing (binary) and continuous contribution structures are thoroughly investigated.
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds**: Feedback structures (contributions, earnings, both; knowledge of time horizon) empirically found to strongly moderate efficiency gains from punishment (Nikiforakis, 2010).
- **chat**: Communication/cheap talk often experimentally manipulated, with effects at least as large as punishment (Bochet et al., 2006).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Comparative studies of reward and joint punishment/reward protocols shed light on these dimensions.

**Indirectly/Partially Informed:**
- **default_contrib**: Only a minority of studies manipulate the contribution default (framing), but some evidence from framing/voting treatments.
- **punishment_id (show_punishment_id)**: Some studies experimentally vary anonymity of punishment, finding that identification can exacerbate feuds or reduce misdirected punishment, but overall evidence is patchy.
- **institutional choice (endogenous vs. exogenous)**: Several studies include endogenous voting or institution formation, revealing its importance as a moderator, but not all prediction settings encode this directly.

**Effectively Missing / Only Contextually Covered:**
- **Fine-grained heterogeneity**: Social/cultural context, trust norms, and gender composition affect punishment impact but are not always encoded in the 14 dimensions.
- **Exact network structure (who can punish whom)**: Some studies test variations, but this is not always captured in standard prediction dimensions.
- **Dynamic environmental feedbacks (changing MPCR or context-dependent payoff functions)**: Covered in a few specialized studies but not standard.
- **Long time horizons beyond standard lab rounds**: Some evidence exists (e.g., 50 rounds), but most studies use 10–20 rounds.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Contextual moderators are not fully captured by standard design dimensions**, such as social norms, reputation, pre-existing trust, or cultural propensities for anti-social punishment. These can powerfully shape the efficiency effect of punishment (Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Vollan, 2008).
- **The institutional form of punishment (peer, consensual, centralized, exclusion, etc.) is decisive**, but not always captured precisely in standard prediction columns. Efficiency effects differ dramatically depending on whether anti-social or counter-punishment is possible.
- **Group composition and matching protocol (partner vs stranger, symmetry/asymmetry of returns)** strongly modulate punishment’s effect on efficiency and are often not parameterized in standard design dimensions (Nikiforakis et al., 2012).
- **Feedback and information structures (showing contributions, earnings, or both)** have outsize effects on how punishment plays out—directly determining whether efficiency rises or falls with punishment (Nikiforakis, 2010).
- **Generalizability from lab to field settings is uncertain**: Field experiments and diverse cultural contexts sometimes yield substantially different efficiency outcomes from punishment, including robust negative effects (e.g., Russia, Colombia, southern Africa field studies).
- **Studies using non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, cooperation frequencies) cannot substitute for direct efficiency data**: The translation from higher cooperation to higher efficiency is not guaranteed, due to the possibility of costly, wasteful, or antisocial punishment.
- **Mechanisms involving communication, rewards, or endogenous group formation often interact non-additively with punishment**, so combined or hybrid treatments require special caution in direct prediction.
- **Some specific design variants (e.g., all-or-nothing contributions, network/monitoring architectures, time-varying or endogenous MPCRs) are underrepresented** for precise quantitative prediction.
- **Potential publication/selection bias**: While the evidence base is large, the impact of non-reports or modest effect sizes is unclear.

**Summary**:  
While the literature provides detailed and high-quality empirical guidance for the prediction of efficiency effects from design features and control efficiency in PGG-like environments with punishment, accurate prediction requires (a) careful mapping of design features (not just presence/absence of punishment), (b) explicit attention to institutional structure, (c) context-awareness for cultural or social norm moderators, and (d) a clear distinction between behavioral and efficiency outcomes. The available evidence shows substantial heterogeneity and boundary conditions, and not all design dimensions are equally well supported or measured.
