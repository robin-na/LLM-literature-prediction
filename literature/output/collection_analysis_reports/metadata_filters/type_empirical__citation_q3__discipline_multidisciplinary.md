# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists predominantly of **empirical, experimental laboratory studies** using public goods games (PGGs) or close variants, with a substantial minority focused on adjacent or related social dilemmas (e.g., step-level PGGs, Snowdrift/PD games, third-party punishment, and networked cooperation games). The evidence base is **broad in coverage** of behavioral mechanisms, design variations, and punishment modalities but **narrower in directly reporting efficiency or payoff-based outcomes in the precise PGG-punishment context** required for the prediction task. Only a subset of studies provide both *control* and *punishment-enabled* average efficiency data under clearly specified standard PGG designs.

Mechanism-focused or theory-driven contributions are limited; most evidence is **empirical and experimental**, with only occasional references to evolutionary or institutional theory (rarely with direct payoff-based modeling or prediction).

# 2) Task Relevance

**Dimensions of task relevance:**

- **pgg_or_variant**:
  - **Exact relevance**: Numerous studies use standard linear PGGs with repeated rounds, specified player counts, and marginal per-capita return (MPCR). Several use variants (binary PGGs, step-level, networked, or dyadic games).
  - **Adjacency**: Many papers are adjacent (e.g., PD, dictator games, trust games), some with meaningful but incomplete transferability.
- **punishment_or_sanctions**:
  - **Exact or close**: Many papers directly manipulate costly peer punishment (sometimes also peer reward), with a few on pool punishment, institutional punishment, or costless moral sanctions.
  - **Adjacency**: Others examine third-party punishment, ostracism, or negative reciprocity as adjacent sanctioning mechanisms.
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
  - **Exact**: A minority of studies provide direct measures of group efficiency, total earnings, or group payoff with and without punishment.
  - **Close/Adjacent**: Many papers focus on **contribution rates, cooperation frequency, punishment frequency,** or **norm compliance**—important but strictly **non-payoff behavioral outcomes**.

**Summary:**  
The literature offers **high direct relevance for PGG structure and punishment manipulations**, but **less comprehensive, though not absent, direct evidence on efficiency or payoff effects** needed for treatment efficiency prediction. Many studies report on cooperation or contribution without translating to group efficiency or reporting both control and punishment-enabled efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (relevant for prediction task):
  - *Efficiency*: Ratio of observed total group payoff to total payoff under full cooperation.
  - *Group payoff/total earnings/welfare*: Often reported, but not always normalized as "efficiency."
  - *Surplus/coins generated*: In some engineering/agent studies.
  - *Key studies providing direct efficiency/payoff metrics*: Simpson et al. (2017), Ozono et al. (2020, 2017, 2016), Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit (2017), Jiang et al. (2013), Grimalda et al. (2016), Dong et al. (2016), Tsvetkova et al. (2018), Köster et al. (2022).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (useful but must not be conflated with efficiency):
  - *Contribution or cooperation rate*: Most common, but not linearly translatable to efficiency when punishment costs are substantial (Simpson et al., 2017; Fehl et al., 2012).
  - *Punishment frequency/volume, norm compliance, retaliation, vendetta rates, emotion-driven punishment*.
  - *Observational variables*: Norms, perception, fairness, trust, etc.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**A) Effect of Peer Punishment on Efficiency: Empirical Synthesis**
- **Punishment typically increases cooperation, but not necessarily efficiency** in standard PGGs: The efficiency gains from higher cooperation often do **not offset** the direct costs inflicted by punishment (Simpson et al., 2017; Fehl et al., 2012; Grimalda et al., 2016).
- **Punishment may *reduce* efficiency**: Especially when punishment is *costly*, vendettas/retaliations occur, or the baseline (control) efficiency is already high, adding punishment can lower average group earnings (Simpson et al., 2017; Fehl et al., 2012; Grimalda et al., 2016).
- **Efficiency gains from punishment depend on game design dimensions**:
  - **Punishment cost/technology**: Lower punishment costs, or higher punishment efficacy per cost, correspond to a greater chance of net efficiency gains (Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017; Jiang et al., 2013).
  - **Number of rounds**: In short games, punishment induces costs with little time to reap benefit from improved cooperation; over more rounds, efficiency can converge with or surpass alternatives (Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017).
  - **Player count/group size**: Vendettas and inefficiency more likely in multi-player games; dyads rarely see costly punishment or efficiency loss (Fehl et al., 2012; Grimalda et al., 2016).
  - **MPCR/difficulty of cooperation**: When base efficiency is high (easy cooperation), adding severe punishment may not improve and can worsen efficiency—mild punishment suffices if anything (Jiang et al., 2013).
  - **Control condition efficiency**: Punishment more likely to increase efficiency in low baseline (control) efficiency scenarios (Jiang et al., 2013; Dong et al., 2016).

**B) Contextual and Qualifying Findings**
- **Costless or moral sanctions (approval/disapproval)** can increase both cooperation and efficiency and outperform costly material sanctions (Simpson et al., 2017).
- **Scope/localization of punishment**: Efficiency gains from punishment are contingent on the alignment of local and global incentives; misaligned incentives diminish the benefit (Ozono et al., 2020).
- **Institutional structures** (e.g., leader-supported punishment, heterogeneity in punishment cost): These can substantially moderate the efficiency effect, especially when the system enables coordination on "strong" punishers (Ozono et al., 2016; Diekmann & Przepiorka, 2015).
- **Combination with peer rewards**: Evidence is mixed; rewards alone may not boost efficiency, but can have positive effects when paired with punishment (Dong et al., 2016; Ozono et al., 2020).
- **Retaliation/vendettas**: Repeated opportunities for punishment increase the likelihood of costly retaliation spirals, reducing efficiency and affecting those involved disproportionately (Fehl et al., 2012).

**C) Non-PGG or Non-Efficiency Studies**  
Behavioral studies using adjacent designs (e.g., PDs, networked or third-party games) provide support for mechanism or boundary conditions but offer little direct quantitative leverage for precise efficiency prediction in the primary task context.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should this literature inform prediction of treatment efficiency from design dimensions plus control efficiency?**

- **Do not infer efficiency gains from increases in cooperation rates alone.**  
  Many studies show *cooperation* increases with punishment, but efficiency does not necessarily follow (Simpson et al., 2017; Fehl et al., 2012; Grimalda et al., 2016).

- **Expect that, in typical PGG lab conditions (moderate group size, repeated rounds, standard MPCR, costly peer punishment), treatment efficiency will not significantly exceed, and may sometimes fall below, control efficiency** unless:
    - The baseline (control) efficiency is low to moderate;
    - Punishment cost is low or the technology is highly efficient (high punishment impact per unit cost) (Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017; Jiang et al., 2013);
    - Institutions mitigate waste (e.g., coordinated enforcement, leader support);
    - Punishment is paired with effective, well-designed rewards.

- **Use control efficiency as a moderator/anchor**:  
  - If control efficiency is already high (due to game structure, framing, or social/moral interventions), punishment is unlikely to improve and may decrease efficiency (Jiang et al., 2013).
  - If control efficiency is low (difficult PGGs, low MPCR), enabling efficient punishment can improve efficiency—but only if the cost does not outweigh the gains (Dong et al., 2016; Jiang et al., 2013).

- **Pay close attention to:**
    - *Punishment cost and magnitude*;
    - *Game duration (num_rounds)*;
    - *Player count (group size)*;
    - *Reward/punishment coexistence*;
    - *Whether chat or summary information enables coordination*.

- **In adjacent or variant designs**, findings are mechanism-affirming but cannot substitute for direct efficiency/earnings outcomes. Use quantitative results only when mapping is clearly justified.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Dimensions Directly Informed**:
- **player_count**, **num_rounds**, **mpcr**: Nearly all relevant direct studies specify these; evidence is robust that group size and MPCR moderate punishment effects on efficiency.
- **all_or_nothing**, **chat**, **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech**: Consistently varied or reported; strong evidence for their moderating roles.
- **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech**: Addressed in a few studies testing interplay with punishment.
- **show_other_summaries**, **show_n_rounds**, **show_punishment_id**: Occasionally reported; less often systematically manipulated, but note that summary information and transparency can affect retaliation and coordination (Fehl et al., 2012; Diekmann & Przepiorka, 2015).
- **default_contrib**: Only rarely manipulated or reported; weak direct evidence.
  
**Dimensions Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed**:
- **punishment_tech** (e.g., pool vs. peer, resource linkage): Discussed in a subset, particularly for resource prioritization or institutional structure (Ozono et al., 2016, 2017).
- **reward_exists** and **reward_cost/tech**: Mentioned but rarely the focus for payoff-based outcomes.
- **chat**, **show_other_summaries**, **show_n_rounds**: March through the mechanism for information provision, coordination, and learning, but not always linked to efficiency outcomes.

**Effectively Missing or Sparse**:
- **default_contrib**: Almost never subjected to systematic variation with reported efficiency-outcome linkage.
- **show_punishment_id**: Rare; only a few studies manipulate or report the identifiability of punishers, though theoretically important for retaliation.
- **reward_cost/tech**: Discussed mostly in mixed sanction studies, but less often with direct efficiency outcomes.
- **contextual moderators**: Demographic, cultural, or individual-difference moderators less frequently tested with efficiency as outcome.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited direct efficiency evidence**: While cooperation and punishment rates are widely reported, comparatively few studies provide *both* control and punishment-enabled group efficiency outcomes.
- **Bias toward lab PGGs**: The strongest evidence is for moderately sized, Western-student-sample, repeated linear PGGs. Generalizability to field or policy interventions is limited.
- **Payoff versus behavioral conflation risk**: Many papers and even some reviews do not clearly distinguish efficiency (total earnings/welfare) from cooperation rate; relying on the latter can be misleading for downstream prediction tasks.
- **Punishment variability**: Few studies map the full parameter space (costs, magnitude, technology, visibility, etc.). Variations in implementation and reporting limit the capacity for precise, granular prediction.
- **Sparse evidence on less-standard design dimensions**: Information conditions (summary displays), default contribution framing, and identity/reputation features are unevenly incorporated.
- **Contextual dependence and outlier mechanisms**: Effects of punishment are shown to depend on control game efficacy, alignment of incentives, and susceptibility to vendettas or antisocial punishment—findings are not universally consistent, and some studies find punishment reduces efficiency (Grimalda et al., 2016), especially in one-shot or all-or-nothing designs.
- **Adjacency versus directness**: While adjacent game designs provide insight into mechanisms and boundary conditions, only a small set of studies should be used for quantifying efficiency prediction in standard PGGs.

---

**Summary:**  
For prediction of average efficiency in public goods games under peer punishment (given game design and control efficiency), the literature provides robust qualitative patterns and useful cues for key design moderators. However, direct quantitative mappings are only available for a subset of design spaces, and overreliance on behavioral (non-payoff) outcomes is a significant risk. Predictions should be anchored in control efficiency, punishment cost/effectiveness, group size, and game duration, with caution for design departures or contextual deviations.
