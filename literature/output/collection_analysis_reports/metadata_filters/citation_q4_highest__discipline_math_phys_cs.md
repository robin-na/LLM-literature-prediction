# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base is **broad, comprehensive, and primarily theoretical**, with a strong focus on **public goods games (PGG) and close variants, punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, and efficiency or related payoff outcomes**. Of the 116 papers in the set, the majority are **theoretical or simulation studies** rooted in evolutionary game theory and statistical physics, supplemented by several laboratory experiments and some literature reviews. Nearly all empirical evidence focuses on strategy frequencies, behavioral outcomes, or conditions for cooperation, with only a minority of studies providing direct experimental/computational measures of group efficiency or welfare, as strictly defined.

Most of the evidence is **directly or very closely related to standard or spatial PGGs** with explicit costly punishment or sanctioning options, although there are also many studies on adjacent models (including indirect reciprocity, reputation, repeated dyadic games, threshold games, collective-risk dilemmas, and others). **Experimental evidence with real payoffs and direct efficiency data is sparse**; most findings are simulated or analytically derived. Mechanism-based explanations, phase diagrams, and stylized models dominate.

Coverage of **design dimensions** from the prediction framework is strong for core parameters (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/effectiveness), but evidence is sparser or absent on communication/chat, framing (default contribution), information/observability, and some technology features.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Target-Relevance Assessment:**  
- **pgg_or_variant:** The set is **dominated by ‘exact’ PGG or very close variants**, especially for theory and simulation work; several studies focus on spatial/network-structured PGGs, pool vs. peer punishment, and parameter-rich PGG designs. Some adjacent coverage of indirect reciprocity and threshold/collective-risk games.
- **punishment_or_sanctions:** **Punishment and sanctioning are almost always a central mechanism** (exact/close), typically as costly peer or institutional punishment, exclusion, or conditional sanctions; a subset of studies examine reward, reputation, or withdrawal as alternatives or complements to punishment. A few focus only on reward or show ‘adjacent’ coverage (e.g., reputation, ostracism, or partner choice treated as sanction).
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** About half the papers address **efficiency or group-level payoff as a main outcome (exact/close)**, with the rest focusing on cooperation/contribution rates, norm compliance, or evolutionary stability (proximate to efficiency but not direct measures). **Direct empirical evidence for treatment–control efficiency differences is limited**; theoretical/simulation studies often report average payoff or conditions for maximal efficiency.

**Specific task fit:**  
- **Highly relevant for mapping punishment-ineligible PGG game conditions to predicted average group efficiency under treatment (punishment enabled).**
- **Most informative when both control efficiency and game design parameters are known.**
- **Less relevant (or only contextual) for scenarios dominated by communication, non-costly sanctions, or reputational enforcement not modeled as explicit costly punishment.**

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Efficiency, Group Earnings):**
- Many theory/simulation studies provide **explicit average payoff or efficiency calculations** (e.g., Cressman et al., 2012; Gintis, 2000; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Yan et al., 2021; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Adami et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2018, 2019).
- **Efficiency** is most often measured as **group payoff relative to full cooperation**. Some studies analyze **welfare, surplus, or total coins generated**.
- Experiments and adjacent mechanism papers may report **earnings**, **market efficiency**, **welfare**, or **mean fitness** (e.g., Brown et al., 2004; Li & Xiao, 2014).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- **Cooperation/contribution rates and punishment frequency** are the dominant reported outcomes in many papers (especially those focused on dynamics, evolutionary stability, or network structure).
- A large fraction of studies report **stationary fractions of each strategy**, phase diagrams, or thresholds for cooperation, rather than direct efficiency.
- Many ‘adjacent’ studies focus on **norm compliance, reputation, retaliation, ostracism**, or **social exclusion** as outcomes.

**Distinction:**  
- Most studies that report only cooperation/contribution rates treat high cooperation as a **proxy** for high efficiency, but do not always account for the **cost of punishment** or other institutional costs, which is critical in prediction of net efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis Across Papers:**

- **Punishment (Costly, Peer or Institutional):**   
  - **Enabling punishment in PGGs generally increases group efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline,** especially when baseline (control) efficiency is low (Gintis, 2000; Cressman et al., 2012; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
  - **Magnitude and direction of the efficiency gain are moderated by:**
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness (cost-to-fine ratio)**—punishment must be sufficiently inexpensive and/or effective to produce net efficiency gains (Perc et al., 2017; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Lee et al., 2022).
    - **Presence of antisocial punishment or retaliation:** **Antisocial punishment or feasible retaliation can negate or reverse efficiency gains** (Rand et al., 2010; Hauser et al., 2014; Janssen & Bushman, 2008; Goette et al., 2012). In some spatial/network settings, second-order freeloading or the structure of interaction can limit the harm of antisocial punishment (Szolnoki & Perc, 2017).
    - **Structure of interaction (spatial/networked vs. well-mixed):** **Punishment is more likely to increase efficiency in spatially structured populations**, where clustering enables cooperators and punishers to support each other (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013, 2017; Helbing et al., 2010; Adami et al., 2016).
    - **Group size and MPCR:** Lower MPCR or larger group size makes sustaining cooperation and efficiency via punishment more difficult (Suzuki & Akiyama, 2007; Hilbe et al., 2015).
    - **Punishment technology:** Institutional/pool punishment, especially when visible and public, can generate higher and more stable efficiency (Cressman et al., 2012; Schoenmakers et al., 2014). Peer punishment often dominates in terms of effectiveness at eliminating defectors, but is sensitive to cost and social context.
    - **Ecological/resource context:** In collective resource dilemmas, punishment only boosts efficiency if underlying resource dynamics allow for sustainable recovery (Chen & Szolnoki, 2018; Yan et al., 2021).

- **Limitations and Contingencies:**
  - **Negative or null effects on efficiency:** If **punishment is too costly, ineffective, or permits antisocial use**, it can reduce, not increase, net efficiency—even below the control baseline. (Rand et al., 2010; Hauser et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2022)
  - **Conditional/Adaptive punishment outperforms unconditional/steady punishment**: Conditional or adaptive mechanisms generally deliver higher efficiency per unit punishment, especially in structured settings (Chen et al., 2014; Perc & Szolnoki, 2012).
  - **Combined reward and punishment may not always outperform punishment alone**: Reward alone often promotes cooperation less efficiently than punishment; combining them yields marginal gains only in limited parameter regions (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Sasaki & Unemi, 2011).

- **Empirical studies echo, but less precisely quantify, these effects**: Laboratory experiments demonstrate that **punishment can prevent decay of cooperation and increase average earnings** relative to no-punishment controls, but results vary, especially when antisocial punishment or competition is possible (Goette et al., 2012; Fehr & Gächter, as cited in Gintis, 2000).

**Summary:**  
- **Most robust predictor: Enabling peer or institutional punishment in a standard PGG, with moderate cost, high effectiveness, and baseline low efficiency, increases treatment efficiency, subject to whether antisocial punishment or retaliation is possible.**
- **Effect is highly sensitive to game design dimensions—especially punishment cost/tech, group size, baseline efficiency, and interaction structure.**

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How Should The Literature Inform Downstream Prediction?**  
- **Expect positive shifts in efficiency with punishment enabled—**especially if the control efficiency (without punishment) is well below 1 and the punishment mechanism is reasonably cheap and effective, and the game design does not enable or incentivize antisocial punishment or easy retaliation.
- **Quantitative effect sizes should be modeled as a function of**:
  - **Punishment cost and magnitude (punishment_cost, punishment_tech):** As cost rises or effectiveness drops, efficiency gains shrink or become negative.
  - **Baseline/control efficiency:** Punishment has little effect (or may even reduce efficiency) if baseline cooperation is already high.
  - **Player count (player_count) and MPCR:** Larger groups or lower MPCR require stronger or more cost-efficient punishment to yield gains.
  - **Interaction/structure:** Spatial/networked games tend to allow punishment to be more efficient at sustaining cooperation, especially near the cooperation/defection threshold.
  - **Rules on targeting/antisocial punishment:** If antisocial punishment is possible—or punishers can be targeted for retaliation—expect smaller or negative efficiency shifts.
- **Information, visibility, and communication features** (chat, show_punishment_id, show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds) are important potential moderators, but direct evidence on their effect on efficiency is thin or only contextual in the current literature set.
- **Rewards, exclusion, and alternative mechanisms**: Where these are present or modeled, they can substitute for or supplement punishment, but the efficiency increment from reward alone is usually less than for punishment in standard PGG settings.

**Crucially:**  
- **Whenever prediction is made using non-payoff outcomes (e.g., contribution rates), correct for the cost of punishment to recover net efficiency**. Many papers show high cooperation at high cost, leading to only marginal, zero, or even negative net efficiency gains.

**When calibration is feasible:**  
- **Use phase diagrams, analytical thresholds, and illustrative parameter sets from the theory papers for mapping design parameters to predicted efficiency gain** (e.g., Perc et al., 2017; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Cressman et al., 2012; Gintis, 2000), adjusted for spatial/network structure and possibility of antisocial punishment.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- **player_count**: Heavily featured in almost every theory/simulation study; group size routinely shown to moderate the effectiveness of punishment and baseline efficiency.
- **num_rounds**: Often analyzed theoretically, with repeated vs. one-shot interaction an important moderator.
- **mpcr**: A central parameter (called synergy, multiplication factor, or benefit-to-cost ratio); strong direct evidence for its impact.
- **punishment_cost**/**punishment_tech**: Detailed, with many phase diagrams and analytic thresholds.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Well studied in a subset of papers, allowing comparison to punishment.
- **all_or_nothing**: Modeled in both continuous and binary (all-or-nothing) contribution simulations.
- **show_n_rounds**: Sometimes addressed, especially in repeated partnership and indirect reciprocity models.
  
**Indirectly/Contextually Informed Dimensions:**
- **chat**: Occasionally included (e.g., Ostrom, 2009) or discussed as communication/monitoring, but rarely modeled directly in payoff terms.
- **default_contrib**: Contribution framing is seldom reported or modeled.
- **show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Indirectly discussed in relation to retaliation, visibility, and information structure, but typically as contextual factors, not manipulated in efficiency analyses.

**Sparse or Missing:**
- **default_contrib, chat, show_punishment_id, show_other_summaries**: Direct quantitative evidence for their influence on efficiency outcomes is almost entirely missing; these features are sometimes mentioned or contextualized, but not the subject of systematic experimental or theoretical analysis.

**Summary of Coverage:**
- **Core payoff and punishment design dimensions are richly documented; information, framing, and communication dimensions are under-developed.**

# 7) Important Limitations

**Key Limitations of This Evidence Set for Downstream Prediction:**
- **Empirical data on group efficiency from laboratory or field experiments are sparse**. Most direct evidence is theoretical or simulation-based, which may not capture real-world nuance or behavioral noise.
- **Many results are conditional on idealized assumptions**—such as no error, no antisocial punishment, specific strategy spaces, or perfect knowledge of game parameters.
- **Results may differ between peer and institutional punishment**, and between continuous and all-or-nothing contribution games.
- **Sparse coverage of information, communication, and framing dimensions** (e.g., chat, default contribution, visibility of punishers), which could matter in practice.
- **Quantitative estimates of treatment–control efficiency change are rare**; most results are qualitative, mechanistic, or illustrated via phase diagrams, not direct with effect sizes.
- **Ambiguity and disagreement remain in zones where antisocial punishment, retaliation, or high punishment cost exists:** some papers suggest efficiency gains, others show null or negative net effects.
- **Results on the effect of population structure (spatial vs. well-mixed) are robust in direction but varied in magnitude**, and not all models map cleanly to typical experimental designs.
- **Payoff-based and cooperation-based outcomes are sometimes conflated:** many studies measure cooperation/contribution rate but neglect to account for costs of punishment when inferring efficiency.
- **Limited transferability to designs with overlapping or combined mechanisms** (e.g., simultaneous reward and punishment, exclusion, partner choice, reputation tracking), unless specifically modeled.

---

**In summary:** The literature consistently supports the claim that **enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like environments tends to increase efficiency relative to control—when punishment is effective, not too costly, and antisocial punishment or retaliation is limited**. However, **the size and direction of the effect are highly sensitive to game design dimensions**, especially punishment cost/effectiveness, group size, structure, and whether negative side-effects of punishment are permitted by the design. **Prediction for unexplored dimensions (framing, communication, visibility) is poorly supported by this set**. For robust quantitative prediction, use theoretical phase diagrams calibrated by baseline efficiency and key design parameters, with strong caveats about limitations for less-studied effects.
