# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is large and methodologically broad, but the core evidentiary base for the downstream prediction task is a substantial subset of experimental lab studies. Most high-relevance papers use standard linear public goods games (PGG) or close structural variants, and they empirically test the effect of enabling punishment (usually peer or central/civic punishment) on group payoffs, total welfare, or efficiency. Some studies extend to field experiments and non-human or agent-based simulations, but the dominant base remains well-controlled experimental economics and social psychology lab studies.

While the majority of exact-relevance studies are empirical and experimentally manipulate punishment mechanisms, there are also many papers measuring closely related behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution or cooperation rate) or focusing on psychological, neural, cultural, or reputational mechanisms that may underlie punishment or norm enforcement. Only a subset directly reports efficiency, defined as total group payoff relative to the maximal possible (full cooperation in all rounds).

A smaller but significant proportion of the evidence is adjacent or weakly relevant, using other social dilemma games (e.g., prisoner's dilemma, common-pool resource, threshold, or trust games), reporting non-payoff behavioral outcomes, or exploring normative, reputational, or neural aspects of sanctioning. These offer indirect insights or moderators but are less directly transferable to the efficiency prediction task.

Overall, the evidence base is broad, rich, and research-grade for behavioral economic prediction requests, but only a portion is narrowly tailored for mapping from full game design dimensions and baseline efficiency to treatment efficiency with peer punishment.

# 2) Task Relevance

### a) PGG or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`)
- **Exact:** Numerous studies employ the standard linear public goods game, using typical game-theoretic parameters (group size 3–5, 10–50 rounds, known/unknown rounds, continuous or all-or-nothing contributions) (e.g., Simpson et al., 2017; Dickinson et al., 2015; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017). Some field-framed and agent-based simulations also use faithful PGG implementations.
- **Close/Adjacent:** Several studies use close PGG variants (e.g., snowdrift, volunteer’s dilemma, step-level PGGs, CPR, trust/reciprocity games), capturing different aspects of collective action but lacking the exact mapping to the classic PGG structure.
- **Weak/None:** There is limited evidence from outside the PGG framework (e.g., Dictator or Ultimatum Games, non-cooperation lab tasks), which focus on other-regarding preferences but lack direct design dimension overlap.

### b) Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`)
- **Exact:** Many experiments directly manipulate the availability, type, or cost/technology of punishment (including peer, central, democratic, institutional, exogenous, and moral-judgment feedback). Most key studies compare a no-punishment baseline to punishment-enabled treatment(s).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some focus on reward-as-sanction alternatives, monitoring/audit schemes, communication, ostracism, or social disapproval—adjacent mechanisms but not implemented as direct, costly PGG punishment.
- **Weak/None:** Some studies offer context on norm enforcement, restitution, or restorative justice in real societies, but do not manipulate punishment in a controlled economic game.

### c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`)
- **Exact:** Numerous lab studies directly report group efficiency (payoff as a proportional share of the maximum possible) or closely related outcomes (group earnings, welfare, surplus, or total coins generated), enabling precise mapping to the prediction task.
- **Close:** A moderately sized group reports group total earnings but may not normalize to the full cooperation benchmark, or discusses impact on inequality in payoffs.
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Many behavioral studies report only contribution rates, cooperation levels, or punishment frequency, not payoff-based outcomes. Some report "mixed" overall effects or focus primarily on mediation and mechanism rather than outcomes.
- **None:** A small portion of the set focuses solely on perceptions, psychological measures, or attitudinal responses without economic outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured in the Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes:**
- Directly measured in the most relevant studies: group efficiency (total group payoff relative to full cooperation), group earnings, welfare, surplus, or average payoffs.
- Sometimes reported as changes in payoff over time, peak vs. late-round efficiency, or earnings broken down by role (e.g., leader vs. peer).
- In studies using adjacent games, related outcomes are total payoff, profit, earnings, or surplus, but not always benchmarked to maximum cooperation.

**Non-Payoff (Behavioral) Outcomes:**
- Very common: contribution rate, cooperation frequency, average individual behavior, punishment/reward assigned, norm compliance, retaliation, or group satisfaction/fairness ratings.
- Others report norm perceptions, trust, trustworthiness, “spiral of distrust,” vicarious learning, social preference indicators, anger/guilt/revenge motivations, or neural/reputational outcomes.


**Distinction:**  
While many papers report both types, **only those reporting direct payoff/efficiency outcomes are fully and directly informative for the prediction task**. Contribution rate or punishment usage alone is not a sufficient proxy for efficiency because punishment can raise cooperation but also destroy value via costs and retaliation cycles.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the highest-relevance, empirically grounded studies, **several robust findings emerge** regarding how punishment affects efficiency in PGG-like environments:

- **Punishment reliably increases contributions/cooperation rates** relative to no-punishment baselines, regardless of whether punishment is peer-based, central, or institutional (Simpson et al., 2017; Dickinson et al., 2015; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017).
- **However, the effect of punishment on group efficiency is highly sensitive to its cost, implementation technology, and institutional structure**:
    - In standard PGGs with costly, decentralized peer punishment, **efficiency often does NOT increase** (Simpson et al., 2017; Nockur et al., 2021; Fehl et al., 2012). Higher contributions are offset (or more than offset) by the deadweight loss from the costs of punishment and cycles of retaliation.
    - **Efficiency increases when punishment is cheap, effective, and well-designed** (centralized, democratic, or group-coordinated), or when punishment is fully deterrent (Dickinson et al., 2015; Kube et al., 2015; Dai et al., 2015; Harrell & Simpson, 2016). Endogenous institutions (voting for punishment) or moral-judgment (costless sanctions) often lead to the strongest efficiency gains.
    - **Judicial error (misapplied punishment)**—especially mistakenly punishing cooperators—substantially reduces efficiency gains or reverses them, even if punishment increases contributions (Markussen et al., 2016).
    - **Scope and alignment**: In games with local vs. global efficiency, or where there is a “harmed minority,” punishment can fail to increase or even decrease efficiency—in those contexts, even “good” punishment may be misdirected or socially wasteful (Ozono et al., 2020; Dekel et al., 2017).
    - **Over-punishment, lack of coordination, or group-funded punishment** can actually lower efficiency compared to no-punishment, especially when the cost of punishment is not internalized or multiple punishers act simultaneously (Ouss & Peysakhovich, 2015; Fehl et al., 2012).
    - **Leadership structure matters**: Centralized punishment by prosocial leaders is more efficient than peer-punishment; when leaders are proself, efficiency gains are weaker (Harrell & Simpson, 2016).
    - **Reward can substitute or complement punishment**: In games with both options, reward is sometimes more effective at increasing efficiency, or at least as effective (Dai et al., 2015; Nockur et al., 2021).
    - **Information conditions** and **transparency** strongly moderate punishment's impact: under imperfect information or noisy signals, punishment loses effectiveness and may be misapplied, reducing efficiency (Nicklisch et al., 2016).
    - **Cultural and normative context** moderate both the willingness to punish and its effects on efficiency, with some settings (e.g., high respect for authority or strong social norms) amplifying or dampening efficiency effects (Vollan et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2023).

- **Baseline (control) efficiency is a strong moderator**:
    - If baseline efficiency is already high, the marginal benefit of adding punishment is often negative, since costly punishment is more likely to create welfare loss than gain (Jiang et al., 2013; Nair et al., 2018).
    - When baseline efficiency is low (i.e., cooperation is hard to sustain), severe and effective punishment may be necessary and can improve efficiency, provided costs don’t wipe out the gains (Jiang et al., 2013).

- **Adjacent/negative findings**:
    - Noncostly or “moral” sanctioning (judgment, gossip) can increase both cooperation and efficiency, often more so than costly punishment (Simpson et al., 2017; Jolly & Chang, 2021).
    - In structural or institutional variants (contest games, negative externality games, CPRs), peer punishment is less effective or even counterproductive, while formal rules, taxes, or compensation mechanisms are more reliable at improving efficiency (Cason & Gangadharan, 2013; Cason & Mui, 2014; Vollan et al., 2013).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based strictly on the evidence in this paper set, **predicting the average efficiency of the treatment game with peer punishment enabled (given design dimensions and control efficiency) should follow these principles**:

- **Enabling decentralized, costly peer punishment does NOT guarantee efficiency gains**. Whether efficiency increases, stays the same, or decreases depends on:
    - **Punishment cost-to-impact ratio**: High-cost and/or low-impact punishment is likely to reduce efficiency, as punishment expenditures (often driven by retaliation and miscoordination) exceed the gains from increased cooperation (Simpson et al., 2017; Nockur et al., 2021).
    - **Structure and transparency of the punishment mechanism**: Centralized, coordinated, or group-democratic mechanisms fare better; peer punishment with multiple independent punishers tends to generate over-punishment. Peer punishment is also highly sensitive to the visibility of actions and punishers (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Ouss & Peysakhovich, 2015).
    - **Baseline (control) efficiency**: If the control game already achieves moderate-to-high efficiency, introducing punishment often results in a net welfare loss (Jiang et al., 2013; Nair et al., 2018).
    - **Marginal per-capita return (MPCR)**: Punishment is more likely to improve efficiency in "difficult" PGGs (low MPCR), as the opportunity for increased cooperation is greatest and the deterrent effect outweighs punishment costs; when cooperation is easy (high MPCR/high control efficiency), punishment adds little or may be costly (Jiang et al., 2013).
    - **Group size and duration**: Smaller, short-term groups with known number of rounds may see higher efficiency if punishment is present and coordinated; large groups and long horizons can see reduced efficiency, especially if retaliation or norm discordance arise (Ozono et al., 2020; Bracht et al., 2008).
    - **Information structure**: Imperfect monitoring or ambiguity leads to wasteful or misdirected punishment, hence lower efficiency (Markussen et al., 2016; Nicklisch et al., 2016).
    - **Presence of harmed minorities or misaligned incentives**: If punishers cannot target only norm violators, or if some group members are harmed by contributions, punishment may not raise efficiency and can worsen outcomes (Dekel et al., 2017).
    - **Normative, cultural, or psychological moderators**: Cultural respect for authority, pre-existing strong norms, or the presence of pro-social leaders enhance the likelihood and impact of efficient punishment, while contested norms or group-level dissent can undermine efficiency effects (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Vollan et al., 2017).

- **Quantitative Prediction:** When control efficiency is low (e.g., < 0.6), and punishment is cheap and effective (low cost, high impact, centralized or well-coordinated), expect a substantial increase in efficiency—possibly up to full cooperation (Kube et al., 2015; Dickinson et al., 2015). When control efficiency is moderate to high or punishment is costly and peer-based, efficiency may stagnate or decrease (Simpson et al., 2017; Nockur et al., 2021). The best empirical estimate is that enabling peer punishment in typical lab PGGs with standard parameters will, on average, either not change or slightly reduce efficiency, unless the above listed moderators are favorable.

- When non-material (costless) moral judgments or social sanctioning are enabled (not peer punishment), **efficiency can increase above both the control and costly punishment conditions** (Simpson et al., 2017).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions (multiple exact or close studies):**
- `player_count` (group size): Well explored, often 3–5; effects of group size on punishment’s efficacy are sometimes addressed.
- `num_rounds`: Extensive variation; both short and long games, one-shot vs. repeated.
- `mpcr`: Commonly manipulated; key moderator of baseline efficiency and the opportunity for punishment to have a positive effect.
- `all_or_nothing` vs. continuous: Most exact studies use continuous; some threshold and all-or-nothing games are included.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Frequently varied; studies assess cost-to-impact ratio, technology (peer, central, democratic), and scope.
- `chat`: Explored as a moderator; sometimes communication is enabled, often not.
- `reward_exists`/`reward_cost`/`reward_tech`: Some key studies compare reward and punishment, or mixed regimes.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Occasionally manipulated or reported; full information typically present in canonical lab settings.
- `show_punishment_id`: Some studies manipulate anonymity or identification of punishers.
- `default_contrib`: Framing as opt-in/opt-out is less commonly varied, but included in some high-relevance work.

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**
- `punishment_tech`: Many papers discuss the distinction between peer, central, or group punishment, but fewer systematically manipulate this alongside other dimensions.
- `reward_exists` and related: Explored in several studies, sometimes reporting efficiency differences between reward, punishment, and mixed schemes.
- `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes manipulated to test retaliation or social signaling, but less often as a primary treatment dimension.
- `chat`, `show_n_rounds`: Sometimes studied as information structure, but effects are often reported as context or secondary moderators.

**Effectively Missing or Sparse:**
- Simultaneous systematic variation across all 14 dimensions is rare; no paper or set manipulates all in a fully factorial way.
- Some design details, such as the specific framing of default contributions or the precise visibility of summaries, are under-explored as standalone predictors.
- The effect of combinatorial moderation (e.g., high player count × long rounds × unknown schedule × peer punishment × high cost) on efficiency is less precisely mapped.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Outcome “contamination”:** Many studies report only contribution rate or behavioral cooperation, which is not the same as efficiency due to the potential for costly punishment to destroy surplus.
- **Missing treatment contrasts:** Some high-detail papers report only on games with punishment, lacking a no-punishment control for direct incremental efficiency effects.
- **Heterogeneity of punishment mechanism:** Conclusions depend strongly on whether punishment is centralized, peer-based, endogenous (voted), exogenous (imposed), or combines with other interventions.
- **Limited external validity:** Lab experiments use student samples, small groups, and artificial stakes; many real-world factors (culture, life experience, long-term relationships) are not fully captured.
- **Sparse multi-dimensional mapping:** Very few studies systematically manipulate more than 3–4 relevant dimensions at once, so interaction effects are only spottily covered.
- **Field/contextual studies:** Adjacent and ethnographic evidence is useful for understanding moderate-to-long-run or cultural effects, but does not provide intervention-level, efficiency-focused experimental outcomes necessary for direct prediction.
- **Reporting and operationalization variance:** Some payoffs are reported as raw totals, others as percent of social optimum; some studies focus on inequality rather than mean efficiency.
- **Ambiguity over effect size or direction:** There is genuine disagreement—sometimes punishment increases or decreases efficiency depending on context and parameter specifics.

**In summary:**  
While the literature is rich and highly relevant for the prediction task, the mapping from design dimensions plus control efficiency to treatment efficiency is only directly informed for a subset of game dimensions and settings. The direction and magnitude of the punishment effect on efficiency is highly sensitive to institutional, structural, and cultural moderators—especially the cost and coordination of punishment, the information environment, and the baseline level of efficiency in control games. Models or predictors built from this literature must carefully account for these moderators, and should not generalize the positive effect of punishment on cooperation to efficiency without considering their impact.
