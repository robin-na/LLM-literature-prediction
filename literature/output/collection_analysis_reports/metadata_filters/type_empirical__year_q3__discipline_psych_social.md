# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is **dominated by empirical, experimental lab studies** focusing on public goods games (PGGs) and direct variants, with a substantial subset examining adjacent social dilemmas or norm enforcement paradigms. Empirical findings are well represented, especially for **standard linear PGGs, with and without punishment mechanisms**. There is a **narrow, strong backbone of studies** closely matching the prediction task—enabling peer, democratic, or centralized punishment in PGGs and observing efficiency/payoff effects (e.g., Nockur et al., 2021; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).

Evidence on **payoff-based outcomes** is present in a moderate fraction of the adjacent and more distant variant studies, but most adjacent studies focus only on behavioral cooperation, norm compliance, punishment assignment, or reputation, not efficiency. A large number of studies address motivational, cognitive, or social moderators of punishment, often without payoff outcomes.

Overall, the **evidence base is strong for classic laboratory PGGs with direct measurement of efficiency/payoff responses to enabling punishment**; moderately broad for related payoff-based outcomes in variants and some real-world/framed field experiments (e.g., CPR games); and thin for less standard institutional and contextual dimensions or for non-lab environments.

---

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) pgg_or_variant:**
- **Exact relevance**: Most of the first-tier evidence is drawn from **standard linear PGGs with repeated rounds**, all key dimensions measured (player count, MPCR, etc.) and with clear mapping to the prediction task.
- **Close/Adjacent/Weak**: Some studies use generalized exchange, CPR, trust games, dictator/ultimatum games, or other social dilemmas. These provide relevant context or mechanisms but are **less directly predictive**.

**b) punishment_or_sanctions:**
- **Exact relevance**: Each target paper in the core sample features explicit punishment mechanisms (peer, democratic, centralized, third-party) and manipulates their presence for treatment vs. control.
- **Close/Adjacent/Weak**: Some variants explore only sanctions via exclusion, negative reputation, or feedback, or focus on informal/social sanctions, which are adjacent but not the same as classic costly punishment.

**c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
- **Exact**: A subset directly reports **group efficiency or total payoff as a primary outcome**.
- **Close/Adjacent/Weak**: Many more report only contribution/cooperation rates, norm compliance, or punishment rates, and draw inferential or indirect conclusions about efficiency. Some link increased cooperation to implied efficiency, but do not present direct payoff measures.

**Synthesis**: The **most confident predictions are supported in repeated, 4- to 5-person lab PGGs with clearly measured efficiency and explicit punishment manipulation**. Predictions are weaker where only adjacent games, informal sanctions, or behavioral outcomes are available.

---

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**: **Efficiency** (group payoff/optimal ratio), group earnings/profits, surplus, and occasionally inequality (e.g., Hauser et al., 2021; Nockur et al., 2021; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Ertör-Akyazi, 2019).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**: **Contribution rate, cooperation rate, punishment frequency, norm compliance**, antisocial punishment, attitudes, reputation, trust, willingness to enforce norms etc. These are much more prevalent, but are explicitly **not direct measures of efficiency** as per the task definition.
- Some studies map increased contribution or compliance to presumed increases in efficiency, yet do **not directly report group payoffs**; caution is required when interpreting such indirect evidence.

---

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Positive Efficiency Effects:**
- **Enabling punishment in standard PGGs has a positive effect on group efficiency** (payoff/profit), especially when punishment is centralized or democratically coordinated (Castillo et al., 2021; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Benard & Barclay, 2020).
- **Peer punishment in small groups with high transparency** and no immunity **boosts efficiency even when punishment is costly**, mainly because the threat, not heavy use, increases cooperation (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).
- **The presence and technical details of punishment (cost, effectiveness), information environment (feedback linking punishment to behavior), and the structure of selection (democracy vs. exogenous)** substantially moderate the efficiency effects (Nockur et al., 2021; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Benard & Barclay, 2020).
- **Anticipation of punishment** (without frequent actual use) can elevate efficiency by raising voluntary contributions (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).

**Neutral/Negative Efficiency Effects:**
- **Peer punishment** is often **less efficient or even reduces efficiency due to overuse and punishment costs**, especially with antisocial punishment or absence of clear linking feedback (Nockur et al., 2021; Vollan et al., 2019; Ahmad & Loch, 2020).
- **Antisocial punishment, high punishment costs, and poorly designed peer mechanisms can negate or reverse efficiency gains** (Nockur et al., 2021; Vollan et al., 2019; Ahmad & Loch, 2020).
- **Centralized punishment, democratic punishment, or designs with feedback/legitimacy** tend to avoid efficiency losses associated with peer punishment (Nockur et al., 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Benard & Barclay, 2020).

**Dependence on Context and Control Efficiency:**
- If **baseline (control) efficiency is already high** (near-optimal cooperation), adding punishment **yields little or can harm efficiency**, mainly through unnecessary punishment costs (Nair et al., 2018).
- **Low control efficiency predicts greater marginal gains from punishment**; when baseline cooperation is minimal, punishment’s addition creates larger efficiency jumps (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct empirical mapping is robust for standard PGGs** (4–5 players, 6–24 rounds, known MPCR, costed punishment): **Control (no-punishment) efficiency is a strong baseline**, and **adding punishment generally increases efficiency** by deterring free-riding, with the size and direction of effect **highly sensitive to the type of punishment mechanism** (peer vs. democratic/centralized), the cost-effectiveness of punishment, and the information environment (whether feedback clearly links punishment to behavior).
- **Peer punishment frequently reduces efficiency compared to more structured/enforced systems** due to over-punishment costs and sometimes antisocial punishment (Nockur et al., 2021; Vollan et al., 2019; Ahmad & Loch, 2020).
- **Democratic, centralized, or legitimacy-enhanced punishment eliminates or reverses efficiency losses** seen with standard peer punishment (Nockur et al., 2021; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
- **Critical design dimensions** for predicting efficiency gains/losses after enabling punishment are:
  - **punishment_cost** and **punishment_tech** (lower cost/higher effectiveness = higher efficiency gains),
  - **feedback/information** (essential for punishment to improve efficiency),
  - **structure/legitimacy of punishment (democratic/centralized > standard peer)**,
  - **control efficiency (low control efficiency = higher potential gains; high baseline = risk for net losses)**.
- **Information environment**: If the design includes **feedback that links punishment to norm violation**, expect positive efficiency effects; **absent feedback, punishment may not raise efficiency** even if it increases cooperation (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).
- **Presence of immunity or ability to avoid sanctions undermines positive effects** (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).
- **Group/cultural composition and prevalence of “strong reciprocators” vs. “norm-keepers”** (those who punish high and low contributors alike) moderate the efficiency effect size; heterogeneity and antisocial punishment can reduce gains (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Vollan et al., 2019).
- **Prediction is best for designs directly matching lab PGGs in parameter space.** For variants (CPR, asymmetric endowments, small groups), expect qualitative patterns to hold, but with reduced certainty.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- **player_count, num_rounds**: Standard lab PGGs (4–5 players, 6–24 rounds); supported by multiple direct
  efficiency comparisons.
- **mpcr**: Most efficient when moderate or high (>0.3); nearly all direct evidence studies specify MPCR.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Multiple studies directly compare different cost/impact ratios and their effect on efficiency.
- **chat**: Generally disabled; a few studies and field experiments compare communication vs. punishment directly (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019).
- **all_or_nothing**: Most studies use continuous contributions, but several test binary/all-or-nothing setups.
- **punishment selection structure**: Clear evidence on peer vs. democratic vs. centralized punishment.

**Indirectly/Contextually Discussed:**
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Some studies mention the availability of round number or feedback, often as context; direct manipulation is less common, but feedback quality is crucial for efficiency impact.
- **default_contrib**: Only contextually addressed via framing studies.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Explored in several adjacent studies; reward typically adds or has complementary effects to punishment but is not the focus of this paper set.
- **asymmetric endowments/inequality**: Some studies address unequal initial resources; effect on efficiency is robust to endowment structure, but visibility of endowments can moderate the effect of punishment/reward (Hauser et al., 2021).

**Effectively Missing:**
- **Dimensions rarely or never manipulated in a way that supports causal payoff-based attribution:** **show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id, default_contrib, reward_tech**.
- **Non-standard institutional features (e.g., complex network structures, very large group sizes, uncommon time horizons) have only contextual or adjacent support.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency/Payoff effects are only available for a subset of the paper set**—the majority of studies report non-payoff outcomes, so the predictive base narrows to about **10–12 highly relevant experimental studies**.
- **Peer punishment may reduce efficiency**, making prediction sensitive to whether the punishment mechanism is standard (open peer), democratic, or centralized. **Failing to specify this will mislead predictions.**
- **Adjacency to the PGG design varies:** Generalization to generalized exchange, CPR, or non-lab contexts is not always warranted (e.g., Kanitsar, 2021; Vollan et al., 2019).
- **Social composition and cultural moderators are recognized as important,** but their effects are not systematically tested across most of the evidence base.
- **Information structure and feedback (e.g., linking punishment to violation) are under-explored;** yet available evidence shows these are key to positive efficiency effects (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).
- **Antisocial punishment, immunity from punishment, and lack of feedback can turn positive to negative effects**—these are often tested only in single studies.
- **Long-term versus short-term effects:** Most studies have short (6–24 round) horizons; simulation evidence for long-term reversal (Szekely et al., 2020) is not tested in human experiments.
- **Reward and mixed sanction systems (punishment + reward) are only peripherally addressed**, limiting prediction for those dimensions.
- **Variants such as threshold games, exclusion, or market-based rules** are less well covered.
- **Real-world observability of effects is questioned by some observational studies, suggesting lab effects may be overestimated in practice**.

---

**Summary**: For the prediction task—**predicting treatment efficiency as a function of design dimensions and control (no-punishment) efficiency**—the literature is most informative for standard repeated PGGs with explicit, well-designed punishment mechanisms and measured efficiency outcomes. **Key predictors are punishment structure (peer vs. centralized/democratic), punishment cost/tech, and information environment**. **Control efficiency provides a strong baseline, with expected efficiency gains contingent on these moderators**. **Ambiguity persists for designs with peer punishment and high antisocial punishment, high baseline efficiency, or missing punishment feedback**. **Predictions must carefully match design dimensions to those covered by payoff-based evidence**; extrapolation outside this parameter space is not well supported by the current literature.
