# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 15 sources, spanning empirical laboratory and field experiments, theory papers, agent-based models, and observational studies. Of these, a minority directly study public goods games (PGGs) or close variants with explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms and report payoff-based measures of outcome. Many papers are conceptual or theoretical, and several empirical papers address only behavioral outcomes or contextually related environments (e.g., resource governance, business networks, or market scenarios).

The evidence for the prediction task is therefore broad in its disciplinary reach (ranging from laboratory PGGs to complex field interventions and agent-based models) but relatively narrow in its direct empirical grounding: only a few sources provide experimental/predictive data linking game design dimensions, punishment, and efficiency or directly related group payoff outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**
- **exact:** A handful of papers are directly about standard public goods games (Wu & Sun, 2022), or use PGG design elements (e.g., step-level or binary PGGs).
- **close:** Several studies use close variants—common pool resource (CPR) dilemmas, third-party punishment games, or repeated resource management settings (Amirova et al., 2022; Herne et al., 2023; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022).
- **adjacent/weak:** Many are conceptual, agent-based, or focus on analogous dilemmas (Liu & Yang, 2018; Suratin et al., 2023; Campbell, 1991; Angourakis et al., 2015).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**
- **exact:** Punishment is a core manipulation or modeled element in Wu & Sun (2022), Amirova et al. (2022), Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022), Ferguson (2021), and Liu & Yang (2018).
- **adjacent/close:** Some highlight related mechanisms (informal sanctions, exclusion, institutional punishment/reward, or social disapproval—Suratin et al., 2023; Angourakis et al., 2015; Campbell, 1991).
- **none:** Several studies do not include any punishment or sanctioning (e.g., Zhang et al., 2019; Herne et al., 2023; Holubcik et al., 2023).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
- **exact/close:** Only a small subset report group efficiency (measured as ratio of achieved to maximal payoff) or directly related monetary outcomes (Herne et al., 2023; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Zhang et al., 2019). Some (Amirova et al., 2022; Wu & Sun, 2022) report behavioral proxies (e.g., total contributions, collective investment), which are close to but not the same as efficiency.
- **adjacent/weak:** Most papers report only behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, norm compliance, investment frequencies, etc.) or theorize about the potential for payoff improvement.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**
  - Group efficiency (explicitly, as a proportion of optimal): Rare—directly measured in Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022) and Herne et al. (2023; though no punishment condition).
  - Total or average group payoff/earnings: Commonly approximated but usually indirect (Wu & Sun, 2022: total contribution; Amirova et al., 2022: collective investment).
  - Profit or surplus: In some market-oriented games (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Angourakis et al., 2015).
  - Welfare/total coins: Rare.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - Contribution rate, free-riding, cooperation frequency: Most commonly reported, especially in papers using theory, psychological manipulation, or non-punishment settings (Gomez-Ruiz & Sánchez-Expósito, 2020; Holubcik et al., 2023; Liu & Yang, 2018).
  - Norm compliance, choice of sanctioning, exclusion decisions.
  - Norm change/behavioral tipping points (Berger, 2021), feedback response.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Effect of punishment on efficiency/group payoff:**
- **Mixed empirical evidence:**  
  - In a field experiment with real farmers (Amirova et al., 2022), enabling punishment (penalty) mechanisms *reduced* collective investment compared to both baseline and communication treatments. The penalty condition crowded out intrinsic cooperation, yielding lower efficiency-related outcomes.  
  - In a lab-based simulation with market competition (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022), introducing a salient, cost-imposing punishment (carbon tax) slightly *increased* group profit/efficiency and renewable energy use after implementation, though the difference was not always statistically significant.
  - Theory/modeling (Wu & Sun, 2022) and agent-based models (Angourakis et al., 2015) generally predict or find that punishment (particularly when coupled with compensation or when not excessive) increases cooperation and total contributions. However, quantitative efficiency/payoff effects are not clearly reported; sanctions that are too harsh can backfire by excluding cooperative participants affected by bad luck.
- **Communication vs. punishment:**  
  - Communication (chat, deliberation) robustly increases efficiency and cooperation (Herne et al., 2023; Amirova et al., 2022), often more reliably than punishment. Where both were tested, chat outperformed penalty in efficiency gains.
- **Nature and framing of punishment:**  
  - Social or non-financial punishment can be more effective or less destructive than financial sanctions (Suratin et al., 2023; Campbell, 1991), although these arguments remain conceptual.
  - Compensation or mixing punishment with restorative elements improves cooperation (Wu & Sun, 2022; Ferguson, 2021).
- **Other moderators:**  
  - The effectiveness of punishment on efficiency may depend on:
    - Game structure (all-or-nothing vs. continuous; step-level thresholds).
    - Player count, rounds, and visibility of outcomes.
    - Cost/size of punishment relative to total stakes or gains.
    - Presence or absence of informal or motivational interventions (consistent contributors, team identity, feedback).
  - Too strict or too lax punishment can both undermine cooperation (Angourakis et al., 2015).
- **Control efficiency implication:**  
  - High baseline efficiency (especially when communication or social identity is present) reduces the potential upside of adding punishment; in some cases, punishment may even depress efficiency by reducing voluntary cooperation (Amirova et al., 2022).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the above, the literature informs the prediction task as follows:

- **Where does punishment increase efficiency?**  
  - In environments where baseline (control) efficiency is low (e.g., no communication, no social identity, low default contributions), punishment (especially if not excessive in cost or administratively complex) may shift outcomes to higher efficiency by deterring free-riding (supported by Wu & Sun, 2022—behaviorally, and Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022—profit-wise, though weakly).
- **When can punishment decrease efficiency?**  
  - Where control efficiency is already bolstered by communication or strong intrinsic motivation, adding punishment may undermine voluntary cooperation, crowd out intrinsic motivation, and decrease efficiency (Amirova et al., 2022; Angourakis et al., 2015).
- **Dependence on design dimensions:**  
  - The effect of punishment depends on cost, type (monetary/social), and the richness of other mechanisms (chat, identity framing, visibility).
  - Punishment that is too costly or too harsh (e.g., severe penalties, exclusion with no adjustment for bad luck) can reduce efficiency.
- **Prediction limitations:**  
  - Most effect claims regarding punishment relate to cooperation rates, not actual efficiency or payoff, and cannot be cleanly translated into predicted efficiency values.
  - Quantitative estimates of the magnitude of efficiency shifts due to introducing punishment, controlling for design dimensions and baseline efficiency, are not provided.
- **Practical implication:**  
  - When predicting treatment efficiency, cautiously expect a nonzero or positive effect of enabling punishment mechanisms only in designs where other cooperation-promoting features are weak, punishment is not excessive, and baseline efficiency is low. In designs with strong communication/social identity or high control efficiency, the effect may be neutral or even negative.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (empirically or via explicit modeling):**
- **player_count & num_rounds:** Frequently specified; impact on equilibrium and stability of cooperation is discussed (Wu & Sun, 2022; Amirova et al., 2022; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Angourakis et al., 2015).
- **chat/communication:** Investigated with strong empirical support; evidence that it independently and robustly increases efficiency (Herne et al., 2023; Amirova et al., 2022).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Sometimes modeled—cost to punisher, nature of punishment, and technological means for its administration are discussed (Wu & Sun, 2022; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Ferguson, 2021).
- **all_or_nothing, mpcr:** Game payoff structure and marginal returns considered in some models (Wu & Sun, 2022; Herne et al., 2023).
- **reward_exists/reward_cost:** Some mention of mixed incentive settings (Liu & Yang, 2018; Suratin et al., 2023), though little empirical payoff data.
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds:** Minor contextual discussion; papers sometimes specify whether players had per-round summaries (e.g., Angourakis et al., 2015; Berger, 2021).
- **default_contrib:** Only contextually referenced (e.g., binary-choice frameworks, opt-in/out framing).

**Indirectly or contextually informed:**
- **show_punishment_id:** Discussed in some agent-based or institutional context (Suratin et al., 2023), with arguments about the moderating effect of anonymity or identification on sanctioning's effectiveness.
- **reward_tech/reward_magnitude:** Rarely a focus.
  
**Effectively missing:**
- Simultaneous and systematic manipulation of the full combination of all 14 prediction dimensions is absent. Most studies only vary a few dimensions at a time and often conflate dimensions (e.g., punishment with chat, or step-level games with all-or-nothing).
- Quantitative relationships between the control game's average efficiency and the treatment game's efficiency (with punishment enabled), as required for the prediction task, are not provided by any source.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Insufficient quantitative prediction:** The existing literature does not provide systematic, quantitative predictions mapping control-game efficiency and design dimensions to expected treatment efficiency under punishment. Most relevant findings are directional, qualitative, or based on behavioral proxies.
- **Ambiguity and conflicting findings:** There is substantive disagreement—while theory often favors punishment as a cooperation promoter, empirical results (especially in real-world or close field settings) show that punishment can sometimes reduce efficiency by crowding out intrinsic motivation.
- **Incomplete coverage of design space:** Many design dimensions critical for nuanced prediction (e.g., the interplay between punishment cost, identification, and reward existence) are only spottily addressed and rarely combined in a factorial manner.
- **Limited reporting of payoff-based outcomes:** Most empirical studies use behavioral proxies rather than direct measures of efficiency, limiting the ability to transfer results to payoff predictions.
- **Context dependency:** Effects of punishment are often conditional on baseline efficacy of other mechanisms (e.g., communication), problem framing, and participant population (lab vs. field, gender composition, pro-social vs. pro-self), but these moderators are often not systematically varied.
- **Lack of replication and standardization:** Outcome reporting conventions vary substantially across papers, leading to difficult cross-study synthesis.
- **Non-transferability:** Much of the theory is too abstract, or the setting too contextually distant, to directly inform the efficiency effects of punishment in standard PGGs with defined design features.

**In summary:**  
The literature base provides partial, context-dependent, and sometimes conflicting evidence for predicting the average efficiency of a PGG-like game when peer punishment is enabled. Few papers report true efficiency effects; most report close behavioral outcomes, with limited mapping to the prediction task. Key design dimensions like player count, punishment cost, and communication are well-discussed, while others are not. Prediction should be cautious, referencing the strong context dependency found in empirical studies, and acknowledging that in some real-world or field designs, punishment may even reduce efficiency despite theoretical expectations to the contrary.
