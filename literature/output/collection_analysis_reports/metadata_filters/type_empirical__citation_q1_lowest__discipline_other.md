# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is predominantly empirical, featuring a mix of laboratory and field experiments (e.g., De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Amirova et al., 2022; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022) as well as several observational and qualitative field studies (e.g., Qirko, 2020; Oria et al., 2018). The direct focus on standard public goods games (PGG) or close variants is present in a minority of papers, with many studies utilizing adjacent or similar social dilemma contexts such as common-pool resource (CPR) games, third-party punishment games, principal-agent experiments, or community-level cooperation problems. 

A few papers (notably De Geest & Kingsley, 2021) provide direct, controlled, empirical evidence on efficiency or group payoff effects of peer punishment in public goods-like environments. Others (Engel, 2019; Amirova et al., 2022; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Wei et al., 2025) offer relevant evidence on related payoff outcomes or collectives' responses to punishment mechanisms. Several studies focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., contributions, cooperation rates) or on the context and process of punishment, without measuring efficiency directly.

As a whole, the evidence base is somewhat broad in its coverage of social dilemmas and experimental approaches, but is relatively narrow in furnishing direct, robust, and systematic evidence linking the enabling of peer punishment to efficiency changes within standard public goods games as determined by the prediction task.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessment across three dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant**:
    - *exact*: A small number of studies (e.g., Engel, 2019; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021) use standard or near-standard public goods or CPR games.
    - *close/adjacent*: Many others employ variants or related social dilemmas (resource harvesting, principal-agent games, third-party scenarios; e.g., Amirova et al., 2022; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Wei et al., 2025) but depart from core PGG mechanics in important ways.
    - *adjacent/none*: Some operate in field or hypothetical settings, only loosely related to PGGs (Qirko, 2020; Oria et al., 2018).

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:
    - *exact*: Some studies manipulate peer punishment or penalty mechanisms directly in the experimental design (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Engel, 2019; Amirova et al., 2022), sometimes including details on cost and targeting.
    - *close/adjacent*: Others discuss or allow for other forms of sanctions, such as third-party punishment, carbon taxes, or institutional sanctions (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Qirko, 2020; Oria et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2025).
    - *none*: Several papers focus solely on communication or informal control with no punishment (Herne et al., 2023; Chang et al., 2021).

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
    - *exact*: A minority explicitly measure efficiency or provide efficiency ratios (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Wei et al., 2025).
    - *close*: Others present proxy or related payoff outcomes (Engel, 2019: total earnings described, not analyzed; Amirova et al., 2022: investment as behavioral proxy).
    - *adjacent/none*: Many focus entirely on behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates, contributions) or qualitative findings, lacking direct efficiency metrics (Gomez-Ruiz & Sánchez-Expósito, 2020; Qirko, 2020; Oria et al., 2018; Hirama et al., 2022).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes** (used for efficiency prediction): 
    - Direct efficiency ratios (group payoff relative to social optimum) or total group earnings/profit are reported in only a few studies (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Wei et al., 2025). In some cases, total earnings or group surplus are described but not analyzed statistically (Engel, 2019; Amirova et al., 2022).
  
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
    - Contribution rate, frequency of cooperation, punishment rate, norm compliance, and strategic behavior (e.g., free-riding responses, investment rate, or restoration activity) dominate the reporting in most studies (Amirova et al., 2022; Engel, 2019; Gomez-Ruiz & Sánchez-Expósito, 2020). 
    - Real-world or qualitative studies focus on self-reported punishment attitudes, trust, group structure, or process (Qirko, 2020; Oria et al., 2018).

- **Distinction explicitness**:
    - Several studies are explicit about only measuring behavior, not efficiency or payoff (e.g., Engel, 2019; Amirova et al., 2022).
    - In some cases, the behavior measured (e.g., collective investment or extraction) serves as a reasonable but incomplete proxy for efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of empirical findings:**

- **Punishment can decrease or increase efficiency, context dependent:**
    - *Negative effects*: In field/real-world or equal-endowment lab settings, enabling punishment often leads to lower efficiency. This is attributed to high punishment costs, poor coordination of punishment, or the crowding out of intrinsic motivation (Amirova et al., 2022; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021—equal endowments).
    - *Positive or neutral effects*: In games with salient inequality, structured opportunities for coordination, or clear social focal points (e.g., low-endowment advantage), punishment can enhance efficiency, particularly for disadvantaged groups (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021—unequal endowments).
    - *Positive but sometimes small effects*: Stylized laboratory experiments in adjacent domains (principal-agent, carbon tax) report efficiency gains with the introduction of costly, salient punishment, but sometimes only after actual implementation and not always with statistical significance (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Wei et al., 2025).

- **Transparency and information structure affect punishment's impact:**
    - Higher transparency about individual punishment may backfire, lowering cooperation and potentially reducing efficiency compared to less precise feedback (Engel, 2019). Thus, the effect of punishment is moderated by the information display, especially feedback about punishment assignments (show_punishment_id, show_other_summaries).

- **Communication is generally beneficial, but not a substitute for or direct moderator of punishment efficacy:**
    - Communication robustly increases efficiency (Herne et al., 2023), but its interaction with punishment is underexplored—several studies include one but not both treatments.

- **Punishment is not always used even if available:**
    - Real-world and field studies (Qirko, 2020; Oria et al., 2018) highlight that the mere availability of punishment does not guarantee its adoption, especially in heterogeneous, large, or low-trust groups.

**Mechanism arguments and theoretical implications:**  

- Multiple studies suggest that punishment's effect on efficiency is sensitive to group structure, coordination, and context (as opposed to automatic efficacy predicted by simple deterrence theory).
- The costliness of punishment, its credibility, and the presence of alternative mechanisms (compensation, communication, leadership) alter outcomes.

**Payoff vs. non-payoff outcomes:**  
- Many studies that find (positive or negative) punishment effects do so on cooperation or contribution rates, not group payoff or efficiency. When efficiency is measured, results are more mixed and context-dependent.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Punishment does not consistently increase efficiency:**  
  Caution is warranted—enabling punishment is just as likely to decrease as to increase efficiency, depending heavily on implementation details and group context. In settings with high intrinsic cooperation or moderate baseline contributions/efficiency, punishment often reduces group payoff due to over-punishment costs or crowding out cooperation (Amirova et al., 2022; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021—equal endowments; Engel, 2019).

- **Inequality and salient coordination norms improve punishment's positive effect:**  
  Where inequality is salient and a norm (such as equal earnings) is available, punishment is more coordinated and can enhance efficiency, especially benefiting lower-endowment members (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).

- **Information structure and transparency can moderate or reverse punishment effects:**  
  High transparency about punishment and contributions can undermine cooperation and thus reduce efficiency, compared with aggregate or group-level information (Engel, 2019).

- **Baseline efficiency in the no-punishment (control) game is crucial:**  
  If the control game already achieves high efficiency, introducing punishment risks costly over-correction and a net loss in payoff or efficiency. Conversely, in low-efficiency baselines, carefully implemented punishment can sometimes improve outcomes (conditional on coordination, cost, and group traits).

- **Design parameters that most strongly mediate punishment's effect:**  
    - Endowment structure/inequality (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021)
    - Punishment cost and technology (almost universally, but especially observed in both experimental and field studies)
    - Information feedback (Engel, 2019)

- **Other factors (player count, rounds, communication, etc.) show indirect or mixed effects** in these papers, or are not tested systematically as moderators of punishment efficiency effects.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed dimensions:**
    - *player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech*: Manipulated or reported as treatment variables in several lab experiments (Engel, 2019; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Amirova et al., 2022; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Wei et al., 2025).
    - *show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id*: Explicitly manipulated in the context of information feedback (Engel, 2019).
    - *chat*: Studied as a communication intervention (Herne et al., 2023; Amirova et al., 2022), with effects noted on efficiency but not in interaction with punishment.
    - *all_or_nothing*: Some studies use binary (all-or-nothing) contribution structures (Gomez-Ruiz & Sánchez-Expósito, 2020; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).

- **Indirectly informed or contextually discussed:**
    - *reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech*: Mentioned as mechanisms in the broader cooperation literature (Gao et al., 2024), but not systematically manipulated in this set.
    - *default_contrib*: Only contextually discussed or implicit in framing, not a focus of analysis.
    - *show_n_rounds*: Included in a few designs (Herne et al., 2023; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Chang et al., 2021), not tied directly to punishment findings.

- **Effectively missing for prediction:**
    - *reward_cost, reward_tech, reward_exists, default_contrib*: Largely unmanipulated or unreported in relevant studies.
    - *show_punishment_id*: Manipulated in terms of transparency but not explored for its interaction with all other design factors.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse direct evidence on PGG efficiency with peer punishment:**  
  Few studies provide exact matches for the prediction target (peer punishment's effect on efficiency in standard PGGs); those that do (e.g., De Geest & Kingsley, 2021) reveal strong contextual dependence rather than consistent directionality or magnitude.

- **Frequent reliance on behavioral proxies:**  
  Many studies infer efficiency impact from behavioral measures (contribution rates, punishment frequency, investment) rather than reporting group payoff or efficiency ratios, which can be misleading due to punishment costs or altered group dynamics.

- **Heterogeneity in game design details:**  
  Studies vary widely on dimensions such as punishment technology, cost, group size, and context, often focusing on singular implementations without systematic variation or full reporting across prediction dimensions.

- **Limited coverage of dimension interactions:**  
  Most studies manipulate a small subset of the 14 design dimensions and rarely test for interactions that are likely to be important (e.g., how communication interacts with punishment, or specific feedback with cost).

- **Field and real-world evidence ambiguous and difficult to generalize:**  
  Qualitative or observational studies often show punishment is rare or context-dependent in practice, with effects that cannot be cleanly mapped to laboratory efficiency outcomes.

- **Potential lack of external validity:**  
  Laboratory findings rarely map cleanly to field or community outcomes (Qirko, 2020; Oria et al., 2018). Contextual moderators (trust, leadership, group identity) are likely important but underexplored.

- **Missing data on reward/positive incentives:**  
  Though identified as important in the literature, the interaction of punishment with rewards is not experimentally addressed in these papers.

**Conclusion:**  
Prediction of efficiency changes due to enabling peer punishment in public-goods-game-like environments should proceed with care: empirical effects are contextually variable and often negative when baseline cooperation is high. Mechanism and theory papers highlight the importance of design nuances and contextual moderators, but lack of systematic experimental coverage across critical dimensions limits the precision and generalizability of predictions.
