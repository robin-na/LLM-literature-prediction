# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists entirely of theoretical work (no direct empirical or laboratory studies), including mathematical models, simulation studies, evolutionary game theory, and integrative reviews. This set is broad in the range of mechanisms, moderators, and dimensions considered, but narrow in terms of direct, empirical effect estimation for the target prediction task. Many papers address standard public goods games (PGG) or very close variants, often with institutional, pool, or peer punishment (or related incentive/reputation mechanisms). Most primary outcomes are efficiency or payoff-based, but a substantial minority focus on behavioral outcomes (contribution, cooperation rates, prevalence of strategies).

There is extensive coverage of game design dimensions in theory—especially player count, group size, MPCR, punishment cost, structure of participation (voluntary vs. compulsory), and the presence or design of reward/sanction systems. Outcomes related to group efficiency, group payoff, or welfare are reported explicitly in many core papers but are also frequently inferred from sustained cooperation or strategy prevalence, sometimes without direct computation of group efficiency. The set lacks empirical heterogeneity and context—lab or real-world data that might moderate theory-driven predictions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. PGG or variant**
- **Relevance:** The majority of papers are *exactly* on the standard PGG or direct variants (e.g., voluntary entry, pool/peer punishment, hybrid reward-punishment schemes). Some address threshold or collective-risk dilemmas, exclusion mechanisms, or adjacent environments such as the Prisoner’s Dilemma with similar sanction dynamics. Overall, coverage is **exact** or **close**.

**b. Punishment or sanctions**
- **Relevance:** Almost all core papers focus *exactly* on punishment or closely related mechanisms (peer, pool, institutional, exclusion, or commitment-based sanctioning). A few focus instead on reward, prior agreements, or exclusion as alternative methods, with punishment as a comparison.

**c. Efficiency or related payoff outcome**
- **Relevance:** Many of the theoretically central papers report outcomes as group efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or material surplus, and provide formulas or simulation results. However, a subset—sometimes including highly-cited mechanism reviews—report only non-payoff behavioral outcomes (cooperation, contribution). Thus, for *efficiency or related payoff outcome*, relevance is **exact** in key papers but only **close** or **adjacent** in others.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes:**  
  *Group efficiency*, *group-average payoff*, *total earnings*, *welfare*, *group surplus*, or *total coins generated* are directly reported in over half the PGG-relevant papers (e.g., Duong & Han, 2021; Chen et al., 2015; Rand & Nowak, 2011; Barrett, 2016; Eldakar & Wilson, 2008).
  
- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  *Contribution rates*, *cooperation rates*, *frequency of punishment*, *punishment assigned*, and normative compliance are common in papers emphasizing evolutionary or strategic dynamics (e.g., Perc, 2013; Fehr & Fischbacher, 2003; Hilbe & Traulsen, 2012). These are correlated with, but distinct from, efficiency.

- **Not Measured or Only Contextual Outcomes:**  
  Some adjacent or theoretical reviews discuss the ability of mechanisms to sustain cooperation or promote pro-sociality without reporting or quantifying efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General pattern:**  
- *Punishment often increases cooperation and can, under appropriate conditions, substantially raise group efficiency compared to control settings without punishment.*  
- *However, punishment does not always increase efficiency.* The benefit or harm to efficiency is highly dependent on details of the game design and punishment mechanism. Key identified moderators include:

**Critical moderators:**
- **Punishment Cost and Magnitude:** High-cost punishment can reduce efficiency (Barrett, 2016; Sigmund et al., 2010); low-cost, high-impact punishment is more likely to be efficient (CLUTTONBROCK & PARKER, 1995).
- **Voluntary vs. Compulsory Participation:** Voluntary entry makes punishment more effective/efficient, sometimes producing phase transitions to high-efficiency outcomes with even small penalties (Sasaki et al., 2012; Hauert et al., 2007; Brandt et al., 2006).
- **Institutional vs. Peer Punishment:** Institutional (pool) punishment is robust but often more costly and less efficient than peer punishment unless reward mechanisms help pool punishment establish itself (Sasaki et al., 2015; Sigmund et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2017).
- **Second-Order Free-Riders:** Efficiency is only achieved if second-order free-riders (cooperators who do not punish) are sanctioned or suppressed; if not, punishment cannot sustain itself and efficiency remains low (Perc, 2012).
- **Anti-Social Punishment:** If anti-social punishment (punishing high contributors) is possible and occurs, punishment does not raise efficiency, and may lower it (Rand & Nowak, 2011; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- **Reputation and Information:** When punishment is observable or reputation is available, punishment is far more likely to yield high-efficiency outcomes (Sigmund et al., 2001; Hilbe & Traulsen, 2012).
- **Coordination and Signaling:** Coordination among punishers (cost-sharing, thresholds, quorum) strongly enhances the efficiency effect (Boyd et al., 2010).
- **Reward Mechanisms:** Rewards facilitate initial cooperation and ease the emergence of effective punishment; optimal efficiency is often achieved by adaptive switching (first reward, then punishment) or hybrid schemes (Chen et al., 2015; Góis et al., 2019; Sasaki et al., 2015).
- **Group Size (player_count), MPCR, and Structure:** Efficiency and the effect of punishment can vary nonlinearly with group size and marginal per-capita return—especially with structured or networked populations (Alvarez-Rodriguez et al., 2021; Perc et al., 2013).
- **Game Framing:** Optional participation (loner/exit option), all-or-nothing vs. continuous contributions, and other design choices shape efficiency sensitivity to punishment (Brandt et al., 2006; Hauert et al., 2002).

**Points of ambiguity or disagreement:**
- *Some models predict that punishment nearly always reduces group efficiency due to its cost unless specific conditions (reputation, effective targeting, coordinated action) are met (Sigmund et al., 2010; Barrett, 2016; Ohtsuki et al., 2009).*
- *Hybrid or adaptive punishment/reward regimes outperform pure punishment in cost-efficiency, with reward critical for establishing cooperation when baseline cooperation is low (Chen et al., 2015; Góis et al., 2019).*
- *In adjacent (indirect reciprocity) settings, costly punishment is rarely efficient compared to exclusion; efficiency gains require finely tuned parameters (Ohtsuki et al., 2009; Panchanathan & Boyd, 2004).*
- *With anti-social or mis-targeted punishment, group efficiency can fall below the control (Rand & Nowak, 2011; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).*

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**a. Direct use of the literature for prediction:**
- If *control game efficiency is low (due to high rates of defection)* and the *game design enables only pro-social, well-targeted, reasonably low-cost punishment (preferably with voluntary participation and some mechanism for coordination or reputation)*, **enabling punishment should increase average efficiency—sometimes dramatically** (Duong & Han, 2021; Sasaki et al., 2012; Eldakar & Wilson, 2008).
- If *punishment is high-cost, anti-social punishment is allowed, participation is compulsory, the impact on defectors is weak, or second-order free-riding is unaddressed*, **punishment may not raise efficiency and can even reduce it** (Barrett, 2016; Sigmund et al., 2010; Rand & Nowak, 2011).
- *Reward mechanisms* greatly moderate predictions: if reward is present as an alternative or complement to punishment, efficiency gains from enabling punishment alone may be reduced, and hybrid or adaptive regimes are optimal (Chen et al., 2015; Góis et al., 2019).
- *Group size and MPCR* modulate the effect, but the direction is rarely reversed—high MPCR and moderate group sizes generally make punishment more efficient (Eldakar & Wilson, 2008; Brandt et al., 2006).
- Information (reputation, knowledge of others’ actions) is a major moderator—without it, punishment loses much of its efficiency benefit (Sigmund et al., 2001; Hilbe & Traulsen, 2012).

**b. Prediction from design dimensions:**
- *Prediction is best calibrated when the following dimensions are known and correspond to those varied in the theoretical models:*  
  - `player_count`, `mpcr`, `all_or_nothing`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `show_punishment_id`, and presence/absence of anti-social punishment or voluntary participation options.
- *Where reward mechanisms, participation structure, or second-order punishment targeting are missing or not specified, predictions should be correspondingly hedged or downweighted.*
- *Baseline control efficiency is useful for predictions when theory provides explicit mapping functions (Duong & Han, 2021); otherwise, it may only give moderate guidance.*

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed (supported by multiple exact PGG+punishment+payoff papers):**
- `player_count` (group size): Repeatedly analyzed as a moderator (e.g., effect of size on punishment stability and efficiency)
- `mpcr`: Central to nearly all models; higher MPCR typically makes punishment more effective
- `all_or_nothing`: Some models (esp. institutional/voluntary) study both continuous and all-or-nothing contributions
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Core focus of payoff-based models
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Present in many hybrid models
- `punishmentExists`: Central experimental manipulation in all these papers

**Indirectly Informed**
- `num_rounds`: Sometimes included in evolutionary models or as parameter sweep but not always reported for strategic, finite games
- `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes referenced as reputation or information availability; direct manipulation less common, but theory supports its importance
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Contextually discussed, especially regarding information flow or temporal structure, but not always manipulated directly

**Only Contextually Discussed**
- `chat`: Discussed as coordination mechanism or for signaling but not established as a design feature in analyzed theory papers
- `default_contrib`: Framing (opt-in/out) not systematically varied in core models; thus, guidance is weak

**Effectively Missing**
- No direct coverage or distinction for the impact of display/framing features not listed above (e.g., fine details of UI, feedback granularity)
- Empirical moderation by subject pool, culture, or real-world context

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of empirical validation:** All findings are theoretical/simulation-based; none use empirical or laboratory data for direct prediction or external calibration.
- **Ambiguity in real-world mapping:** Many models assume perfect rationality, information, or institution implementation; practical results may differ with error, noise, or cultural factors (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- **Partial outcome coverage:** Some “efficiency” outcomes are inferred from cooperation rates rather than directly quantified payoff ratios; predictions using behavioral proxies have additional uncertainty.
- **Limited coverage of all design dimensions:** Several prediction variables (e.g., chat, default contribution framing, or user interface design) are rarely if ever varied or linked to treatment efficiency in the models considered.
- **Conditional and moderator-dependent effects:** The net effect of punishment on efficiency is almost never unconditional—design specifics (e.g., presence of anti-social or second-order punishment, optional participation, reward, and coordination mechanisms) are crucial.
- **Restricted generality on payoff impact:** Several reviews (e.g., Sigmund et al., 2010; Barrett, 2016) emphasize that *although punishment increases cooperation, it does not always increase group efficiency*, especially when cost/impact ratios are unfavorable or punishment is misapplied.

---

**Summary for Predictive Use:**  
Theoretical literature offers strong, model-based guidance for the conditions and design dimensions under which enabling punishment in PGG-like environments leads to higher efficiency. The positive effect is neither universal nor automatic; it depends on mechanisms suppressing second-order free-riding, avoiding anti-social punishment, leveraging reputation/information, and often, the presence of voluntary participation or reward mechanisms. Prediction of efficiency change from enabling punishment should be contextually modulated for all these factors, and predictions may be unreliable if the design falls outside the well-studied parameter regimes or if intuitive mapping between model and real-world implementation is lacking. Empirical validation and calibration remain outstanding needs.
