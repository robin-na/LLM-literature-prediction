# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 38 studies, predominantly laboratory experiments, with a strong empirical focus. Most papers use experimental public goods games (PGGs) or close variants to analyze the impact of punishment and related institutional features. The set is relatively broad for the punishment-in-PGGs prediction task, featuring diverse game structures, population samples, and institutional manipulations.

- **Empirical Breadth**: Nearly all papers are direct lab experiments; a few are field experiments or observational studies.
- **Directness**: Strong coverage of classic linear PGGs with and without peer punishment, though several studies extend to close variants (common-pool resources, minimum effort games, modified insurance games, etc.).
- **Outcomes**: Many papers measure both behavioral (contribution/cooperation rates, frequency of punishment) and payoff-based (efficiency, group earnings, surplus) outcomes.
- **Payoff Focus**: About half the papers report efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes directly; others focus on underlying behaviors.
- **Heterogeneity**: There is substantial diversity in institutional and contextual features (punishment structure, information treatments, communication, agreement mechanisms, etc.), allowing for nuanced comparisons, though this can also complicate synthesis.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**: *Mostly exact or close relevance.*

- Most papers use standard repeated PGG or clear variants (exact), with several evaluating closely related social dilemmas (close). Some papers employ adjacent paradigms (e.g., repeated PD, insurance pool) providing mechanistic or contextual relevance.

**punishment_or_sanctions**: *Strong relevance; most papers analyze costly punishment mechanisms (exact), including subtle variants (centralized vs. peer, restricted/legitimate punishment, endogenous audit mechanisms).*

- Several also consider reward, communication, or institution design as modifiers to punishment’s effects. A minority focus strictly on reputational or social sanctions, or exclude punishment entirely (none/weak).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: *Substantial but not full relevance.*

- About half the papers report group efficiency, total payoff, welfare, or surplus (exact/close).
- Several provide only indirect evidence, via contribution rates or cooperation frequencies (adjacent), requiring inference for efficiency.
- Others report only on non-payoff behavioral outcomes, social perceptions, or norm evaluations (weak/none).

**Summary**: The set is highly relevant to the core prediction question (“What is the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in public goods games?”), with some studies also illuminating boundary conditions and moderators.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (“efficiency,” group payoff, surplus):**

- Directly measured in many PGG lab studies as group earnings relative to the fully cooperative social optimum (e.g., Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Dannenberg, 2016; Kamijo et al., 2014; Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Dorrough et al., 2017; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Lierl, 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Lévy-Garboua et al., 2017; Wright, 2013; Wilson & Wu, 2017; Kopányi-Peuker et al., 2017).
- Occasionally only indirectly inferred from contribution rates where direct payoff is not reported (e.g., Nelissen & Mulder, 2013; Irwin et al., 2014).

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**

- Contribution or cooperation rates (most studies).
- Frequency/type of punishment assigned (antisocial vs. prosocial).
- Trust, norm perceptions, emotional drivers, or reputational motivations (e.g., Seip et al., 2014; Eriksson et al., 2017).
- Participation or turnout (Myers, 2016), compliance (Hsu, 2013), or resource allocation (Lyle, 2017).

**Distinctions**:

- Some findings about punishment increasing cooperation do **not** translate to increased efficiency, because the cost of punishment can outweigh gains from higher cooperation.
- Studies with only behavioral outcomes cannot be used as direct evidence for payoff-based prediction without explicit payoff mappings.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## When and How Punishment Increases Efficiency

- **Legitimacy and Structure Matter**: Punishment increases efficiency if it is *prosocial*, legitimate, and targeted at free riders (Faillo et al., 2013; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Kamijo et al., 2014; Zheng & Nie, 2013).
- **Feedback and Transparency**: Efficiency gains require full feedback or transparency about contributions and punishment eligibility (Faillo et al., 2013).
- **Punishment Costs**: Costly punishment, particularly in a centralized or restricted (designated punisher) form, helps avoid excessive/antisocial punishment and can raise efficiency. Costless or decentralized punishment may reduce efficiency due to wasteful or antisocial punishment (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Fischer et al., 2016).
- **Institutional Design**: Relative punishment (only the worst violators) is sometimes more efficient than absolute punishment; centralization only helps under specific conditions (Kamijo et al., 2014; Fischer et al., 2016).
- **Production Technology**: The game’s production function is a major moderator: in weakest-link (complementary) public goods games, punishment has a substantially larger and more robust impact on efficiency than in linear PGGs, where antisocial punishment can erase any gains (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
- **Group Size Effects**: The effect of enabling punishment on efficiency varies with group size and the identity structure (anonymous vs. identified punishment) (Zheng & Nie, 2013).
- **Communication**: The availability of communication drastically increases efficiency (with or without punishment), and can neutralize negative punishment effects such as retaliation or counter-punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).

## When Punishment Fails or Reduces Efficiency

- **Antisocial or Excessive Punishment**: If punishment is largely antisocial (punishing contributors) or excessive (e.g., in the absence of legitimacy or clear targeting), efficiency falls (Faillo et al., 2013; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Dorrough et al., 2017).
- **Complex or Nonlinear Games**: In nonlinear or more realistic resource dilemmas, peer punishment often has no effect or even reduces efficiency, while communication increases it (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016).
- **Punishment Power Instability**: Instability or inequality in punishment roles (e.g., "race for power") can reduce efficiency via wasteful punishment (Dorrough et al., 2017).
- **Sanction Institutions**: When punishment is non-legitimate or easily retaliated, efficiency doesn’t improve (Lierl, 2016; Hsu, 2013).

## Robust Patterns

- **Punishment works best as a credible deterrent with social legitimacy and focused targeting, particularly when game structure and feedback support norm enforcement and transparency.**
- **Merely enabling punishment does not guarantee efficiency gains—details of punishment technology, communication, institutions, and baseline efficiency all matter heavily.**

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct Prediction Inputs From the Literature**:

- ***If control efficiency (no punishment) is low and the design includes:***
    - Legitimate, targeted, and costly punishment with full feedback,
    - No excessive antisocial punishment or retaliation,
    - Appropriate group size/information environment,
    - Optionally, communication,
    
  **Then**: Enabling punishment will likely *substantially increase efficiency.* The predicted average efficiency may approach social optimum if legitimacy mechanisms (e.g., restricted punishment, public IDs) and feedback are provided (Faillo et al., 2013; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Dannenberg, 2016; Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014).

- ***If the design lacks legitimacy, has costless or decentralized punishment (and is in a culture or setting prone to antisocial punishment), or has unstable punishment roles,***
    
  **Then**: Enabling punishment may not increase efficiency, and may lower it if punishment becomes wasteful (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Dorrough et al., 2017).
 
- ***If communication is enabled or can be used alongside punishment,***
    
  **Then**: Efficiency is often greatly increased, sometimes rendering punishment unnecessary or redundant (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
  
- ***If the game is a non-linear, complex common-pool resource game,***
    
  **Then**: Peer punishment often fails to raise efficiency; communication and institutional design become more important (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).

- ***If a clear, ambitious agreement on full cooperation is established in addition to enabling punishment,***
    
  **Then**: Punishment can dramatically improve efficiency by enforcing the agreement (Dannenberg, 2016).

- ***If group size is increased and punishment is anonymous,***
    
  **Then**: Efficiency gains from punishment may diminish or become negative unless legitimate mechanisms are provided (Zheng & Nie, 2013).

**In all cases, control efficiency (as measured or inferred in the ‘no punishment’ version) is the required reference point, but design dimensions modulate the treatment effect.**

- *Do not overpredict efficiency gains from punishment without considering potential antisocial punishment, information/transparency structure, and whether the specific punishment institution supports legitimacy and targeting.*

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**

- **player_count**: Frequently varied and analyzed (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014; Faillo et al., 2013; common across PGG experiments).
- **num_rounds**: Laboratory studies typically specify (e.g., 6, 10, 15, 20, or 30 rounds), with some attention to dynamic effects.
- **chat**: Directly manipulated in some studies, shown to moderate efficiency (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
- **all_or_nothing**: Both all-or-nothing and continuous contribution games are present; production technology (linear/weakest-link/threshold) strongly moderates effects (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
- **default_contrib**: Some mention of default framing, but limited direct evidence on opt-in/opt-out effect.
- **mpcr**: Always specified; higher MPCR is generally associated with higher baseline cooperation, but not a central focus for punishment effect size.
- **punishment_cost**: Central to many studies; higher cost often moderates punishment's overall effect on efficiency (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017).
- **punishment_tech**: Detailed (designated vs. peer, absolute vs. relative, public ID, ability to counter-punish); crucial for effect size and direction (Faillo et al., 2013; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Kamijo et al., 2014; Lierl, 2016).
- **reward_exists/cost/tech**: Less commonly implemented, but a few studies include or compare rewards to punishment (Irwin et al., 2014).
- **show_n_rounds & show_other_summaries**: Information transparency, summary feedback, and monitoring are often manipulated and found to be important for legitimacy and effectiveness (Faillo et al., 2013; Andrighetto et al., 2016).
- **show_punishment_id**: Directly manipulated, shown to affect efficiency and legitimacy (Zheng & Nie, 2013).

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**

- **default_contrib**: Framing is sometimes reported but rarely a focus of prediction; only limited contextual evidence.
- **reward_exists/cost/tech**: Included in some studies as alternatives or supplements to punishment, but less central than punishment itself; evidence is suggestive that rewards alone or in combination can increase cooperation, but payoff impacts versus costs less clear.
- **show_n_rounds**: Sometimes a minor manipulation; more strongly encountered as a transparency variable.
- **show_other_summaries**: Indirectly discussed through “full feedback” treatments.

**Effectively Missing/Sparse:**

- Interactions involving less common design dimensions (e.g., default_contrib, reward tech/cost, show_n_rounds) are less systematically addressed.
- The dimension of whether the number of rounds is shown to players is rarely discussed as a key moderator for punishment effects.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Generalizability**: Laboratory PGGs may not represent real-world collective action settings; field evidence is sparse and suggests lower prevalence of costly punishment and potentially lower efficiency gains (Berger & Hevenstone, 2016).
- **Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes**: Several findings about increased cooperation/contributions with punishment do *not* translate to payoff improvements; predictions must not conflate cooperation rates with efficiency. *
- **Conditionality is Crucial**: Many efficiency gains are conditional on strong design choices (legitimacy, transparency, communication, production technology), and absent such features, punishment may not help and can harm efficiency.
- **Culture and Population**: Some experiments find strong moderation by cultural context (e.g., levels of antisocial punishment, acceptance of punishers).
- **Antisocial and Wasteful Punishment**: Punishment is sometimes used antisocially or competitively, reducing group earnings below the control (no punishment) baseline; predictions must be attentive to such possibilities.
- **Dimension Coverage**: Not all 14 specified design dimensions are systematically varied across papers; some are only peripherally informed.
- **Game Structure Sensitivity**: Effects are particularly sensitive to the linearity vs. nonlinearity of payoff structure, group size, and information environment.
- **Modeling/Mechanism Gaps**: Few studies provide predictive models mapping from specific design dimension values and control efficiency to treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency in a quantitative fashion.

---

*Bottom Line*:  
The aggregated literature confirms that enabling peer punishment in public goods games sometimes substantially increases efficiency relative to a no-punishment baseline—**if** punishment is legitimate, targeted, costly enough to deter frivolous or antisocial use, and supported by transparency and clear institutional design. However, in the absence of these moderators, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency. Efficient and accurate prediction thus requires specifying key design dimensions (especially those relating to punishment structure, cost, legitimacy, and information environment), not just the efficiency observed in the punishment-free control. Ambiguity and occasional disagreement persist, especially in more complex, less controlled, or culturally variable settings.
