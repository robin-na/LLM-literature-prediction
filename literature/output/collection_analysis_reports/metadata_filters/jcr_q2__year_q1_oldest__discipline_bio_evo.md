# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists predominantly of theoretical papers (12/19) with some empirical studies (experimental: 3/19; observational: 2/19; field: 1/19; review/framework: 1/19). Theoretical contributions overwhelmingly focus on evolutionary and mechanistic models of cooperation and punishment, often referencing or modeling public-goods-game-like environments. Only a minority of studies provide direct empirical tests, mostly in adjacent paradigms (trust games, mutualisms) or in naturalistic field/lab/observational contexts rather than controlled PGG laboratory experiments typical in economics. The evidence base is broad in its theoretical scope—covering mechanisms, evolutionary conditions, and variants—but relatively narrow in its coverage of direct, empirical, game-by-game efficiency outcomes with and without punishment.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**
- **Exact relevance**: Several key theoretical papers model public goods games (PGG) or variants directly ((Baker & Rachlin, 2002); (Forsyth & Hauert, 2011); (Archetti & Scheuring, 2011)).
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Many papers apply or generalize from related environments (n-player Prisoner's Dilemma, mutualisms, evolution of cooperation) ((Gardner & West, 2004); (POLLOCK, 1988); others).
- **Empirically**, few studies use actual PGG lab designs; most empirical work focuses on adjacent games (trust game, dyadic mutualisms).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**
- **Exact/close relevance:** Most papers include punishment as a mechanism (either as the main focus or an important variant) ((Baker & Rachlin, 2002); (Gardner & West, 2004); (POLLOCK, 1988); (Henrich & Henrich, 2006); (Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011); others).
- Some address both punishment and reward, but the effect of reward-only treatments is less central to the prediction task.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
- **Exact/close relevance:** A few theoretical studies provide direct or close modeling of efficiency (group payoff, welfare, surplus) as a function of game design and punishment ((Baker & Rachlin, 2002); (POLLOCK, 1988); (Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011); (Forsyth & Hauert, 2011); (Archetti & Scheuring, 2011)). Some empirical studies report only behavioral outcomes (contributions, trustworthiness) rather than group efficiency or total payoffs ((Baum et al., 2012); (Raihani & McAuliffe, 2012)).
- Overall, many findings are only **indirectly** relevant for efficiency, focusing instead on cooperation rates or equilibrium frequencies.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes:**
- *Directly modeled or reported:* group payoff, mean efficiency, total returns, population mean payoff ((Baker & Rachlin, 2002); (Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011); (POLLOCK, 1988); (Archetti & Scheuring, 2011); (Forsyth & Hauert, 2011)).
- *Indirectly discussed:* evolutionary “fitness,” modeled as group-level consequences of cooperation and punishment.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- Most empirical studies report cooperation rates, contribution rates, trustworthiness, punishment frequency, aggression as punishment proxies ((Baum et al., 2012); (Bshary & Grutter, 2005); (Raihani & McAuliffe, 2012); (Bshary & Bronstein, 2011); (Bshary & Bshary, 2010); (Cisarovsky et al., 2012)).
- Studies using evolutionary models often focus on conditions for the *maintenance* or *emergence* of cooperation/punishment, not on explicit payoff or efficiency measures.

**Distinction:**
- Only a subset of studies provide or model payoff-based outcomes that map directly onto “efficiency” as needed for the downstream prediction task.
- Many studies only measure *behavioral* outcomes—these support mechanisms but are not equivalent to predictions about group efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Theoretical Mechanisms and Parameter Dependencies:**
- **Punishment increases cooperation, usually increasing efficiency** above non-punishment controls when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly ((Baker & Rachlin, 2002); (Gardner & West, 2004); (POLLOCK, 1988); (Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011)).
- The efficiency benefit of punishment depends strongly on:
    - **Punishment cost**: Low cost makes punishment more likely to increase efficiency.
    - **Punishment effectiveness**: Large penalty (relative to cost) increases efficacy.
    - **Player count/group size and population structure**: In larger or less structured groups, the benefit is diluted and punishment may become ineffective ((POLLOCK, 1988); (Bshary & Bshary, 2010)).
    - **Power asymmetries/protection of punishers**: Efficiency gains are larger when punishers have additional protection or status ((Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011)).
    - **Repeated interactions** (num_rounds): More rounds increase the impact of punishment ((POLLOCK, 1988); reflected also in partner control models).
    - **Behavioral association between punishment and cooperation received**: Efficacy is increased if punishing leads directly to later cooperation ((Gardner & West, 2004); (Henrich & Henrich, 2006)).

- **Reward can also increase efficiency**, but is usually not as effective as punishment in maintaining high levels of cooperation over time ((Forsyth & Hauert, 2011); (Baker & Rachlin, 2002)).

**Empirical Moderators and Mechanistic Qualifiers:**
- **Group identity and context** can moderate punishment's effect: its efficacy may depend on in-group/out-group distinctions ((Baum et al., 2012)).
- **Non-payoff findings** consistently show that punishment increases cooperation *behaviorally*, but whether this translates into higher group efficiency depends on the cost structure and correlation between punishment and cooperation.

**Limits and Boundary Conditions:**
- **High punishment cost, large group size without structure, or symmetric vulnerability of punishers** can result in punishment failing to increase or even reduce efficiency ((POLLOCK, 1988); (Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011)).
- Some evolutionary models suggest punishment is not favored unless linked to other mechanisms (high relatedness, positive genetic/cultural linkage) ((Lehmann et al., 2007); (Gardner, West & Barton, 2007)); these do *not* provide predictions about experimental efficiency changes but warn that punishment effects may fail in one-shot, anonymous, or unstructured contexts.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Qualitative prediction**: Enabling peer punishment in a PGG-like game will (in most parameter regimes typical of laboratory settings) *increase* group efficiency relative to the no-punishment control, especially when punishment is effective (large penalty per unit cost), player groupings are stable (multiple rounds, structured), and punishers are not excessively vulnerable to retaliation or cost.
- The size of the efficiency gain is most strongly moderated by **punishment cost, punishment effectiveness**, and **group size/structure**. Smaller, repeated, or structured groups and low-cost, high-magnitude punishment favor strong efficiency gains. If punishment is weak or prohibitively costly, efficiency gains may not materialize.
- **Baseline (control) efficiency** is informative—heterogeneity in control condition outcomes is explained by the public goods function (linear vs. threshold), group size, and return rate (MPCR), as modeled in (Archetti & Scheuring, 2011).
- **Inductive limitations**: Most evidence is theoretical; empirical validation of predicted efficiency effects is sparse and often indirect. Most behavioral evidence (on contributions/cooperation) supports the same qualitative pattern but does not directly provide efficiency numbers.
- **Mechanistic exceptions**: Population structure (or its absence), cost asymmetries, and social/cultural norms can produce divergent outcomes even under otherwise similar parameters.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count**: Multiple papers (especially theory) address group size as a moderator: smaller groups magnify punishment effect, large groups dilute it ((POLLOCK, 1988); (Fletcher & Zwick, 2006); (Gardner & West, 2004)).
- **num_rounds**: Role of repeated play for effectiveness and sustainability of punishment is directly modeled in several theory papers; empirically only examined in adjacent settings.
- **mpcr**: Frequently discussed as a key moderator in both theory and empirical settings; affects temptation to defect and cost-benefit calculus for both punishers and cooperators.
- **all_or_nothing**: Some theoretical models and experiments make explicit this assumption; findings directly apply to binary-choice/volunteer's-dilemma forms.
- **punishment_cost / punishment_tech**: Heavily modeled; cost-effectiveness of punishment is pivotal in nearly all relevant theory papers.
- **reward_exists / reward_cost / reward_tech**: Several theory papers cover both punishment and reward designs, though reward is less effective at high efficiency maintenance.
- **show_punishment_id**: Mentioned as theoretically important for social norm enforcement and anti-free-rider coalitions ((Boehm, 1997)), but little explicit modeling.

**Indirectly/Contextually Covered:**
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries**: Mentioned in passing, relevance for punishment as a mechanism to be effective in repeated play, otherwise less directly modeled.
- **chat, default_contrib**: Little or no direct modeling; only occasional mention as possible moderators.
- **punishment_magnitude**: Modeled as part of cost/effectiveness parameters but not always reported separately.

**Effectively Missing:**
- Most studies do not provide systematic, separate results for **chat**, **default_contrib** (framing), **show_other_summaries**, or explicit **punishment/reward technology** as distinct, parameterized game dimensions.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Scarcity of empirical, PGG-specific efficiency data:** While the theoretical literature is rich and models are sophisticated, direct experimental evidence on *efficiency* changes (not just cooperation rates or contributions) with and without punishment in varied PGG design conditions is sparse.
- **Payoff-behavior distinction:** Many findings about increased cooperation under punishment are based on non-payoff outcomes. The translation to actual efficiency gains depends strongly on punishment cost and game structure, not always accounted for in behavioral studies.
- **Limited parameterization:** Most studies do not, or cannot, systematically vary all design dimensions relevant to the prediction task. Dimensions such as **chat**, **default_contrib**, and some visibility/framing elements are typically absent or unmodeled.
- **Context and external validity:** Non-laboratory and non-human studies (mutualisms, field observations) offer mechanistic insights but have limited transferability to standard economic PGG environments.
- **Theoretical optimism:** Models frequently predict substantial efficiency benefits from punishment under favorable conditions, but real-world and experimental data sometimes show ambiguous or conditional results, especially as costs or group sizes scale up.
- **No direct quantification of efficiency increase:** Though theory strongly predicts a positive effect of punishment on efficiency under many conditions, quantitative prediction of the *magnitude* of the gain for specific parameter sets is generally not possible using only this literature.

**In sum:** This paper set offers robust theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment increases efficiency in PGG-like games—especially when punishment is effective, not prohibitively costly, and group structure is favorable—but provides limited direct empirical efficiency measurements and cannot finely resolve all design-dimension moderators required for systematic prediction across novel game variants.
