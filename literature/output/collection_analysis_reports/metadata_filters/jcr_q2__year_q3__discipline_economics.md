# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is **large** (101 papers) and includes a strong mix of **empirical laboratory experiments** (the majority), with some **empirical field experiments** and a smaller subset of **theory/simulation** papers. For the target prediction task—mapping from public-goods-game-like (PGG) design dimensions and control efficiency to predicting efficiency under punishment—the set covers a wide array of standard and variant PGGs, with substantial attention to punishment institutions. The empirical evidence is sufficiently broad to synthesize both robust regularities and conditionalities in punishment's effect on efficiency (group payoff), but also reveals many key moderators. There is strong concentration of directly relevant empirical work: many papers report direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes with and without punishment, and further explore moderators such as group size, heterogeneity, information structure, cost/technology of punishment, and social context. Theory papers play a smaller, more contextual role. Overall, the evidence base is extensive and highly relevant, though complexity and context-dependence of effects are apparent.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Relevance Dimensions:**
- **pgg_or_variant:** The majority of directly relevant papers have `exact` or `close` relevance: canonical linear PGGs, threshold PGs, and common pool resource (CPR) games with public good/extraction frames. Adjacent and weakly relevant papers address social dilemmas with related interaction structures.
- **punishment_or_sanctions:** The most relevant papers implement standard peer and/or centralized punishment (`exact`) or sanctions in the PGG. Some report on exclusion/ostracism, centralized/automatic punishment, or third-party punishment (still `exact` or `close`). A small number cover adjacent enforcement mechanisms (e.g., rule or quota enforcement, symbolic exclusion) or rewards.
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** Many core PGG punishment experiments report **direct efficiency or group payoff** as a ratio to the socially optimal benchmark (`exact`), supporting direct predictions. Some papers report "close" payoff-based outcomes (e.g., group earnings, surplus) or provide strong behavioral evidence when direct efficiency is not reported. Many others focus exclusively on contributions/cooperation (not efficiency), which is not equivalent for prediction.

Overall, the **task relevance is very high**: most papers are `exact` or `close` on all three axes, directly informing the prediction of treatment efficiency in PGGs with and without punishment.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based Outcomes (Efficiency, Group Payoff, Welfare, Surplus, Total Earnings):**
  - Widely reported in directly relevant empirical papers (e.g., (Gürerk et al., 2018); (Engl et al., 2021); (Angelovski et al., 2018); (Arechar et al., 2018); (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019); (Marcin et al., 2019); (Kol'veková et al., 2021); (Deffains et al., 2019)). Efficiency is often measured as the ratio of group earnings to the full-cooperation benchmark.
  - Some papers only present contributing rates (behavioral), then **infer efficiency**; a distinction is made when this occurs.
  - Variants (CPR, threshold, exclusion mechanisms) also frequently report group-level payoffs, net earnings, or surplus.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Contribution Rate, Cooperation Rate, Punishment Frequency, Norm Compliance):**
  - Many papers measure contribution or cooperation rates as their primary outcome, sometimes without reporting group efficiency. Where only behavioral outcomes are reported, the link to efficiency is indirect and can be misleading for prediction.
  - Punishment behavior (prosocial/antisocial), targeting, and norm enforcement are frequently analyzed as mechanisms but are **not** direct substitutes for payoff-based outcomes for prediction.

- **Ambiguity or Omitted Outcomes:**
  - Some otherwise relevant studies lack direct efficiency or payoff outcomes (Otten et al., 2021; Kirchkamp & Mill, 2020), or are ambiguous in translation from contributions to efficiency depending on MPCR/punishment cost.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis Across Papers:**

- **Enabling punishment in repeated PGGs under standard parameters (e.g., group size 3–5, 10–20 rounds, MPCR 0.4–0.6, cost/impact 1:3) reliably increases efficiency/group payoff compared to no-punishment controls**, except in well-specified exceptions:
  - **Robust positive effect:** Strong, direct evidence (e.g., (Gürerk et al., 2018); (Engl et al., 2021); (Gürdal et al., 2021); (Angelovski et al., 2018); (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019); (Arechar et al., 2018); (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019); (Dannenberg et al., 2020); (Kol'veková et al., 2021); (Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020)) that enabling punishment increases efficiency when conditions are met.
  - **Conditional/negative or null effects:** Punishment can **fail to increase** or even **reduce efficiency** when:
      - **Antisocial punishment is frequent** (Bruhin et al., 2020; Vollan et al., 2019; Drouvelis et al., 2021)
      - **Ethnic heterogeneity/in-group bias** undermines norm enforcement (Mantilla et al., 2021)
      - **Information is incomplete or ambiguous**, leading to mis-targeted punishment (De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; De Geest & Kingsley, 2019)
      - **Punishment is too costly** (Dannenberg et al., 2020; Ramalingam et al., 2019)
      - **Institutional/punishment design is suboptimal** (e.g., only one punishment channel: Glöckner et al., 2018; delayed feedback: Waichman & Stenzel, 2019)
      - **Behavioral context dampens punishment's effectiveness** (e.g., emotional context: Lee & Min, 2021)
      - **Network structure enables antisocial punishment or retaliation** (Fatas et al., 2020; Kamei, 2020)
      - **Threshold/CPR or extraction games differ in punishment's cost-benefit** (Vollan et al., 2019; De Geest & Stranlund, 2019; Selles et al., 2020)

- **Institutional details matter:** Effectiveness and efficiency gains are **very sensitive** to variation in punishment form (peer vs. centralized, leader, third party), cost, visibility, observability, and information feedback (Glöckner et al., 2018; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Kamei, 2018).

- **Endogenous vs. exogenous institution choice:** Endogenous adoption often yields milder, sometimes more effective punishment per unit, but efficiency gain is mostly driven by the presence—not mode—of punishment (Marcin et al., 2019; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020).

- **Interaction with other mechanisms:** Communication (chat), reward, and group voting can amplify, substitute, or moderate punishment's effect (Koch et al., 2021; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020).

- **Contextual moderators:** Population/cultural heterogeneity, group composition, baseline expectations, emotional context, and reputation systems can **flip or modulate** the direction and size of the efficiency effect.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Prediction Rule Supported by the Literature:**
- **In standard repeated linear PGGs with observed contributions, moderate group size, and punishment with favorable cost/impact ratios, enabling peer (or centralized) punishment will increase average efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline, with the treatment-control efficiency difference depending systematically on:**
    - **MPCR**—Higher social return increases potential for efficiency gains but also baseline efficiency; larger MPCR often means less need for punishment.
    - **Punishment cost/technology**—Lower cost, higher impact, and well-targeted punishment mechanisms (especially when coupled with full information and feedback) produce stronger efficiency gains.
    - **Group composition and context**—Efficiency gains are largest in homogeneous groups with little antisocial punishment and when baseline efficiency is low (i.e., punishment "restores" cooperation); when antisocial punishment or in-group bias is prevalent, gains are absent or negative.
    - **Information structure**—Perfect information about contributions and endowments is necessary for punishment to increase efficiency; with incomplete information, punishment may reduce efficiency (De Geest & Kingsley, 2019; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).
    - **Behavioral environment**—Emotional state (anger promotes, happiness undermines punishment’s effect), baseline expectations, and presence of countervailing social norms can critically affect the efficiency gains (Lee & Min, 2021; Engel, Kube, & Kurschilgen, 2021).
    - **Institutional design**—The effectiveness and cost of the punishment institution (e.g., peer vs. centralized, costliness, visibility, ability to coordinate) shape the efficiency effect.

- **Control efficiency is a useful baseline:** The lower the control (no-punishment) efficiency, the more scope for punishment to increase it, *if* other moderators are favorable. In some contexts, control and treatment efficiency are very similar—notably when punishment is costly, poorly targeted, or antisocial punishment is high.

- **Naïve extrapolation is dangerous:** Simple mapping from design + control efficiency to predicted treatment efficiency is only valid when context/parameters are similar to mainline studies. Significant moderators as above must be taken into account.

- **Quantitative estimates:** Literature gives effect sizes: efficiency increases of 10–30+ percentage points are typical where punishment works well (e.g., Arechar et al., 2018; Gürerk et al., 2018; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019), but neutral or negative changes are possible under unfavorable moderators.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count` (group size): Directly manipulated and analyzed; large heterogeneity in effects and over-punishment risk for large N (Kamei, 2020).
- `num_rounds`: Most studies are repeated games (10–30 rounds); treatment duration effects are documented.
- `mpcr`: Extensively varied in the literature; known to affect baseline and treatment efficiency.
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`: Detailed cost-impact structures, timing (immediate/delayed), centralization, and observability are frequently reported and shown to moderate efficiency gains.
- `chat`: Communication's presence/absence is often manipulated and can substitute for or amplify the effect of punishment (Koch et al., 2021).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Included in several studies to contrast effects with punishment; rewards sometimes increase efficiency more than punishment (Gürerk et al., 2018).
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`: Information structure (who knows what, and when) substantially mediates outcomes.
- `all_or_nothing`, `default_contrib`: Evaluated in binary/constrained PGGs and shown to moderate efficiency gains in all-or-nothing or threshold settings.

**Indirectly or Contextually Informed:**
- `default_contrib`: Manipulated less frequently; discussed in framing/choice architecture studies.
- `show_punishment_id`: Directly studied in some third-party punishment work; shown to affect targeting and deterrence.

**Sparse or Missing:**
- Some subtle dimensions (e.g., precise framing of default contributions, informational subtleties, highly novel punishment technologies) receive little direct study or are mentioned only contextually.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Conditionality and Moderators:** The efficiency effect of punishment is not universal—it is modulated by antisocial punishment, group heterogeneity, information structure, emotional context, institutional details, and social/cultural variables. Predictive models must account for these moderators.
- **Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes:** Not all "successful cooperation" translates into higher efficiency; costly or mis-targeted punishment—and especially antisocial punishment—can erode or reverse gains.
- **Measurement and Reporting Gaps:** Some studies report only contributions or behavioral compliance, not efficiency or group payoff, leading to inference rather than direct measurement.
- **External Validity:** Most evidence is from lab experiments with moderate-size groups and canonical structures; evidence for large-N, highly heterogeneous, or field settings is more limited.
- **Scope of Design Dimensions:** Although core dimensions are well covered, some technical or new design features may be underrepresented in the literature.
- **Potential Publication Bias:** The overwhelming focus on standard linear PGGs with punishment (and efficiency increases) may mask settings where punishment is ineffective or harmful; negative or null results are underreported relative to positive findings.
- **Non-punishment Interventions:** The presence of alternative or concurrent mechanisms (rewards, communication, endogenous institution choice) complicates causal attribution to punishment alone.
- **Noise in Effect Size Estimates:** Heterogeneity in reporting, sample frames, and game variants means that quantitative estimates should be treated with caution and contextually benchmarked.

**In summary:**  
The literature strongly supports predicting that enabling (well-designed) peer or centralized punishment in standard, repeated PGGs will increase efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline, *if* punishment is not too costly, information is sufficient, antisocial punishment is rare, and the group context is favorable. However, these effects are highly context-dependent, and the presence of key moderators can erase or reverse them. Control game efficiency plus explicit attention to design dimensions and context should guide quantitative prediction, not simple extrapolation.
