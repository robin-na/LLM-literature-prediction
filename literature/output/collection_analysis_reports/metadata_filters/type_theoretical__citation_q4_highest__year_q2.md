# 1) Evidence Base

The literature base consists almost entirely of **theoretical and simulation-based studies** (no direct empirical/experimental reports). The coverage is **broad in terms of models, mechanisms, and variants of public goods games (PGGs)**, with an emphasis on evolutionary and spatial models, game-theoretic mechanism design, and the dynamics of cooperation under various forms of punishment, exclusion, and reward. The evidence directly addressing the **prediction of efficiency effects from enabling punishment (peer or institutional) in standard PGG designs is strong at the theoretical level** and covers a wide swath of game design parameters such as group size, cost and effectiveness of punishment, marginal per-capita return (mpcr), number of rounds, and the presence of network/spatial structure.

However, the paper set is **lopsided towards theory and simulation**: empirical support is predominantly cited via reviews and meta-analyses, rather than new data. Furthermore, **most primary outcomes are derived, modeled, or simulated efficiency or payoff metrics**—not empirical efficiency rates from laboratory or field experiments. There is a diversity in environments (well-mixed vs. spatial, compulsory vs. voluntary participation, pure vs. hybrid incentive mechanisms) but the heterogeneity in model details and definitions adds some ambiguity in direct application for prediction tasks.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **Pgg_or_variant**: **exact** – The majority of studies address classic PGGs or precisely specified variants (e.g., with exclusion, threshold, or hybrid incentive structures). Some works are adjacent (iterated PDs, Snowdrift, trust/ultimatum games), but most primary findings map directly to the standard multi-player PGG structure or very close relatives.
- **Punishment_or_sanctions**: **exact** – Punishment—both peer and institutional, as well as exclusion mechanisms—is central to most works. Both positive (reward) and negative (punishment/exclusion) incentives are systematically modeled. Several papers further distinguish forms (prosocial, antisocial, mutual, conditional, adaptive), and discuss implementation details (cost, target, observability, possibility of antisocial use).
- **Efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: **exact/close** – Many studies report group efficiency or total payoff (often normalized to the fully cooperative benchmark). Others report very close outcomes: group achievement (success in threshold games), surplus, or welfare. Several influential studies report only behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates, prevalence of strategies), which are tightly linked but should not be conflated with efficiency. **Where only behavioral data are available, this is clearly noted in the digest.**

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related (exact/close):**
  - **Group efficiency**: Ratio of actual group payoff to full-cooperation payoff; explicit in core theory works and several model-based reviews.
  - **Total earnings/payoff/welfare/surplus**: Used as synonyms or proxies; in PGG models these usually reflect "efficiency".
  - **Group achievement (threshold games)** and **average payoff** metrics (in both absolute and normalized forms).

- **Non-payoff behavioral:**
  - **Cooperation/contribution rate**: Prevalent as the main outcome in many models (e.g., strategy frequencies at equilibrium, fraction of cooperators).
  - **Norm compliance, punishment frequency, retaliation, prevalence of strategy classes (e.g., punishers, loners, excluders)**.

- **Key distinctions**:
  - Several studies discuss **mechanisms that produce high cooperation without corresponding efficiency gains** due to high punishment cost or antisocial punishment. Some models provide explicit phase diagrams that map behavioral outcomes to payoff-based regions.
  - Many papers use cooperation prevalence as a proxy for efficiency, but **warn that the cost of incentives must be netted out** to determine efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## a) **Direction of Punishment's Effect on Efficiency**
- **Punishment can increase efficiency in PGGs—but only under specific conditions.** The main positive effect is found when punishment is not prohibitively costly, is effectively targeted to defectors, and is not hijacked by antisocial users (Perc et al., 2017; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013, 2017; Adami et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2015; Wolitzky, 2013; Sasaki et al., 2015).
- **High punishment cost, inefficient implementation, or antisocial/counter punishment can wipe out efficiency gains, or even make efficiency worse than control.**
  - **Antisocial punishment** undermines efficiency unless effectively suppressed (Hauser et al., 2014; Kurzban et al., 2015; Sylwester et al., 2013; Barrett, 2016).
  - Efficiency gains are **fragile** if punishment becomes common but ineffective, or costs accumulate due to mutual retaliation.

## b) **Key Moderators and Game Design Dimensions**
- **Player count / group size**: Larger groups make voluntary reward/punishment less efficient; spatial structure (network clustering) is necessary for punishment to sustain efficiency in larger populations (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013, 2017; Wolitzky, 2013; Gavrilets, 2015; Barrett, 2016).
- **Punishment cost and effectiveness**: Low cost and strong effectiveness support efficiency gains; high cost or low effectiveness can reduce overall efficiency (Perc et al., 2017; Sasaki et al., 2015; Adami et al., 2016; Barrett, 2016).
- **Marginal per-capita return (MPCR)**: Punishment is more beneficial in mid-to-low MPCR regimes where cooperation is otherwise hard to sustain (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Adami et al., 2016).
- **Punishment technology (peer/pool, exclusion vs. costly punishment)**: Exclusion mechanisms often outperform standard costly punishment in sustaining high efficiency (Sasaki & Uchida, 2013; Liu et al., 2017; Li et al., 2015).
- **Reward mechanisms**: Joint reward and punishment can outperform either alone in establishing and maintaining cooperation, but with diminishing returns when cost is high (Chen et al., 2015; Sasaki et al., 2015).
- **Information and monitoring**: High transparency and network observability enhance the positive effect of punishment (Wolitzky, 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).

## c) **Design Interactions and Dynamics**
- **Nonlinear and non-monotonic effects**: Sometimes, intermediate punishment costs are optimal; too much or too little can harm efficiency (Chen et al., 2014; Perc et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2015).
- **Conditional/adaptive punishment**: Mechanisms that allow punishment to scale with the norm violation or to be used adaptively (e.g., only after cooperation is established) minimize costs and maximize efficiency (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Chen et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2014).
- **Spatial/network structure**: Local interactions and the ability to form clusters of cooperators/punishers support higher efficiency (Szolnoki & Perc, 2017; Adami et al., 2016; Perc et al., 2013, 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should these findings inform predictions of treatment efficiency given design dimensions and control efficiency?**

- **Enable punishment only if punishment cost is moderate and effectiveness is high:** If the control efficiency is low (indicating difficulty in sustaining cooperation), enabling punishment is predicted to increase efficiency only if the parameters avoid excessive punishment cost and suppression of antisocial punishment (Perc et al., 2017; Barrett, 2016; Kurzban et al., 2015; Hauser et al., 2014).
- **Effect size contextualized by control efficiency and moderators:** The expected gain from enabling punishment is larger when baseline (control) efficiency is low and game design falls within theoretical/empirical "safe zones" for effective, targeted punishment.
- **Incorporate key design features**:
  - Player count (smaller groups -> more likely positive effect; large groups require structured interactions or institutional mechanisms);
  - Number of rounds (more rounds favor efficiency gains via punishment, as longer-term threats become credible; Kurzban et al., 2015);
  - MPCR (intermediate or low values benefit more from punishment-enabled transitions to cooperation);
  - Punishment cost/tech (exclusion often more efficient than straightforward costly punishment, especially in large/populated settings; Liu et al., 2017; Sasaki & Uchida, 2013);
  - Reward mechanisms (joint reward can enhance or mediate efficiency, especially for establishing cooperation in large populations; Chen et al., 2015; Sasaki et al., 2015).
- **Caveat when antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is allowed**: In such settings, enabling punishment may have zero or negative effect (Hauser et al., 2014). Predictor should flag design features that permit or foster antisocial punishment.
- **Use phase diagrams and comparative statics where available:** Several theory papers provide explicit mappings from game parameters to efficiency outcomes (Perc et al., 2017; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
- **Spatial and network structure is a strong positive moderator:** Where players can form clusters or localized enforcement, efficiency gains are more robust (Szolnoki & Perc, 2017; Wolitzky, 2013; Adami et al., 2016).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- `player_count` (group size): **Repeatedly analyzed**; major moderator.
- `num_rounds`: **Direct attention** (Kurzban et al., 2015; Wolitzky, 2013).
- `all_or_nothing`: **Present in models**; influences dynamics (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Chen et al., 2015).
- `mpcr`: **Central in almost all core theory works**.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: **Main axes** of comparative statics and phase diagram results (Perc et al., 2017; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013, 2017; Wolitzky, 2013).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: **Frequently modeled as joint/alternative mechanisms** (Chen et al., 2015; Sasaki et al., 2015).
- `show_other_summaries` (information/monitoring): **Explicitly discussed** (Wolitzky, 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).

**Indirectly Informed/Contextual:**
- `chat`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`: Not systematically analyzed, but occasionally referenced as social/contextual moderators (Parks et al., 2013).
- `default_contrib`: Very seldom addressed; some context on contribution framing.
- `punishment_magnitude`: Not always explicitly parameterized, but sometimes folded into cost/tech effectiveness.
- `spatial/network structure`: Addressed via parameterized models, though not always in exact predictor dimension terms.

**Missing/Sparse:**
- `chat`, `default_contrib` (framing), `show_punishment_id` (anonymity) have **limited direct analysis** in efficiency terms.
- **Richest evidence is for cost, effectiveness, size, and structure dimensions.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical data is scarce**: Most results are theoretical or simulation-based; only indirect or secondary empirical references are present.
- **Behavioral-proxy vs. efficiency ambiguity**: Many findings on increased cooperation do not always translate to efficiency gains due to the cost of incentives.
- **Sparse attention to certain dimensions**: Communication, default framing, and feedback/identity dimensions are rarely scrutinized with respect to efficiency outcomes.
- **Antisocial/counter punishment ambiguity**: Results diverge sharply when antisocial punishment is possible; empirical magnitude, prevalence, and triggers are uncertain.
- **Relevance to peer vs. institutional punishment**: Most mechanisms are modeled at the institutional (pool) or peer level, but direct translation to specific implementation details may not be justified without further context.
- **Network/population structure generalizability**: Robustness of findings from spatially structured models to well-mixed or real-group environments is sometimes unclear.
- **Lack of quantitative effect sizes**: While directionality and moderators are well-mapped, precise effect magnitude estimation is not available from this theoretical/simulation literature.
- **Assumption-dependent predictions**: Phase transitions and predicted efficiency zones depend on strong assumptions (e.g., rational actors, no implementation error, full observability).

---

**In summary:**  
The literature—heavily theoretical—strongly supports that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like settings is **conditioned by group size, punishment cost/effectiveness, MPCR, and structure**. Generally, **punishment increases efficiency when it is not too costly, is effectively targeted, and antisocial use is prevented**. However, efficiency can decline if these moderators are not carefully controlled in the game design. **Effect predictions should explicitly incorporate these moderators, the observed control efficiency, and remain cautious when behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates) are reported without clear net payoff calculations.**
