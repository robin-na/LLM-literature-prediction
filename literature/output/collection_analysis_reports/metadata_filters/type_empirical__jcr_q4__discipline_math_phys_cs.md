# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of 27 primarily **empirical laboratory experiments**, with a smaller contingent of field and theoretical studies. Within the empirical studies, most adopt **classic or variant public goods game (PGG) structures** or adjacent team/coordination games. Several focus directly on **punishment in PGGs**; others address **punishment, reward, or information feedback** in adjacent games (e.g., trust, delegation, or minimum-effort). The body is **broad** with respect to behavioral mechanisms (e.g., motivations for punishment, role of visibility, network density), but **narrower** in terms of precise coverage of every game design dimension relevant to the prediction task. The evidence is weighted toward **experimental laboratory findings on the impact of peer or centralized punishment on efficiency and related payoff outcomes**, with a lesser but notable inclusion of adjacent settings (e.g., trust, coordination). Where reported, studies provide both **empirical estimates of group efficiency** and **insight into design-conditional variation**. However, **not all papers report (or prioritize) group efficiency as an outcome**, with several focusing on cooperation rates or behavioral proxies.

# 2) Task Relevance

## pgg_or_variant
- **exact relevance**: Many papers deploy standard linear PGGs or close variants (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Pancotto et al., 2023; Kanitsar, 2021; Peng, 2022; Nax et al., 2018).
- **close/adjacent relevance**: Several studies use games structurally similar to PGGs but with key differences (e.g., minimal effort games, trust games, delegation games, all-or-nothing formats, CPRs, or asymmetric roles) (e.g., Calabuig et al., 2024; Herne et al., 2022; Lec et al., 2023).
- **weak/none**: A subset applies to more distant settings (e.g., principal-agent budget reporting, two-player sequential lottery games), which limits generalizability (e.g., Chen, 2012; Dato & Friehe, 2025).

## punishment_or_sanctions
- **exact relevance**: Direct, parameterized punishment opportunities (peer, centralized, or institutional) are either introduced or compared as treatments in many PGG/lab experiments (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **close/adjacent/weak**: Other forms of sanctions (e.g., exclusion, non-monetary disapproval, reward, redistribution, refusal to cooperate) or pure information disclosure are explored in adjacent games or as institutional alternatives (e.g., Grund et al., 2020; Becchetti et al., 2018; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020).

## efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome
- **exact relevance**: Several papers **directly report group efficiency or total earnings** as a key outcome (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Peng, 2022; Lec et al., 2023).
- **adjacent/weak relevance**: Other papers **primarily report behavioral outcomes** (contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency) and must be cautiously interpreted regarding efficiency (e.g., Pancotto et al., 2023; Noussair et al., 2015).
- **none**: Some provide little or no information on efficiency, focusing instead on experimental mechanisms or non-payoff behaviors (e.g., Dato & Friehe, 2025; Chen, 2012).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (efficiency/group payoff/total earnings/welfare/surplus):**
    - Directly analyzed in several standard and variant PGG punishment studies, especially those with lab designs and multi-round structures (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Lec et al., 2023; Kanitsar, 2021; Peng, 2022).
    - Closely tracked and compared between punishment-enabled and control/no-punishment conditions.
    - Some adjacent games (delegation, minimum effort, CPR) also report group efficiency or total payoffs.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
    - Contribution rates, cooperation rates, punishment/reward frequencies, norm compliance, justification, intention assessment.
    - Many papers rely substantially (or exclusively) on these as primary outcomes, and only infer or speculate about efficiency (e.g., Pancotto et al., 2023; Grund et al., 2020; Dato & Friehe, 2025).
    - Some studies link these behavioral measures to payoff possibility, but do not directly calculate net efficiency after accounting for the costs of punishment/reward.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment typically increases efficiency in standard linear PGGs,** but:
    - The magnitude of the gain is **moderate and variable**—dependant on group-level punishment norms (e.g., prevalence of strong reciprocators, norm-keepers), institutional context, and specific game design (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
    - In lab PGGs, **centralized punishment or formal sanctions often outperform informal/peer punishment** in increasing efficiency (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021).
    - **Enabling peer punishment raises net group earnings only if punishment is used primarily to deter free-riding and not wasted on antisocial punishment or reciprocal feuds;** costliness, observability, and the potential for retaliation can reduce or negate efficiency gains (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Kanitsar, 2021).
    - If punishment is **costless, efficiency gains are more robust;** with costly punishment, net effects depend on the size of punishment costs compared to cooperation gains (Kanitsar, 2021).

- **Game structure and context matter:**
    - **Group openness (migration allowed) increases the effectiveness of punishment** due to higher threats from out-group selection, with larger efficiency gains in these open societies (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
    - **Punishment with full information, multiple stages, and fixed IDs enables retaliation and “feuding,”** which can fully offset any contributions gains, leaving group efficiency unchanged or reduced (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - In **adjacent game forms (team investment, trust, minimum effort, delegation, etc.)**, the efficiency effect is highly sensitive to whether punishment deters uncooperative behavior **without excessive cost**; in several cases, **punishment increases cooperation/effort but not group efficiency** because of its costs (Herne et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024; Abbink et al., 2004).

- **Reward mechanisms, when present, can also increase efficiency,** sometimes outperforming or complementing punishment, especially when implemented as a formal or group-voted mechanism (Peng, 2022).

- **Public information or monitoring is not a substitute for punishment,** and can reduce efficiency if it increases mimicry of uncooperative behavior without credible sanction (Becchetti, Conzo, & Degli Antoni, 2015).

- **Cultural, group composition, and selection effects are important:** Both the heterogeneity of norms and the endogenous choice or selection process for sanctioning institutions can moderate the effect size and direction (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **When predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions:**
    - **Enabling punishment in standard linear PGGs typically increases efficiency moderately,** especially if punishment is neither overly costly nor prone to feuds/retaliation (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021).
    - **Magnitude of increase is directly moderated by design dimensions:**
        - **Punishment Cost/Technology (punishment_cost, punishment_tech):** Efficiency gains are larger with lower-cost and higher-effect punishment (Kanitsar, 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
        - **Information Structure (show_punishment_id, punishment_tech):** Visible, traceable punishment with multiple opportunities per round or known identities can enable costly retaliation and erode efficiency gains (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
        - **Group Structure (player_count, open/closed):** Larger and more open groups benefit more from centralized/formal punishment mechanisms (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
        - **Reward Exists:** Combined or majority-vote reward mechanisms can increase efficiency, especially in the absence of punishment (Peng, 2022).
- **If the control group is already very efficient, the marginal gain from enabling punishment may be small or negative** (Castillo et al., 2021; Calabuig et al., 2024).
- **In adjacent or non-standard games, increased cooperation via punishment does not guarantee efficiency gains:** The net effect depends on the cost structure, frequency of punishment, and resulting group payoff accounting for all costs (Herne et al., 2022; Abbink et al., 2004; Calabuig et al., 2024).
- **Designs that allow cheap or especially effective punishment, limited opportunities for retaliation, or centralization tend to produce more reliable efficiency gains.**
- **Behavioral outcome data (contribution/cooperation rates) may be an imperfect proxy for efficiency, especially if punishment costs are large.** Use only direct efficiency outcomes or adjust predictions accordingly.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Varies across studies (2–16); effects generally consistent across typical lab sizes (4–5), but some findings on group size dependency (especially for open vs. closed groups, centralized vs. peer punishment).
- `num_rounds`: Most experiments are repeated games (6, 10, 16, 30 rounds), supporting direct transfer of findings.
- `mpcr`: Frequently specified and manipulated (typically 0.3–0.5).
- `all_or_nothing`, `chat`: Contribution format (continuous/all-or-nothing), communication settings, and their interactions with punishment are explored.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Central to most punishment studies; effects of cost, fine-to-cost ratio, number of punishment stages, ID visibility, retaliation possibility are directly tested.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Considered in studies combining or comparing reward mechanisms.
- `show_n_rounds`: Outcomes under known/unknown time horizons sometimes compared.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Investigated primarily in the context of transparency, information feedback, and retaliation.
  
**Indirectly informed/contextually discussed:**
- `default_contrib`: Framing (opt-in/opt-out) is occasionally manipulated or discussed, but is not a primary determinant in most papers.
- Aspects like endogenous group formation (open societies, migration) are relevant but not always parameterized across all design dimensions.

**Sparsely covered or effectively missing:**
- Specific interactions among multiple design features (e.g., default_contrib × reward_exists × chat) are rarely jointly manipulated.
- The interaction of all 14 prediction dimensions, or their algorithmic combinatorial effects on efficiency, are not directly assessed.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited coverage of all prediction-relevant design dimensions:** While many dimensions are addressed individually, their interactive effects are often unspecified or untested.
- **Heterogeneity in mechanisms and contexts:** Papers vary in punishment type (peer, centralized, third-party), institutional legitimacy, network/density, observability, making generalization beyond specific designs risky.
- **Outcomes sometimes inferred:** Where efficiency is not directly reported, inference from behavior may be misleading if punishment costs are high.
- **Adjacency of many papers:** Results from trust, coordination, or other non-PGG games may not generalize straightforwardly to classic PGGs.
- **Potentially unmeasured moderators:** Social/cultural context, group composition (e.g., prevalence of norm-keepers/reciprocators), and repeated group selection can moderate punishment impacts but are rarely parameterized for prediction.
- **Summary/aggregate data limits:** Many studies lack complete joint reporting of control and treatment group efficiencies necessary for direct quantity prediction across all 14 dimensions.
- **External validity is unclear:** Studies caution that effects seen in lab settings may not fully translate to field environments (Noussair et al., 2015).

**In sum:**  
The literature provides strong, nuanced evidence that enabling punishment in public-goods settings will frequently—but not invariably—increase efficiency, with effect size and reliability moderated by punishment design (cost, information, centralization), group structure (openness, player count), and the efficiency of the baseline (control). Prediction is well-informed for standard PGG design features, less so for complex or combined manipulation of multiple dimensions, and weakest where only behavioral (not payoff) outcomes are available or settings diverge from PGG structure.
