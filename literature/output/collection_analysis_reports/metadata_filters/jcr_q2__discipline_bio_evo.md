# Evidence Base

**Nature of the Paper Set:**  
The paper set comprises a broad mix of theoretical and empirical work, including formal models from evolutionary biology and game theory, laboratory and field experiments, ethnographic syntheses, and conceptual or mechanistic reviews. Empirical papers are mostly laboratory experiments or field experiments in human-centric and ecological contexts (e.g., fisheries, irrigation, animal mutualisms), while theoretical contributions often employ mathematical models structured around public goods games (PGGs) or their close variants.

**Breadth for the Prediction Task:**  
Regarding the prediction of treatment efficiency (i.e., group payoff efficiency when peer punishment is enabled in PGG-like environments), the evidence base is relatively broad in its disciplinary coverage but spotty in direct empirical data bearing on payoff-based efficiency outcomes. There are several exact or close matches (both theory and empirical) addressing efficiency in PGGs with and without punishment (notably, Eldakar et al., 2013; Javaid & Falk, 2015; Gelcich et al., 2013; POLLOCK, 1988), but many papers focus only on non-payoff behavioral outcomes, punishment use, or cooperation rates, and/or on non-PGG or adjacent game structures. Direct, quantitative, multi-arm experimental evidence for the impact of punishment on group efficiency is limited, with much of the mechanistic and moderator evidence remaining theoretical or indirect.

# Task Relevance

## 1. Public Goods Game or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`)
- **Exact Relevance:** Core theoretical and some empirical work are conducted in standard or canonical PGGs (e.g., Eldakar et al., 2013; Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Bone et al., 2014; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011).
- **Close/Adjacent Relevance:** Several studies use common pool resource (CPR) games, repeated prisoner’s dilemmas, threshold or nonlinear public goods games, or other social dilemmas structurally related to PGGs (Javaid & Falk, 2015; POLLOCK, 1988; Gardner & West, 2004; Sasaki et al., 2016; dos Santos et al., 2014).
- **Weak/None:** Some papers work in related but non-group or dyadic games (dictator, trust, or ultimatum) or discuss concepts outside of explicit PGGs.

## 2. Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`)
- **Exact Relevance:** Many of the central theory and empirical papers directly manipulate the presence or absence of peer (or group) punishment mechanisms in PGGs or close analogues (Eldakar et al., 2013; Bone et al., 2014; Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Javaid & Falk, 2015).
- **Close/Adjacent Relevance:** Several use sanctions more generally (including rewards, external or probabilistic punishment, or third-party punishment) or describe enforcement roles, social exclusion, reputation mechanisms, or informal threats (Perez et al., 2015; Baum et al., 2012; Boehm, 2014).
- **Weak/None:** A subset discuss cooperation mechanisms without any explicit experimental or modeled punishment dimension.

## 3. Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`)
- **Exact/Close:** A restricted subset actually measure or model group efficiency or total group payoff as a primary outcome with and without punishment in PGG or CPR environments (Eldakar et al., 2013; Javaid & Falk, 2015; Gelcich et al., 2013; POLLOCK, 1988; Forsyth & Hauert, 2011 for reward; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011).
- **Adjacent:** Many papers instead focus on behavioral or intermediate outcomes (contribution rate, norm compliance, punishment frequency) and only discuss (but do not measure) efficiency implications.
- **Weak/None:** There is limited evidence providing quantitative effect sizes or cross-dimensional interactions for efficiency outcomes, especially from direct experimental contrasts.

# Outcomes Measured In The Literature

## Payoff-Related Outcomes
- **Directly measured:**  
  - *Efficiency or total group payoff:* Some papers present explicit group efficiency ratios, average earnings, or welfare outcomes as a function of punishment design (Eldakar et al., 2013; Javaid & Falk, 2015; Gelcich et al., 2013).
  - *Related constructs:* Group surplus, welfare, or total coins/earnings generated used as proxies for efficiency (Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011).

## Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes
- **Commonly measured:**  
  - Contribution/cooperation rate, norm compliance, frequency/targeting of punishment, coordination success, reputation-based behavior, and use of enforcement roles.
  - Neurological and psychological mechanisms of punishment motivation; punitive behavior in response to inequity or norm violation.
  - In animal and observational studies: frequency of cooperative or punitive behaviors, spatial structuring, partner selection, or sharing behaviors.

## Distinction:
- **Importantly, most papers reporting behavioral outcomes do not connect them directly to group efficiency as defined by total payoff relative to the fully cooperative optimum.**  
  - When guidance is drawn from such sources, their relevance to payoff prediction is only indirect.

# Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **On Average, Enabling Punishment Increases Efficiency**
  - In most standard or repeated PGGs with moderate baseline efficiency, enabling (especially peer) punishment typically increases group efficiency by suppressing free-riding, up to the point where punishment costs do not outweigh cooperative gains (Eldakar et al., 2013; Baker & Rachlin, 2002; POLLOCK, 1988; Gelcich et al., 2013).
  - The effect is more pronounced when punishment is effective (the cost to the target is high relative to the punisher’s cost), group size is small, and there is opportunity for repeated interaction (Eldakar et al., 2013; Gardner & West, 2004).

- **Boundary Conditions and Moderators Are Crucial**
  - When the control (no-punishment) baseline efficiency is already high (e.g., due to strong intrinsic norms, social visibility, or pre-existing coordination), introducing punishment may reduce efficiency by adding unnecessary costs, coordination problems, or increasing waste (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
  - Institutional context (public vs. private punishment, probabilistic vs. certain punishment, peer vs. centralized mechanisms) changes the size and sometimes the direction of efficiency effects (Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011; Gelcich et al., 2013).

- **Punishment Is Not Universally Effective**
  - When punishment is costly, ineffective, or poorly targeted, or when “antisocial” punishment (punishing cooperators) predominates (or cognitive overload occurs), the expected efficiency gains may be reduced or eliminated (dos Santos et al., 2014).
  - Agent-related characteristics (social capital, cohesion, past group performance) can modulate the response: sanctioning boosts efficiency most in cohesive/high-social-capital groups, less so in fragmented or normless settings (Gelcich et al., 2013).

- **Theory Converges on Mechanisms, but Experimental Quantification Is Scarce**
  - Theory and simulations consistently predict that peer punishment can sustain high efficiency when appropriate structural/dynamic preconditions are met (Eldakar et al., 2013; POLLOCK, 1988; Gardner & West, 2004; Sasaki et al., 2016).
  - Population structure (multiple groups, repeated play, group-level competition) is critical for punishment to yield efficiency gains (POLLOCK, 1988; Gardner & West, 2004).
  - Some models indicate that too high a punishment cost or insufficient linkage between punishment and cooperation undermines group efficiency or the evolutionary stability of punishment (Lehmann et al., 2007; Inglis et al., 2014).

- **Design Features Matter**
  - Efficiency gains are larger when:
    - *Punishment cost per unit is low;* (Eldakar et al., 2013; Gardner & West, 2004)
    - *Group size is small to moderate;*
    - *The number of rounds is high (repetition strengthens effect);*
    - *Information about contributions/punishment is well structured (Gelcich et al., 2013).*
  - In some close/adjacent evidence, rewards can also increase efficiency, but often less robustly or stably than punishment (Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; Sasaki & Uchida, 2014).

# Prediction Guidance

- **Most useful prediction heuristic:**  
  *Given the average efficiency of the control (no-punishment) game and the game design dimensions, expect that enabling peer punishment will usually increase average efficiency—sometimes dramatically—unless baseline efficiency is already near optimal or the punishment mechanism is poorly designed or costly.*
  
- **Magnitude and direction of the effect depend on:**
  - *Punishment cost:* If low relative to effect, efficiency gain is larger (Eldakar et al., 2013).
  - *Group size and repetition:* Smaller/medium groups and more rounds increase chances for punishment to work (Eldakar et al., 2013; POLLOCK, 1988).
  - *Baseline control efficiency:* If control is very high (e.g., due to existing strong norms/social information), efficiency may stay flat or fall (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
  - *Social information/feedback:* Visibility of contributions and outcomes is supportive of punishment's effectiveness (Gelcich et al., 2013; dos Santos et al., 2013).
  - *Institutional details:* Peer punishment is usually more effective than top-down/external punishment when other conditions are matched (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Gelcich et al., 2013).

- **When not to expect an efficiency increase:**
  - *High baseline efficiency or norm strength;*
  - *High punishment cost, antisocial punishment, or poorly structured sanctions (Javaid & Falk, 2015; dos Santos et al., 2014);*
  - *Single-shot games or fully unstructured/anonymous settings unless other mechanisms link cooperation and punishment (Lehmann et al., 2007).*

- **Dimension-level gaps:** For some design features—especially chat, all_or_nothing, default_contrib, show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id, or rewards—the literature provides limited and inconsistent evidence for their moderating effects on the efficiency impact of punishment.

# Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions** (clear across theory or empirics):
- `player_count`: Group size is a key moderator, especially in small/medium groups (Eldakar et al., 2013; POLLOCK, 1988; Gardner & West, 2004).
- `num_rounds`: Number of rounds strongly supports effectiveness and sustainability of punishment (Eldakar et al., 2013).
- `mpcr`: Marginal per-capita return shapes both the baseline and the payoff from increased contributions (Eldakar et al., 2013; Gardner & West, 2004).
- `punishment_cost`: Central, with direct evidence that lower cost increases efficiency gains from punishment (Eldakar et al., 2013; Ubeda & Duéñez-Guzmán, 2011).
- `punishment_tech`: Peer/individual vs. collective/institutional punishment and implementation details often discussed (Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Gelcich et al., 2013).

**Indirectly Informed/Moderately Addressed**:
- `all_or_nothing`: Used in several theoretical and empirical designs without always isolating its moderating effect.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Some theory and experiment examine rewards as an alternative or supplement to punishment (Forsyth & Hauert, 2011; Sasaki & Uchida, 2014).
- `show_other_summaries`: Social information is reported as an important contextual moderator (Javaid & Falk, 2015; Gelcich et al., 2013).
- `show_punishment_id`: Sometimes reported (Levy, 2022; Boehm, 2014), with qualitative suggestions but few direct tests.

**Contextually Discussed/Weakly Covered**:
- `chat`: Occasionally present but rarely isolated as a moderator of efficiency effect (Perez et al., 2015).
- `default_contrib`: Framing effects are rarely isolated specifically for punishment’s efficiency impact.
- `show_n_rounds`: Discussed rarely or in passing, with limited direct evidence.

**Effectively Missing/Very Sparse**:
- Precise interactions between multiple dimensions (e.g., the joint impact of chat and punishment tech) are seldom tested.
- Manipulations of certain UI framing or information features (such as `show_n_rounds` or `show_punishment_id`) in relation to efficiency are very limited or absent.

# Important Limitations

- **Payoff/Efficiency Outcome Scarcity:** Direct, empirical evidence—particularly experimental—that contrasts efficiency in punishment-disabled and -enabled PGGs across a variety of game designs is relatively scarce in this set.
- **Behavioral/Payoff Distinction Often Blurred:** Many studies focus on behavioral indicators (contribution rates, punishment frequency) as if they supported payoff predictions, but actual net efficiency impact is often not measured.
- **Moderator Generalization Hazard:** Theoretical findings about parameter effects (group size, punishment cost, population structure) may not fully translate to laboratory or field settings due to omitted variables (individual differences, social context, implementation details).
- **Control Baseline Sensitivity:** Some of the strongest evidence that punishment increases efficiency comes from designs with low to moderate control efficiency; if control games are already highly efficient (due to strong norms or public information), enabling punishment may reduce efficiency.
- **Dimension Gaps:** For several design features crucial to the prediction task (e.g., communication/chat, all-or-nothing vs. continuous, contributor identity display, default contribution framing), available evidence is weak or contextual rather than directly informative.
- **Empirical/Theoretical Imbalance:** Many findings are theoretical or simulation-based; robust cross-study experimental results that quantify size and boundary of efficiency improvements from punishment remain rare.
- **Heterogeneity in Game Variants:** Some studies use CPRs or threshold games, which are structurally similar to PGGs but can yield different dynamic responses to punishment, limiting direct generalization from these variants to standard PGGs.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature provides robust theoretical support, and some experimental evidence, that enabling peer punishment in not-too-efficient PGGs raises group efficiency, especially when punishment is inexpensive, groups are not too large, and the game is sufficiently repeated. However, empirical demonstrations of the full range of efficiency effects across the 14 prediction dimensions are limited, and outcomes are strongly context-dependent. When using this literature for prediction, one should be wary of generalizing from behavioral to payoff measures and attend carefully to baseline efficiency and dimension-level gaps.
