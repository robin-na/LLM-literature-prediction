# Evidence Base

The paper set includes 51 sources with a mix of theoretical (modeling and simulation) and empirical (lab and field experiment, observational) studies. Among the most task-relevant for prediction are several theory papers modeling efficiency or average payoff in public goods games (PGGs) with and without punishment, and a small but important number of direct experimental papers measuring group efficiency (payoff) under control and punishment conditions. The empirical literature more frequently reports on behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation or contribution rates) than on efficiency or group payoff.

The evidence base is broad regarding punishment and cooperation in social dilemmas, but is narrower for direct, quantitative estimates of the effect of enabling punishment on **efficiency** (total group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum) within the design dimensions most relevant to the prediction task.

# Task Relevance

Relevance is summarized below along three axes:

## 1. `pgg_or_variant`  
- **exact**: A significant subset (particularly among the theoretical studies and some empirical experiments) use exact PGG or standard PGG variants as their main setting (e.g., Duong & Han, 2021; Liu et al., 2018, 2019).  
- **close**: Many empirical studies examine adjacent games (threshold public goods games, common-pool resource games, or repeated PDs on networks) that share important structural similarities but differ on critical design points (e.g., collective-risk dilemmas, spatial PGGs).
- **adjacent/weak/none**: Roughly half the papers are adjacent or less relevant, focusing on one-shot PDs, social dilemmas without explicit sanctioning, or real-world norm enforcement outside of controlled game paradigms.

## 2. `punishment_or_sanctions`
- **exact**: Several core theory papers directly manipulate or model the presence/absence and properties of peer or institutional punishment (Duong & Han, 2021; Liu et al., 2018, 2019).
- **close/adjacent**: Many others examine alternative or additional sanctions (exclusion, reward, third-party punishment), sometimes under non-PGG settings.
- **weak/none**: A notable fraction discuss norm enforcement or prosociality in the absence of any formal punishment mechanism, or only in the sense of reputational or observational incentives.

## 3. `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
- **exact**: A small subset (notably the key theory papers and some empirical papers: e.g., Liu et al., 2018, 2019; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019) report average group payoff or efficiency as the main outcome.
- **close**: Others report "group achievement" (e.g., proportion of groups reaching a threshold) or welfare as a proxy (Góis et al., 2019), or explicitly relate their behavioral findings to likely impacts on efficiency without direct measurement.
- **adjacent/weak/none**: Most of the empirical literature—especially field and observational studies—focuses on non-payoff behavioral outcomes, such as cooperation, punishment frequency, or norm compliance, and does not report efficiency.

# Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related (efficiency, group payoff):**
  - Measured directly in a minority of studies, typically theory or simulation (Duong & Han, 2021; Liu et al., 2018, 2019) or in certain lab experiments (Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019).
  - Indirect (or adjacent) proxies include "group achievement" (fraction of groups succeeding), welfare (sum of earnings), or ecological resource level.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - Most frequently, papers report on individual or group contribution rates, cooperation frequency, prevalence or targeting of punishment, or changes in norm perceptions.
  - Some extensively analyze the effects of game design, social context, or punishment/reward parameters on these behavioral metrics without linking to total payoffs.
  - Even among studies that hypothesize increased cooperation should lead to increased efficiency, many do not measure or quantitatively analyze payoff-based outcomes.

- **Important distinction:** Several comprehensive review and theory papers (e.g., Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; van Dijk & De Dreu, 2021) explicitly warn that increased cooperation due to punishment does not necessarily translate to higher efficiency due to punishment's costs, especially when antisocial punishment or retaliation is common.

# Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Synthesized Empirical and Theoretical Trends

- **Punishment can promote cooperation and sometimes increases efficiency**, but the relationship is far from universal.
  - **Theory/simulation results** (Duong & Han, 2021; Liu et al., 2018, 2019) demonstrate that punishment *can* increase group efficiency relative to control, but only under specific parameter regimes: punishment must be effective (high impact per cost) and not routinely misapplied (e.g., absence of antisocial punishment or corruption).
  - **Empirical results** are more equivocal: some experiments show little or no gain in group payoff with punishment, as increased contributions are offset by the costs of administering and receiving punishment (Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018).

- **Design dimensions strongly moderate punishment's efficiency effect:**
  - **Player count (group size):** Theory predicts sensitivity to group size, with increasing size not always improving the effect of punishment—sometimes diminishing it due to increased anonymity or reduced monitoring (Duong & Han, 2021; Alvarez-Rodriguez et al., 2021).
  - **Number of rounds:** Repeated interaction often enhances the effect of punishment on sustaining cooperation, but only if information about past actions is observable and retaliation is limited (van Dijk & De Dreu, 2021).
  - **Punishment cost and magnitude:** The cost-benefit ratio is central; if cost is too high relative to impact, efficiency gains are erased or reversed (Lohse & Waichman, 2020). Antisocial or misdirected punishment further erodes efficiency (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
  - **Normative or institutional constraints:** Efficiency gains depend on norms or institutions that target punishment at defectors rather than cooperators; otherwise, antisocial punishment can dominate (Pleasant & Barclay, 2018; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).

- **Contextual moderators and boundary conditions:**
  - **Antisocial punishment and partner competition** can entirely reverse the expected effect of punishment, driving efficiency below control levels (Pleasant & Barclay, 2018; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
  - **Resource or ecological context:** In common-pool resource games, punishment only improves efficiency when ecological conditions (e.g., resource growth rate) allow; otherwise, overexploitation may persist (Chen & Szolnoki, 2018).
  - **Cultural and institutional context:** The effect and acceptability of punishment vary cross-culturally and with societal structure (Henrich & Muthukrishna, 2021; Eriksson et al., 2021).

- **Variance across punishment mechanisms:**  
  - **Peer punishment**: more prone to antisocial targeting; efficiency gains are fragile.
  - **Pool or institutional punishment/third-party punishment:** can support higher efficiency, especially with monitoring/reporting and reduced misapplication (Duong & Han, 2021; He et al., 2019), but often at the risk of bureaucratic cost.
  - **Exclusion or hybrid mechanisms** (switching between punishment and exclusion or reward): These mechanisms may outperform pure punishment in both sustaining cooperation and supporting efficiency (Liu et al., 2018, 2019; Yang et al., 2018).

## Contradictions and Ambiguities

- While some models and empirical studies support efficiency gains from punishment, *a substantial subset* show **no gain or even a reduction in efficiency** due to wasted resources on punishment and punishment-based feuding (Li et al., 2018; Gross & De Dreu, 2019).
- Many studies attribute these null or negative effects to "wasteful" or antisocial punishment, uncoordinated retaliation, or high fixed costs of sanctioning.

# Prediction Guidance

Given the evidence:

- **Strongest guidance is for PGGs with modeled/theorized payoff outcomes**: The efficiency effect of enabling punishment is positive *only* when punishment is sufficiently low-cost, highly effective against defectors, and antisocial punishment is minimal (Duong & Han, 2021; Liu et al., 2018, 2019). In these cases, theoretical formulas can be used to estimate expected efficiency from design dimensions plus baseline (control) efficiency.
- **If punishment is costly, misapplied, or mutual feuding occurs**, efficiency often does *not* increase and may decrease, even if cooperation frequency rises (Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018).
- **Quantitative prediction using only control efficiency and game design is limited** by ambiguity on antisocial punishment, context (e.g., partner competition), and whether institutional or normative constraints prevent destructive use. Adjustments must be made for the presence of such moderators.
- **Reward mechanisms, when present, often outperform punishment** in efficiency, and hybrid strategies can optimize outcomes by switching between reward and punishment as cooperation becomes established (Yang et al., 2018; Góis et al., 2019).

In short: **Predictions should incorporate parameters for punishment targeting, effectiveness/cost ratio, and presence of antisocial retaliation or feuding**, in addition to standard design dimensions and control efficiency. Where these are uncertain, point estimates for efficiency gains from punishment are unreliable.

# Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed by the literature:**
- `player_count` (group size): Regularly addressed and modeled (Duong & Han, 2021; Liu et al., 2018, 2019).
- `num_rounds`: Key in studies of repeated games and evolutionary settings.
- `all_or_nothing`, `mpcr`: Integral to most theoretical and empirical designs.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Central to all theory and many empirical studies; strong evidence for their moderating effects.
- `reward_exists`, sometimes `reward_cost` and `reward_tech`: Well represented when both punishment and rewards are studied.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Intermittently reported, sometimes affecting outcomes via information and transparency.

**Indirectly informed or contextually discussed:**
- `chat`: Occasionally referenced as a factor influencing cooperation, but not systematically analyzed for its interaction with punishment and efficiency.
- `default_contrib`: Framing effects mentioned in some cases, but not a primary focus in efficiency outcome studies.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, and especially `show_punishment_id` (punisher identity/feedback): Sometimes mentioned as impacting antisocial punishment or norm enforcement, but evidence is limited and mostly indirect.

**Sparse or effectively missing:**
- Systematic, empirical data on the interaction between many of the 14 design dimensions, especially regarding transparency (`show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`), communication (`chat`), and initial framing (`default_contrib`).
- Clear empirical data mapping *all* dimensions simultaneously to efficiency outcomes; most studies manipulate only a subset, leaving many interactions underspecified.

# Important Limitations

- **Scarcity of direct, empirical payoff data:** Most studies do not measure "efficiency" (normalized group payoff) but instead focus on behavioral proxies, making quantitative prediction of efficiency effects from punishment difficult.
- **Limited factorial manipulation:** Most evidence varies only a few design dimensions at a time, preventing robust inference across the full prediction space.
- **Confounds from context and mechanism:** Efficiency impact depends on unmeasured moderators—antisocial punishment, norm strength, institutional constraints, partner choice context—that are typically not captured directly by the 14 prediction dimensions.
- **Ambiguity in generalizability:** Theory results often assume infinite, well-mixed populations, or deterministic evolutionary processes, which may diverge from finite lab or real-world group settings.
- **Reward and hybrid mechanisms:** Studies increasingly highlight the superiority of reward or hybrid mechanisms, but direct, empirical head-to-head comparisons with punishment (on efficiency) in PGGs are rare.
- **Cultural and ecological heterogeneity:** Several reviews warn that punishment effects vary with societal norms, resource dynamics, and group composition—constructs often missing from typical game design parameterizations.
- **Reporting bias and experimental demand:** Some evidence suggests that observed rates of punishment (especially third-party) may be inflated in lab studies, and that not all settings outside the lab generalize.

---

**In summary:**  
While the literature base models and sometimes empirically quantifies how punishment affects efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, only a handful of sources directly report the relevant payoff-based outcomes under controlled variation of key design dimensions. Strong, generalizable prediction of efficiency gains from punishment, given standard design parameters and control efficiency, is therefore limited. Prediction should be qualified by recognizing key boundary conditions (punishment cost-effectiveness, institutional/antisocial targeting, and context dependencies) identified across both theory and empirical literatures.
