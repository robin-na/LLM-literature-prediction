# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises a large and diverse mix of **empirical experimental studies**—mostly lab-based, with a minority of field experiments—as well as a significant number of **theoretical and conceptual papers**. The empirical studies extensively cover repeated linear public goods games (PGG) with variations in group size, number of rounds, punishment institution, and additional design features. Several studies use close variants of PGGs such as common-pool resource (CPR) games or principal-agent games. A smaller subset of papers uses adjacent or non-PGG environments (e.g., trust games, dictator games), often to study related mechanisms.

**Relevance for the prediction task is high**: Many papers report on efficiency or closely related payoff-based outcomes in games with and without punishment, using standard PGG parameters. There is, however, considerable heterogeneity in punishment implementation, reporting (whether payoff outcomes or just contribution rates), and moderators such as communication, group heterogeneity, and institutional detail.

**Overall, the evidence base is strong, broad, and empirically rich for the focal prediction task**, though some key design dimensions are less systematically explored, and a subset of papers provides only conceptual or indirect evidence.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact:** The majority of empirical studies use standard linear PGG or very close variants (repeated VCM, framed field PGG, etc.).
- **close:** Some studies use closely related CPR or principal-agent games (e.g., Casari & Plott, Ostrom, Rigdon).
- **adjacent/none:** A minority use trust/dictator games, exclusion/ostracism games, or theoretical models abstracted from PGG.

**b) punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact:** Many studies provide direct manipulation of peer punishment (costly, voluntary, observable/unobservable, etc.).
- **close/adjacent:** Several explore alternative sanctions (ostracism, exclusion, social disapproval, rewards, monitoring) or non-material punishment.
- **none:** A few only discuss punishment theoretically or contextualize its role.

**c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact/close:** A large proportion measure group efficiency, total group payoff, or surplus, making them directly useful for prediction.
- **adjacent/weak/none:** Some report only behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates, norm compliance), with payoff implications inferred but not measured.

**Conclusion:** Most studies are of **exact or close relevance** to the three target dimensions, especially regarding typical lab PGGs with and without punishment and explicit measurement of efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes:**  
  - **Directly Measured:** Many key studies report group efficiency (total earnings as a % of social optimum) or related measures (group payoff, welfare), explicitly connecting punishment design with payoff outcomes (e.g., Sefton et al., Noussair & Tucker, Arechar et al., Lopez et al., Kroll et al., Casari & Plott).
  - **Related Payoff Measures:** Some report group or individual earnings, surplus, or welfare, which are convertible to efficiency metrics.
  - **Less Common:** A subset provide only indirect evidence: estimates can be inferred from group contributions and MPCR but are not always directly reported.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  - **Common:** Many studies report contribution or cooperation rates, punishment frequency, norm compliance, feuding, or emotional consequences as the main outcomes.
  - **Caution:** While strongly correlated with efficiency, these do not always imply, nor can they substitute for, direct payoff evidence.

**Distinction Maintained:** In this literature, contribution increases from punishment do not automatically translate into efficiency gains, due to the cost of assigning/receiving punishment. Several studies highlight this tradeoff or ambiguity.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Synthesis:**

- **Punishment usually boosts contributions, but the efficiency effect is heterogeneous**:
  - In **standard PGG designs** (4–5 players, 10–20 rounds, no chat, known MPCR, anonymous), **enabling peer punishment generally increases efficiency above the control (no-punishment), after the initial learning or punishment-cost phase** (Sefton et al., 2007; Arechar et al., 2018; Noussair & Tucker, 2005; Kroll et al., 2007).
  - **The net gain depends critically on the cost-effectiveness of punishment**: If the punishment cost is too high relative to its impact (low punishment_tech), increased contributions are offset by high punishment-related losses and efficiency may not rise (Bochet et al., 2006; Tan, 2008; Casari & Luini, 2009).
  - **The structure of the punishment institution is crucial**:  
    - When mechanisms prevent anti-social punishment (punishing high contributors), filter punishment through consensus, or focus sanctioning efficiently (e.g., consensual institutions or ostracism), the efficiency gains are *much higher* (Casari & Luini, 2009; Maier-Rigaud et al., 2010).
    - In contrast, when punishment is unfiltered, perverse punishment can reduce or even reverse efficiency gains (Bochet et al., 2006; Nikiforakis & Engelmann, 2011).
  - **Communication is a powerful moderator**:
    - Rich communication (free-form chat, face-to-face) can, by itself, raise efficiency as much or more than punishment; in these settings, adding punishment yields little extra benefit (Bochet et al., 2006; Oprea et al., 2014; Charness & Yang, 2014).  
    - Punishment in the absence of communication is less effective, and in games where communication is enabled, it is often redundant.
  - **Punishment effectiveness and efficiency gains decline with higher group heterogeneity**:
    - In homogeneous groups and those differing in capability (not in valuation), punishment is most effective (Kölle, 2015; Tan, 2008).  
    - When valuation heterogeneity is present, punishment may not increase efficiency and can worsen inequality and group outcomes.
  - **Counter-punishment (feuding) undermines efficiency gains from punishment**:
    - If the institution allows for revenge or repeated cycles of punishment (e.g., fixed IDs, multi-stage punishment), efficiency falls and sometimes drops below control levels (Nikiforakis & Engelmann, 2011).
  - **Other moderators**:  
    - Asymmetry in punishment power (one player with higher impact/cost) does not affect efficiency, provided average effectiveness is held constant (Nikiforakis et al., 2010).
    - Intermediaries (indirect punishment channels) substantially reduce punishment’s positive effect (Coffman, 2011).
    - Endogenously chosen punishment institutions sometimes yield higher efficiency, but also carry risk of failure to implement (Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020).
    - Social sanctions (shame, social disapproval) can be more efficient than monetary punishment under some circumstances, especially where monetary fines are high (Lopez et al., 2012; Carpenter & Seki, 2011).

**Theoretical/Mechanism Arguments:**
- Effective and not-too-costly punishment stabilizes cooperation and supports efficient equilibria (Sethi & Somanathan, 2003; Ostrom, 2006).
- The evolution and stability of efficient cooperation via punishment are sensitive to the cost, observability, and institutional detail; the stability is not guaranteed (Sethi & Somanathan, 2003; Sylwester et al., 2013).
- Cultural and institutional factors can heavily moderate effects, especially via anti-social punishment or norm adherence.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based on aggregate literature:

- **When games match the canonical lab PGG dimensions** (4–5 players, 10–20 rounds, no chat, known round number, continuous contributions, MPCR=0.4–0.5, no identity disclosure, standard peer punishment, moderate cost-to-impact ratio):  
  - **Expect a positive effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency relative to the same control game.**
  - However, **the effect is moderate in magnitude** (e.g., increases from ~40–50% to ~60–70% efficiency; see Sefton et al., Arechar et al., Casari & Plott, Ostrom).
  - The increase is smaller or delayed if the cost of punishment is high, anti-social punishment is prevalent, feuds are possible, or group heterogeneity is based on valuation rather than capability.

- **Dimension-moderated adjustments:**
  - **Punishment Cost/Punishment Tech:** The effect on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost-effectiveness of punishment. Lower cost or higher impact per unit amplifies efficiency gains; higher cost can offset or even outweigh contribution increases.
  - **Communication (chat):** If chat or rich communication is enabled, marginal benefits from enabling punishment are small or zero, as communication itself nearly closes the efficiency gap.
  - **Information about identities/behavior (show_punishment_id, show_other_summaries):** Full anonymity supports positive punishment effects; revealing identities can increase the risk of anti-social punishment/feuding, reducing efficiency.
  - **Player Count & MPCR:** Higher MPCR and smaller groups slightly amplify the effect; larger groups and low MPCR diminish it.
  - **Game heterogeneity:** Efficiency gains are limited if group members have highly different valuations or when anti-social punishment is not filtered.

- **Control efficiency is predictive**, but must be adjusted with institutional and design moderators. For games with low control efficiency (typical in baseline PGG), enable punishment can yield substantial but not universal improvements.

- **Where design features go outside the standard range** (e.g., presence of intermediaries, highly costly punishment, feuding, highly heterogeneous groups, communication enabled), **predict efficiency gains from punishment to be negligible or negative**.

- **Reward/Anticipatory Social Incentives:** These generally have weaker or less persistent effects than punishment, except in special cases with strong social norms.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count**, **num_rounds**: Varied systematically, with most studies using 3–5 players and 10–20 rounds. Standard values well supported.
- **mpcr**: Explicitly manipulated and analyzed in many studies (e.g., Sefton et al., Carpenter et al.).
- **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech**: Directly informed by multiple studies; their values are critical moderators of the punishment effect on efficiency.
- **chat**: The presence or absence of chat/communication is a major focus and strong moderator in several papers.
- **all_or_nothing / continuous**: Both modalities represented, with most lab games using continuous contributions.
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds**: Common, and their presence is usually specified.
- **reward_exists**: Some studies explicitly test the effect of rewards; evidence shows rewards are generally weak moderators compared to punishment.

**Indirectly or Only Contextually Informed Dimensions:**
- **show_punishment_id**: Less systematically explored; a few studies look at anonymity vs. known identities, with indirect implications for feuding/anti-social punishment.
- **default_contrib**: Not directly manipulated in most studies (only contextually discussed).
- **reward_cost, reward_tech**: Few studies systematically vary reward design; evidence is notably weaker/more indirect.

**Effectively Missing/Sparse:**
- **punishment_exists**: Used as a treatment/control toggle in most studies but not systematically cross-analysed as a dimension itself.
- Some studies do not specify all dimensions, limiting parameterized prediction.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Anti-social Punishment/Feuding Risk:** Many studies warn that if institutions do not prevent anti-social punishment or allow feuding, punishment can reduce efficiency below control. These effects are not always predictable from baseline efficiency or simple design parameters.
- **Heterogeneity in Punishment Institutions:** The effect of punishment is highly sensitive to the fine details of institutional design (e.g., consensus or individual, filtering mechanisms, possibility of counter-punishment), which are not always codified in design dimensions.
- **Payoff Outcomes Not Always Reported:** A significant fraction of studies report only non-payoff behavioral outcomes.
- **Limited Variation on Certain Dimensions:** Some design variables (default_contrib, identity visibility, certain types of reward) are underexplored; findings are less robust or missing for these.
- **Extrapolation to Complex/Field Environments:** Results from standard lab games may not extrapolate to field environments with strong social norms, complex group structure, or high baseline efficiency.
- **Communication as a Confound:** High effectiveness of communication in increasing efficiency means that in prediction, its presence overshadows marginal punishment effects, making their interaction hard to separate.

**In summary**:  
The literature is robust for standard lab public goods games, supports confident qualitative prediction (direction and major moderating factors), but lacks the full parameter-identification for all design dimensions, and is especially sensitive to institutional details and social context that may not be captured in the basic 14 dimensions. Quantitative prediction should account for these uncertainties and be conservative in out-of-sample game types.
