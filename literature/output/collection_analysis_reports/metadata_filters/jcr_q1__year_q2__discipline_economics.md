# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set comprises 47 papers, with a strong empirical core—primarily lab and field experiments complemented by several theoretical contributions. The empirical studies focus heavily on classic and variant public goods games (PGGs), often manipulating or enabling punishment institutions, and frequently measuring or reporting efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes. The theoretical work largely analyzes efficiency in repeated PGG frameworks with punishment or institutional enforcement, often offering formal bounds or comparative statics for key design dimensions.

Coverage is broad and deep for standard PGGs with peer (informal) and formal (centralized) punishment, with additional evidence for adjacent game types (common-pool resource games, trust games, threshold public goods, partnership games, etc.) involving punishment-like mechanisms. The vast majority of the literature is experimental, with direct measurement of group efficiency or payoff in both baseline and punishment-enabled conditions. However, a notable fraction of papers—especially in field or adjacent contexts—report only behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates, norm compliance) rather than efficiency per se.

In sum, the evidence base is sufficiently broad and empirically grounded to support synthesis for prediction in PGG-like environments, but is less comprehensive in rare institutional variants (e.g., with ostracism, dynamic endowments, or strongly heterogeneous groups).

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact:** Most core empirical and theoretical studies (e.g., Dickinson et al., 2015; Nicklisch et al., 2016; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016; Markussen et al., 2016; Kube et al., 2015; Dai et al., 2015) focus directly on standard or slightly modified linear PGGs.
- **Close:** Several studies extend to common-pool resource games, threshold PGGs, or related collective-action settings (e.g., Freytag et al., 2014; Gatiso et al., 2015; Vollan et al., 2013; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015).
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Some papers (e.g., trust games, partnership, dynamic public goods) contribute adjacent evidence on punishment mechanisms or efficiency, but less directly inform classic PGG prediction.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact:** Substantial subset includes explicit manipulation of peer punishment, centralized/formal sanctions, or exogenous punishment variations (e.g., Dickinson et al., 2015; Markussen et al., 2016; Kube et al., 2015).
- **Close:** Some studies consider reward as a foil to punishment or examine hybrid/alternative institutions (e.g., Dai et al., 2015; Gangadharan et al., 2017).
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Remaining literature explores informal punishment-like exclusion, rule-based restrictions, or meta-norms (e.g., Riedl et al., 2016; Mathew, 2017), which may be less directly actionable for classic peer-punishment design.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact:** A large portion measures efficiency (group payoff relative to potential max), total earnings, or group welfare directly (e.g., Dickinson et al., 2015; Markussen et al., 2016; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Others report group payoffs or surrogate metrics where efficiency can be inferred but is not explicitly presented as a ratio (e.g., Dal Bó & Dal Bó, 2014).
- **Weak/None:** Several papers focus solely on behavioral metrics (contributions, punishment assigned) or examine payoff implications primarily through theoretical argument.

Overall, the literature is highly relevant and direct for the prediction task, especially regarding standard PGGs with manipulation of punishment and with explicit efficiency metrics, but there is greater ambiguity and indirectness for adjacent or institutionally complex environments.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**
- **Directly Measured:** Many studies report efficiency as defined (group total payoff / maximum possible), group earnings, welfare, or similar aggregate monetary outcomes (Dickinson et al., 2015; Markussen et al., 2016; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016; Dai et al., 2015; Kube et al., 2015; Cason & Gangadharan, 2013).
- **Indirectly Measured:** Some provide group payoffs or welfare but not the efficiency ratio, requiring inference (Dal Bó & Dal Bó, 2014; Nicklisch et al., 2016).
- **Theoretical Payoff/Bounded Outcomes:** Theoretical papers specify mathematical efficiency bounds or explicit welfare given strategies and design (Wolitzky, 2013; Ali & Miller, 2016).

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- **Contributions/Cooperation Rates:** Frequently reported, often as a proxy for efficiency, but not equivalent (Weng & Carlsson, 2015; Gürerk et al., 2014; Vollan et al., 2017).
- **Punishment Frequency/Type:** Some studies measure how often, how much, or what type of punishment is assigned, focusing on the mechanism (Ouss & Peysakhovich, 2015; Dickinson & Masclet, 2015).
- **Norm Compliance, Trust, Discrimination:** Other studies focus on the prevalence and stability of social behavior (Gneezy et al., 2016; Angerer et al., 2016).

**Distinction:**  
Prediction should be based only on the direct or inferable payoff-based outcomes; behavioral results should be interpreted as supporting or moderating evidence, not as efficiency itself.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**1. Enabling punishment in standard PGGs (exact relevance) usually increases efficiency, but not always.**  
- **Positive effect:** Across canonical experiments with well-calibrated punishment (peer or formal), group efficiency rises sharply compared to no-punishment controls, particularly when punishment is sufficiently deterrent and targeted at free riders. Gains can approach 90–100% of possible earnings (Dickinson et al., 2015; Markussen et al., 2016; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016; Kube et al., 2015).
- **Moderation:**  
  - The efficiency gain is larger when the punishment regime is chosen by group vote (endogenous) rather than imposed (exogenous).
  - Peer punishment is sometimes more efficient than formal sanctions, particularly when it is cost-effective and well-targeted (Markussen et al., 2016).
  - Emotional context matters: venting/cooling-off before punishment can reduce excessive, inefficient punishment, maximizing efficiency (Dickinson & Masclet, 2015).

**2. Institutional and informational design are strong moderators.**  
- **Information accuracy:** When information about others' contributions is noisy, punishment may be misapplied to cooperators, reducing or eliminating efficiency gains (Nicklisch et al., 2016; Markussen et al., 2016).
- **Judicial error:** Type I errors (punishing cooperators) are particularly efficiency-destroying. The efficiency benefit of punishment disappears or reverses as error probability rises above moderate levels (Markussen et al., 2016).
- **Audit and centralization:** Intermittent, ambiguous audits with high-severity sanctions can be more efficient (for similar expected cost) than frequent low-severity audits. Centrally administered punishment can match or exceed peer punishment under moderate noise, provided false positives are rare (Dai et al., 2015).

**3. Reward, communication, and group composition also matter.**  
- **Reward vs. Punishment:** Rewards alone do not always deliver the same efficiency gains as punishment; in some settings, rewards are less or equally effective, and sometimes combining both is most efficient (Dickinson et al., 2015; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016).
- **Communication/Chat:** Communication robustly increases efficiency, sometimes more consistently than punishment (Noussair & van Soest, 2014), but with diminishing effects as group size increases.
- **Heterogeneity:** Efficiency gains from punishment or rewards are smaller and less reliable in heterogeneous groups with conflicting normative priorities (Gangadharan et al., 2017).
- **Game framing/action space:** If the game allows players to undo others’ contributions (claim/taking games), baseline efficiency is low, and punishment may not help (van Soest et al., 2016; Dekel et al., 2017).

**4. Contexts where punishment does not increase efficiency or is counterproductive:**  
- **Harmed minorities:** With negative externalities for some players, punishment use can become misdirected (toward contributors), reducing efficiency (Dekel et al., 2017).
- **Dynamic games/endogenous inequality:** In dynamic PGGs, especially as inequality emerges, antisocial punishment increases, and efficiency can drop compared to control (Gächter et al., 2017).
- **Weak institutional control:** If punishment is group-funded, probability of apprehension is low, or multiple punishers can “over-punish,” the efficiency gain is reduced or negative (Ouss & Peysakhovich, 2015).

**5. Adjacent and context-specific findings:**  
- Non-PGG environments (coalition games, trust games, field resource dilemmas) often show efficiency gains from well-targeted, deterrent punishment but are less directly informative for standard PGG predictions.  
- Punishment effectiveness is frequently contingent on cultural context, institutional legitimacy, and the method of imposition (Vollan et al., 2017; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Rule (for standard PGGs):**  
- Given control game efficiency and the 14 design dimensions, enabling peer punishment is expected to _increase_ efficiency unless:
  - Punishment is expensive relative to its impact
  - Information is noisy, leading to errors (especially punishing cooperators)
  - Contribution heterogeneity or harmed minorities are present
  - Dynamic endowments or early inequalities foster antisocial punishment
  - Institutional design enables over-punishment or group-funded punishment costs

**Specific Design Moderators (with empirical and theoretical support):**
- **player_count:** Moderate-sized groups (3–5) with stable membership yield robust efficiency gains from punishment. Gains diminish or become ambiguous in very large or small groups.
- **num_rounds:** Sufficiently many rounds are required for reputational/reciprocal punishment to work; efficiency gains may subside if game is too short (or, in dynamic games, too long with persistent inequality).
- **chat:** Communication typically increases efficiency, and can substitute for or amplify punishment’s effectiveness.
- **all_or_nothing:** Evidence is primarily from continuous-contribution games; effects in binary (all-or-nothing) games are less explored.
- **default_contrib:** Very little direct evidence; framing may have modest but less critical effects.
- **mpcr:** A higher MPCR (return per capita) makes punishment more effective at raising efficiency, as the marginal benefit from increased cooperation is greater.
- **punishment_cost/punishment_tech:**  
  - Lower cost and greater impact (punishment effectiveness) increase efficiency gains.
  - High error rates or insufficient deterrence make punishment less effective or even damaging.
  - Centralization is not always superior: peer punishment can outperform formal sanctions if well-calibrated.
- **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech:** Rewards alone are less consistently effective; combining with punishment sometimes helps, but not universally.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Feedback and transparency (showing others’ outcomes, identities) may increase targeting accuracy and efficiency gains, but can backfire if it fosters retaliation or shame-based antisocial punishment.

**Control Efficiency as a Moderator:**  
- The lower the control (no-punishment) efficiency, the greater the expected marginal gain from enabling punishment—provided above moderating conditions are favorable.

**Heterogeneity and cultural context:**  
- Heterogeneous returns or values decrease punishment’s efficacy; cultural norms around authority, legitimacy, and meta-norms (punishing the non-punishers) moderate outcomes.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed by empirical comparison in multiple studies:**
- **player_count:** Heavily manipulated and analyzed; group size effects are well characterized.
- **num_rounds:** Direct focus in repeated vs. one-shot games.
- **chat:** Several studies examine the impact of communication.
- **mpcr:** Standard manipulation; effects reported on efficiency and marginal return.
- **punishment_cost and punishment_tech:** Central moderators in nearly all core punishment studies.
- **reward_exists:** Some studies compare rewards to punishment and/or hybrid designs.

**Indirectly or contextually discussed:**
- **all_or_nothing:** Present in some designs; most core evidence is for continuous games. All-or-nothing PGGs are less directly addressed.
- **default_contrib:** Framing (opt-in/opt-out) is rarely manipulated or reported.
- **reward_cost, reward_tech, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Occasionally discussed or present as background conditions; limited explicit evidence on their moderation of efficacy.

**Effectively missing or underutilized in this literature set:**
- **Dynamic action spaces, asymmetry, ostracism, and complex punishment escalation (e.g., ostracism with/without forgiveness)**
- **Detailed exploration of framing, such as default contribution modes; these are very rarely the central dimension.**
- **Formal manipulations of some information features (e.g., show_punishment_id) are sporadic or indirect.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Over-representation of lab, Western-context games:** Field evidence and cross-cultural replications are limited, and some field results (particularly in resource and community management) highlight divergent or even reversed patterns compared to lab PGGs.
- **Ambiguity in adjacent or complex environments:** Results are much less consistent for heterogeneous groups, games with negative externalities/minorities, dynamic or endogenous endowment settings, and systems with overlapping punishment and reward opportunities.
- **Non-uniform reporting of efficiency:** In many adjacent or field studies, only contribution or behavioral outcomes are measured, requiring inference (and sometimes over-interpretation) to estimate efficiency effects.
- **Punishment error and anti-social punishment:** Efficiency gains evaporate or reverse when punishment institutions permit error or antagonistic targeting, but clear guidance on predicting these risks from surface-level design dimensions is less developed.
- **Sparse evidence for rarely manipulated dimensions:** Effects of default contribution framing, show_punishment_id, or fine-grained reward/punishment technology choices are under-explored.
- **Potential publication bias:** Null or negative results (punishment failing to increase efficiency or reducing it) may be under-reported relative to positive findings, though several negative and mixed results are present.
- **Interdependence with control condition:** The effect of enabling punishment can substantially depend on baseline group dynamics and efficiency; accurate prediction requires high-quality baseline data as input.
- **Limited temporal/generality scope:** Most studies examine short-run or fixed-duration interactions; long-term or real-world institutional evolution is less well represented.

---

**In summary, this literature robustly supports the prediction that enabling peer punishment in standard PGGs generally increases efficiency over baseline, but with important caveats related to error, social context, group heterogeneity, punishment cost/impact, and information structure. Prediction models should condition efficiency estimates on these moderators, rely primarily on payoff outcomes, and exercise caution in extending to non-canonical or highly institutionalized environments.**
