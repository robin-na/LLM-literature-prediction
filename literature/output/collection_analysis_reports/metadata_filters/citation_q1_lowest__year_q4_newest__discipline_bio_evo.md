# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is broad and multidisciplinary, encompassing 32 papers that cover experimental (lab) studies and a wide range of theoretical and computational models. About a third of the papers offer direct empirical or theoretical evidence regarding efficiency impacts of punishment mechanisms in public goods games (PGGs) or their close variants. Many others discuss adjacent environments (e.g., n-person Prisoner's Dilemma, indirect reciprocity, partner choice) or focus on behavioral outcomes such as cooperation rates, norm compliance, and trust.

- Empirical coverage: Several lab experiments directly examine treatment efficiency or group payoff in PGGs with/without punishment (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Nhim et al., 2023). Some examine closely related mechanisms but do not report efficiency.
- Theoretical/modeling coverage: Multiple papers use analytic or simulation-based models to uncover parameter dependencies and mechanisms (e.g., Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Powers et al., 2023; Cooney, 2025).
- Breadth: The scope includes standard PGGs, institutional punishment, indirect and direct peer punishment, costless sanctions, partner choice, and other collective action dilemmas. Some works explore extended or hybrid incentive schemes (reward, walk-away, group extinction, etc.).
- Depth: Evidence on the predicted relationship between enabling punishment and group efficiency as a function of design dimensions is strongest for standard PGGs with explicit, costly punishment, and more ambiguous (or missing) for peer punishment variants, institutional complexity, and games with multiple overlapping mechanisms.

# 2) Task Relevance

A summary assessment for three target-relevance dimensions:

**a. PGG_or_variant**
- *Exact*: Approximately half the papers explicitly model or experiment with standard public goods games or direct n-person Prisoner's Dilemma structures (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
- *Close*: Several analyze variants such as “others-only” PGGs, threshold games, or public bads; these still inform PGG-like efficiency outcomes (e.g., Del Ponte et al., 2025).
- *Adjacent or Weak*: Many papers focus on repeated dyadic games, collective risk, indirect reciprocity, or n-person environments lacking core PGG features (e.g., reciprocity models, partner choice) or are primarily concerned with dictator games or trust games, which offer only background context.

**b. Punishment_or_sanctions**
- *Exact*: Direct empirical and theoretical studies examine costly punishment, institutional (centralized) punishment, and strict enforcement (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Nhim et al., 2023).
- *Close*: Some address reward or hybrid incentive schemes, reputation-based or peer-based sanctions, and alternative mechanisms (partner switching, group-level extinction), offering relevant but not equivalent treatments of punishment (e.g., Kroumi, 2025; Duong et al., 2024).
- *Adjacent/Weak*: A notable share considers only non-costly or indirectly punitive mechanisms, emotion-based or norm-based compliance, or discusses punishment as a background concept.

**c. Efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**
- *Exact*: A subset of experimental and theoretical papers directly measure or mathematically define efficiency as group payoff relative to the social optimum (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Cooney, 2025).
- *Close*: Other works discuss group payoff, welfare, or surplus as primary outcomes, providing strong proxies for efficiency (e.g., Nhim et al., 2023).
- *Adjacent/Weak*: Many papers report only on contribution rates, cooperation rates, punishment frequency, or trust behaviors—behavioral outcomes that do not always translate simply into efficiency changes and must be distinguished accordingly (e.g., Kido & Takezawa, 2024; Odouard et al., 2023).

**Summary:** The literature directly addresses the prediction task in a minority of papers; many others provide contextual or indirect insights, with efficiency as the measured outcome much less common than behavioral proxies.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes** (directly relevant for efficiency prediction):
- **Group efficiency/group payoff**: Several empirical and theoretical studies report group payoff as the proportion of maximum possible, i.e., efficiency (Sparks et al., 2024; Nhim et al., 2023; Cooney, 2025).
- **Welfare/surplus**: Occasionally used interchangeably with efficiency, especially in modeling papers.
- **Total earnings/coins**: Raw group output, which can be normalized to efficiency.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (must not substitute for efficiency without caution):
- **Contribution rate / cooperation rate**: Widely reported. High contribution rates generally correlate with higher efficiency, but not necessarily (costs of punishment reduce payoff; e.g., Nhim et al., 2023).
- **Punishment frequency/amount assigned**: Used to characterize sanctioning but not directly linked to group welfare.
- **Norm compliance, trust, reputation, emotion expression**: Outcome measures in several studies, relevant for behavior mechanisms but not directly for efficiency.

**Ambiguity:** Some papers infer higher efficiency from increased cooperation but do not calculate or report group payoff (Kido & Takezawa, 2024; Graser et al., 2025).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## a. **Punishment can raise or lower efficiency depending on costs, parameters, and implementation:**
- **Costly punishment generally increases efficiency when:**
    - Punishment cost is not too high relative to fines (e.g., Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
    - The punishment mechanism is well-calibrated to deter defection with minimal execution (Powers et al., 2023; Murase, 2025).
    - The structure enables coordination to critical mass of punishers, especially in long games (Sparks et al., 2024).
    - Peer punishment is structured so that antisocial punishment is suppressed and defectors are correctly targeted (Sparks et al., 2024; Cooney, 2025).
- **Punishment can reduce efficiency when:**
    - Costs are high, and the increased cooperation does not compensate for the loss from paying punishment costs (Nhim et al., 2023; Cooney, 2025; Kurokawa, 2023).
    - The group is small, or punishment-induced spite is possible (Kurokawa, 2023).
    - Sanctioning is voluntary and easily avoided (Del Ponte et al., 2025).
    - There is miscalibration—over- or under-punishing, execution errors, or misperception of defection (Murase, 2025).

## b. **Contextual moderators:**
- **Group size (player_count):** Larger groups require lower per-punisher costs or institutional arrangements. The effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is often non-linear with group size (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Powers et al., 2023; Kurokawa, 2023).
- **Duration (num_rounds):** Longer games allow the positive effects of punishment to accumulate, especially in lab experiments (Sparks et al., 2024).
- **Mechanism details matter:** Institutional punishment, peer punishment, automatic strict enforcement, and reward/tax-based regimes have distinct efficiency outcomes (Nhim et al., 2023; Duong et al., 2024).
- **Baseline (control) efficiency is predictive:** If baseline efficiency is high, punishment may add little or even decrease efficiency. If baseline is low, and parameters/frequencies are favorable, punishment can cause a dramatic gain (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Sparks et al., 2024).

## c. **Non-payoff outcomes inform indirectly:**
- Higher cooperation/contribution rates usually imply higher efficiency, but only if punishment costs don’t outweigh cooperation gains (Kido & Takezawa, 2024; Odouard et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2025).

## d. **Boundary conditions and caveats:**
- Voluntary and avoidable punishment has negligible or adverse effects on efficiency (Del Ponte et al., 2025).
- Norms, conformity, and sanctioning can reinforce each other; norm internalizers magnify cooperation gains but do not on their own guarantee efficiency boosts (Odouard et al., 2023).
- In some adjacent environments, group-level sanctions, selective extinction, or partner choice produce efficiency gains functionally similar to punishment, but are not directly comparable.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How the literature should inform prediction of treatment efficiency from design dimensions plus control efficiency:**

- **Magnitude and direction:** Expect that enabling peer or institutional punishment will increase efficiency if (a) punishment costs are moderate/low relative to benefits, (b) design ensures defectors are targeted and anti-social punishment is minimized, and (c) control efficiency is substantially below optimum. Efficiency gains accumulate more strongly in long games and in medium-sized groups.
- **Conditional on design:** If punishment is costly, high-frequency, and misaligned (e.g., imposed when not needed, high execution errors), it can reduce efficiency compared to control—especially in small groups or when punishment is not institutionalized (Nhim et al., 2023; Kurokawa, 2023).
- **Critical dimensions:** For prediction, pay special attention to: player_count (group size), num_rounds, punishment_cost, mpcr, and the type of punishment (punishment_tech). Show caution in extrapolating to all-or-nothing games, public bads, cases with only weak or voluntary punishment, and designs allowing evasion.
- **Baseline dependence:** Given a control efficiency, literature suggests treatment efficiency with punishment depends on both the shift in contribution/cooperation (adjusted for cost) and on the sufficiency/coverage of punishment among the group (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Sparks et al., 2024).
- **Indirect findings:** If only cooperation rates are reported, cautiously estimate efficiency gains by considering expected costs of punishment as a drag on group payoff (Nhim et al., 2023).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**
- **player_count**: Explicitly analyzed in most empirical/theoretical efficiency papers.
- **num_rounds**: Centerpiece in lab experiments and theoretical models of repeated interaction.
- **mpcr**: Modeled as benefit-to-cost ratio, direct moderator of cooperation and effect of punishment.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Central to theoretical and experimental exploration of efficiency impacts.
- **all_or_nothing**: Discussed in several studies modeling threshold or binary games.
- **chat**: Operationalized as a control variable or treatment in some empirical studies (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Reward/tax regimes and hybrid incentive designs scrutinized in a handful of theoretical and experimental works (Duong et al., 2024; Nhim et al., 2023).

**Indirectly informed:**
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds**: Sometimes manipulated, mostly for norm salience or framing, less often as determinants of efficiency.
- **default_contrib**: Contextually present in some designs (Del Ponte et al., 2025), but not focal in efficiency analyses.
- **show_punishment_id**: Mentioned as affecting psychological/behavioral dynamics, but not modeled for efficiency impact.

**Effectively missing or minimally addressed:**
- The interaction of many dimensions (e.g., chat × punishment_cost, reward × punishment) is only sparsely treated; rich combinatorial spaces are rarely fully covered.
- Direct consideration of opt-in vs. opt-out default framing is rare.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Payoff vs. behavior:** Many papers report only contribution or cooperation rates, not efficiency, making quantitative prediction for efficiency shifts when enabling punishment challenging in those contexts. Punishment often shifts behavior, but the net impact on payoff depends on costs and design.
- **Generalizability:** Some findings are highly parametric (valid only for the specific group size, rounds, or game type considered); results in institutionalized punishment contexts may not generalize to peer punishment or “all-or-nothing” PGGs.
- **Boundary conditions and exceptions:** Some studies find null or negative effects of punishment on efficiency, especially when (a) punishment is costly and frequent, (b) players can avoid participation, or (c) groups are small (Nhim et al., 2023; Del Ponte et al., 2025; Kurokawa, 2023).
- **Missing dimensions:** Not all 14 design dimensions are systematically varied or analyzed; evidence is strongest for player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, and punishment_cost/tech. Other variables (e.g., chat, default_contrib, show_punishment_id) receive limited direct analysis for efficiency outcomes.
- **Confounding mechanisms:** Mechanistically different forms of “punishment” (e.g., walk-away, group extinction, reputation-based sanctions) may not translate directly into the same efficiency effects as peer or institutional punishment.
- **Lack of field evidence and ecological external validity:** Most findings are from lab or theoretical contexts, with less clarity about real-world institutional complexity and heterogeneity.

---

**In summary:** The literature base directly addresses the core prediction task (treatment efficiency as a function of design and control efficiency) in a minority of cases, but provides robust mechanism insights and boundary conditions. Prediction should be dimension-aware and cautious, incorporating both the possibility of positive efficiency impacts of punishment (especially under favorable cost/benefit and game structure parameters) and the real possibility of efficiency loss if punishment mechanisms are unclearly designed, highly costly, or easily evaded. Pay special attention to the relevance and directness of the outcome measures reported when using papers to inform prediction.
