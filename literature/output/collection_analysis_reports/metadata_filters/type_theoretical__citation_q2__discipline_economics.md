# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists exclusively of **theoretical papers** (40 in total), with no original empirical or experimental evidence presented. Theoretical models span canonical public goods games (PGGs), close variants (e.g., threshold PGGs, repeated joint production), and a diverse set of adjacent game-theoretic paradigms (e.g., repeated prisoner’s dilemma, trust/donor games, bargaining games, and networked games). The theories are sophisticated and often calibrated to classic empirical studies, incorporating evolutionary, dynamic, and institutional mechanisms. Outcomes analyzed are primarily efficiency or closely related group payoff metrics, but some papers focus on cooperation rates or norm compliance. The dimensional coverage across papers is broad in terms of concepts, but there are notable gaps in empirical calibration, heterogeneity of design features, and direct mapping to all 14 prediction dimensions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance**: The core evidence comes from theory papers directly modeling PGGs with peer punishment (e.g., Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Dutta et al., 2021; Kranz, 2010; Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2021; Hwang, 2017), offering close matches to the prediction context.  
- **Close relevance:** Some papers address threshold PGGs, CPR games, or repeated joint production games—structurally similar but not always covering all design features of PGGs (e.g., Kol’veková et al., 2021; Congleton & Vanberg, 2001; Buchholz et al., 2014).
- **Adjacent relevance:** Many models analyze variants or related dilemmas (repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, trust games, network exclusion), which supply theoretical mechanisms but lack precise correspondence to standard PGGs.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact/close relevance:** Nearly all theory papers explicitly incorporate punishment or sanctions, often as the main intervention of interest.
- **Partial/indirect relevance:** Some discuss endogenous sanctions via network exclusion or partner choice (e.g., Auerswald et al., 2018), analogously informative for punishment’s institutional role.
- **Contextual relevance:** A subset considers only reward, or discuss punishment as a secondary mechanism.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact/close relevance:** Payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, welfare, surplus, average group payoff) are primary in most theoretical models (e.g., Kranz, 2010; Buchholz et al., 2014; Dutta et al., 2021).
- **Some focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (cooperation/contribution rates), but still often link these to implied welfare or surplus.
- **Gaps:** Direct quantification or experimentally calibrated effect sizes for efficiency under varied game parameters is scarce.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes:**
- **Efficiency** (ratio of realized group payoff to the cooperative optimum) is explicitly analyzed in many theory papers (e.g., Dutta et al., 2021; Kranz, 2010; Kol’veková et al., 2021; Chassang & Zehnder, 2016).
- **Group payoff/Welfare** metrics are often mathematically derived, either as endpoints of evolutionary dynamics or equilibrium solutions.
- **Related metrics:** Surplus, total coins generated, and welfare are used interchangeably with efficiency.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- **Contribution rate/Cooperation rate:** Commonly modeled as an internal step to payoff/efficiency outcomes, but not a substitute for efficiency prediction (e.g., Darcet & Sornette, 2008; Schunk & Wagner, 2021).
- **Punishment frequency/punishment assigned**: Modeled as determinants of enforcement costs and efficiency (e.g., Yu et al., 2016).
- **Norm compliance/antisocial punishment**: Discussed as moderators of cooperation efficacy, not directly as efficiency outcomes.

**Explicitly, most payoff-related findings are theoretical predictions or model outputs, not empirical observations.**

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### General Findings
- **Strong theoretical consensus** that enabling punishment in canonical PGGs leads to substantial increases in group efficiency and welfare, provided:
  - Punishment is effective (not too costly relative to its deterrent effect).
  - The initial share of compliers/internalizers is non-trivial (not too many initial free-riders).
  - The strategic structure does not allow for widespread retaliation, extortion, or antisocial punishment.  
  (e.g., Dutta et al., 2021; Kranz, 2010; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Kol’veková et al., 2021)

- **Moderators of positive efficiency effects:**
  - **Punishment cost and technology:** Efficiency gains are stronger with low-cost, high-impact punishment (Hwang, 2017; Kranz, 2010).
  - **Player count/group size:** Classical logic suggests larger groups should have lower baseline efficiency due to increased free-riding. However, models demonstrate that, with well-coordinated punishment, the efficiency benefit of introducing punishment can persist or even increase with group size (Hwang, 2017; Buchholz et al., 2014).
  - **Baseline (control) efficiency:** If the no-punishment (control) game is already at high efficiency (due to strong norms/internalization or available reward/mechanisms), the marginal benefit of punishment is smaller.
  - **Institutional features and design details** (e.g., show_punishment_id or the possibility of antisocial punishment) can make efficient cooperation either stable or fragile (Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2021; Schunk & Wagner, 2021; Barron & Guo, 2021).
  - **Coordination among punishers:** Coordination can amplify the efficiency gains (Hwang, 2017).

### Caveats and Heterogeneity
- **Efficiency impacts are not universally positive:**  
  - If punishment is too costly, misapplied, or can be leveraged for extortion or antisocial purposes, efficiency can stagnate or decline (Barron & Guo, 2021; Chassang & Zehnder, 2016; Schunk & Wagner, 2021).
  - **Unequal or biased punishment:** If only non-elites are punished, efficiency for the whole group may rise, but subgroups may lose welfare and inequality increases (Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2021).
  - **Excessive punishment** in steady-state cooperative environments can lower welfare due to unnecessary costs (Yu et al., 2016).

### Influence of Alternative or Supplementary Mechanisms
- **Reward exists**: Models compare reward vs. punishment and find that under some conditions, prosocial reward or motivational regimes achieve higher efficiency than punishment regimes (Dughera, 2022; Chassang & Zehnder, 2016).
- **Communication/chat:** Communication robustly increases efficiency, sometimes independent of (and even substituting for) punishment (Noussair & van Soest, 2014).
- **Reputation and partner choice**: Mechanisms such as ostracism, endogenous exit, and reputation-based partner selection substitute for direct punishment and can drive efficiency (Congleton & Vanberg, 2001; Auerswald et al., 2018; Stahl, 2011).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Prediction of treatment efficiency (with peer punishment enabled) should generally presume a positive increment over control efficiency**, especially for designs closely resembling canonical lab PGGs (4–6 players, 10 or more rounds, standard MPCR, moderate-cost, direct punishment modes), as evidenced by Dutta et al. (2021), Carpenter & Matthews (2010), and Kranz (2010).

- **The effect size (increment in efficiency) is moderated by several core design dimensions:**
  - **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** Lower cost and higher effectiveness yield greater efficiency gains.
  - **Player count:** Larger groups do not necessarily see a reduction in punishment’s marginal efficiency effect if punishers can coordinate.
  - **Control efficiency baseline:** If the baseline is very low due to rampant free-riding, punishment can trigger a phase-shift to high efficiency if evolutionary/learning dynamics allow cooperators to coordinate; otherwise, effects may be muted.
  - **Punishment misuse risks:** Where punishment can be exploited for antisocial purposes, or extortion is possible, efficiency gains may not materialize or may reverse.
  - **Visibility/identifiability (show_punishment_id):** Transparent punishment mapping to norm violation promotes effectiveness.

- **Adjustments should be made for contextually discussed, but not directly parameterized, moderators:**
  - **Norm consensus, cultural context, communication, allowance of rewards, and asymmetries in punishment application** can all shape outcomes and should temper straightforward quantitative predictions when information is lacking or ambiguous in these domains.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count**: Explicitly analyzed in most models.
- **num_rounds**: Modeled as repeated or finite-horizon PGGs in several key theories.
- **all_or_nothing**: Directly modeled (e.g., binary contribution structures in theory papers).
- **mpcr (Marginal Per-Capita Return)**: Central parameter in nearly all payoffs-based models.
- **punishment_cost**/**punishment_tech**: Core to nearly all theoretical analysis addressing the target task.
- **reward_exists / reward_cost / reward_tech**: Contrasted or supplementary in several models (not universally, but with strong coverage where addressed).

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**
- **chat**: Considered as a moderator in review/theory (Noussair & van Soest, 2014), less so in mathematical models.
- **default_contrib**: Framing effects are acknowledged as potential moderators, but rarely parameterized.
- **show_n_rounds**/**show_other_summaries**/**show_punishment_id**: Addressed directly in models discussing information structure and observation, but not always as an explicit design dimension with calibratable parameter.
- **reward_exists / reward_cost / reward_tech**: Discussed in some, not all, and rarely with mutual exclusivity relative to punishment.

**Effectively Missing:**
- **Empirical data for directly estimating conditional efficiency by parameter sweep across dimensions is absent.**
- **Calibration to chat, default_contrib, summary or transparency features is largely missing or anecdotal.**
- **No direct mapping of 'show_punishment_id' as a randomized or systematically varied feature in efficiency models—mostly discussed in mechanism narratives.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Absence of empirical estimation:** The entire paper set is theoretical; findings rest on modeled outcomes, assumptions regarding agent behavior, and calibration to prior empirical work but do not report new experimental data or statistical effect sizes. This constrains direct model training or validation.
- **Limited coverage of the full combination of 14 prediction dimensions:** While core parameters (group size, mpcr, punishment cost, etc.) are richly analyzed, many design features such as chat, visibility options, baseline contribution framing, and information symmetry are discussed only conceptually or are missing parameterized analysis.
- **Structural heterogeneity and adjacency:** A substantial number of models are adjacent to, not exact matches for, canonical PGGs, leading to uncertainty about generalizability—especially for games with institutional variations (reward vs. punishment, centralized vs. decentralized sanctions, networked vs. fixed groups).
- **Ambiguity in cases of antisocial punishment, extortion, or norm conflict:** The literature highlights the possibility of negative or null effects of punishment on efficiency in settings with extortion or high rates of antisocial punishment, but the prevalence, magnitude, and conditionality of these cases are not directly specified or resolvable from theory alone.
- **Long-run versus short-run effects:** Some models note that high efficiency may emerge only after many rounds of learning/evolution or under specific initial conditions, which may not match typical experimental time horizons.
- **Non-monotonic and multi-equilibria results:** Multiple papers stress the risk of path dependence, sudden collapse, or multiple stable equilibria under different parameter settings—introducing qualitative uncertainty around predicted efficiency when punishment is enabled.
- **No evidence for interaction effects among multiple less-standard features (e.g., chat plus reward plus punishment with variable group identity features).**

---

**In summary,** this theory-focused literature set provides strong **mechanism-level and comparative static guidance** supporting a generally positive impact of peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, especially as a function of punishment cost, group size, and baseline efficiency. **However, quantitative predictions for novel combinations of nuanced design features should be made cautiously, prioritizing exact matches to canonical models and noting gaps and moderate uncertainty where empirical calibration or coverage is missing.**
