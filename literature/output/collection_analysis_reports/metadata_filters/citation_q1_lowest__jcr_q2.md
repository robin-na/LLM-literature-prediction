# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 217 sources, including both **empirical (mainly lab experiments) and theoretical** works. The set is **broad for public-goods dilemmas** and contains a significant number of studies reporting directly on standard PGGs or closely related variants with manipulations of peer punishment. Many studies report **payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group payoff) alongside behavioral measures.** There is also substantial theory, both classic and simulation-based, addressing mechanisms and comparative statics.

Empirical papers are dominant for standard lab PGGs; field and observational studies are less frequent but present. Some theoretical works offer closed-form predictions or equilibrium conditions for efficiency under specific punishment regimes. While the evidence is diverse, there is **ample directly relevant, high-quality empirical evidence** for punishment-on-vs-off efficiency in core PGG designs.

# 2) Task Relevance

### pgg_or_variant
- **exact:** Most key empirical and some theoretical papers use standard PGGs (linear or binary), often with canonical lab designs. These are highly relevant.
- **close:** Several papers use threshold public goods, common pool resources, market externalities, or repeated PDs/ultumatum—adjacent but not exact PGGs.
- **adjacent/weak/none:** Many included papers focus on adjacent dilemmas, bargaining games, or non-economic field experiments and cannot be mapped directly to public goods games' efficiency predictions.

### punishment_or_sanctions
- **exact:** Many papers manipulate **peer punishment**, ostracism, or centralized punishment within canonical/standard PGG designs.
- **close:** Inclusion of **reward mechanisms**, third-party punishment, group exclusion, or policy-based sanctions (taxes, fines) broadens coverage but can reduce direct comparability.
- **adjacent/weak:** Indirect consequences for defectors, descriptive studies of norm enforcement, and narrative punishment analogs are only contextually informative.

### efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome
- **exact:** About half the relevant experimental studies report **group earnings, efficiency, welfare, or total coins generated**—in line with the desired prediction target.
- **close/adjacent:** Many others focus mainly on contribution rates or compliance rather than payoff, inferring possible efficiency effects. Some provide indirect or partial welfare analysis.
- **weak/none:** A significant proportion of behavioral and psychological studies measure only attitudes, punishment assigned, or decision frequency, not efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (directly relevant):**  
   - Efficiency: defined as group earnings relative to the cooperative optimum.
   - Group/treatment average payoff, welfare, surplus.
   - Total group coins/tokens/endowment growth (in some resource/market experiments).
   - In some theory, analytically derived expected payoffs under equilibrium.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (not directly efficiency):**  
   - Contribution/cooperation rates.
   - Frequency/magnitude of punishment, norm compliance.
   - Voting, exclusion frequency, partner choice, norm endorsement.
   - Attitudes, beliefs, or intentions about punishment/fairness (especially in noneconomic or psychological studies).
   - Most studies distinguish clearly between payoff and non-payoff outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant to Prediction

### Cross-paper synthesis of findings on **treatment efficiency** (punishment-enabled vs. control):

**General Patterns:**
- **Punishment in standard PGGs:**
  - **Often increases group efficiency** compared to no-punishment baselines, especially where the control efficiency is low and punishment is well targeted and not too costly [(Zhang et al., 2024, JARUNGRATTANAPONG, 2022, Benard & Barclay, 2020, Kamei, 2024, Joseph et al., 2025, Gürdal et al., 2021)].
  - The effect is **largest with deterrent, effective, and/or institutionally administered punishment**, and when anti-social punishment is minimal.
  - **Weak, untargeted, costly, or automatic punishment can fail to increase—and may decrease—efficiency**, as costs outweigh increased contributions [(Botelho et al., 2022, Casari & Tavoni, 2024)].

- **Punishment effectiveness depends critically on design dimensions:**
   - **Punishment cost and tech (cost-to-impact ratio):** Lower cost and higher impact improve efficiency effects; high costs can reverse gains, making punishment net costly [(Wu et al., 2014, Zhang et al., 2019, Sun et al., 2025)].
   - **Network/punishment structure:** Incomplete or structured networks outperform "complete" peer-punishment networks (where everyone can punish everyone), due to bystander effects and diffusion of responsibility [(Peng & Fan, 2023, Bühren et al., 2025)].
   - **Framing and institutional context:** Framing cooperation as an "act of commission" (Take game) or providing leadership/democracy can amplify punishment's efficiency effect [(Ramalingam et al., 2019, Benard & Barclay, 2020, Kim et al., 2025)].
   - **Magnitude of punishment:** Small, non-deterring punishments may do little or even harm efficiency (Ye et al., 2023).

- **Caveats and exceptions:**
   - **Antisocial punishment** (punishment of cooperators) and retaliation reduce efficiency, particularly where communication is limited or group heterogeneity is high [(Kim et al., 2025, Casari & Tavoni, 2024)].
   - **When punishment is too cheap or easy to coordinate** (low group threshold)—as in group expropriation—arbitrary or excessive punishment can reduce efficiency [(Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017)].
   - **Counter-punishment ("punishment of punishers") can lower efficiency** [(Casari & Tavoni, 2024, Robbett, 2019)].

- **Mechanism arguments from theory:**
   - **Formal/institutional punishment** (even if costly) can reliably achieve high efficiency when set at deterrent levels, especially when endogenously preferred by players [(Kamei, 2024, Gürdal et al., 2021, Huang et al., 2024)].
   - **Peer punishment** is most efficient when rare but potent; frequently used peer punishment is likely to erode gains because of its cost [(Sun et al., 2024, Glöckner et al., 2018, Ramalingam et al., 2019)].

- **Institutional and contextual moderators:**
   - **Democracy or endogenous institution choice:** Democratic selection of sanctioning rules or leaders increases efficiency effects [(Benard & Barclay, 2020, Schories, 2022)].
   - **Cross-cultural robustness:** Positive efficiency effects of punishment hold across cultures, but magnitude may vary depending on prevailing social norms and trust in institutions [(Gürdal et al., 2021, Kamei et al., 2025)].
   - **Group size and MPCR:** Effects typically demonstrated in 4-5 person groups; efficiency gains from punishment are higher when baseline returns to cooperation are low (low MPCR) and baseline efficiency is low [(Wu et al., 2014, Zhang et al., 2019)].
   - **Information and transparency:** Making punishers/rewarders anonymous or visible can modulate efficiency via changing incentive structures and likelihood of antisocial punishment [(Benard & Barclay, 2020, Kim et al., 2025)].

- **Empirical findings overwhelmingly favor efficiency gains with well-designed peer punishment** in lab PGGs, though there are notable exceptions, especially where punishment is costly, mis-applied, or insufficiently targeted.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should this literature inform efficiency prediction, given control game efficiency and design dimensions?**

1. **If control (no-punishment) efficiency is low, and punishment is enabled with a well-targeted, not-unduly-costly peer or institutional mechanism, expect a significant efficiency gain.**
   - Historically, most direct PGG experiments see an increase of **10-40%** in group payoff relative to optimal payoff, depending on MPCR, cost-to-impact ratio, and whether antisocial punishment is present [(JARUNGRATTANAPONG, 2022, Kamei, 2024)].

2. **If the punishment available is costly, not highly effective, or frequently misapplied (e.g., broad anti-social punishment), expect at best a small positive effect, and sometimes a net decrease in efficiency.**
   - Several studies report **increased contributions but lower overall efficiency** when punishment costs outweigh cooperation gains [(Casari & Tavoni, 2024, Botelho et al., 2022, Kim et al., 2025)].

3. **Pay particular attention to the following design dimensions for generalization:**
   - **Player count and group size:** Most evidence is with 4-5 players; effects weaken or destabilize in much larger groups unless punishment institution is centralized or formal [(Wu et al., 2014, Zhang et al., 2019, Ye et al., 2023)].
   - **Punishment cost and impact ratio (punishment_cost, punishment_tech):** Lower cost and higher impact are strongly associated with positive efficiency effects [(Huang et al., 2024, Wu et al., 2014)].
   - **Network/punishment structure:** Networked or pairwise punishment structures can outperform full 'everyone-punishes-everyone' designs due to less redundancy and lower diffusion of responsibility [(Peng & Fan, 2023, Bühren et al., 2025)].
   - **MPCR (marginal per-capita return):** Punishment is most impactful at intermediate MPCRs—very low MPCRs can render even punishment ineffective, very high MPCRs can promote high baseline efficiency even without punishment [(Gürdal et al., 2021, Wu et al., 2014)].
   - **Framing and explicit feedback:** All-or-nothing vs. continuous contribution, revealed/concealed identity, and communication laws strongly moderate efficiency outcomes [(Benard & Barclay, 2020, Kim et al., 2025)].

4. **Incorporate indirect and theoretical evidence with caution:**
   - Theory supports the possibility of large efficiency gains even with minimal punishments if institutional design supports credible threats [(Huang et al., 2024, Wu et al., 2014)]. However, real-world anti-social punishment and mis-targeted costs must be considered.

5. **If baseline (control) efficiency is already near-optimal, the incremental effect of enabling punishment will be small or even negative, due to unnecessary punishment expenditures.**

6. **Expect the greatest uncertainty where design dimensions differ from the lab-PGG norm (e.g., large groups, highly heterogeneous returns, complex network structures); in these cases, theoretical mapping from model parameters to efficiency is more hazardous.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- **player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, all_or_nothing, punishment_tech**: Carefully varied and analyzed in most experimental studies. **Empirical payoff effects are tied to these parameters.**

**Indirectly Informed / Contextually Discussed:**  
- **chat, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Sometimes present or manipulated, not always systematically varied, but seen to moderate (mainly through effects on anti-social punishment and norm salience).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Discussed in comparative/literature-review papers, occasionally included as treatments or moderators.
- **default_contrib:** Occasionally manipulated in studies of framing but not systematically linked to punishment effects.
  
**Effectively Missing or Sparse:**  
- **punishment_tech details beyond cost/impact ratios (e.g., minimum group size for punishment), targeting algorithms:** Only a handful of studies consider coordination costs or technology in fine detail (see Hugh-Jones & Perroni, 2017).
- **Data on larger or more heterogeneous groups:** Sparse; most findings are from smaller, homogeneous groups.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **External validity** is limited: Most direct evidence comes from lab experiments with highly controlled environments, homogeneous subject populations (students), and limited group sizes (n=4-5). Effects in "real-world" public goods, large groups, or field environments may differ.
- **Behavioral/psychological outcomes dominate some adjacent literature:** Many studies measure only cooperation/contribution or punishment frequency; these cannot be assumed to map cleanly onto efficiency effects.
- **Mixed findings where anti-social punishment is substantial:** Especially in settings with non-anonymous or heterogeneous groups, or incomplete information about others, punishment can become misapplied, eroding or reversing efficiency gains.
- **Efficiency effects are highly sensitive to punishment cost and targeting:** Costly, untargeted, or weak punishment often fails to improve efficiency, and can reduce it.
- **Many design dimensions are not systematically cross-varied:** Only the main axes (cost, effectiveness, group size, MPCR) are directly evidenced for efficiency mapping; other features like visibility, dynamic updating, and complex network structures are less well covered.
- **Heterogeneity in baseline (control) efficiency is not always reported:** Some studies focus on relative differences without absolute efficiency baselines, complicating prediction mapping.
- **Theory may overstate efficiency gains by assuming perfect monitoring or optimal equilibrium play; actual laboratory behavior often shows more noise, anti-social punishment, or norm-based behavior.**

---

**Summary**:  
The literature provides **robust and direct empirical support** for the positive effect of well-designed, not-too-costly peer punishment on efficiency in small-group, standard public goods games, especially when baseline efficiency is low. **Prediction of treatment efficiency** should be sensitive to control efficiency, punishment cost/impact, structure, and social context. In many experimental settings, enabling punishment yields significant efficiency gains, but in other designs—especially where punishment is costly, anti-social, or misaligned—efficiency does not improve, and can even decline. Indirect and theoretical evidence reinforces these findings but must be mapped cautiously when design dimensions or context differ. Many adjacent behavioral results cannot be used for quantitative efficiency prediction. Empirical design variation is most comprehensive for group size, rounds, MPCR, and punishment cost/tech, while other design dimensions are sparsely evidenced. External and ecological validity, and the effect of unmeasured moderators (such as culture, communication, or real resource context), remain key limitations.
