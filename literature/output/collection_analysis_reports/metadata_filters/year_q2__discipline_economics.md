# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set is broad and heavily weighted toward empirical, experimental lab studies of public goods games (PGGs) and their close variants. It includes numerous theory papers and several field experiments, but the core evidence for the prediction task is empirical, with standardized reporting on payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group profit, or total earnings). Nearly all high-relevance studies use classic linear PGGs or direct variants, with a focus on the causal effect of enabling (vs. disabling) punishment—most often peer punishment, but also centralized and exogenous punishment institutions—on individual and group-level outcomes. Many studies directly report both control (no-punishment) and treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency measures, typically using real monetary payoffs and well-specified design dimensions. The evidence base is stronger for smaller group sizes (3–5 players), moderate numbers of rounds (10–20), standard MPCRs (marginal per capita return), and basic punishment technologies. Some studies systematically vary individual dimensions (network structure, punishment cost, legitimacy, group heterogeneity), offering comparative insights.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. PGG or variant (`pgg_or_variant`):**  
- **exact**: The vast majority of high-quality studies use exact PGGs—linear VCMs (Voluntary Contribution Mechanisms) with continuous or all-or-nothing contributions—and their canonical variants (e.g., threshold PGGs, taking games).
- **close/adjacent:** Several papers extend to common-pool resource dilemmas, trust games, or repeated PDs with punishment, providing contextually related but not exact PGG settings.

**b. Punishment or sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`):**  
- **exact**: Most relevant empirical and theory papers directly manipulate the presence, type, or structure of punishment/sanctions, especially peer punishment, institutional punishment, or variants thereof.
- **close/adjacent:** Some studies include exogenous or group-level sanctions, exclusion, or probabilistic punishment, and a few focus on reward or communication as (imperfect) substitutes or complements.

**c. Efficiency or payoff outcomes (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`):**  
- **exact**: Many studies report efficiency in the canonical sense (group earnings/maximum possible), with direct no-punishment/punishment-enabled contrasts.
- **close:** Some report total earnings, welfare, or surplus, which are direct payoff-based outcomes.
- **adjacent/weak:** Many other studies focus on behavioral outcomes (contribution/cooperation rates, norm compliance, frequency of punishment) and only indirectly on payoffs.
- **none:** A significant number of papers are outside the direct prediction domain, focusing on mechanisms, beliefs, or context without payoff reporting.

# 3) Outcomes Measured in the Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (primary for prediction):**  
  - Efficiency (group payoff as a share of the full-cooperation benchmark)
  - Group earnings, total profit, surplus, or mean payoff
  - Dynamic changes in group profit over time

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
  - Contribution rates, cooperation frequencies
  - Punishment frequency/intensity/targeting
  - Norm enforcement, compliance rates, emotion/psychological responses
  - Institutional preference or self-selection, voting for institution types
  - Retaliation and antisocial punishment rates

**Important distinction:** Many papers report behavioral outcomes only. For prediction, only those reporting efficiency/payoff metrics are directly usable; others provide insight into likely mechanisms or moderators only with explicit caveats.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of empirical findings:**

- **Heterogeneity of efficiency effects:** Enabling punishment in standard PGGs **can** increase efficiency over control, but the direction and magnitude are highly sensitive to design details—especially punishment effectiveness, cost, legitimacy, group size/homogeneity, information, and possibility for counter-punishment or antisocial punishment.

- **Positive efficiency effects:**
    - Peer punishment, when not too costly and when targeted legitmately at defectors, commonly increases group efficiency (e.g., Dickinson et al., 2015; Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Grieco et al., 2017; Wang & Qin, 2015; Qin & Wang, 2013).
    - Centralized or deterrent punishment, especially when voted on or matched to group norms, can approach social optimum (Markussen et al., 2014; Kamijo et al., 2014; Kube et al., 2015).
    - Redistributive punishment (where fines are recycled as rewards) can turn standard punishment from efficiency-reducing to efficiency-increasing (Page et al., 2013).

- **Neutral or negative efficiency effects:**
    - High punishment costs, poor targeting (antisocial punishment), network asymmetries, or illegitimacy of the punishment institution can negate or reverse efficiency gains (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
    - Heterogeneity—either in endowments, marginal returns, or social/cultural context—often moderates or even reverses the effect (Kingsley, 2016; Kölle, 2015; Dekel et al., 2017; Bortolotti et al., 2015).
    - The presence of antisocial punishment can make efficiency decrease with punishment—especially in non-student/general population samples or in groups with high inequality (Gächter et al., 2017; Bortolotti et al., 2015).

- **Crucial moderators:**
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** High punishment cost (relative to impact) erodes gains; costless or low-cost punishment more reliably increases efficiency (Dugar, 2013).
    - **Information structure/feedback:** Full, accurate feedback (including punisher identity) reduces antisocial punishment and increases targeting; noisy or missing feedback undermines efficiency gains (Nicklisch et al., 2016).
    - **Legitimacy:** Institutions seen as legitimate (e.g., majority approval, legitimacy of who can punish) support positive efficiency effects (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Faillo et al., 2013).
    - **Game framing and rules:** Combining punishment with communication, agreements, or framing (moral/legal) can yield stronger and more sustained efficiency improvements (Dannenberg, 2016; Engel & Kurschilgen, 2013; Dal Bó & Dal Bó, 2014).
    - **Punishment network structure:** Complete or symmetric punishment networks generate higher efficiency than incomplete, pairwise, or asymmetric ones (Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
    - **Reward vs. punishment:** In several studies, rewards out-perform or complement punishment for efficiency; mixing both rarely outperforms rewards alone (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Page et al., 2013; Dugar, 2013).

- **Limits of generalizability:**
    - Many field or context-adjacent studies show much weaker or null effects of punishment on efficiency, or even negative effects when real-world norms or pre-existing social capital are hostile to punishment (Noussair et al., 2015; de Melo & Piaggio, 2015; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015).

**From theory:**
- Formal models reinforce that punishment can sustain full efficiency if costs are not prohibitive and monitoring/auditing is effective; group size, patience (discount rate), and network connectivity are all key for the size of the efficiency gain (Levine & Modica, 2016; Wolitzky, 2013; Hwang, 2017).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

## What the literature implies for predicting treatment efficiency:

- **Baseline:**
    - If you know the efficiency of the control (punishment-off) game **and** have a standard PGG design (4–5 players, continuous contributions, MPCR 0.3–0.5, typical punishment cost/effectiveness, full feedback), then enabling peer punishment should be predicted to increase efficiency—often substantially (10–30 percentage points)—*unless* one or more negative moderators are present.
- **Adjust for moderators:**
    - **Punishment cost:** High cost-to-impact ratios (e.g., 1:1 or worse) or high fixed costs reduce or erase efficiency gains.
    - **Antisocial punishment prevalence:** High, especially in general population or low-norm environments, predicts little or no efficiency gain.
    - **Network/institution asymmetry:** Incomplete or asymmetric punishment networks, or limited monitoring, weaken or negate positive effects.
    - **Group heterogeneity:** Norm conflict (endowment, valuation heterogeneity, presence of harmed minority) weakens punishment's effect on efficiency.
    - **Information:** Noisy or limited information about contributions or punishment actions reduces targeting and effectiveness.
    - **Legitimacy:** Illegitimate or externally imposed punishment reduces efficiency; endogenous/voted/legitimate punishment institutions are more effective.
    - **Context:** Some field settings, real-world organizations, or culturally distinct samples (e.g. field studies in fishing, forestry, or high-corruption contexts) often show weaker or negative efficiency effects.
- **Critical dimension-level rules:**  
    - **Redistributive punishment** (fines paid to others) is more efficient than burned-cost punishment.
    - **Exclusion/ostracism** as a sanction is highly effective in increasing efficiency in partner-selection or networked games.
    - **Rewards** (especially costless) can sometimes substitute for or outperform punishment in raising efficiency.

- **If the design deviates from canonical PGGs:**
    - With high group size (>8), complex network structures, private/minority knowledge, or altered action spaces, the direction and effect size become more ambiguous and should be predicted as attenuated or possibly negative unless direct contrast evidence is available.

## *Summary*:  
For most standard lab PGGs, **expect efficiency to increase upon enabling peer punishment, with effect size proportional to punishment cost, legitimacy, and information accuracy, and negatively moderated by group/parameter heterogeneity, antisocial punishment, or design features that reduce targeting or efficacy.** The presence of these mediators must be explicitly checked in the prediction input—the downstream model cannot extrapolate from control efficiency alone without consideration of these core dimensions.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Well-informed dimensions:**
- `player_count` – Commonly manipulated and reported.
- `num_rounds` – Standardized (10–20 rounds) in most empirical studies.
- `mpcr` – Explicitly varied and documented in nearly all direct studies.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` – Central to many design contrasts (cost-to-impact, effectiveness, presence of redistributive punishment, exclusion, centralization, or probability/severity).
- `all_or_nothing` vs. continuous contributions – Both forms well represented.
- `chat` (communication) – Frequently manipulated as moderator in lab settings.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds` – Almost always specified and sometimes used as experimental variables.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` – Directly manipulated in comparative punishment/reward studies.

**Indirectly or contextually discussed:**
- `default_contrib` – Only rarely reported as an explicit design feature.
- `show_punishment_id` – Manipulated in some studies on anonymity and legitimacy (e.g., Zheng & Nie, 2013), but often held constant elsewhere.

**Sparse or missing dimensions:**
- Some institutional details—such as **the ease/cost of acquiring punishment rights**, **the symmetry of the punishment network**, or **probability of judicial error**—are treated in-depth in a minority of studies but left constant or unreported in most.
- **Non-canonical settings** (very large groups, extremely high or low MPCR, field contexts): evidence is scattered and often less directly comparable.
- **Cultural/normative context**, while acknowledged as a critical moderator in field and cross-country studies, is not parameterized in most lab studies.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Generalizability:** Most findings are from controlled lab environments; field evidence is more ambiguous, often with cultural/contextual moderators that can invert lab findings.
- **Coverage gaps:** Certain design combinations (e.g., very large groups, highly dynamic or multiplexed networks, extreme punishment institutions) are underexplored.
- **Context restriction:** Efficiency effects observed in typical lab populations (students in small groups) often do not extend to general populations, large real-world groups, or settings with pre-existing institutional or cultural norms.
- **Nonlinear/interaction effects:** Many studies show that efficiency impacts are not linear or additive functions of design dimensions; instead, they depend on complex interactions (e.g., network structure × legitimacy × punishment cost).
- **Over-reliance on contribution rates:** Many studies infer efficiency changes from observed contribution rates, but without direct measurement of payoff/welfare, which can be misleading if punishment costs are high or antisocial.
- **Temporal dynamics:** Some efficiency gains are only observed in later rounds, with early costs offset by subsequent high cooperation; short experiments may underestimate the potential for sustained gains.
- **Missing data for certain dimensions:** Predictions for rare or new combinations of design features may be unreliable, as most evidence comes from relatively standard environments.
- **Co-presence of rewards or exclusion:** In treatments enabling both punishment and reward/exclusion, the separate effect of punishment may be hard to isolate.
- **Measurement bias:** Some studies emphasize preferences, norms, or emotional outcomes without directly measuring efficiency, limiting their predictive value for payoff-based tasks.

---

**In summary:** The paper set provides an unusually comprehensive and high-quality evidence base for predicting efficiency effects of enabling punishment in PGG-like environments, but effect sizes depend on central design dimensions—primarily punishment cost/effectiveness, legitimacy, network structure, group homogeneity, information, and the potential for antisocial punishment. Predictive models should attend closely to these moderators and not extrapolate beyond the empirical scope of well-studied parameter spaces.
