# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises a **large and fairly rich collection (73 papers)** of primarily **empirical, experimental, lab-based studies**. The core of the evidence base directly investigates repeated and one-shot public goods games (PGG) or close variants and focuses on the effect of peer punishment and related sanctioning/reward institutions. There is **a mix of standard linear PGGs, variants with heterogeneity, common-pool-resource (CPR) games, and a few adjacent trust, contest, or bargaining games**. The vast majority of studies are **empirical**, with few purely theoretical/mechanism-focused pieces; theory is mostly used to frame designs or interpret findings.

For the **downstream prediction task**—forecasting average efficiency when punishment is enabled, given control efficiency and game design—this paper set is **substantively broad at the core (standard PGGs) but thinner for adjacent or derived variants**. Some important moderators (e.g., information structure, legitimacy, group size, anti-social punishment, type of monitoring) are addressed repeatedly but not always with direct efficiency outcomes. **Payoff-based outcomes (especially group payoff/efficiency) are well covered**, but some studies focus primarily on contributions or other behaviors.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Relevance by target dimension**:

| Dimension                     | Typical Relevance Label | Notes                                                                                            |
|-------------------------------|------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| pgg_or_variant                | exact / close          | Most papers are exact (standard linear PGGs), several are close (CPR, allocation, or networked)  |
| punishment_or_sanctions       | exact / close          | Many papers examine peer punishment or direct sanctions; some investigate adjacent mechanisms     |
| efficiency_or_related_payoff  | exact / close / adjacent | Most focus on efficiency/total payoff; some only on behavioral (contribution/compliance) outcomes |

**Details**:

- **PGG or Variant**: The literature is **highly relevant** (exact/close) for standard PGGs and very relevant for linear and binary (all-or-nothing) variants. Several close studies extend to CPR, coalition, or networked structures with parallel strategic features.
- **Punishment or Sanctions**: Multiple studies are *exact* (explicit peer/centralized punishment in PGG); others are *close* (e.g., exclusion, reward, monetary/non-monetary approval, group monitoring).
- **Efficiency or Related Payoff**: Many empirical studies report *exact* efficiency outcomes (ratio of actual to maximal possible group payoff). Several others report only on contributions or norm compliance, which are only *adjacent* for payoff prediction purposes.

**Gaps**: Some studies, particularly those focused on ostracism, coalition formation, gossip, or commitment mechanisms, discuss PGGs but do not systematically report efficiency outcomes. Adjacent studies (contest games, trust games, bargaining) are less applicable to the core prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes** *(directly informative for prediction)*:
- **Efficiency**: Often measured as group earnings as a % of social optimum / maximal payoff.
- **Average group earnings/payoff/welfare**: Sometimes used interchangeably with efficiency.
- **Total coins or surplus generated by the group**.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** *(not the direct basis for efficiency prediction, but informative for mechanism)*:
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**: Choices of individuals in each period (often highly correlated with efficiency, but not identical if punishment costs are high).
- **Punishment frequency/usage**: How often and whom players punish; total points spent on punishment.
- **Norm compliance or pattern changes**: E.g., enforcement of fairness norms, proportion of groups enforcing proportional or equal-contribution rules.
- **Ostracism, coalition formation, reputation measures**.

**Distinctions**:
- *Some papers show increased cooperation with punishment but lower efficiency due to heavy punishment costs (e.g., Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Colombier et al., 2011).*
- *Others record direct increases in efficiency due to effective, well-targeted punishment or the presence of supporting institutions (e.g., Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014).*
- *Certain studies only provide behavioral measures (not efficiency), necessitating caution in transferring their insights to efficiency prediction.*

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Synthetic evidence on punishment effects from PGG and close variants:
- **Punishment can increase average efficiency—but not always, and sometimes reduces it explicitly**.
  - *Enabling peer punishment often increases contributions and, in many standard linear PGGs, raises efficiency to high/near-maximal levels (Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Kamei, 2019).*  
  - *However, when punishment is cheap and overused (Anderson & Putterman, 2006), or when antisocial punishment is frequent (Chen, 2022; Faillo et al., 2013 Baseline), group efficiency may not improve and can even fall below control due to resources lost in punishment exchanges.*

- **The cost and structure of punishment mechanisms matter critically**:
    - *Punishment is most efficiency-enhancing when it is (a) costly enough to limit overuse, (b) targeted at clear norm violators (legitimate punishment), (c) not subject to cycles of retaliation or excessive counterpunishment (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Chen, 2022).*
    - *Legitimacy, whether conferred by procedural design (right to punish only freeriders; democratic or voted institutions) or by information cues (public identification of punishers, observed bystanders), enhances punishment's positive effect on efficiency (Faillo et al., 2013; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Gatiso & Vollan, 2017).*
    - *Institutional settings (punishment anonymity, public ID, monitoring, centralized vs. peer, endogenous enactment) are consistent moderators.*

- **Feedback and information structure are crucial moderators**:
    - *The effect of punishment on efficiency depends on which feedback is provided—contribution feedback supports efficiency, while earnings feedback may undermine it. Showing both can lead to excessive punishment and lower efficiency (Nikiforakis, 2010).*
    - *Visibility of punishers, frequency of feedback, and opportunity for communication/agreements also affect outcomes (Faillo et al., 2013; Nikiforakis, 2010; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Dannenberg, 2016).*

- **Game design features interact with punishment effects**:
    - *Group size: In small groups, anonymous punishment is more effective; in larger groups, public punishment (ID revealed) is necessary to maintain efficiency gains (Zheng & Nie, 2013).*
    - *MPCR (marginal per capita return): Higher MPCR tends to make cooperation more attractive, often supporting the efficiency benefits of punishment but in some designs only conditional on other features such as network or agreement (Dannenberg, 2016; Ball et al., 2025).*
    - *Matching protocol, number of rounds, and ability to repeatedly interact with partners (partner vs. stranger vs. random matching) also significantly mediate punishment’s effects (Kamei, 2019; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).*

- **Punishment can backfire**:
    - *When counterpunishment is possible or when punishment is unconstrained, efficiency can decline below no-punishment levels (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Colombier et al., 2011).*
    - *Antisocial punishment (punishment of high contributors) can erode the efficiency benefit, especially in heterogeneous or poorly legitimized environments (Chen, 2022; Faillo et al., 2013).*
    - *Punishment's net welfare effect is especially likely to be negative when it is applied broadly, is cheap compared to its destructive power, and is not aligned with group norms (Colombier et al., 2011; Anderson & Putterman, 2006).*

- **Reward or communication may outperform punishment**:
    - *Pure reward mechanisms often yield comparable or greater efficiency enhancement at lower group cost (Chen, 2022; Colombier et al., 2011; Faillo et al., 2013).*
    - *Communication/coalition mechanisms can often substitute for or outperform punishment for efficiency (Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Ball et al., 2025; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013).*

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**When predicting efficiency with punishment enabled in PGG-like environments, given control efficiency and design dimensions:**

- **Do not expect universally positive impacts from enabling peer punishment.**  
  - *The average effect is positive in many lab-based, standard linear PGGs; but variation is large and context-dependent. Resource waste via over-punishment, counterpunishment, or antisocial punishment can offset or reverse efficiency gains (Chen, 2022; Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).*

- **Adjust predictions based on key design moderators explicitly documented:**
  - *High efficiency gains are most likely in: small-to-moderate groups, with clear/contribution feedback, high enough (but not excessive) punishment cost, and legitimacy/restriction on who can punish (Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Faillo et al., 2013; Zheng & Nie, 2013).*
  - *Expect no or negative effect if: punishment is unconstrained, cheap (so overused), anonymous in large groups, or if counterpunishment is possible and monitoring is too perfect (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Shreedhar et al., 2020).*
  - *If the control efficiency is already high, the scope for gain may be limited; in low-control-efficiency cases, well-structured punishment can result in dramatic increases, but only with supporting structure (Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014).*

- **Pay careful attention to the following dimensions for prediction:**
   - *punishment_cost and punishment_tech*: lower cost increases use (but may lower efficiency due to resource destruction).  
   - *player_count (group size)*: public identification/legitimacy becomes more important as group size rises.
   - *show_other_summaries and feedback*: nature of information provided about others’ actions strongly modulates effectiveness.
   - *chat/communication*: can greatly enhance cooperation and efficiency, sometimes more than punishment itself.
   - *all_or_nothing vs. continuous contributions*: fine-tuned punishment works best with continuous choices; binary settings may reduce subtlety.
   - *reward_exists*: often, the presence of rewards leads to higher or similar efficiency at lower cost.

- **Use caution when transferring results from studies reporting only contribution/cooperation rates, as high contributions do not guarantee high efficiency (especially if punishment is costly or misapplied).**

- **Mapping from control efficiency is sometimes straightforward, but moderators can flip the sign of the effect (i.e., make enabled-punishment efficiency lower than control).**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed (multiple studies, direct link to efficiency/treatment effect):**
- `player_count`: Repeatedly studied (3–4–8–12 range), with group size effects directly mapped to efficiency change.
- `num_rounds`: Both one-shot and repeated games; length and horizon are key moderators.
- `mpcr`: Major moderator, tested in varied levels; higher usually supports efficiency gains from punishment.
- `punishment_cost` / `punishment_tech`: Central for net efficiency effects, often manipulated experimentally.
- `show_other_summaries`: Feedback type (contributions, payoffs, both) criticial for punishment outcomes.
- `chat`: Directly tested as both an alternative and joint factor.
- `all_or_nothing`: Both binary and continuous PGGs are tested, with continuous supporting more nuanced punishment.
- `reward_exists`: Directly compared to punishment in a number of studies.
- `show_n_rounds`: Game horizon visibility/uncertainty; especially important for endgame effects.

**Indirectly Informed / Partial Context:**
- `default_contrib`: Only rarely manipulated/framed (mostly 0 vs. 1 opt-in framing).
- `show_punishment_id`: Information about punisher identity is shown to be important in some (Zheng & Nie, 2013), less so in others.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: When reward is present, often jointly studied with punishment, but not always enough to independently estimate effects.
- `punishment_exists`: Studied as the treatment indicator, but the mechanism details (allowed, restricted, endogenous, etc.) are often key.

**Contextually Discussed, Little Quantitative Evidence:**
- `show_other_summaries`: Sometimes more detail is needed than "on/off"—the *type* of summary matters.
- `chat` and communication: Sometimes tested as treatments, sometimes just mentioned as background.

**Effectively Missing for Direct Prediction:**
- Multi-level summary features, rare design features (e.g., network topology, real-time commitment, very large/small groups) are less directly addressed.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Not all identified design dimensions are consistently manipulated or reported** across papers, especially for reward parameters, default contribution framing, or detailed feedback/monitoring information.
- **Adjacent and non-PGG studies (trust games, contests, network partner selection, CPR with endogenous group formation)** provide partial mechanism evidence but are not always quantitatively transferable to efficiency prediction in standard PGGs.
- **Efficiency/total payoff is not always reported even when contributions or compliance are**—where efficiency is not measured directly, predictions must be made with caution.
- **Antisocial punishment and counterpunishment effects are highly variable and may be under-reported or context-dependent**—in real-world-like, less-controlled environments, net negative efficiency effects might be more common.
- **Legitimacy, feedback structure, procedural choices (voting/democracy vs. imposition) and heterogeneity** are often highlighted as key moderators, but the pool of studies providing direct, quantitative efficiency effects under these moderators is still limited, possibly restricting confidence in prediction for unusual combinations.
- **Most evidence is from lab experiments, sometimes with student samples and limited external validity for field or large-scale applications**.
- **Game-theoretic and mechanism design predictions are sometimes included but not always systematically compared to empirical results**.  
- **Some variants introduce communication, reputation, coalition, or assignment features not captured directly by the core 14 prediction dimensions**; care is needed to separate their effects from those of punishment per se.
- **Ceiling/floor effects:** Where control efficiencies are already high (due to communication, other incentives) the marginal gain from punishment may be small or negative; this is rarely explicitly modeled in the prediction guidance.
- **Confidence intervals for the effect sizes are sometimes wide, and occasionally studies directly conflict in settings with similar parameterizations (especially regarding punishment cost and antisocial punishment in heterogeneous groups).** Preservation of ambiguity is warranted.

---

**Summary**:  
The literature provides a strong empirical base with *direct relevance* to predicting the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in PGG and close variants, conditional on game design dimensions and control efficiency. The effect size, direction, and presence is *highly* context- and design-sensitive, with multiple interaction effects between feedback, punishment cost, legitimacy, group size, and communication. The best-informed design dimensions are group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and feedback structure. Limitations stem from partial reporting, non-payoff primary outcomes, adjacent but not exact environments, and laboratory scope. Caution is needed when extrapolating beyond environments with *direct* evidence to those with only *adjacent* or *contextual* support.
