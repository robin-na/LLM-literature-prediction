# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists predominantly of **empirical, experimental lab studies** on public goods games (PGGs) and their direct variants, complemented by a smaller subset of field and observational studies, and a few mechanism/theory arguments. The breadth covers a wide spectrum of design choices: group size, institution types (peer, leader, third-party punishment; reward; exclusion), and payoffs. However, **the base is narrow with respect to direct evidence for the *downstream prediction task*:** relatively few papers report both control (punishment-disabled) and treatment (punishment-enabled) group efficiency, and many focus solely on behavioral outcomes like cooperation or punishment frequency. Several studies use adjacent games (e.g., threshold PGGs, contest games, networked dilemmas, prisioner's dilemma) or focus only on variations in norms, context, or psychological factors.

**Summary:** The evidence pool is strong for PGG behavior and mechanism insight, but only a limited subset addresses the precise prediction target (efficiency change when peer punishment is enabled in a PGG).

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact:** Multiple studies (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Ozono et al., 2020; Harrell, 2019) use standard linear or binary PGGs with or without punishment and reward.
- **close/adjacent:** A number explore close variants (threshold games, team contests, ROSCA games, repeated PDs with network structures), which may inform but are not identical to classic PGGs (e.g., Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018; Koike et al., 2018).
- **adjacent/weak/none:** Some studies are further removed (real-world norm enforcement, dictator/trust games, field/ethnographic studies).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact:** Many core studies (Kamijo et al., 2020; Ozono et al., 2020; Harrell, 2019, etc.) directly manipulate peer punishment as a treatment; several compare types (peer, leader, third-party, collective).
- **adjacent/weak:** Several focus on exclusion, restorative or alternative sanctions, or only discuss punishment contextually.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact:** A minority report group efficiency/total payoff directly (Kamijo et al., 2020; Ozono et al., 2020; Harrell, 2019; Heine & Stenger, 2020; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018).
- **close:** Others infer welfare or payoff changes via group success (e.g., threshold games), or via reported earnings.
- **adjacent/weak:** The majority report behavioral outcomes only (contribution rate, cooperation), often inferring about efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes** (efficiency, total/group payoff, welfare, surplus, coins/earnings):
- Measured *directly* in a few high-relevance PGG studies (e.g., Kamijo et al., 2020; Ozono et al., 2020; Harrell, 2019; Heine & Stenger, 2020; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018).
- *Closely-related* outcomes include reported group success rates (Liao et al., 2021, retracted but design described), mean payoffs, or probability of public good being provided.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
- Much more common: contribution or cooperation rates, punishment frequency/intensity, norm compliance, prosocial choices, exclusion/voting, emotional/psychological states.
- Most studies reporting increased cooperation with punishment do *not* verify whether efficiency increases (because punishment is costly and can sometimes reduce net group welfare despite higher contribution).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Synthesis of Empirical Results

- **Punishment increases contribution/cooperation rates** in standard PGGs (Harrell, 2019; Lefebvre & Stenger, 2020; Weber et al., 2018). This does **not always translate into higher efficiency**, because the costs inflicted by punishment may outweigh the gains from greater cooperation (Kamijo et al., 2020; Heine & Stenger, 2020; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019; Li et al., 2018).
- **Efficiency effects of peer punishment are highly context-dependent**:
    - When the public good is locally or globally *inefficient* (MPCR<1), punishment alone does **not** increase efficiency; sometimes has a **null or even negative effect** due to cost (Kamijo et al., 2020; Ozono et al., 2020; Heine & Stenger, 2020).
    - In **team contest/threshold/competition environments**, enabling punishment can exacerbate inefficiency due to over-contribution, futile sanction cycles, or "feuds" between groups/strategies (Heine & Stenger, 2020; Gross & De Dreu, 2019).
    - In **multigenerational/threshold public goods** (Lohse & Waichman, 2020), peer punishment increases group success probability but *does not always improve efficiency* due to punishment costs offsetting benefits.
    - **Networked and heterogeneous environments** can affect efficacy. Sometimes punishment reduces the benefits of reciprocal structures (Li et al., 2018).
- **Comparison to reward:** Enabling peer reward (even cost-balanced) often has a *stronger, more robust positive effect on efficiency* than punishment alone—especially where pure cooperation does not maximize welfare (Kamijo et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2018).
- **Institutional design matters:** Centralized/leader punishment is *more efficient* than distributed peer punishment, because coordination/rent-dissipation/counter-punishment are minimized (Harrell, 2019).
- **Collective sanctions** (punishing all for one) are **inefficient and less fair**, dampening the positive effect of peer punishment (Chapkovski, 2021).
- **Identity effects**: Gender (Chapkovski, 2021), status, and local norms may moderate punishment effects, though these are less reliably mapped to efficiency.

## Mechanism and Theory Claims

- **Punishment is often deployed for norm enforcement** and supports cooperation, but its ability to enhance group welfare is limited by its costliness and application patterns.
- **Exclusion and restorative mechanisms** may outperform punishment for sustaining efficiency in some real-world and lab settings (Koike et al., 2018; Riedl et al., 2021; Wiessner, 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Baseline efficiency (control, no-punishment) is a critical moderator**:  
    - If *control efficiency is already high* (due to high MPCR, small group, or strong baseline cooperation), enabling punishment might yield *little or no marginal efficiency gain*, and sometimes reduces welfare due to added costs.
    - If *control efficiency is low* (due to low MPCR, misaligned incentives, or large/heterogeneous groups), peer punishment may increase cooperation but the net efficiency effect strongly depends on the cost-impact ratio and frequency of counterproductive sanctioning.
- **Design dimensions with strong evidence:**
    - **MPCR and local incentive structure:** If MPCR<1 or local incentives are not aligned, do *not* expect peer punishment to increase efficiency (Kamijo et al., 2020; Ozono et al., 2020).
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness ratios:** Only low-cost, high-impact designs offer *potential* for efficiency gains; high-cost or ineffective punishment erodes or negates efficiency benefits (Heine & Stenger, 2020; Bond, 2019; Li et al., 2018).
    - **Institution type:** Peer punishment is less efficient than well-functioning centralized punishment (Harrell, 2019). Collective sanctions and rule-based impersonal punishment generally *do not* raise efficiency (Chapkovski, 2021; Koike et al., 2018).
    - **Reward-exists:** When reward and punishment are both enabled, efficiency is almost always higher in the reward condition; reward-only outperforms punishment-only (Kamijo et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2018).
    - **Game length and repetition:** Limited direct evidence, but longer games may allow punishment-driven norms to persist; however, efficiency gains are usually not cumulative and may fade if punishment costs accumulate (Lefebvre & Stenger, 2020).
- **Sparse or ambiguous dimensions:** Effects of **chat, contributor default, visibility/feedback (show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, showPunishmentId)** are *rarely isolated*; most studies fix these and do not analyze variation.

**Overall, for the downstream prediction task:**  
- **Do not assume efficiency gains from peer punishment unless** (a) MPCR is high/above threshold and (b) punishment cost is low relative to impact.
- Expect *no significant change or even reduced efficiency* in environments with inefficient public goods, high punishment costs, or contest/competition structures.
- When both reward and punishment can be enabled, **reward is much more reliably pro-efficiency**.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed by Multiple Papers:**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (ratio, actor, anonymity), `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`
- *These are typically specified and varied across studies, with direct evidence on their role in modulating punishment's effect on efficiency.*

**Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
- `all_or_nothing` (most use continuous choice, though binary/binary-threshold are tested)
- `chat` (rarely manipulated, typically absent to avoid communication effects)
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` (often held constant, with some exceptions, but not usually analyzed as moderators)

**Functionally Missing or Sparse:**
- `default_contrib` (whether contribution is opt-in vs opt-out—almost never varied or discussed, little or no evidence for its effect on efficiency change with punishment)
- `reward_tech` (`reward_magnitude`, impact per reward cost—less directly examined, though peer vs impersonal reward is discussed in a few studies)
- Effects of feedback/visibility and identification of punishers are relatively unexplored as causal moderators.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Many studies report only behavioral outcomes (contribution rates), not efficiency, and evidence for efficiency must be inferred**—potentially misleading if punishment is costly and overused.
- **Control (punishment-disabled) baseline efficiency is often not reported**, complicating absolute treatment effect estimation.
- **Parameter space (design dimensions) is not fully covered or orthogonally varied**; most studies use small groups, short games, and fixed/no communication.
- **Heterogeneity in punishment cost/impact is poorly mapped**—few studies systematically contrast different punishment tech (cost/impact, anonymity).
- **Cultural and psychological moderators** (social context, group status, emotion, reputation) are highlighted as important but rarely quantitatively tied to efficiency changes.
- **Potential for publication/reporting bias** against reporting null or negative efficiency results in peer punishment conditions.
- **External validity is limited for large, real-world populations**, as most experiments use small, relatively homogeneous lab samples.
- **Adjacent and mechanism studies (e.g., exclusion, restorative justice, reward) provide important context** but are not fully substitutable for direct empirical estimates of punishment-on-efficiency in standard PGGs.
- **Structural dimensions like dynamic networks, self-reliance options, and exclusion are contextually important** (Gross et al., 2020; Koike et al., 2018), but *effects are not consistently measured as changes from punishment-disabled control*.

---

**In summary:**  
- The body of evidence most strongly supports that the efficiency effect of peer punishment in public goods games is context-dependent and often weak, null, or negative unless design parameters strongly favor efficiency gains (high MPCR, low punishment costs, well-coordinated exclusion, absence of over-sanctioning). Reward, when available, delivers more robust efficiency improvements. Many moderating design dimensions remain underexplored with respect to efficiency even as their behavioral effects are well mapped. Prediction of efficiency gains from enabling punishment should err on the conservative side unless the above positive conditions are explicitly met and control efficiency is low.
