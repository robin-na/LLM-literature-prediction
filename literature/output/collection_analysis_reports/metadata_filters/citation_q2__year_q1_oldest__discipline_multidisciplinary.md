# 1) Evidence Base

The current paper set consists of **13 papers** with a mix of empirical laboratory and field experiments, simulation/modeling, and theory or mechanism-focused reviews. The empirical evidence base includes several **lab experiments using public goods games (PGGs) or closely related economic games** (e.g., Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Chung et al., 2011; Ma et al., 2012; Clavien et al., 2012). Several papers provide **theoretical or agent-based modeling** perspectives tailored to public goods dynamics or adjacent resource-sharing environments (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Galan et al., 2011; Duénez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012; Jaffe, 2008; Szilagyi & Somogyi, 2010).

The scope of the literature is **narrow in the number of studies with exact relevance**, as only a few papers combine (i) exact PGG structure, (ii) punishment or sanctions as a manipulation, and (iii) efficiency or payoff outcomes. The remainder support mechanisms, provide adjacent context, or focus on non-payoff behavioral outcomes. There is a **strong foundation for establishing the likelihood and boundaries of punishment effects in PGGs**, but **comprehensive dimension-by-dimension quantitative prediction** (across all 14 predictors) is not uniformly possible with this set.

# 2) Task Relevance

### PGG_or_variant
- **Exact relevance:** Several papers use exact or very close PGGs (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Chung et al., 2011).
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** A number of papers use PGG-like or networked resource-sharing settings (Galan et al., 2011; Duénez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012; Jaffe, 2008).
- **Weak/none:** Some analyze very different behavioral games (ultimatum, dictator, or real-world networks) or entirely conceptual contexts (Clavien et al., 2012; Hock & Fefferman, 2011; Tobeña, 2009; Szilagyi & Somogyi, 2010).

### Punishment_or_sanctions
- **Exact relevance:** Direct manipulation of punishment or sanctioning is addressed in Eriksson & Strimling, 2012 (empirically), Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Galan et al., 2011; Duénez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012; Jaffe, 2008 (all theoretically).
- **Close/adjacent:** Some papers address punishment-like rejection (Ma et al., 2012) or inherent policing but not as an experimental manipulation (Kümmerli, 2011).
- **None:** Some papers lack a punishment or sanction component (Chung et al., 2011; Szilagyi & Somogyi, 2010).

### Efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome
- **Exact:** Only a few papers directly report **efficiency, group payoff, or related payoff outcomes under punishments** (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Duénez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012).
- **Close/adjacent:** Some derive or discuss expected payoffs theoretically (Galan et al., 2011), but most focus on **cooperation rates, punishment behavior, or norm compliance**.
- **Weak/none:** Several report **only behavioral or attitudinal results**, or do not include efficiency/welfare measures.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (central to prediction task):**
  - **Total group payoff/efficiency/welfare**: Directly measured in some PGG lab experiments (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012), modeled in theory work (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Duénez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012), and estimated in close variants.
  - **Marginal per-capita return (MPCR), payoffs per round**: Sometimes reported as contextual parameters.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
  - **Contribution rate/cooperation rate:** Frequently recorded (Galan et al., 2011; Jaffe, 2008).
  - **Punishment frequency/intensity:** Explored in theoretical/behavioral models.
  - **Norm compliance/rejection rates:** Measured in adjacent games (Ma et al., 2012), voting against norm violators (Clavien et al., 2012).
  - **Punishment motivation or psychological correlates:** Explored in Tobeña, 2009; Ma et al., 2012.

> **Distinction:** Although behavioral outcomes (e.g., increased cooperation) are correlated with efficiency, only a subset of the literature actually measures or models efficiency/payoff directly, as required by the prediction task.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical and theoretical support for the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG(-like) games is present, but nuanced:**

- **Strong external punishment institutions can substantially increase group efficiency,** pushing payoffs close to the social optimum, especially in groups that otherwise show low baseline cooperation. Self-imposed or weak punishment is often insufficient to yield efficiency gains, particularly among initially non-cooperative groups (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).
- **Threshold/phase transition effects:** The efficiency gains from punishment may occur nonlinearly; only when punishment is sufficiently strong/reachable (e.g., high coverage, low cost, strong negative payoff for defectors) does efficiency increase markedly. Too weak or too localized punishment can result in little to no efficiency gains (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Duénez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012).
- **Network structure matters:** High clustering and interconnectedness in networked games (agent-based PGG-like models) significantly increase time spent in cooperative (and thus high expected payoff) states. Conversely, networks with low clustering or high average degree may experience norm collapse, reducing efficiency (Galan et al., 2011).
- **Role of benefit to punishers:** Even a small benefit to punishers (e.g., status/reputation or minor payment) can help achieve “righteousness”, stabilizing cooperative equilibria and sustaining higher collective payoffs (Duénez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012).
- **Absence of direct efficiency effect in some settings:** Several papers without explicit punishment mechanisms (Chung et al., 2011; Szilagyi & Somogyi, 2010) or with punishment as a contextual feature (Kümmerli, 2011; Clavien et al., 2012) provide baselines or help explain mechanisms, but cannot directly inform the effect of punishment on efficiency.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For the specific task—predicting the average efficiency of a PGG (or close variant) with peer punishment enabled, given design dimensions and control efficiency—the literature provides the following guidance:**

- **Expect efficiency to increase, often substantially, when strong external punishment is enabled in standard PGGs**, provided that the punishment is sufficiently severe, the enforcement is broad/reliable, and the cost-to-impact ratio is favorable (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012).
- **The magnitude of efficiency gain is not guaranteed for all punishment implementations.** Weak, local, costly, or endogenous punishment produces little or no efficiency gain, especially with non-cooperative groups (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012). Baseline levels of cooperation in the control game can moderate the effect size—low initial efficiency implies more potential gain.
- **Sharp nonlinearities may occur:** Efficiency may remain close to baseline (control) until punishment crosses a strength/reach threshold, at which point efficiency approaches the social optimum (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Duénez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012).
- **Key design dimensions influencing prediction, as supported by evidence, are:** punishment cost, punishment coverage/tech (reach and reliability), magnitude/severity of punishment, and whether the institution is externally imposed or self-set.
- **Dimensions not addressed (e.g., chat, reward mechanisms, visibility attributes)** cannot be reliably included in quantitative predictions using only the present paper set.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed dimensions:**
  - **player_count, num_rounds, mpcr:** Frequently specified in empirical and theoretical models.
  - **punishment_cost, punishment_tech (coverage/reach, severity):** **Strongly** emphasized in both empirical and theoretical work as moderators/threshold setters (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Bodnar & Salathé, 2012; Duénez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012).
  - **all_or_nothing:** Sometimes specified as a variant or operationalization.
- **Indirectly/contextually discussed:**
  - **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Considered in adjacent theoretical models (Duénez-Guzmán & Sadedin, 2012).
  - **show_punishment_id:** Only contextually discussed (Clavien et al., 2012), not connected to efficiency.
- **Effectively missing or not addressed:**
  - **chat:** Not examined in conjunction with punishment and efficiency.
  - **default_contrib:** Not explicitly varied or analyzed.
  - **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds:** Only present in control settings (Chung et al., 2011), not linked to punishment effects.
  - **reward_magnitude:** Not isolated as an experimental parameter for punishment prediction.
- **Dimensions covered empirically, but not with efficiency outcomes:** Some dimensions (e.g., visibility of punishers, framing, or institution generation) are referenced in non-efficiency contexts or in adjacent games.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited direct evidence on efficiency outcomes:** Only a subset of the papers actually report efficiency or group payoff, especially for the difference between punishment-off and punishment-on conditions in PGGs. Many others focus on behavior or mechanisms, limiting quantitative calibration.
- **Sparse/absent coverage of several design dimensions:** For many dimensions (chat, default_contrib, information/visibility conditions), the available evidence is either contextual or missing, impeding fine-grained prediction.
- **Parameter specificity and external validity:** While some findings are robust in lab PGGs, threshold and phase-transition effects are often **theoretical or simulation-based**, and generalizability to all PGG variants or broader settings may not hold.
- **Confounding of institution origin and strength:** Evidence suggests externally imposed institutions have larger efficiency effects than self-governed ones, but empirical cases of endogenous peer punishment leading to high efficiency are rare in the set.
- **Payoff-behavior distinction:** Many papers blur or do not precisely distinguish between cooperation rate and efficiency, or use the former as a proxy for welfare, which is **problematic for payoff-based prediction**.
- **Gaps in network structure and real-world translation:** While some modeling addresses the impact of network structure, these results rely on modeled rather than measured efficiency, with limited real-world empirical corroboration.

---

**Summary:**  
**Prediction of efficiency effects from enabling peer punishment in PGGs is strongly and directly supported _only under certain institutional and parametric conditions_ in this literature set. The main drivers of efficiency improvement are external imposition, severity/reach of punishment, and cost structures. Evidence is sparse for many other design dimensions, and the connection between behavioral and payoff outcomes, while correlated, is not always direct or reliable for prediction without further empirical data or context.**
