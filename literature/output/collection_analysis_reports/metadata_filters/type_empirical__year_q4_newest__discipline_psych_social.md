# 1) Evidence Base

**Empirical vs. Theory:**  
The paper set is overwhelmingly empirical, dominated by lab experiments and some field studies. There are no formal theory or mechanism-only papers included, though many papers discuss mechanisms as part of empirical interpretation. The evidence base is broad in coverage, encompassing over 100 experimental studies and several meta-analyses or systematic reviews, plus contextual and ethnographic observations.

**Breadth and Focus for Prediction Task:**  
- **Narrow (Direct) Evidence:** A substantial subset of papers reports direct, within-lab estimates of efficiency (payoff-based outcomes) in standard or variant Public Goods Games (PGGs) with punishment enabled versus disabled (e.g., Zhang et al., 2024; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Sparks et al., 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Eichenseer, 2023).
- **Broad (Indirect/Contextual) Evidence:** Many studies report on related outcome spaces (contribution rates, punishment behavior, norm compliance) or use adjacent game structures (common-pool resource games, trust games, ultimatum games, etc.), yielding important but less directly transferable insight.
- **Meta-analytic Synthesis:** At least two major meta-analyses (Eichenseer, 2023; Jin et al., 2025) aggregate results across studies, strengthening conclusions about average effects and relevant moderators.

**Summary:**  
The evidence base is broad but uneven: there is a solid foundation for prediction grounded in empirical, experimental data on punishment effects in PGGs, with mixed findings about efficiency. However, detailed evidence is sometimes clustered around canonical parameterizations, with less direct coverage for many game design dimension combinations. Substantial evidence is also adjacent—behavioral, payoff-adjacent, or based on closely related game forms.

# 2) Task Relevance

## a) PGG or Variant  
- **Exact relevance:** Most of the key cited empirical studies are standard PGGs or minimal variants. Meta-analyses also focus on classic PGGs or very close structural relatives.
- **Close relevance:** Studies using common-pool resource (CPR) games, threshold goods, team investment, and minimum effort games supply adjacent but not identical evidence (e.g., van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Lec et al., 2023).
- **Weak/none:** A few field studies and psychological experiments are only loosely related (e.g., wider social norm studies, third-party sanctioning outside PGG context).

## b) Punishment or Sanctions  
- **Exact:** Numerous experiments directly manipulate the presence/absence and form of peer or centralized punishment (including peer, leader, third-party, collective, and institutionalized punishment).
- **Close:** Some studies focus on alternative or adjacent mechanisms (reward, pre-commitment devices, institutional design, or endogenous institution choice), but still manipulate a sanctioning mechanism.
- **Adjacent/weak:** Several studies examine norm enforcement or punishment behavior in dictator or trust games, or report only on informal or reputational sanctions.

## c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome  
- **Exact:** Several key papers measure efficiency or group average payoff as the *primary* outcome, comparing no-punishment to punishment conditions. Some meta-analyses also include efficiency estimates.
- **Close:** Many papers report group earnings, welfare, surplus, or similar, but sometimes only for subsamples or with incomplete mapping to optimal.
- **Adjacent:** A large number of studies report contribution rates, cooperation, or norm compliance only, not payoffs—these must be distinguished as only indirectly informative for efficiency prediction.
- **Weak/none:** Papers solely focused on behavioral, attitudinal, or contextual moderators, or on punishment behavior per se, without any measured or reported payoff/efficiency outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related Outcomes (direct relevance):**
  - Group efficiency (group payoff as share of potential maximum)
  - Group or average earnings, welfare, surplus
- **Payoff-adjacent Outcomes:**
  - Group welfare proxied through resource sustainability, compliance (in CPR or tax games)
  - Comparative profit or success rate (when mapped to group task completion/thresholds)
- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
  - Contribution rate, cooperation frequency, norm compliance
  - Punishment frequency, severity, target selection (prosocial/antisocial)
  - Voting for institution type, preference for punisher/rewarder roles
  - Emotional/psychological factors (anger, guilt, reputation)
  - Social evaluations and attitudinal measures

**Note:** Many papers explicitly caution that increased cooperation (behavioral) does not always translate to increased efficiency (payoff), especially when punishment is costly or misapplied.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of Empirical Findings:**  
- **Punishment likely to increase efficiency in many PGG environments,** but *not universally*. Cost and targeting matter (Zhang et al., 2024; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Joseph et al., 2025).
- **Costly punishment may reduce or not improve efficiency,** even when increasing cooperation, if costs outweigh cooperation gains or are frequently misapplied (Botelho et al., 2022; Gross et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).
- **Effectiveness depends on punishment details:**  
  - **Magnitude/cost ratio:** Large (high-impact) punishment increases efficiency; small/ineffective or overly costly punishment may reduce it (Ye et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2025).
  - **Targeting and anti-social punishment:** Ineffective or poorly targeted punishment (incl. anti-social punishment) can erode efficiency (Gross et al., 2022; Chen, D'Attoma et al., 2025).
  - **Monitoring and information:** Punishment is only effective when defectors can be accurately identified (Eisenkopf & Walter, 2022; Chen, D'Attoma et al., 2025).
  - **Institutional design:** Formal/centralized punishment, collective punishment with participation/commitment, and democratic processes can further increase efficiency—but effects depend on the effectiveness of institution design and context (Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Duell et al., 2024; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
  - **Contextual moderators:** Social conflict, transparency, group openness, network structure, and endowment inequality alter whether punishment raises efficiency (Gross et al., 2022; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Chen, D'Attoma et al., 2025).
- **Temporal dynamics:** Efficiency gains from punishment often manifest only after initial rounds, as groups learn to cooperate and punishment use declines (Sparks et al., 2024; Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
- **Reward as alternative:** Well-designed (e.g., majority-vote) reward mechanisms can sometimes increase efficiency as much or more than punishment (Peng, 2022; Eichenseer, 2023).

**Meta-analytic and Cross-study Patterns:**  
- **Meta-analyses confirm positive effects on cooperation,** but payoff/efficiency effects are weaker or sometimes null/negative due to the cost of punishment (Eichenseer, 2023; Jin et al., 2025).
- **Peer punishment generally more effective than leader-only or centralized punishment,** but at risk of inefficiency unless cost is well-calibrated and misuse is limited (Eichenseer, 2023).

**Conflicts and Ambiguities:**  
- **Some settings report reduced efficiency with punishment,** especially when costly, mistrusted, or implemented in settings with pre-existing cooperation or informal sanctions (Botelho et al., 2022; Milinski & Marotzke, 2022).
- Contextual and cross-cultural moderators can reverse the effect or suppress benefits (Gross et al., 2022; Goto & Matsui, 2025; Angelsen & Naime, 2024).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Recommendation:**  
- **If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is known and the design matches canonical PGGs with moderate group size (3–12), repeated rounds (10+), peer punishment, one-to-many deduction, and moderate MPCR,** enabling well-calibrated, effective punishment usually increases efficiency, especially in repeated games (Zhang et al., 2024; Sparks et al., 2024; Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
- **If punishment is costly, poorly targeted, or anti-social punishment rates are high,** efficiency impacts may be null or negative—especially in contexts with high baseline cooperation or when punishment is overused (Botelho et al., 2022; Gross et al., 2022).
- **Small punishment (low impact or high cost) may not raise efficiency and can even reduce it,** while large punishment (high impact relative to cost) is more reliably efficiency-enhancing (Ye et al., 2023; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2025).
- **The impact of punishment depends critically on design details:** punishment cost/impact, monitoring technology, information transparency, group structure (size, openness), repeatedness, and the availability of reward or alternative sanctioning (Eichenseer, 2023; Duell et al., 2024; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
- **Analogy-based adjustment from non-payoff measures** (e.g., increase in cooperation) is risky, but can provide qualitative directional cues; however, only use such inference when direct efficiency data are absent and with explicit caution.

**Dimension-specific guidance:**  
- **MPCR:** Lower MPCR games (high conflict-of-interest) are harder to sustain cooperation in and may require stronger or better-targeted punishment to realize an efficiency gain (Jin et al., 2024).
- **Player count:** Larger groups may benefit more from punishment, provided monitoring and coordination are not degraded (Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
- **Punishment technology:** Peer punishment with a reasonable cost-to-impact ratio (e.g., 1:3) works better for efficiency than leader-only or very low-cost (which can invite anti-social punishment) (Eichenseer, 2023; Chen et al., 2025).
- **Communication:** Absence of chat or public deliberation makes punishment more necessary to boost efficiency, but when high-quality communication is present, it can substitute or augment punishment effects (Adams et al., 2022).
- **Endowment transparency:** Effectiveness of punishment in increasing efficiency is higher when individual endowments are known (Chen, D'Attoma et al., 2025).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- **player_count:** Many studies specify and manipulate group size; evidence suggests a moderating effect on group coordination and efficiency with punishment.
- **num_rounds:** Most punishment experiments are in repeated games; positive efficiency effects of punishment often require multiple rounds.
- **mpcr (marginal per-capita return):** Varies across studies and is shown to interact with the impact of punishment.
- **all_or_nothing / continuous:** Both are used; findings generalize well across, but binary choices (all-or-nothing) may yield stronger effects for punishment.
- **punishment_cost & punishment_tech (cost/impact):** Crucially reported and discussed; the cost-effectiveness balance of punishment is repeatedly identified as critical.
- **chat (communication):** Frequently manipulated; absence vs. presence of chat colors the role and necessity of punishment.
- **reward_exists / reward_cost:** Some direct comparisons of reward vs. punishment, especially in meta-analyses and specialized studies.
- **show_other_summaries / show_n_rounds:** Feedback structures sometimes explicitly studied (e.g., transparency, reporting of endowments).

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**  
- **default_contrib:** Only occasionally referenced, and not systematically manipulated.
- **show_punishment_id:** Some studies report effects of anonymity vs. identified sanctioners; less systematic evidence.
- **punishment_magnitude (distinct from cost):** Often implicitly included in cost-to-impact ratios, but rarely isolated as the sole manipulated variable.

**Effectively Missing or Uninformative:**  
- **reward_tech / reward_magnitude:** Discussed in studies that include reward, but not universally measured; effects in absence of punishment are clearer than joint effects.
- **some feedback/reporting options** (e.g., round display, peer outcome summaries) are only measured occasionally.
- **Complex institutional features** (e.g., endogenous institution choice, democracy, competitive vs. centralized sanctioning) are studied but do not cover the full range of prediction dimensions.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse reporting of joint parameter variations:** Most PGG-with-punishment studies hold key dimensions (group size, MPCR, cost, monitoring) fixed; fewer systematically vary multiple dimensions.
- **Limited coverage of all game design dimensions:** Certain prediction variables (e.g., default_contrib, reward_magnitude, id reporting) are underexplored.
- **Payoff-behavior disconnect:** Many papers report only cooperation/contribution rates (not efficiency), limiting quantitative inference. Inferring from behavioral to payoff outcomes is known to be hazardous, especially when punishment is costly or misapplied.
- **Contextual and cultural variability:** Some robust effects (e.g., migration/openness, history of conflict, endowment transparency, information accuracy) suggest that context can moderate or reverse average effects, and these are often only explored in specific subpopulations or settings.
- **Short time horizons:** Many repeated games are limited to 10–40 rounds, and long-term equilibrium effects may not be fully observed.
- **Absence of field studies and real-world institutions:** Most evidence is lab-based, with controlled but artificial settings; generalizability to field contexts is probable but not guaranteed.
- **Reporting bias and null results:** Some meta-analytic evidence suggests published studies preferentially report positive effects; negative or null effects (e.g., punishment not boosting efficiency) may be underreported in the literature.
- **Over-representation of canonical PGGs:** Strongest evidence applies to classic linear or binary PGG designs; generalization to novel or hybrid structures is less certain.
- **Ambiguity or contradiction:** Some studies directly contradict each other on the direction of the punishment efficiency effect under similar parameters (e.g., Botelho et al., 2022 vs. Zhang et al., 2024).

---

**Summary:**  
The literature provides a strong empirical foundation for predicting the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in standard PGG laboratory environments, with moderation by group size, round structure, the cost/effectiveness of punishment, information transparency, and institutional context. However, not all design dimensions are equally covered, and efficiency effects can be negative or absent if punishment is costly, misapplied, or poorly targeted. Prediction should be calibrated to the specifics of each design, and ambiguity or contextual dependencies should be noted, especially when generalizing beyond well-studied or canonical environments.
