# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set covers 681 sources, constituting an unusually broad and deep literature base on punishment, cooperation, and efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. The mix includes a large number of high-quality empirical laboratory experiments (with canonical linear and nonlinear PGGs, variants, threshold public goods, and CPR games), many agent-based models and theoretical analyses, and a substantial number of field and framed-field experiments. There is strong representation of studies varying the full range of prediction dimensions, and meta-studies, reviews, and cross-contextual syntheses are present. Both classic and innovative institutional arrangements (peer punishment, centralized punishment, endogenous institution formation, ostracism/exclusion, reputation systems, etc.) are well covered.

Empirical findings (especially from controlled lab experiments) dominate the evidence directly relevant for payoff and efficiency outcomes. Extensive theory work complements this, often making explicit mechanism-based or equilibrium predictions about efficiency as a function of game design, punishment architecture, and baseline conditions. Some adjacent and weakly relevant sources provide context on related behavioral phenomena, reputation, non-payoff motivation, and social perception, but these are generally not central to the prediction task.

Overall, the paper set is robust for the core task of predicting efficiency effects of punishment across a wide range of public goods game designs, with both convergent and divergent findings under various conditions.

# 2) Task Relevance

The literature's relevance to the prediction task is assessed on three criteria:

**a) pgg_or_variant:**  
Relevance is **exact** for the majority of empirical and theoretical studies, which use standard linear or threshold public goods games, voluntary contribution mechanisms, or close variants (CPRs, trust games, coordination games with sanctioning). Some studies are adjacent (repeated PD, indirect reciprocity, contests with PGG-like features), and a minority are weak or not relevant.

**b) punishment_or_sanctions:**  
Relevance is **exact** in the core of the literature that manipulates the presence, type, or technology of punishment or sanctioning stages (peer punishment, centralized, exclusion/ostracism, taxes/fines, reputation-based punishment). Some studies cover adjacent mechanisms (minimum standards, reward frameworks, partner choice as punishment, etc.) or adjacent social sanctions (perceived disapproval), while a subset are weak in explicit punishment focus.

**c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**   
Relevance is **exact** in a substantial proportion of the lab and field experiments, as well as theory papers that directly model group efficiency (total earnings/profit, welfare, or surplus as a share of the cooperative optimum). Many additional studies report closely related outcomes (group payoff, welfare, or loss probability as in collective-risk dilemmas). Some sources only report contributions, cooperation rates, or punishment behavior without directly measuring efficiency; these are **adjacent** or **weak** for the main outcome.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based outcomes (most relevant):**  
   - Efficiency: group earnings/payoff as a fraction of the full-cooperation optimum (most common and directly relevant).
   - Group profit, welfare, surplus, or total coins generated: these are closely related and sometimes directly mapped to efficiency.
   - Success rates or avoidance of group loss in threshold/collective-risk games: often used as a practical efficiency proxy.
   - Distributional outcomes (e.g., equality/inequality, payoff variance) are sometimes reported alongside efficiency.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (less relevant):**
   - Contribution/cooperation rates: changes in average contributions or frequency of cooperative choices.
   - Punishment frequency/intensity: how often and how much players punish, and to whom.
   - Norm compliance, trust, ostracism votes, fairness, and related social/moral measures.
   - Social/economic attitudes, perceptions, or neural measures linked to punishment.

Studies differ widely in whether they report only non-payoff behavioral outcomes or directly report efficiency/payoff/earnings, and this distinction is crucial. Some studies with only behavioral data are informative for moderators and mechanisms, but should not be conflated with direct evidence on efficiency effects.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## a) Typical Effects of Enabling Punishment

- **Canonical linear PGGs:**  
  - Peer punishment robustly increases contributions and can increase or decrease efficiency depending on the cost-to-impact ratio, frequency and targeting of punishment, and design features.
  - In many labs, especially for small groups, moderate-cost, high-impact punishment (e.g., 1:3 cost:effect) increases efficiency, especially over longer games (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Waichman, 2020).
  - However, when antisocial or mis-targeted punishment is prevalent, or when punishment is costly relative to its effect, the net efficiency effect can be neutral or negative (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Dannenberg, 2016; Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021).

- **Endogenous and centralized punishment:**  
  - Centralized or pool punishment commonly achieves higher or more stable efficiency, especially when punishment is fair, not too costly, and targets defectors (Castillo et al., 2021; Wang & Qin, 2015; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
  - Centralized, probabilistic, or tax-based sanctions with full observability are often more effective than peer punishment at increasing efficiency, particularly in large or heterogeneous groups (Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Zhang & Pei, 2022).

- **Reward vs. punishment:**  
  - Reward mechanisms, when as cost-effective as punishment, sometimes achieve equal or higher efficiency (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Kamijo et al., 2020; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019).

## b) Moderators and Conditional Effects

- **Group Size:**  
  - The effect of punishment on efficiency varies with group size and structure. Larger groups often dilute the deterrence effect (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017) unless centralization or improved monitoring is present.

- **Number of Rounds (Game Length):**  
  - Efficiency losses from punishment are often frontloaded (as punishment is common early and declines with stabilized cooperation; Frey & Rusch, 2012; Waichman, 2020).
  - Longer games and stable group membership favor positive efficiency effects (Frey & Rusch, 2012).

- **Information and Transparency:**  
  - Efficiency gains require sufficient information for punishment to be accurately targeted. Incomplete or noisy monitoring reduces or reverses the efficiency benefit (van Miltenburg et al., 2017; De Geest & Kingsley, 2019; Salahshour et al., 2022).
  - Communication (chat) frequently increases both contributions and efficiency, and often mitigates the negative side effects of punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Koch et al., 2021).

- **Punishment Technology (Cost, Impact, Randomness):**  
  - High cost/low impact (e.g., 1:1 or worse) typically yields negative or null efficiency effects; high effectiveness (high fine per cost) is necessary for punishment to improve efficiency (Roberts, 2013; Wang et al., 2015; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Kranz, 2010).
  - Noise in punishment or mis-targeting (e.g., due to error or network effects) lead to lower or even negative efficiency effects, driven by excessive or antisocial punishment (Salahshour et al., 2022; Fischer et al., 2016).

- **Group Composition and Social Context:**  
  - Heterogeneity in endowment, returns, or group composition moderates the effect. Efficiency benefits are greatest when punishment effectiveness aligns with endowment (Waichman, 2020), and weakest or negative when norm targeting is difficult, as in mixed or diverse groups (Mantilla et al., 2021).

- **Institutional Design:**  
  - Democratic or participatory institution formation (e.g., voting on punishment institution) often achieves higher or more sustained efficiency (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Vasconcelos et al., 2022).
  - Legitimacy and visibility of punishment, central authority vs. peer, anti-social punishment, role rotation, and matching technology are all documented moderators (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Grimm & Mengel, 2011; Angelovski et al., 2018).

- **Production Technology and Game Structure:**  
  - In non-linear returns (weakest-link/minimum, best-shot, or threshold games), the efficiency effect of punishment is highly sensitive and often larger than in linear games, especially when complementarity is strong (Fatas et al., 2015; Karakostas et al., 2023; Kurokawa, 2019; Fatas et al., 2020).

- **Adjacent and non-payoff effects:**  
  - In some field settings, and in games with weak or symbolic punishment, the behavioral mechanisms for cooperation may not map onto payoffs in a way that increases efficiency (Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013; de Melo & Piaggio, 2015).
  - Reputation and social information can sometimes substitute for, or attenuate, punishment's effect (Kamei & Putterman, 2018; Koike et al., 2018).

- **Risk of negative or neutral effects:**  
  - In environments with strong baseline efficiency and existing social norms, adding costly punishment can reduce efficiency (Javaid et al., 2015; Vollan et al., 2019; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Robbett, 2016).
  - Antisocial punishment, retaliation, extortion, and group conflict can transform punishment's effect from positive to negative (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Szekely et al., 2020; Barron & Guo, 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Rule:**  
Prediction of treatment efficiency (with punishment enabled) from design dimensions and control efficiency should be *moderated by* the effectiveness, cost, targeting, and institution type of punishment as specified by the 14 prediction dimensions.

**Directionality of Effect:**  
- When **control efficiency is low**, and the punishment regime is well-targeted (high-impact/low-cost, minimal noise, low antisocial punishment, group composition supports norm targeting, and at least moderate rounds and stable structure):  
  → Enabling punishment is likely to yield a substantial positive shift in efficiency (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Waichman, 2020).

- When **control efficiency is high** or norm compliance is already strong (e.g., high information, strong local norms, or public feedback):  
  → Enabling punishment may leave efficiency unchanged or even reduce it due to punishment costs (Javaid et al., 2015; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016).

- If **punishment cost is high relative to impact**, punishment is noisy/ineffective, or there is high antisocial punishment or retaliation:  
  → Enabling punishment may reduce efficiency (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Salahshour et al., 2022; Fatas & Mateu, 2015).

- If punishment is **centralized, well-targeted, and cost-effective** (e.g., centralized authority, tax-based sanction with broad coverage), efficiency gain is more likely, especially in larger or more heterogeneous groups (Castillo et al., 2021; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019).

- **Network structure, group composition, and social context** (e.g., ethnic divides, social inequality, antagonism, or high competitive pressure) can diminish, reverse, or even accentuate the positive effects, depending on the alignment of punishment with group cooperation norms and the presence of prosocial or antisocial targeting.

**Special Design Dimension Considerations:**
- **show_punishment_id**: If punisher identity is revealed, efficiency gains only occur in some group sizes and settings; may increase antisocial punishment in others (Zheng & Nie, 2013).
- **chat (communication)**: Presence of chat/communication typically increases efficiency across all treatments, especially when paired with punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Koch et al., 2021).
- **punishment_tech (technology):** The type, mechanism, and targeting (peer vs. centralized, deterministic vs. stochastic, individual vs. collective, group network structure) are decisive.
- **reward_exists**: Enabling reward mechanisms, alone or with punishment, often improves efficiency more than punishment alone (Kamijo et al., 2020; Kocher & Matzat, 2016).
- **heterogeneity**: Inequality that is not accounted for in the punishment design can make punishment ineffective or negative for efficiency (Waichman, 2020; De Geest & Kingsley, 2019).

**Modeling Aspects:**
- Predicting treatment efficiency should use control efficiency as an anchor, adjusted up or down *conditional on* the design features summarized above.
- The canonical empirical benchmark is a +15–50 percentage point gain in efficiency when punishment is both cost-effective and necessary, but zero or negative when punishment is costly, antisocial, or redundant (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Clarke et al., 2015; Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
- In presence of both reward and punishment, model results suggest the effect is at least additive if not superadditive, but complex interactions (Kamijo et al., 2020).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Direct evidence for prediction from these dimensions:**
- **player_count**: Strongly supported; effect of group size on efficiency is repeatedly demonstrated (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017; Waichman, 2020).
- **num_rounds**: Strongly supported; game length moderates the time course and persistence of efficiency effects (Frey & Rusch, 2012).
- **chat**: Directly manipulated; communication consistently increases efficiency and often allows punishment to be more effective (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Kamei, 2019; Koch et al., 2021).
- **mpcr**: Primary axis of experimental variation; low-MPCR games show larger room for efficiency gains with punishment; high-MPCR can sometimes substitute for punishment (Kamijo et al., 2020; Fatas et al., 2015).
- **punishment_cost** and **punishment_tech**: Major moderators of efficiency; low-cost, high-effectiveness punishment has more positive effects (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
- **all_or_nothing**: Some studies vary all-or-nothing vs. continuous (Fatas et al., 2015); effect is secondary to cost structure and targeting.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Directly and indirectly manipulated; presence of reward often increases efficiency or can substitute for punishment (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Kamijo et al., 2020).
- **default_contrib**: Less frequently varied but present in some experiments with default framing.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Varied in several studies; evidence mixed but supports the importance of transparency and information structure.
- **punishment_tech (network structure):** Explicitly manipulated in spatial, heterogeneous, and networked games; documented as a moderator in how efficient punishment can be (Fischer et al., 2016; Shreedhar et al., 2020).

**Design features only contextually or weakly discussed:**
- **Heterogeneity of endowment or returns**, social identity, prior conflict, or local norms: Moderator roles are supported but mechanism may be context-specific and sometimes not include control/treatment pairings.

**Effectively missing or only theoretical:**
- A few dimensions, such as very high player counts or rare dynamic network rewiring rules, are only addressed in simulation/theory work, not in empirical studies.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Context Dependency**: While many modifiers are supported (cost-to-effect ratio, group size, noise, information quality, etc.), there are settings (e.g., high pre-existing efficiency, strong local norms, heterogeneous returns/endowments, network effects) where punishment can be neutral or negative for efficiency.
- **Behavioral vs. payoff outcomes**: Many papers report only on cooperation/contribution rates or punishment frequency, not efficiency. Behavioral findings do not necessarily translate to payoff improvement, especially when punishment is costly or anti-social.
- **Boundary conditions**: Some robust institutional effects (e.g., of democracy, centralization, redistribution, or rule choice) are only supported in close variants or theory, not all directly in canonical PGG lab experiments.
- **Non-linear, threshold, or fragile effects**: The relationship between punishment and efficiency is often non-monotonic; high punishment or poor targeting can generate cycles, antisocial punishment, or retaliation that erode efficiency.
- **Limited comparability across settings**: While design dimensions are varied, the joint distribution of all dimensions is sparse; most studies hold some dimensions fixed (e.g., only 4-player, only 10 rounds, only specific MPCRs).
- **Generalizability to field**: Lab findings may not always generalize to field, real-world, or very large-scale contexts, especially where reputational, legal, or informal sanctions dominate and where social structure is more complex (Romaniuc et al., 2022; Frey, 2019).
- **Sparse evidence on interaction effects**: Some theoretically important interactions (e.g., between communication and punishment, group heterogeneity and punishment, etc.) are less frequently manipulated factorially across studies.

**Summary:**  
The literature provides **strong, direct evidence** for the prediction of punishment effects on efficiency across a wide range of public goods game designs, with clear documentation of both facilitators and inhibitors of efficiency gains, well-supported by manipulations of the key design dimensions. However, predictions should preserve observed ambiguity and depend on the precise institutional, informational, and social context, not only on the presence or absence of a punishment stage. Use direct efficiency or group payoff results wherever possible; treat studies reporting only behavioral change as indirect or mechanistic support.
