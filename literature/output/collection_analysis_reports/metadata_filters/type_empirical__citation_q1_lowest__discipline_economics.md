# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is large and comprehensive (173 papers), comprising almost exclusively **experimental, empirical laboratory studies**, with a modest but notable minority of field experiments and a handful of observational studies. The evidence primarily centers on **public goods games (PGG) and closely related variants**, with extensive manipulation of punishment, reward, communication, and institutional design features. The core focus is experimental, with almost all papers providing direct or close empirical evidence rather than theory or mechanism-only arguments.

Within this evidence base, a substantial number of studies report on **payoff-based outcomes**—notably group efficiency, earnings, or welfare—using standard definitions (total payoff as a share of full-cooperation optimum). Others focus on behavioral correlates (contributions, compliance, punishment assigned), which, while relevant, are rigorously distinguished from efficiency in this analysis. The evidence set is **broad and multi-dimensional for the prediction task**, particularly for canonical linear/public goods games with and without peer punishment, but with variable coverage and strength for some game design dimensions and adjacent environments (e.g., CPRs, trust games, coalition games).

---

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**  
- **Relevance: exact or close**  
  The majority of included papers use either the canonical linear public goods game or a direct variant (binary, threshold, multi-level, or institutional adaptation). Evidence from adjacent social dilemmas (trust, CPR, coordination, coalition games) provides additional but less direct support.

**punishment_or_sanctions**  
- **Relevance: exact or close**  
  There is strong emphasis on experimental manipulation of punishment and sanctioning mechanisms: peer punishment, centralized/formal punishment, ostracism, exclusion, and to a lesser extent, reward and mixed sanction-reward settings. Most relevant studies provide conditions both with and without punishment, closely matching the core prediction scenario.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**  
- **Relevance: mixed**  
  A substantial subset of papers (**exact or close relevance**) report direct measures of group efficiency, average payoff, welfare, or surplus, enabling precise treatment effect estimates. However, many studies (**adjacent or weak relevance**) report only non-payoff behavioral outcomes, or focus on compliance/contribution without quantifying efficiency (especially for CPR/adjacent settings).

---

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes include:**
- **Efficiency:** Ratio of observed group earnings to the full-cooperation benchmark.
- **Group payoff/welfare/total earnings:** Direct sum of participant payoffs, often explicitly reported.
- **Surplus or coins generated:** Sometimes as normalized or absolute measures.
- **Related aggregate outcomes:** E.g., group-level welfare in CPR experiments, total market earnings in market/trust games.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (clearly distinguished):**
- **Contribution rates/cooperation rates:** Frequently measured, often reported alongside, but not interchangeable with, efficiency.
- **Punishment frequency/amount assigned, norm compliance rates:** Used to interpret mechanisms, not as primary efficiency indicators.
- **Voting behavior, exclusion/ostracism, communication content, strategies used:** Inform about institutional choices and behavioral moderation.
- **Inequality, trust/trustworthiness, reputation obtained, emotional responses:** Often secondary outcomes.

**Notable distinctions:**
- Several studies report **increases in contributions** with punishment but *no efficiency improvement* due to punishment costs (e.g., Chen, 2022; Glöckner et al., 2018; Casari & Tavoni, 2024).
- Some studies (especially with centralized or formal punishment) **directly report efficiency gains** from enabling punishment under specific institutional or parameter regimes (e.g., Kamei, 2024; Gürdal et al., 2021; Joseph et al., 2025).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical findings:

- **Punishment's effect on efficiency is context-dependent:**
  - **Punishment often increases efficiency** in standard, repeated PGGs when the punishment is well-targeted, moderate-cost, and anti-social punishment is rare or minimized (Kamei, 2024; Gürdal et al., 2021; Joseph et al., 2025; Jarungrattanapong, 2022; Lim & Zhang, 2020).
  - **Efficiency gains are not universal:** In many standard lab PGGs, **the cost of punishment offsets efficiency gains from increased cooperation**, resulting in **no net efficiency improvement, or even efficiency losses** (Fehr et al., 2010; Glöckner et al., 2018; Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Robbett, 2019; Chen, 2022).
  - **Key moderators:** The sign and magnitude of punishment effects depend on at least:
    - **Number of rounds/time horizon:** Efficiency gains more likely in longer games; short games often see net losses due to costly punishment (Fehr et al., 2010).
    - **Punishment cost/impact ratio (punishment_cost, punishment_tech):** Costless or highly effective punishment promotes efficiency; costly, weak punishment often destroys surplus (Kanitsar, 2021; Glöckner et al., 2018).
    - **Network structure/who can punish whom (punishment_tech):** Complete punishment networks may diffuse responsibility and reduce efficiency; incomplete networks can improve targeting and efficiency (Peng & Fan, 2023).
    - **Formal/centralized vs. peer punishment (punishment_tech):** Centralized/formal punishment is often less prone to anti-social punishment and more effective, but only if properly designed and monitored (Kamei, 2024; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
    - **Group composition and heterogeneity (mpcr, player_count):** Heterogeneous returns and group structures can undermine beneficial punishment effects or invite anti-social punishment (Chen, 2022; Peng & Fan, 2023; Kim et al., 2025).
    - **Endogenous institution choice:** When players can choose institutions, they may prefer punishment mechanisms only if these deliver higher efficiency; otherwise, punishment is rejected (Botelho et al., 2022).
    - **Presence of reputation/monitoring (show_other_summaries, monitoring cost):** Efficient punishment requires accurate, affordable monitoring; too costly monitoring blocks efficiency gains (Nicklisch et al., 2021).
    - **Enforcement probability/credibility:** Only certain/credible punishment sustains efficiency (Alt & Zhang, 2020; Kanitsar, 2021).
    - **Framing (give/take, leadership, symbolic authority):** Framing can potentiate or mitigate efficiency gains (Ramalingam et al., 2019; Jiang & Wang, 2024; Xu, 2023).

- **Comparison and competition with rewards:**
  - **Reward mechanisms** generally outperform punishment in efficiency, especially in heterogeneous groups or when reward is majority-based or costless (Chen, 2022; Peng, 2022; Faillo et al., 2020).
  - **Simultaneous punishment and reward:** The presence of both can augment or even reinforce each other's effect, but the comparative efficiency still hinges on costs and misuse (Pevnitskaya & Ryvkin, 2022).
  - **Pure social/approval mechanisms** (e.g., rating systems) can improve efficiency if costless, but their effect vanishes when there is any cost (Faillo et al., 2020).

- **Institutional and demographic effects:**
  - **Cultural context and group composition (leaders, gender, societal norms)** substantially moderate punishment's efficiency effect (Jiang & Wang, 2024; Gürdal et al., 2021; Kamei et al., 2023).
  - **Third-party and higher-order punishment:** These can improve efficiency, particularly via more targeted or competitive punishment, but effects may fail to materialize in some social/cultural contexts (Krügel & Maaser, 2025; Kamei et al., 2025).

- **Design dimensions with strong evidence:**  
  - **Player count (player_count):** Effects are robust in small groups (3-5); larger or changing groups introduce substantial complexity.
  - **Number of rounds (num_rounds):** Short games often see net efficiency loss with punishment; longer horizons (20–50 rounds) allow efficiency gains as punishment's deterrence is realized and costs taper with stabilized cooperation.
  - **Marginal per-capita return (mpcr):** Higher MPCR (returns to cooperation) allows for greater efficiency gains from punishment; very low MPCR settings seldom benefit (Botelho et al., 2022).
  - **Punishment cost and effectiveness (punishment_cost, punishment_tech):** Lower cost, higher impact, and targeted technology (especially institutional or formal) more reliably yield efficiency increases.

## Areas of ambiguity and disagreement:

- Some studies show **clear net efficiency gains** from enabling punishment (Kamei, 2024; Gürdal et al., 2021); others find the gains are **neutral or negative** (Fehr et al., 2010; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; Botelho et al., 2022).
- **Anti-social punishment** and poor targeting often erode or reverse efficiency gains.
- In settings with **heterogeneous group returns or roles**, punishment may fail or even reduce efficiency (Peng & Fan, 2023; Chen, 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).
- **Network/institutional structure:** Dense networks or ambiguous punishment settings diffuse responsibility, undermining deterrence/efficiency.
- Empirical findings are more robust and generalizable **within standard PGG lab settings** than in field, CPR, market, or trust-adjacent contexts.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

### High-confidence predictions:
- **Canonical repeated PGGs** (small group, moderate to high MPCR, moderate-cost, well-targeted punishment): Enabling peer or formal punishment is predicted to *increase efficiency relative to control*, especially if anti-social punishment is suppressed and monitoring is accurate and affordable (Kamei, 2024; Gürdal et al., 2021; Jarungrattanapong, 2022).

### Conditional/Qualified predictions:
- **Short games (≤ 10 rounds):** Enabling punishment is likely to *decrease or have negligible effect on efficiency* due to immediate resource destruction from punishment (Fehr et al., 2010; Botelho et al., 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024).
- **High-cost or poorly targeted punishment:** If punishment is expensive, diffuse, or misapplied, *efficiency will not improve*, and may decline (Botelho et al., 2022; Kanitsar, 2021; Glöckner et al., 2018).
- **Heterogeneous groups/returns:** Efficiency effect is ambiguous; anti-social punishment and poor targeting frequently negate efficiency gains (Chen, 2022; Peng & Fan, 2023).
- **Incomplete/unstable punishment networks:** Efficiency gains are fragile and context-dependent; incomplete networks may outperform complete ones only if targeting of free-riders is better (Peng & Fan, 2023).
- **Voluntary/enforceability of punishment:** If punishment is optional, avoidable, or not enforced, *no efficiency gain should be expected* (Del Ponte et al., 2025; Robbett, 2019).
- **Reputation/information structure:** Absence of affordable, accurate monitoring drastically reduces punishment’s positive effect on efficiency (Nicklisch et al., 2021).

### Moderators to emphasize in model-based prediction:
- Use **control efficiency** as a predictor, moderated by the absolute and relative cost/impact of punishment, number of rounds, MPCR, group size, and network/institutional details.
- **Interaction effects:** For games with endogenous institution choice, previous experience with single-group PGG, and leader selection, include these as interaction terms or categorical moderators.
- **Design parameters are critical**—even small variations (punishment_cost, monitoring cost, network structure, framing) can pivot efficiency effects in or out of the positive range.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**  
- **player_count:** Extensively varied and studied directly.
- **num_rounds:** Frequently manipulated; time horizon consistently shown as an important moderator.
- **chat/communication:** Robust evidence on effects of chat being present/absent, with communication generally amplifying or sometimes undermining punishment effects.
- **all_or_nothing (binary vs. continuous):** Both tested; binary designs may amplify the salience of free-riding/punishment.
- **mpcr:** Heavily studied; strong evidence for its moderating effect.
- **punishment_cost/punishment_tech:** Central to most studies; exact values and tech (targeting, centralization, (non-)deterrence) matter.
- **show_other_summaries/show_n_rounds:** Presence/absence and level of information feedback are relevant and sometimes manipulated.
- **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech:** Several studies include joint punishment/reward conditions.

**Indirect/contextual evidence:**  
- **default_contrib:** Some reference via opt-in/opt-out or contribution framing, but less directly analyzed.
- **show_punishment_id:** Occasionally manipulated; evidence mainly about punishment’s anonymity (rarely about ID revelation specifically).

**Sparse or missing dimensions:**  
- **Some highly specific details—e.g., the precise interface for technological implementation (punishment_tech at a technical level), reward magnitude when not directly studied, simultaneous manipulation of all 14 dimensions—are not covered in all possible combinations.**  
- **Evidence on effects of enabling/disabling default contributions, or complex multi-level summary feedback, is limited relative to core dimensions.**

---

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Heterogeneity of game designs:** Despite rich coverage across main PGG dimensions, evidence for all possible design combinations is incomplete; non-standard, field and hybrid games, and rare dimension combinations (e.g., unusual group sizes or multi-level public goods with unique feedback/punishment structures) are underrepresented or not directly informative.
- **Behavioral outcomes ≠ efficiency:** Many studies emphasize contribution/punishment frequency over direct payoff outcomes; predictions must not be based on increases in cooperation/contribution alone.
- **Ambiguity and context-dependence:** Empirical findings on punishment’s efficiency effect are highly sensitive to punishment cost/effectiveness, targeting, MPCR, time horizon, institutional structure, heterogeneity, and cultural context—leading to disagreements in the literature.
- **Limited external validity:** Most evidence is from laboratory environments with highly controlled, simplified representations of public goods and punishment; generalization to field or ecologically complex settings is necessarily cautious.
- **Sparse on rare design dimensions:** Some design dimensions (identity revelation, simultaneous manipulation of chat and punishment, rare feedback interface details, default contribution framing) lack extensive empirical mapping to efficiency outcomes.
- **Temporal scope and learning:** In short games, punishment is less effective or net-destructive; only in longer time horizons do some potential efficiency gains fully materialize.
- **Interaction effects unexplored:** While many moderators are individually studied, higher-order interactions (e.g., chat × punishment × group size × heterogeneity) are less systematically examined.
- **Reliance on median effects:** High variance and group heterogeneity in many studies caution against overconfident point prediction without accounting for underlying distributional ambiguity.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature base is robust and highly relevant for modeling and predicting the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments under standard laboratory conditions, with strong direct evidence for most design dimensions. The effect of punishment on efficiency is highly contingent upon institutional, parametric, and contextual moderators. Reliable predictions should be grounded in game design features and control efficiency, with careful attention to context-dependence and explicit, dimensional mapping. Gaps remain for rare or complex game designs and for environments where only behavioral (not efficiency) outcomes are reported.
