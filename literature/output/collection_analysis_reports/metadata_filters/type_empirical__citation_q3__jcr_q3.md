# 1) Evidence Base

The reviewed paper set is moderately broad, comprising 25 empirical studies that are almost entirely experimental, with only one observational field intervention and a small number bordering on more theoretical or mechanistic emphasis. There is heavy empirical representation of laboratory public goods games (PGGs) and close variants, especially those specifically manipulating punishment or sanction conditions. Nearly all primary outcomes are behavioral (e.g., cooperation/contribution rates, punishment rates), yet a significant subset reports on efficiency or group payoff as a primary or secondary outcome. The sample also includes studies on adjacent social dilemmas, trust games, inspection games, and behavioral experiments exploring punishment's individual drivers and moderators.

PGGs with direct efficiency outcomes in punishment versus control conditions—most relevant for the downstream prediction task—are well-represented, with several large-N, multi-condition experiments. Several papers instead report only behavioral or attitudinal outcomes, such as cooperation, punishment assigned, or psychological attitudes, and a few cover adjacent settings (e.g., dyadic exchanges, trust or contest games, and field observations) that are structurally similar but not PGGs. Mechanism- and theory-driven arguments are nearly always paired with direct empirical testing.

Overall, the evidence base is empirically strong for typical lab PGGs with punishment, less so for diverse modifications or field settings, and varies notably in whether direct efficiency outcomes are reported.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessment of the literature on three dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant:**
  - *exact*: Numerous papers are exact public goods games (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Irwin et al., 2014; Nelissen & Mulder, 2013; Greiff & Paetzel, 2016; Gianotti et al., 2019).
  - *close*: Some employ repeated social dilemmas, trust, contest, or inspection games that are structurally similar (Wilson & Wu, 2017; Bohnet & Baytelman, 2007; MOLM, 1994; Rauhut, 2009; Lacomba et al., 2014; Fehr & Sutter, 2019; Nunney et al., 2022).
  - *adjacent/weak*: Studies on dyadic, dictator, or field norm environments (Townsend et al., 2020; Jung et al., 2014; Berger & Hevenstone, 2016; Pfattheicher, 2015; Berger, 2021; Chen & Hauser, 2005) are more distant and mainly contextually informative.

- **punishment_or_sanctions:**
  - *exact*: Many experiments manipulate punishment presence, cost, legitimacy, or structure directly (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
  - *adjacent/close*: Others examine gossip, sanctions, or other forms of norm enforcement, or use less standard implementations (Fehr & Sutter, 2019; Lacomba et al., 2014; Berger, 2021).
  - *weak/none*: Several do not experimentally manipulate punishment at all, or only in non-PGG formats.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**
  - *exact/close*: Some papers report direct group efficiency or aggregate payoff outcomes (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Wilson & Wu, 2017; Lacomba et al., 2014; Fehr & Sutter, 2019).
  - *adjacent*: Several report contribution/cooperation rates, which may map closely to efficiency in basic PGGs but not in all designs.
  - *weak/none*: Some focus exclusively on behavioral, attitudinal, or emotional outcomes (Eriksson et al., 2017; Berger & Hevenstone, 2016).

In sum, coverage is strongest for lab PGGs with punishment and measurable efficiency outcomes; less so for field settings, unconventional sanctioning mechanisms, or non-payoff-based social dilemmas.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes:**
  - Several papers report *group efficiency, total earnings, welfare, or group payoff*; these are labeled “efficiency_or_payoff” (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Wilson & Wu, 2017; Lacomba et al., 2014; Fehr & Sutter, 2019; Nunney et al., 2022).
  - Some studies use *proxy outcomes* (trust sent/returned in trust games, destruction rates in conflict/inspection games) that are closely related to surplus or efficiency but may not correspond precisely (Bohnet & Baytelman, 2007; Rauhut, 2009).

- **Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
  - Many studies report *contribution/cooperation rates*, *punishment frequency*, and *retaliation* (Irwin et al., 2014; Nelissen & Mulder, 2013; Greiff & Paetzel, 2016; MOLM, 1994; Gummerum et al., 2022).
  - Attitudinal and psychological measures are sometimes primary (e.g., satisfaction, trust, anger), especially in cross-cultural or emotion-focused work (Eriksson et al., 2017; Berger & Hevenstone, 2016; Jung et al., 2014).

- **Distinction:** The literature consistently distinguishes between behavioral indicators and actual payoff-based efficiency, but some inference from behavioral to efficiency outcomes is present, particularly in standard linear PGG formats where the mapping is relatively straightforward.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the most directly relevant empirical papers, several crosscutting findings emerge:

- **Effect of Enabling Punishment:**
  - *Positive efficiency effects* are commonly observed when punishment is costly, well-targeted (prosocial/legitimate), and combined with appropriate feedback/institutional design—especially with centralized or democratic structures (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
  - *Negative or neutral effects* are found when punishment is costless, unrestricted, or prone to antisocial misuse; under these circumstances, efficiency can fall due to excessive or misdirected punishment (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013).
  - The *presence and cost of punishment* are major moderators; costless punishment tends toward inefficiency, while costly punishment can, paradoxically, be more effective by increasing legitimacy and reducing waste.

- **Institutional Structure Matters:**
  - Rules restricting *who can punish whom* (“legitimate” punishment) and mechanisms for information feedback are critical—both increase efficiency by reducing antisocial punishment and over-punishment (Faillo et al., 2013).
  - Democratic or centralized punishment systems are usually more efficient than decentralized, individually-triggered peer punishment (Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Kamijo et al., 2014).

- **Magnitude and Structure of Punishment:**
  - The *cost/impact ratio* and deterrence threshold are important; only sufficiently deterrent (not necessarily draconian) sanctions increase efficiency, and in some cases, only “relative” or “targeted” punishment (punishing worst violators) is effective (Kamijo et al., 2014).
  - If sanctions are too weak, efficiency gains can be lost; if they are too severe or misallocated (e.g., costless or with wrong payoff division), efficiency may decrease (Wilson & Wu, 2017; Kamijo et al., 2014).

- **Communication/Feedback:**
  - Communication (chat), feedback about peer behavior, and visibility of group outcomes interact powerfully with punishment and can substitute for or amplify its effect in some environments (Faillo et al., 2013; Nunney et al., 2022).

- **Results by Control Efficiency:**
  - When baseline (no-punishment) efficiency is high, adding punishment may not improve—sometimes even reducing—efficiency, especially with non-legitimate or excessive punishment.
  - When baseline efficiency is low, appropriately structured punishment systems are more likely to yield substantial efficiency gains.

- **Adjacent Settings:**
  - Findings from trust games, inspection games, and contests generally support the conclusion that sanction schemes can improve efficiency if credibly deterrent and if their cost does not outweigh their benefits, but with strong sensitivity to design specifics (Bohnet & Baytelman, 2007; Rauhut, 2009; Lacomba et al., 2014; Fehr & Sutter, 2019).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How the literature should inform downstream prediction, conditional on design dimensions and baseline efficiency:**

- *Punishment’s impact on efficiency is highly conditional*: Key moderators are the **structure, cost, and legitimacy** of punishment, as well as available feedback and communication. Predicting treatment efficiency requires careful attention to whether:
  - Punishment is costly to assign (cost per point) versus costless.
  - Rules restrict punishment to legitimate/prosocial use, as opposed to unrestricted or antisocial application.
  - Punishment is centralized, democratic, or peer-based.
  - Feedback and peer outcomes are visible (enabling targeted, prosocial punishment).

- *Strong, direct evidence* indicates that enabling **costly, legitimate, and feedback-supported punishment** in PGGs increases efficiency, especially when control efficiency is low (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014). Enabling **costless or unrestricted peer punishment** may reduce efficiency, even below control (no-punishment) levels, due to excessive or misdirected punishment.

- *Design dimensions must be linked to outcome predictions*:
  - **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech**, and **reward_exists** are directly implicated.
  - **show_other_summaries** and **chat** are critical for enabling prosocial use of punishment.
  - **player_count** and **num_rounds** are less frequently found to interact with punishment’s main effect, except via their impact on the sustainability of cooperation.
  - **mpcr** and **all_or_nothing** may moderate how punishment affects marginal incentives, but evidence is less direct.

- *Baseline (control) efficiency is crucial*: Predictions should be anchored to control-game efficiency. Where control efficiency is already high (e.g., due to high MPCR or communication), the marginal benefit of punishment may be neutral or negative, particularly if the punishment system is poorly designed.

- *Behavioral outcome data (contributions, cooperation)* should only be used as a proxy for efficiency if payoff mapping is unambiguous; otherwise, avoid inferring efficiency gains from increased cooperation alone.

- *Ambiguity persists* regarding the effect of punishment in novel, more complicated, or real-world contexts; lab results may overstate frequency and effectiveness of punishment-induced efficiency gains (Berger & Hevenstone, 2016; Eriksson et al., 2017).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed**:
- **player_count**: Standardized at 3 or 4 in most direct experiments, with little variation in effect, but included in most models.
- **num_rounds**: Usually 6–15; longer games may allow for building up efficiency gains with “democratic” punishment (Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
- **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech**: Richly explored; critical for predicting efficiency impacts.
- **mpcr**: Varied in PGGs; important for marginal gains from cooperation (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013).
- **chat**, **show_other_summaries**: Feedback and communication are extensively manipulated and shown to moderate the efficiency impact of punishment.
- **all_or_nothing**: Used variably, but less central to main findings.

**Indirectly/contextually discussed or missing**:
- **default_contrib**: Rarely manipulated as a framing; only one paper refers to opt-in versus opt-out.
- **reward_exists**, **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**: Only a few papers include reward as a symmetric or alternative mechanism.
- **show_n_rounds**, **show_punishment_id**: Occasionally manipulated, but not consistently linked to efficiency (Greiff & Paetzel, 2016; Berger & Hevenstone, 2016).
- **punishmentMagnitude/rewardMagnitude** (Note: Not always specified in reviewed studies).

In summary, dimensions most usable for informing prediction are punishment cost, technology (centralized/democratic/prosocial rules), availability of feedback/communication, and sometimes MPCR.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Overrepresentation of lab settings**: Most evidence is derived from student populations in controlled, repeated lab games. Findings may overstate the frequency, effectiveness, or legitimacy of punishment compared to real-world field contexts (Berger & Hevenstone, 2016; Eriksson et al., 2017).
- **Sparse evidence for several prediction dimensions**: Direct evidence is lacking or limited for dimensions like reward symmetry, default contribution framing, and identification of punishers/rewarders.
- **Potential for design-sensitive reversals**: The efficiency benefit of punishment is highly sensitive to cost, legitimacy rules, and feedback structures; poorly tuned systems may decrease efficiency, especially if punishment is costless or antisocial.
- **Adjacent-game extrapolation risk**: Evidence from inspection games, trust games, or contest environments is useful mainly for qualitative hypotheses, not quantitative prediction or design-specific calibration.
- **Reliance on proxy outcomes in some studies**: In studies lacking direct efficiency or payoff reporting, contribution rate is often used as a surrogate, which can be misleading in nonstandard or nonlinear payoff games.
- **Long-term impacts and “crowding out”**: Some settings show that the imposition of external sanctions may crowd out intrinsic motivation, affecting long-term efficiency and social welfare in ways not fully captured by short-run lab experiments (Bohnet & Baytelman, 2007).

### **Concluding Guidance**

The experimental literature robustly supports that enabling **costly, legitimate, and well-structured punishment** (especially with full feedback/communication) increases efficiency in public goods games, particularly from low-efficiency baselines. However, careless design (costless/unrestricted punishment) can reduce efficiency even below control. Most, but not all, of the 14 prediction dimensions are empirically supported for efficiency prediction; reward symmetry, default framing, and some visibility aspects lack sufficient evidence. Real-world generalizability and longer-term consequences—such as motivation crowding—remain key challenges unaddressed by most studies in this set.
