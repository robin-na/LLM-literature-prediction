# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of 13 papers, encompassing a mix of **empirical laboratory experiments** (e.g., Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Seip et al., 2014; Martin et al., 2014) and **theoretical/modeling studies** (e.g., Hauser et al., 2014; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2014). Most empirical studies use classic or modified public-goods games in the lab, with a few employing adjacent social dilemma paradigms such as the iterated prisoner's dilemma (IPD) or dice-based norm testbeds. On the theory side, several papers use evolutionary game models or agent-based simulations.

For the downstream prediction task—efficiency impact of enabling punishment in PGG-like environments—the evidence base is **broad** in theoretical mechanism coverage for PGGs and peer/institutional punishment, but **empirical evidence is concentrated in a few key studies**. There are some papers adjacent to, but not directly inside, the target prediction context (notably for punishment, payoff/efficiency, or for PGG as opposed to other social dilemmas).

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) pgg_or_variant:**  
- *exact:* Eight papers directly study public goods games (Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Hauser et al., 2014; Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Chen & Perc, 2014; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Seip et al., 2014; Han et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2014).
- *adjacent or weak:* Several papers study closely related environments (e.g., IPD: Martin et al., 2014; Hilbe et al., 2015a, 2015b).
- *none:* A few papers (Corgnet et al., 2015; Diekmann et al., 2015) use other contexts like allocation games or norm violation/cheating games.

**b) punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *exact:* Five papers explicitly investigate punishment or sanctioning within PGGs (Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Hauser et al., 2014; Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Chen & Perc, 2014; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Seip et al., 2014).
- *adjacent:* Han et al. (2017) and some IPD papers discuss related mechanisms (commitment, strategic punishment) but do not manipulate punishment as the predicted treatment.
- *none or weak:* Several papers do not include punishment (e.g., Hilbe et al., 2015b; Corgnet et al., 2015; Diekmann et al., 2015).

**c) efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *exact:* Four to five papers report group payoff or efficiency as a primary outcome (Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Hauser et al., 2014; Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Han et al., 2017; Hilbe et al., 2015b).
- *close or adjacent:* Some interpret from cooperation rates or suggest potential payoffs (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Chen & Perc, 2014), but the outcome is not explicitly group efficiency.
- *none:* Papers with only behavioral or psychological outcomes, such as anger or norm compliance (Seip et al., 2014; Diekmann et al., 2015).

**Summary:**  
The strongest, most task-relevant evidence (exact-exact-exact) comes from Reuben & Riedl (2013), Hauser et al. (2014), and Schoenmakers et al. (2014). Other papers offer either adjacent task relevance or contextual support.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes**:
- *Group efficiency/average payoff*: Explicitly reported in Reuben & Riedl (2013), Hauser et al. (2014), Schoenmakers et al. (2014), Han et al. (2017), Hilbe et al. (2015b), and Martin et al. (2014).
- *Welfare or surplus*: Only occasionally explicitly stated, usually via efficiency or sum of payoffs.
- *Indirect indicators*: Some papers infer likely efficiency changes from cooperation rates under structural assumptions (e.g., Szolnoki & Perc, 2013), but do not directly report group payoff or efficiency.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:
- *Contribution or cooperation rate*: Widely reported as a primary outcome in theory (Chen & Perc, 2014; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013), and empirical (Reuben & Riedl, 2013, also reports).
- *Punishment frequency/intensity*: Behavioral mechanism focus in Seip et al. (2014), and some theory.
- *Norm compliance/violation contagion, motivation, beliefs*: Reported in Seip et al. (2014), Diekmann et al. (2015), Corgnet et al. (2015).

**Distinction**:  
Only studies directly reporting group efficiency/payoff as a primary outcome can be confidently used for treatment efficiency prediction. Others, focused on cooperation rates or mechanisms, cannot be straightforwardly mapped to efficiency without strong additional assumptions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment Robustly Increases Efficiency** (when anti-social punishment is minimal or excluded):  
  - Substantial, robust increases in group efficiency occur when enabling peer punishment in linear PGGs across both homogeneous and heterogeneous groups (Reuben & Riedl, 2013). Many experimental PGG groups even attain full efficiency.
  - Theoretical modeling supports that institutionally enabled, visible punishment (pool punishment institutions) leads to stable high-efficiency outcomes, especially when per-punishment cost is low and punishment is credible and visible (Schoenmakers et al., 2014).

- **Anti-social Punishment Can Eliminate Gains**:  
  - When anti-social punishment is possible (i.e., punishers can punish cooperators), punishment no longer increases group efficiency and may become neutral or negative for group payoff (Hauser et al., 2014).

- **No Punishment, Large Groups = Low Efficiency**:  
  - Without punishment, especially in groups with more than a few members, both theory and experiment show rapid convergence to low efficiency (Hilbe et al., 2015b; Reuben & Riedl, 2013). Direct reciprocity (without costly punishment) is insufficient to sustain cooperation as player count increases.

- **Institutional and Mechanism Design Factors Matter**:  
  - The efficiency impact of punishment is moderated by the structure of the punishment (conditional vs. unconditional), its cost-to-fine ratio, and public visibility or information about sanctions (Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
  - Games permitting more pro-social than anti-social punishment—or using institutional mechanisms to reduce anti-social punishment—show greater efficiency gains.

- **Other Mechanisms (like Commitment) Can Also Substantially Raise Efficiency**:  
  - Commitment mechanisms not strictly classified as punishment can lead to similar efficiency gains as punishment, though findings are not always directly translatable to the classic peer punishment effect in PGGs (Han et al., 2017).

- **Behavioral Outcomes Align, But Do Not Equal, Efficiency Gains**:  
  - Empirical studies show that punishments are often targeted at defectors, with emotional triggers (anger) strongly tied to costly punishment behaviors (Seip et al., 2014), supporting the plausibility of punishment increasing contributions and, by extension, efficiency.

- **Transparency and Information Provision Affect Efficiency** (without explicit punishment):  
  - Transparent games where other players’ choices and payoffs are revealed yield higher efficiency even absent explicit costly punishment (Martin et al., 2014).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct Evidence**:
  - When predicting the efficiency of a PGG-like game with peer punishment enabled (vs. control), a **substantial efficiency increase** is expected if punishment is pro-social and does not facilitate anti-social targeting (Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2014). Many groups will reach near full efficiency.
  - **Exception:** If the punishment mechanism allows anti-social punishment (punishing cooperators for cooperating), expect **no improvement** or even a decrease in group efficiency (Hauser et al., 2014).

- **Moderators from Design Dimensions**:
  - **Player count**: Higher baseline efficiency with fewer players; in absence of punishment, efficiency drops sharply with more players (Hilbe et al., 2015b). Punishment effect size is robust across group sizes in key experiments, but extreme group sizes not tested.
  - **MPCR**: Higher MPCR makes cooperation easier; punishment may yield smaller marginal improvements when control efficiency is already high.
  - **Punishment cost and tech**: Lower cost-to-fine ratios, predictable and visible punishment (especially institutional “pool” punishment) raise efficiency more substantially (Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
  - **Group heterogeneity**: Predicted norm enforcement and efficiency level can be influenced by heterogeneity in endowments or returns, but overall punishment effect is robust (Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
  - **Information structure**: Showing other players’ actions/payoffs can further boost efficiency, even without explicit punishment mechanisms (Martin et al., 2014).

- **Use Caution**:
  - If the design enables anti-social punishment or lacks visibility about norm enforcement, **do not expect efficiency improvement**.
  - Papers using non-payoff outcomes (contribution rates, emotions, norm violation rates) should only be interpreted as weak, mechanism-level support without efficiency data.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed**:
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`  
  (directly analyzed or controlled in most PGG-punishment-payoff studies; see Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Hauser et al., 2014; Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
- `chat`, `all_or_nothing`  
  (discussed and/or manipulated in several empirical and theory papers, though less central).

**Indirectly Informed**:
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`  
  (information structure and visibility, relevant in Martin et al., 2014; Schoenmakers et al., 2014, but rarely the central treatment).

**Only Contextually Discussed or Weakly Informed**:
- `default_contrib`  
  (occasionally described, but not examined as a key moderator for punishment’s impact).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`  
  (reward mechanisms are a background focus in some theory work, but not central to empirical efficiency outcomes or the prediction task).

**Effectively Missing**:
- Few papers address the effects of `chat` (direct peer communication) or `default_contrib` (framing defaults) as primary moderators within punishment-enabled PGGs.
- No direct evidence on `reward_x` dimensions in relation to efficiency when punishment is manipulated; reward as a mechanism is only theoretically modeled or contextually contrasted.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse Empirical Evidence for Specific Combinations**:  
  - Only a handful of studies (notably Reuben & Riedl, 2013) directly report **empirical** efficiency outcomes for PGGs with/without peer punishment; much of the literature is theoretical or based on indirect inferences.
  - Few studies systematically vary or jointly manipulate the full set of game design dimensions, limiting fine-grained prediction.

- **Ambiguity Due to Anti-social Punishment**:  
  - Key divergence in findings depends on whether anti-social punishment is permitted (Hauser et al., 2014). Unless game design features preclude anti-social punishment, outcome predictions have significant uncertainty.
  
- **Contextual Gaps**:  
  - Many adjacent papers (using IPD, allocation games, or norm violation paradigms) provide only partial analogies and do not manipulate or measure punishment or efficiency as targeted.

- **Payoff vs. Behavioral Outcomes**:  
  - A substantial fraction of the evidence base explains *how* punishment occurs and what motivates it (anger, norm violation rates), but does not link these mechanisms to changes in group efficiency.

- **Generalizability**:  
  - Most studies use moderate group sizes, finite rounds, and controlled lab procedures; extrapolation to field settings, larger groups, or continuous/real-fee punishments is uncertain.

- **Sparse Evidence for Some Design Dimensions**:  
  - Key game design aspects (e.g., chat, contribution framing, visibility of punishers, parallel rewards) are not robustly examined as moderators or treatments within efficiency-outcome studies.

---

**In summary**, the literature provides strong—though not universal—support for the expectation that enabling peer punishment in PGG-like environments increases efficiency, so long as anti-social punishment does not dominate. The prediction for treatment efficiency should be moderated by the game’s structural features and the known baseline (control) efficiency, with heavy caution applied if anti-social punishment is feasible or if design aspects (player count, punishment cost, visibility) are outside tested ranges. Many design dimensions remain empirically or theoretically underexplored in relation to treatment efficiency.
