# 1) Evidence Base

**Mix of Empirical vs Theory Papers:**
- The paper set includes 29 papers, with a reasonably balanced mix of empirical experimental studies (notably Sparks et al. 2024; Micheli et al. 2021; Watson et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2021) and a substantial number of formal theoretical and modeling papers (e.g., Greenwood et al. 2018; Cooney 2025; Kurokawa 2023; Murase 2025; Duong et al. 2024; Kristensen et al. 2025).
- Most empirical papers are lab experiments; theory papers are mathematical models varying from analytical to simulation-based.
- Only a small subset provides direct, empirical estimates of group efficiency or payoff under peer punishment enabled/disabled, with others using related payoffs, cooperation rates, or more distal proxies.

**Narrow or Broad for the Prediction Task:**
- The evidence base is **moderately broad** on the general mechanisms by which punishment (and reward) affect cooperation and efficiency in public-goods-like environments.
- However, **direct empirical evidence on treatment efficiency effects in PGGs with the full set of design dimensions is limited**, with many papers focused on adjacent games, indirect mechanisms (e.g., reputation, exclusion, institutional tagging), or theoretical boundaries rather than quantitative effect sizes in standard PGGs.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`):**
- *Exact*: A significant minority of papers use exact PGGs (e.g., Sparks et al. 2024; Greenwood et al. 2018; Liu & Guo 2010; Duong et al. 2024).
- *Close/Adjacent*: Many rely on close variants (n-person Prisoner’s Dilemmas, indirect reciprocity, mutual aid, or resource dilemmas).
- *Weak/None*: Several focus on non-PGG social dilemmas or one-shot games without the full PGG structure.

**Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`):**
- *Exact*: A subset manipulates actual material punishment in the game (Sparks et al. 2024; Greenwood et al. 2018; Duong et al. 2024; Cooney 2025; Murase 2025).
- *Close/Adjacent*: Many model punishment-like mechanisms indirectly (e.g., exclusion, social pressure, institutional tagging, reprobation, or group extinction).
- *Weak/None*: Several key empirical studies do not manipulate or include punishment (e.g., Liu et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2021; Laird 2018).

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`):**
- *Exact*: About a third address efficiency or explicit group-level payoffs as a primary outcome (Sparks et al. 2024; Greenwood et al. 2018; Duong et al. 2024; Kristensen et al. 2025; Cooney 2025; Murase 2025).
- *Close/Adjacent*: Several use proxies (maximum sustainable yield, fixation probability, welfare, group fitness).
- *Weak/None*: Many report only contribution rates, cooperation prevalence, or punishment frequency, and not total group payoff or efficiency.

**Summary:**
- The set provides some **exact and close coverage** of all three key relevance dimensions, but there are gaps for empirical, PGG-specific, payoff-outcome-focused studies. Many findings are theoretical, modeling based, or only indirectly relevant to the target efficiency prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes:**
- **Direct Measures:** Group efficiency (actual over maximum possible payoff), group earnings, total welfare, group fitness, surplus (Sparks et al. 2024; Greenwood et al. 2018; Duong et al. 2024; Cooney 2025; Kurokawa 2023; Kristensen et al. 2025).
- **Proxies:** Maximum sustainable yield (Furuzono et al. 2013), fixation probability of cooperation (Fontanari & Santos 2024; Kroumi 2025).
- **Parameter Characterization:** Analytical formulas and boundaries for expected payoff given punishment cost/benefit tradeoffs (Murase 2025; Greenwood et al. 2018; Cooney 2025).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- Contribution rate, cooperation frequency/judgments (Micheli et al. 2021; Kido & Takezawa 2024; Liu et al. 2020; Morsky & Akçay 2021).
- Normative judgments (Liu et al. 2020).
- Punishment assigned and frequency (Kurokawa 2023; Syi 2014).
- Social information effects on willingness to be spiteful/altruistic (Watson et al. 2024).

**Distinctions:**
- Many papers reporting increased cooperation with punishment do **not** follow through to group efficiency or payoff outcomes—particularly when punishment is costly and can offset the benefits (e.g., Micheli et al. 2021).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Findings:**
- **Direct PGG Laboratory Evidence (High Relevance):**
    - **Costly peer punishment raises long-term group efficiency** in repeated PGGs despite initial costs, provided punishment is tangible; costless disapproval is not efficacious (Sparks et al., 2024).
    - **Centralized reward and punishment (probabilistic admin):** Both can increase cooperative contributions above baseline, with financial incentives being stronger than social ones; direct efficiency measured not reported, but implied to track contributions (Micheli et al., 2021; payoff outcome is adjacent).

**Theoretical/Mechanism Findings:**
- **Thresholds and Moderators:**
    - **Punishment only increases efficiency if its cost-to-impact ratio and frequency clear a threshold** determined by benefit/cost structure; low frequency or high cost can nullify or reverse efficiency gains (Greenwood et al., 2018; Liu & Guo, 2010; Cooney, 2025; Murase, 2025).
    - **Punishment effectiveness curves are often non-monotonic:** Both theory and some modeling show that while intensifying punishment can initially increase efficiency, too high a cost or excessive use hurts efficiency (Kurokawa, 2023; Cooney, 2025; Murase, 2025).
    - **Game structure and group size moderate effects:** For certain game/punishment parameters, larger groups make efficient punishment harder (Kurokawa, 2023); in others, group-level selection or extinction can counteract this (Kroumi, 2025).
    - **Detectability of defection is critical** to punishment’s efficiency returns (Murase, 2025): With poor detection, punishment is more efficient, as it’s rarely executed but strongly deters.

- **Indirect Effects and Contextual Factors:**
    - **Social norm structure and information environment:** Punishment is more effective at sustaining efficiency when punishers are easily identified and their actions are observable (Castro et al., 1998), but can be undermined by perception or execution errors (Inaba et al., 2016).
    - **Institutional or group-level sanctions can be effective substitutes** for peer punishment if implemented with selectivity or information structures that favor cooperators (Ghachem, 2016; Furuzono et al., 2013; Kroumi, 2025).

**Mixed or Contradictory Results:**
- **Some models robustly predict that punishment lowers efficiency** unless the group is small and/or punishment costs are very low (Kurokawa, 2023; Garay et al., 2014).
- **Others show strong efficiency gains from punishment** but only under specific circumstances (e.g. specific parameter ranges for cost, group structure, benefit, and detectability), indicating the effect size is highly context-dependent.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For Predicting Efficiency When Peer Punishment Is Enabled:**
- **Direct, empirical lab PGG studies support** that enabling peer (costly) punishment increases long-run efficiency relative to control, particularly for smaller player counts, moderate MPCRs, and longer games (Sparks et al., 2024). However, early rounds may show lower efficiency due to punishment costs, with gains realized across repeated play.
- **Prediction should be conditional on design dimensions:**
    - If **punishment cost** is low-to-moderate and the punishment magnitude is sufficient, expect positive efficiency effects—especially when baseline (control) efficiency is low.
    - If **player count** is high and/or punishment cost-to-impact ratio is high, expect muted or potentially negative efficiency effects (Kurokawa, 2023; Cooney, 2025).
    - If the **environment makes defection hard to detect** or information about others is coarse, the efficiency gain from punishment is stronger (Murase, 2025; Larson, 2016).
    - If **punishers are rare** or normatively discouraged, or if punishment targets are unclear or error-prone, then enabling punishment may fail to increase efficiency (Greenwood et al., 2018; Inaba et al., 2016).
- **Behavioral outcomes should not be conflated with efficiency**: Increased cooperation does not always increase group payoff if costly punishment is frequent and does not deter free-riding efficiently.
- **Where control game efficiency is already high, additional efficiency gains from punishment are often marginal or negative** due to punishment costs.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count:** Frequently discussed; key moderator in efficiency effects (small groups → higher payoff returns to punishment; large groups → diluted punishment effectiveness).
- **num_rounds:** Longer games allow for punishment to take effect and yield efficiency gains through deterrence rather than frequent use (Sparks et al., 2024).
- **mpcr:** High MPCR can facilitate cooperation and make punishment more effective.
- **punishment_cost & punishment_tech:** Core to almost all theoretical treatments of punishment efficiency. Explicit threshold and nonlinearity highlighted.
- **all_or_nothing:** Presents across models and some empirical studies; modifies the effects of punishment.
- **chat:** Sometimes included, but less frequently tied to efficiency effects.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries:** Occasionally included, mainly in models about informational context or observability (Inaba et al., 2016).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Some theory and empirics (Duong et al., 2024; Billard, 1996) consider combinations.

**Indirectly Informed or Contextual Dimensions:**
- **default_contrib:** Rarely reported; some framing studies include opt-in/opt-out, but direct links to payoff effects are sparse.
- **showPunishmentId:** Only occasionally modeled; theory suggests identification increases deterrence, but empirical evidence is limited.
- **reward_cost, reward_magnitude:** Mostly in models comparing reward to punishment strategies (Duong et al., 2024).

**Effectively Missing or Minimally Addressed:**
- **showPunishmentId, default_contrib, some reward parameters**: Direct empirical evidence on how visibility of punishers or default framing interacts with efficiency is minimal.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse direct empirical evidence:** Only a few studies provide experimental, quantitative estimates of treatment-group efficiency after enabling punishment in standard, multi-round PGGs (Sparks et al., 2024).
- **Strong reliance on theory and adjacent mechanisms:** Many findings are based on mathematical modeling, and the results are sometimes not directly transferable to standard PGGs—especially in human lab or field settings.
- **Overreliance on non-payoff behavioral outcomes:** Many empirical studies report contribution or cooperation rates but not group payoff or efficiency, making inference about real efficiency effects indirect.
- **High context dependence and conditionality:** Theoretical models repeatedly emphasize that punishment’s effect on efficiency is conditional on group size, cost-to-impact ratio, detection accuracy, and social norm structure. These conditions are not always specified in experiments or are held constant.
- **Limited dimensional coverage:** Design dimensions relevant for real-world or varied prediction are inconsistently addressed. Some (e.g., chat, visibility of punishers, default contributions) are rarely manipulated or linked to payoff-based outcomes.
- **Ambiguity and disagreement in returns to punishment:** Some modeling work suggests strong efficiency gains from punishment (given low cost and strong deterrence), while others find that punishment is neutral or even harmful except in unusually favorable circumstances (e.g., Kurokawa, 2023).
- **Adjacent games may not fully capture PGG payoffs:** Many studies use Prisoner’s Dilemma, indirect reciprocity, or resource management analogs. These are informative but make translation to actual PGG efficiency somewhat speculative.

---

**Summary:**  
This literature set provides strong conceptual and theoretical foundations and some direct evidence that enabling costly peer punishment in PGG-like environments can increase efficiency relative to control, but only when game parameters (especially punishment cost and group size) are favorable. The efficiency gains are driven by deterrence and persist over longer games but not with costless (symbolic) disapproval. The prediction task should account for these moderators and the clarity that higher cooperation does not always translate to higher efficiency if the cost of punishment is high or overused. Coverage on some detailed intervention design dimensions is limited, and while adjacent games and theory offer rich insight, their findings are not always directly applicable to core PGG efficiency prediction.
