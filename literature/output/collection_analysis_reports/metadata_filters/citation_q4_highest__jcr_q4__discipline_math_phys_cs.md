# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set consists exclusively of theoretical papers, with no empirical or direct experimental data on actual public goods game (PGG) interventions. Only one paper (Bowles & Gintis, 2004) constructs a model directly focused on public-goods-like environments and links punishment to efficiency. The remaining three are broader or adjacent theory reviews and models, some focusing on indirect reciprocity, iterated dyadic games, or critique of game interpretation and framing. The coverage is thus conceptually broad in the realm of cooperation and punishment theory, but very narrow—bordering on sparse—regarding direct multi-player, repeated PGG laboratory or field evidence on efficiency consequences of punishment mechanism design.

# 2) Task Relevance

Each paper's relevance to the key dimensions is as follows:

- **pgg_or_variant**: Only Bowles & Gintis (2004) is `close` (it models public-goods-like scenarios); others are `adjacent` or `close` but not directly PGG (Okada, 2020 is `adjacent`, Kraines & Kraines, 1993 is `adjacent`—focused on iterated PD, and Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006 is also only `adjacent`).
- **punishment_or_sanctions**: Bowles & Gintis (2004) and Okada (2020) are `exact` and `close` respectively; Kraines & Kraines (1993) is `adjacent` (punishment only as endogenous payoffs), Hagen & Hammerstein (2006) is `adjacent` through critique.
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: Bowles & Gintis (2004) and Kraines & Kraines (1993) are `exact` (model/group efficiency/payoff focused); Okada (2020) and Hagen & Hammerstein (2006) are `adjacent` (behavioral focus, not directly on payoff).

Overall, only one paper in this set is both directly relevant and grounded in efficiency outcomes for multi-player public-goods settings with punishment. The others provide theoretical or contextual insights but lack direct mapping to the prediction scenario.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**
- *Efficiency, group payoff, average payoff, and welfare* are directly modeled and discussed in Bowles & Gintis (2004), and are central to their conclusions about the impact of punishment in public-goods environments.
- Kraines & Kraines (1993) analyzes efficiency and payoff outcomes, but only in dyadic settings (two-player iterated Prisoner's Dilemma), not in group or PGG structures.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- Okada (2020) is centered on behavioral outcomes such as cooperation stability, norm compliance, and evolutionary viability rather than direct payoffs or efficiency in PGGs.
- Hagen & Hammerstein (2006) focuses on conceptual critiques about participant behavior, framing, and psychological mechanisms, without addressing payoff outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Across the papers, the following main findings emerge:

- **Positive Effect of Punishment on Efficiency (Theoretical, PGG-like):**
  - Bowles & Gintis (2004) provide robust theoretical support: introducing costly peer punishment in public-goods-like repeated group settings enables high levels of cooperation and efficiency, relative to baseline games without punishment. This result is shown to be robust to group size, number of rounds, varying marginal per-capita returns (mpcr), and a range of punishment costs. They directly link the mechanism (costly peer punishment) to maintenance of high efficiency, with model group payoffs approaching fully cooperative levels depending on parameterization. This is a theoretical, not empirical, result.

- **Conditions for Punishment Efficacy (Outside PGG):**
  - Okada (2020) underscores that the impact of punishment on cooperation—and thus indirectly on efficiency—depends on the structure of norms, local reputation systems, and error rates. However, they caution that these results do not directly apply to PGGs, especially due to the inability to selectively cooperate.

- **Framing and Contextual Moderators:**
  - Hagen & Hammerstein (2006) argue that efficiency and punishment outcomes in experiments may be heavily influenced by how participants interpret the setting, suggesting that context and framing not captured by mechanism design features could moderate or confound the observed efficiency effects of punishment.

- **Adaptive Learning and Mutual Cooperation (Dyadic):**
  - Kraines & Kraines (1993) show high efficiency is achievable in noisy repeated Prisoner's Dilemma via adaptive learning (Pavlovian strategies), with implicit endogenous punishment and reward, but do not address group or peer-sanction models as in PGGs.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct Guidance:**
- The strongest support for prediction comes from Bowles & Gintis (2004): enabling costly peer punishment in repeated public-goods-like games with heterogeneous agents leads to substantially higher group efficiency and sustained cooperation compared to punishment-absent controls, broadly robust across values of group size, round number, mpcr, and punishment cost.

**Indirect and Model-based Considerations:**
- Okada (2020) implies that norm structure, the visibility and assessment of reputational information, and error rates moderate the efficacy of sanctions: if the implemented punishment rules lack reliable reputation updating or support only cruder forms of norm adherence, efficiency gains may be lower or unstable.
- Kraines & Kraines (1993) and Hagen & Hammerstein (2006) warn that adaptation, framing, and learning/set-up features, not encapsulated in the 14 formal prediction dimensions, could alter outcomes—especially if some features systematically vary between studies/games.

**Uncertainty Caveat:**
- No empirical PGG evidence is present in this paper set; thus, while theoretical predictions are strong in the direction of higher efficiency with punishment, the quantitative effect size, boundary cases (e.g., very high punishment cost, weak punishment technology), and real-world generalizability are not observable in this literature.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed (modeled specifically for efficiency effect):**
- *player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost* (Bowles & Gintis, 2004) — All parameters are shown to interact with punishment's impact on efficiency, with robustness to variation in these.

**Indirectly Informed:**
- *all_or_nothing* (Okada, 2020, Kraines & Kraines, 1993)—modeled in adjacent, non-PGG frameworks, but influences thresholding and learning in some variants.
- *punishment_tech* (Okada, 2020)—discussed via the nature of punishment visibility and effectiveness, but not mapped to efficiency outcomes.
- *num_rounds* (all theory papers) — considered as condition for strategy evolution/learning.

**Only Contextually Discussed:**
- *chat, default_contrib, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech* — not directly modeled or tested for effects on efficiency when punishment is enabled versus disabled. Hagen & Hammerstein (2006) raise the possibility that framing-related dimensions may matter, but do not provide direct or modeled analysis.

**Effectively Missing:**
- *Most visibility and reward-based dimensions* receive minimal or no attention with respect to their impact on efficiency under punishment; that is, *chat, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech, default_contrib* are not explored as moderators of efficiency.

# 7) Important Limitations

- The paper set is purely theoretical: there is no empirical or experimental evidence directly measuring efficiency change from enabling punishment mechanisms in PGGs.
- Only one paper (Bowles & Gintis, 2004) models the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in public-goods-like games directly and comprehensively across several design dimensions; it still abstracts from many experimental or real-world contingencies.
- There is little to no evidence regarding the effect of punishment when reward options are also available, or the effect of visibility, default framing, or communication—dimensions mapped in the 14 predictors.
- Contextual and framing variables (Hagen & Hammerstein, 2006) are not operationalized in prediction dimensions, but may systematically alter observed effects.
- Efficiency-related findings in Kraines & Kraines (1993) and Okada (2020) are limited to dyadic or indirect reciprocity environments, which may differ fundamentally from group public goods settings.
- The theoretical nature and high abstraction level mean that all efficiency estimates are subject to the accuracy and realism of model assumptions about agent types, information, and rationality.
- There may be disagreement or ambiguity about the magnitude or universality of punishment’s effect on efficiency, especially outside controlled, theory-aligned environments.

**In summary:** This literature set makes a strong theoretical case—from one paper—for a positive effect of enabling costly peer punishment on efficiency in public-goods-type environments, robust across a core subset of game design dimensions (player count, rounds, mpcr, punishment cost). The rest of the theoretical literature emphasizes the importance of context, norm structure, and learning, but provides limited or no direct mapping from design dimensions to efficiency outcomes in PGGs. Empirical confirmation, moderation by less-modeled features, and actual implementation contingencies are all missing. Caution is warranted in generalizing predictions, beyond the robust theoretical baseline.
