# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 31 papers that include a mix of empirical lab experiments (about one-third), formal theoretical models (about half), and a smaller fraction of field or simulation work. The base is moderately broad along the spectrum of social dilemma environments (encompassing standard public goods games (PGG), close variants, coordination games, trust and delegation games, and threshold collective action games), though only a subset directly addresses standard PGGs under peer punishment with group-level efficiency outcomes. Theoretical work is well-represented, providing mechanisms and formal predictions, while empirical papers often report quantitative treatment effects. There is good coverage of parameterized game designs (e.g., group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost), but many contexts are adjacent (coordination games, prisoner's dilemma, or trust games) rather than PGGs per se.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:  
- *Exact*: Roughly a third of the papers study standard or optional public goods games (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Botta et al., 2021).
- *Close*: Several important studies use closely related collective action environments or generalized exchange (e.g., Kanitsar, 2021; Bolle, 2021; Peng, 2022 for rewards).
- *Adjacent*: Many models and experiments focus on coordination, trust, investment, donation, or team games, which share features but differ in payoff externalities or institutional structure (e.g., Herne et al., 2022; Lec et al., 2023; Peña et al., 2024).

**punishment_or_sanctions**:  
- *Exact*: Rigorous coverage of costly peer or formal punishment in standard or optional public goods games (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Botta et al., 2021).
- *Adjacent/Close*: Studies of third-party, coordinated, exclusionary, or institutional punishment; some use structural analogues like refusal to cooperate or partner choice (Di Guida et al., 2021; Kurokawa, 2022).
- *None/Weak*: Papers focusing only on rewards or monitoring without sanctioning (Peng, 2022; Becchetti et al., 2015).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- *Exact*: Several key PGG and variant papers report group efficiency, total earnings, or average payoffs (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Kanitsar, 2021).
- *Close*: Adjacent studies focus on coordination efficiency, welfare, or surplus, sometimes using these interchangeably with group payoff (Lec et al., 2023; Gueth & Otsubo, 2023; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018).
- *Adjacent/Weak*: Some rely mainly on behavioral measures (contributions, cooperation rates) with only inference to payoff effects (Pancotto et al., 2023; Grund et al., 2020).

**Summary**:  
While the set supplies multiple sources with *exact* or *close* relevance across all three dimensions, there is substantial reliance on adjacent citations, especially for payoff outcomes in non-PGG games, and some key design and outcome dimensions are only sparsely informed for standard PGGs with peer punishment.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related (efficiency, group payoff, surplus, welfare):**  
- Many papers report or model group efficiency (ratio of actual to maximal group payoff), e.g., Suleiman & Samid (2021), Cobo-Reyes et al. (2022), Botta et al. (2021), Kanitsar (2021).
- Surplus and average earnings are evaluated both for baseline (no-punishment) and for punishment-enabled games; these are core to the prediction task (Lec et al., 2023; Gueth & Otsubo, 2023).
- Some empirical and theory studies focus on social cost minimization, not just group contributions (Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).
- Several adjacent studies rely primarily on non-payoff metrics but infer payoff effects based on contribution rates or equilibrium selection (Pancotto et al., 2023; Grund et al., 2020; Liu & Riyanto, 2017).

**Behavioral (not efficiency):**  
- Frequent reporting of contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency, exclusion, replacement, or reaction times.
- Some measure norm compliance, preference types (strong reciprocators, norm-keepers), or emotional states, often with presumed but unquantified links to group welfare (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Dato & Friehe, 2025; Andersson, 2020).

**Reward mechanisms:**  
- Reward-enabled games are less frequent and often are contrasted to punishment for comparative effects on efficiency (Peng, 2022; Nax et al., 2018).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical (standard and optional PGGs):**

- **Punishment generally raises efficiency, but effect size varies heavily** with cultural/social context and group composition (especially the proportion of "strong reciprocators" vs. norm-keepers), as shown in exact-relevant lab PGGs (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Magnitude of efficiency gains is moderate and highly variable**; positive effects hinge on anticipation/credibility of sanctions more than on realized deterrence.
- **Game design parameters matter:** Lower punishment costs, higher effectiveness, and certain institutional structures (e.g., centralized vs. peer) can strengthen payoff gains (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Botta et al., 2021).
- **Network structure and punishment cost are key:** Peer punishment increases efficiency in standard PGGs when costless, but the effect is negligible or null when punishment is costly or when the network is sparse/generalized rather than a full public good (Kanitsar, 2021).
- **Institutional (coordinated) punishment requires sufficient cost-effectiveness to yield efficiency gains; otherwise, costs can outweigh cooperation increases** (Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Calabuig et al., 2024).
- **Punishment's efficiency benefit is strongest in settings with compulsory participation** (Pancotto et al., 2023, inferred from contributions), and formal sanctions outperform informal ones especially in open/migrant groups (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).

**Theoretical:**

- **Thresholds for punishment efficacy:** Sufficient punishment fraction/strength is required for punishment mechanisms to move groups from low-efficiency to high-efficiency equilibria (Botta et al., 2021; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025).
- **Parameter dependency:** Group size (player count), MPCR, and punishment cost/efficiency thresholds are rigorously modeled (Botta et al., 2021; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
- **Behavioral/fear-of-punishment mechanisms:** Models with cognitive biases (e.g., prospect theory) show mild/infrequent punishment often deters sufficiently to support high group efficiency (Uchida et al., 2024).
- **Volunteer’s dilemma and PD results carry forward:** The possibility and size of efficiency gains depend on whether punishment structures can eliminate non-participation or defection equilibria (Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Annen, 2011).

**Negative or ambiguous findings:**

- **Costly punishment can reduce efficiency:** Some adjacent games (trust, delegation, team investment) find that even if punishment increases compliance or coordination, the associated welfare costs often offset or outweigh the efficiency gains, particularly when punishment is frequently used (Herne et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).
- **Antisocial or norm-keeper punishment can hurt efficiency:** When punishment is not targeted at defectors but at norm deviants (on both sides), efficiency gains from punishment evaporate or reverse (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **No strong spill-over from behavioral to payoff outcomes:** Many studies caution that increased contributions do not always translate to higher efficiency, especially when sanction/reward costs are high or punishment is misapplied.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

The literature supports several concrete guidance points for downstream prediction of treatment efficiency when enabling peer punishment in PGG-like environments:

- **Punishment almost always increases efficiency relative to no-punishment controls in standard PGGs**, *provided that* punishment is not prohibitively costly, is sufficiently credible/anticipated, and is mainly directed at defectors (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
- **Magnitude of efficiency gain is strongly dependent on**:  
  a) *Cultural/social context* (fraction of strong reciprocators vs. norm-keepers),  
  b) *Punishment cost-to-effectiveness ratio*,  
  c) *Group size and MPCR*, and  
  d) *Institutional structure* (peer vs. centralized punishment).
- *Control phase efficiency is a useful baseline;* the size of the efficiency jump upon enabling punishment is larger when the control efficiency is low (i.e., substantial free-riding), but smaller in already high-cooperation environments (support from both empirical and theory).
- **Punishment may yield no efficiency gain or even net loss** when:
  - The cost of punishment is high relative to its deterrence effect.
  - Punishment is unfocused or antisocial (targeted at cooperators or high contributors).
  - The network structure does not support effective monitoring or sanctioning.
- **Secondary/moderating design factors** (from direct or close evidence):  
  - Compulsory vs. optional contribution, information visibility, migration/endogenous group formation (increase efficiency gains when punishment is enabled, especially with open groups or observable punishment capability).
  - Cost sharing in institutional punishment, and rules determining who can punish, affect critical mass and threshold effects.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (well-populated by empirical or theoretical results):**  
- `player_count`: Multiple studies formally model and empirically test efficiency effects at varying group sizes (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Kanitsar, 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
- `num_rounds`: Many experiments use multi-round repeated play, with effects shown to be stronger in longer games (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
- `mpcr`: Central in almost all formal and experimental PGG work; threshold and equilibrium results are highly sensitive to MPCR.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Directly parameterized in most exact/close papers; effect on efficiency gain and optimal punishment rates is explicitly modeled (Botta et al., 2021; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
- `all_or_nothing`: Both all-or-nothing and continuous contributions are studied, often affecting punishment patterns and efficiency outcomes.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_tech`: Discussed directly in some studies (Peng, 2022; Nax et al., 2018), though less globally represented across the punishment literature.

**Indirectly informed / contextually discussed:**  
- `chat`, `default_contrib`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: While many papers specify these as experimental parameters, their effects on efficiency gains from punishment are less systematically isolated; `show_punishment_id` (visibility/observability) is highlighted in few adjacent/theory papers (Li et al., 2023; Gueth & Otsubo, 2023).
- `punishment_exists/reward_exists`: Ubiquitous as treatment variables but less commonly studied in fine interaction (e.g., enabling both in the same design).

**Sparse/missing:**  
- Effects of `chat`/communication are variably included, but the direct moderating effect of chat on efficiency gains from punishment is not systematically tested across papers.
- `default_contrib` and identity-revealing dimensions are less reported, with little evidence for their independent effect on the efficiency gains from punishment.
- `show_n_rounds` and summary information are discussed but not typically as focal moderators of punishment-induced efficiency changes.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Adjacent vs. exact context:** Many papers use adjacent (not exact) PGG structures (e.g., coordination, trust, PD, donation, or team games), so efficiency findings must be mapped with caution.
- **Behavioral–payoff distinction:** Some reports infer payoff gains from elevated cooperation rates, but this translation is not always reliable (especially when punishment/participation is costly, or anti-social punishment is present).
- **Unobserved moderators:** Cultural context, group heterogeneity, and norm prevalence are repeatedly shown to moderate the efficiency gains of punishment, but are often exogenous or unobserved in experimental setups.
- **Mechanism implementation details:** Variability in punishment implementation (peer vs. centralized, costless vs. costly, targeted vs. untargeted) means efficiency findings may not generalize across all forms of punishment "enabled" in a prediction setting.
- **Information about control efficiency:** Not all papers report both control and treatment phase efficiencies in directly comparable settings, sometimes necessitating indirect inference.
- **Sparse evidence on interaction effects:** Many design variables (e.g., communication, information feedback, identity reveal) are not systematically cross-examined as moderators of punishment-induced efficiency changes.
- **Reward and hybrid mechanisms:** Fewer studies rigorously compare punishment and reward or study both simultaneously; their interaction effects on efficiency remain less explored.
- **No field or long-run evidence:** Very few papers assess longer-term or real-world settings; most results are constrained to short-run lab environments.
- **Treatment adoption and frequency:** The actual use of punishment, especially costly punishment, is often lower than offered in the design, meaning that the mere enabling of punishment sometimes has less effect than theoretical maxima would predict.

**In summary:**  
The literature base is moderate-to-strong for predicting treatment efficiency in standard PGGs with peer punishment, with direct empirical and theoretical backing for key design dimensions and outcome links. However, the generalizability to novel or hybrid designs, or to environments heavily featuring features like communication, migration, or identity reveal, is weaker due to a reliance on adjacent games and limited multifactorial tests. Caution should be taken when extending findings outside of standard PGG peer punishment contexts, or when reasoning from behavioral to payoff outcomes in the absence of directly reported efficiency data.
