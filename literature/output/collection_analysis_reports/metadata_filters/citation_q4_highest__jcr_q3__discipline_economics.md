# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is balanced between empirical lab experiments (the majority) and a substantial minority of theory papers. Empirical studies focus on standard linear public goods games (PGGs) with and without punishment, but also include variants (e.g., network structures, trust games, group formation, sender-receiver games). Theoretical contributions range from general repeated game theory to psychological models of norm compliance and reciprocity.

For the core prediction task—estimating average group efficiency with peer punishment enabled versus disabled in PGG-like settings—this set is relatively broad in behavioral mechanism coverage but only moderately deep in direct, fully relevant payoff outcome evidence. Several papers supply exact or close empirical payoff/efficiency outcomes for the PGG-with-punishment versus control comparison (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Nikiforakis, 2010; Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007). Others provide outcome-adjacent results or indirect mechanistic insight.

There are notable strengths:
- Many empirical papers report not just behavioral outcomes but group efficiency/payoff in control and treatment.
- Diverse experimental manipulations (punishment cost, feedback, monitoring tech) allow for moderator analysis on several design dimensions.

But key limitations also arise:
- Several studies focus mainly on contributions, norm compliance, or behavioral responses without reporting payoff/efficiency.
- Some "adjacent" evidence covers trust games or repeated prisoner's dilemmas, offering theoretical parallels but limited direct transfer.
- Field studies and real-world institutional variation are largely missing; almost all are lab-based.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant**:  
- **Exact**: Empirical core is strong—at least half the papers are experiments with standard or nearly standard PGGs (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Nikiforakis, 2010; Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Several include trust/investment games, sender-receiver deception, and repeated PDs—useful for mechanism (e.g., punishment, reciprocal strategies), less so for precise payoff prediction in PGGs.
- **None**: A few theory or experiment papers are not PGGs or close variants (e.g., Sánchez-Pagés & Vorsatz, 2007).

**Punishment or Sanctions**:  
- **Exact**: Almost all central studies manipulate or analyze peer punishment or non-strategic sanctions directly as experimental variation.
- **Close/Adjacent**: Some treat punishment as part of a broader enforcement or group formation regime, or study punishment-like mechanisms (e.g., gossip, reputational sanction).
- **None**: A small subset discusses only reward, reputation, or anonymity without any punishment mechanism.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**:  
- **Exact**: About one-third of the papers provide direct, quantitative efficiency or group payoff measurements (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Many report contributions/prosociality as primary, assuming high correspondence with efficiency (but not always justified if punishment is costly).
- **Weak/None**: Several papers only contextualize or theorize about efficiency without reporting it or focus primarily on non-payoff behaviors.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related (Efficiency, Group Payoff, Welfare):**
- *Directly measured*: Kube & Traxler (2011); Nikiforakis (2010); Reuben & Riedl (2013); Anderson & Putterman (2006); Denant-Boemont et al. (2007); Ahn et al. (2008); Lippert & Spagnolo (2011, theory); Takahashi (2010, theory).
- *Reported as net efficiency or total earnings*: Some empirical studies, including the effect size and conditions under which punishment raises/lowers efficiency.
- *Modeled but not quantified*: Theory papers (e.g., Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007; Hilbe et al., 2015).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral (Contribution, Cooperation, Norm Compliance, Punishment Frequency):**
- *Primary or only measured*: Aoyagi & Fréchette (2009), Anderson & Stafford (2003), Carpenter et al. (various), Carpenter et al. (2004); Galbiati & Vertova (2008); López-Pérez (2008); Sánchez-Pagés & Vorsatz (2007).
- *Punishment frequency/cost, norm enforcement patterns, compliance rates*: Widely used proxies, but several studies note costs of punishment can offset contribution gains, impacting efficiency.

**Other/Contextual (Information, Reputation, Feedback, Status):**
- *Reputation and feedback structures*: Charness et al. (2011); Nikiforakis (2010); Levine & Pesendorfer (2007), among others.
- *Group formation and social proximity*: Ahn et al. (2008); Bicchieri et al. (2022).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Effect of Peer Punishment on Efficiency**:  
  Enabling peer punishment often substantially increases group efficiency in lab PGGs, with many groups reaching or approaching full efficiency that is unattainable in the absence of punishment. This effect is robust across group homogeneity/heterogeneity, as per Reuben & Riedl (2013), and changes most dramatically from low baseline efficiency (with rampant free-riding) to high efficiency (with punishment-enabled) (Kube & Traxler, 2011; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).

- **Punishment Cost is a Critical Moderator**:  
  When punishment is cheap, it is used more, often leading to high cooperation but lower efficiency because punishment itself is costly and can outweigh payoff gains from increased contributions (Anderson & Putterman, 2006). High-cost punishment is used less; sometimes efficiency is highest at intermediate cost due to the balance between contribution and punishment expenses.

- **Punishment Can Reduce Efficiency**:  
  If punishment is unconstrained (multi-stage, permits counterpunishment/retaliation), or applied to high contributors, it may lower welfare and even result in efficiency below the no-punishment condition (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Anderson & Putterman, 2006).

- **Information and Feedback Structure Matter**:  
  The type of feedback (contributions vs. earnings vs. both) critically affects how punishment influences efficiency (Nikiforakis, 2010). Showing contribution feedback supports higher efficiency with punishment; earnings feedback or combined information may drive down efficiency due to excess or misdirected punishment.

- **Social/Normative Moderators**:  
  Status of central players (Eckel et al., 2010), endogenous (voted) vs. exogenous punishment (Tyran & Feld, 2006), and group formation rules (Ahn et al., 2008) all moderate punishment’s efficacy for raising efficiency. Enabling participants to vote in punishment regimes or target low-status leaders increases the efficiency-boosting effect.

- **Punishment Technology and Monitoring**:  
  Effectiveness of punishment depends on ability to monitor/punish others. If monitoring is restricted, punishment may be ineffective (Carpenter, 2007), indicating `punishment_tech` is an important dimension.

- **Other Mechanisms (Gossip, Reputation, Reward)**:  
  Non-punishment sanctioning (e.g., gossip, reputation) can improve efficiency even without explicit punishment (Charness et al., 2011; Fonseca & Peters, 2018). The magnitude of efficiency gains is lower when the information is inaccurate, but still substantive.

- **Repeated vs. One-shot Games**:  
  The benefit of punishment for efficiency is larger in repeated contexts; finite (short) games may not sustain cooperation even with punishment (Engle-Warnick & Slonim, 2006; theory: Takahashi, 2010).

- **Information about Punishing Identities**:  
  Theories and some empirical results suggest that the ability to identify punishers may increase the efficacy of punishment in sustaining high efficiency (Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

This literature provides solid guidance for predicting efficiency in PGGs with punishment enabled, especially in classic, well-specified lab settings. Empirical studies suggest that, in the absence of rare design features or high punishment costs, adding peer punishment to a linear PGG typically yields a large relative efficiency gain over the control, sometimes approaching the full-cooperation benchmark.

**However, accurate prediction requires factoring in:**
- *Punishment cost and magnitude*: Too low a punishment cost can backfire on efficiency; higher costs reduce punishment use and can optimize net welfare.
- *Information/feedback structures*: Showing only contribution feedback supports stronger positive effects of punishment; mixing or showing only earnings can dissipate gains.
- *Monitoring/technology constraints*: Punishment is effective only if deviation is observable and punishers have reach; limited monitoring restricts the beneficial effect.
- *Social moderators*: Special group structures (status, voting) can strongly moderate or even reverse usual effects.
- *Potential for counterpunishment*: Multi-stage or identity-exposing designs enabling retaliation subvert efficiency gains.

Thus, for the downstream task (predicting punishment-enabled efficiency):
- **If control game efficiency is low** (typical for no-punishment PGGs), and the design has conventional punishment with moderate-high cost, no counterpunishment, and contributions are observable, expect a **substantial increase in efficiency** when punishment is enabled.
- **If punishment is very cheap, monitoring is unconstrained, or feedback reveals both earnings and contributions**, gains may be offset or reversed by overpunishment or inefficient targeting.
- For **non-standard environments** (complex group structure, endogenous law, gossip-driven sanction), predictions should be more conservative and account for the possibility of no gain or only partial gain relative to control.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed (papers with exact or close empirical results by dimension):**
- **player_count**: Repeatedly and explicitly manipulated (Kube & Traxler, 2011; Carpenter, 2007; Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Ahn et al., 2008).
- **num_rounds**: Varied across studies; some examine finite vs. indefinite effects (Nikiforakis, 2010; Engle-Warnick & Slonim, 2006).
- **mpcr**: Reported and sometimes varied in core papers (Kube & Traxler, 2011; Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
- **punishment_cost**: Central moderator, directly tested in several (Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Carpenter, 2007).
- **punishment_tech**: Monitoring and targeting tech detailed in Carpenter (2007), Denant-Boemont et al. (2007), Lippert & Spagnolo (2011).
- **show_other_summaries**: Feedback structure and summary format studied directly in Nikiforakis (2010) (critical effect on outcomes).
- **show_punishment_id**: Theoretically highlighted (Levine & Pesendorfer, 2007), informs predictions on effectiveness of punishment.

**Indirectly or Occasionally Informed:**
- **all_or_nothing**: Most experiments are continuous, but some explicitly feature discrete (Galbiati & Vertova, 2008).
- **chat**: Few studies enable chat; generally not a focal moderator for payoff impact, but theory papers on communication (Lippert & Spagnolo, 2011) suggest its potential importance.
- **default_contrib**: Rarely manipulated directly; one adjacent study (Bicchieri et al., 2022) examines framing.
- **show_n_rounds**: Usually a background feature; indefinite/finite game horizon effects are discussed in passing or in theory.
- **reward_exists/ reward_cost/ reward_tech**: Only a small subset (Falk & Fischbacher, 2006; Galbiati & Vertova, 2008) discuss reward, but rarely with direct efficiency measurement.

**Sparsely or Not Informed:**
- Combined reward and punishment regimes are only occasionally tested.
- Effects of identity exposure for punishment/rewarders are mainly theoretical.
- Framed defaults and experimental demand characteristics are minimally addressed.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Over-reliance on Lab Evidence**: Most findings are from highly controlled lab settings. Real-world heterogeneity, endowments, and institutions may produce less or more pronounced effects.
- **Under-specification of Multi-dimensional Interactions**: Few papers manipulate more than one or two dimensions simultaneously, limiting predictions about interaction effects (e.g., how low punishment cost interacts with feedback structure and network topology).
- **Sparse Data on Reward and Combined Regimes**: The literature only marginally addresses reward as opposed to punishment, limiting insight into contexts where both are enabled.
- **Assumptions about Contribution-Efficiency Link**: Many studies measure only contributions or cooperation, inferring efficiency gains, but when punishment is costly or misdirected, the correlation can be weak or negative.
- **Information/Feedback Structure Effects Not Fully Systematized**: While shown to be crucial, only a few papers empirically vary the format, making it challenging to generalize.
- **Limited Field and Real-World Institutional Data**: External validity for environments outside the lab and simple PGG designs is not established.
- **Scant Evidence on Some Design Dimensions**: Dimensions such as default contribution framing, reward tech, and identification or reputation mechanisms receive little direct attention with respect to efficiency.
- **Heterogeneity in Reported Metrics**: Not all studies use or define efficiency in a consistent manner, complicating cross-study quantification.

---

*In summary: The literature provides reliable, well-qualified prediction support for efficiency outcomes with peer punishment enabled versus disabled in PGG lab games, especially when standard design dimensions are specified. However, careful adjustment is necessary to account for cost structure, information environment, group structure, and moderator dimensions, and predictions should recognize documented exceptions and the limits of cross-setting generalization.*
