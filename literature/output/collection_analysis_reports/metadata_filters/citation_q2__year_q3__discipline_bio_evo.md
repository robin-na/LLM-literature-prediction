# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set includes 23 papers spanning both **theoretical** and **empirical** (laboratory and field) studies. The mix leans toward theory and simulation-based modeling, but several laboratory experiments and real-world field experiments are also present. The coverage is fairly **broad in relation to public goods games** (PGGs) and their variants, but **narrower** regarding direct empirical estimates of group efficiency changes due to punishment. There is notable diversity in the types of games and punishment mechanisms discussed (from peer punishment to exclusion, institutional costs, and external monitoring), as well as in the level of abstraction (individual- to community-level, laboratory to field, standard to applied variants).

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**  
- **Exact relevance** for traditional PGGs is met by a strong subset of the papers (esp. Fang et al., Wang & Lv, Burton-Chellew & Guérin, Murase & Baek, Salahshour [2021a], Li et al., Chen et al., Frey). Close variants (CPRs, linear division of labor) are also included (Wegmann & Musshoff, Nakamaru et al., Lee et al., Joshi et al.).  
- **Relevance label**: Majority exact or close; several adjacent/weak.

**Punishment or Sanctions:**  
- **Exact relevance**: Several papers directly manipulate or model punishment or sanction mechanisms (Fang et al., Wang & Lv, Burton-Chellew & Guérin, Murase & Baek, Nakamaru et al., Wegmann & Musshoff). Some analyze adjacent mechanisms (costly institutional entry, reputation, information/gossip) or do not include punishment (context papers).
- **Relevance label**: Substantial subset exact, others adjacent, some none.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**  
- **Direct measurement**: Fewer papers; several report group efficiency or explicit payoff (Fang et al., Wang & Lv, Wegmann & Musshoff, Murase & Baek, Salahshour [2021a], Nakamaru et al., Kurokawa).  
- **Many others** rely on behavioral proxies (contribution rates, cooperation frequency) or are silent on payoff/efficiency.
- **Relevance label**: Some exact, some close/adjacent, many weak/none.

In summary, **relevance for the prediction task is moderate**—the PGG structure and punishment mechanisms are often present, but direct efficiency/payoff outcomes are less universally reported, and empirical evidence is limited for standard PGGs.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Supporting Prediction Task):**
- **Efficiency**: Ratio of observed group payoff to social optimum; reported directly in a subset (Fang et al., Wang & Lv, Murase & Baek, Wegmann & Musshoff, Nakamaru et al.)
- **Group/average payoff, welfare, surplus, total earnings**: Used as proxies when efficiency is not calculated (Wang & Lv, Wegmann & Musshoff, Salahshour [2021a], Nakamaru et al., Kurokawa).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates**: Most prevalent; studies often measure how much is contributed or withheld (Burton-Chellew & Guérin, Li et al., Chen et al., Frey, Schlaepfer).
- **Punishment frequency/type, norm compliance, retaliation**: Measured in mechanistic/neural context papers (Baumgartner et al., Morese & Palermo, Müller-Leinss et al.).
- **Behavioral processes**: Such as norm perception, strategy adoption, or information spread.
- **Adjacency caveat**: Contribution rates are predictive of (but not identical to) group payoff/efficiency, especially when intervention (punishment) also involves cost.

**Distinction**:  
Most studies that report direct efficiency or group payoff are **theoretical or simulation-based**. Laboratory and field experiments more often report cooperation/contribution rates, only occasionally providing clear efficiency or payoff data.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Overall Impact of Punishment on Efficiency:**
- **Efficiency increases with effective, low-cost punishment:** Both theory and field experiment papers (Fang et al., Wang & Lv, Wegmann & Musshoff, Nakamaru et al., Murase & Baek) consistently find that **punishment increases efficiency** when the cost of punishment is low relative to its effectiveness, and when monitoring/exclusion is practical.
- **Potential for destruction when punishment is costly:** Empirical evidence (Burton-Chellew & Guérin) and some theory (Fang et al.) show that **if punishment is costly or easily abused (e.g., frequent antisocial punishment), group efficiency can decrease** relative to control (punishment costs exceed cooperation gains).
- **Institutional design matters:** Externally imposed or coordinated punishment (Wegmann & Musshoff, Nakamaru et al.) yields higher and more reliable efficiency improvement than decentralized peer punishment, ameliorating problems of cost and anti-social enforcement.
- **Moderators include group size, MPCR, presence of reward, and social context:**  
    - **Large fines and low punishment cost** favor positive efficiency effects (Wang & Lv).
    - **Performance-based incentives for authorities** improve outcomes (Fang et al.).
    - **Low MPCR/enhancement factor**: Punishment is particularly effective when cooperation is otherwise unattractive (Salahshour [2021a]).
    - **Information/reputation mechanisms**: Can substitute for or supplement punishment in boosting cooperation (Chen et al., Schlaepfer), but direct effects on efficiency not assessed.
- **Ambiguity and boundary cases**:  
    - For moderate/highly profitable games (high MPCR) or where punishment is too costly, enabling punishment *may not* increase efficiency, or may crowd out prosocial types (Wegmann & Musshoff, Fang et al., Burton-Chellew & Guérin).

**Behavioral Outcomes Versus Efficiency:**
- **Punishment is often frequent, but not always welfare-improving:** Some studies report high rates of punishment with little or negative efficiency gain (Burton-Chellew & Guérin).
- **Norms and commitment:** Theoretical papers emphasize the need for committed or coordinated punishment for sustained cooperation and efficiency gains (Akdeniz & van Veelen, Cofnas).

**Field Evidence:**
- The only real-world field experiment (Wegmann & Musshoff) provides *quantitative* evidence that externally monitored punishment can raise efficiency substantially (from 86% to 97%).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Drawing from the literature, the following guidance applies for predicting **efficiency with punishment enabled relative to control**:

- **If control efficiency is below optimal, enabling punishment typically increases efficiency**—but only when punishment is not prohibitively costly and is implemented in a way that avoids excessive antisocial or self-interested punishment (Fang et al., Wang & Lv, Nakamaru et al., Wegmann & Musshoff).
- **Magnitude depends on design dimensions:**
    - **Low punishment cost, high punishment effectiveness, strong or centralized monitoring, and high fines relative to defecting gain** favor greater efficiency boosts (Fang et al., Wang & Lv, Nakamaru et al.).
    - **Decentralized, peer-to-peer punishment with ambiguous or anonymous roles and high cost** often leads to less improvement, no improvement, or negative effects due to retaliation, antisocial enforcement, or cost-driven welfare loss (Burton-Chellew & Guérin).
    - **Institutional forms (exclusion, external fines, entrance costs) can mimic or exceed peer punishment effects** (Fang et al., Salahshour [2021a], Nakamaru et al.)
- **Group size (player_count), rounds, and MPCR**:  
    - Larger groups and low MPCRs generally increase the need and impact of punishment, but also the cost and risk of antisociality; optimal punishment design may differ accordingly (Wang & Lv, Fang et al.).
- **Social structure and communication (chat)**:  
    - Communication alone boosts efficiency less reliably than institutionalized punishment (Wegmann & Musshoff).
- **Reward mechanisms can substitute or complement punishment** (Wegmann & Musshoff), but evidence regarding their relative efficiency impacts is thin.

**Caveats:**
- When punishment mechanisms are poorly designed, enable antisocial behavior, or have high costs, enabling punishment can reduce group efficiency.
- Field and applied contexts support the theoretical prediction but underscore the need for real mechanisms (random monitoring, fines, coordinated norms) rather than relying solely on decentralized peer punishment.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- `player_count`: Frequently manipulated or modeled; evidence on moderation of punishment’s effects.
- `num_rounds`: Common; repeated interaction is necessary for punishment’s effects.
- `all_or_nothing`: Present in many models. Discretization affects punishment/cooperation dynamics.
- `mpcr`: Central parameter; modulates temptation to defect and effectiveness of punishment.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Both directly and systematically varied in multiple studies (esp. Fang et al., Wang & Lv).
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Occasionally present; more relevant in theories of information/risk.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Only a few papers mention or manipulate these; relevant but sparse in direct evidence.
- `chat`: Tested in a few field/lab studies; evidence for communication as moderating factor is limited for efficiency.
- `show_punishment_id`: Discussed in theoretical context (Cofnas) but rarely empirically manipulated.

**Indirectly or Contextually Discussed Dimensions:**  
- `default_contrib`: Sometimes mentioned in framing but rarely manipulated or analyzed for payoff impact.
- `show_punishment_id`, `reward_*`: Discussed in terms of reputational impact or modeling but not as major variables in direct efficiency outcomes.

**Effectively Missing:**  
- Many papers do not directly inform dimensions such as `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id`, or reward-related variables (`reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`) with respect to efficiency impacts.
- Few papers present quantitative interactions between *all* relevant dimensions; most are focused on small subsets.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Few direct empirical tests of efficiency change in standard PGGs after enabling punishment:** Most efficiency evidence comes from theory/simulation or from variant/corollary games, with only a handful of field/lab empirical studies reporting efficiency as primary outcome.
- **Behavioral outcomes often stand in for payoff:** Many studies report cooperation or punishment actions, not actual group efficiency or payoff—these are not always aligned, especially when punishment costs are nontrivial.
- **Contextual mismatch:** Several relevant models (e.g., exclusion, third-party/leader punishment, institutional entrance cost) do not map directly onto the decentralized peer punishment structure of the canonical PGG, complicating prediction transfer.
- **Sparse data on key dimensions:** Not all design features (reward, default contribution framing, punishment/reward technology specifics, transparency) are covered thoroughly or experimentally varied.
- **Boundary conditions are ambiguous:** The literature documents both efficiency-enhancing and efficiency-destroying effects of punishment, with conditionality depending on parameter values—costly, frequent, or antisocial punishment can undermine or reverse gains.
- **Quantitative calibration is lacking:** Little directly guides the *magnitude* of predicted efficiency change across the parameter space; most guidance is qualitative or identifies directionality only.
- **Potential for generalization error:** Institutional features shown to boost efficiency (e.g., external monitoring, exclusion, entrance barriers) may not generalize to pure peer punishment designs.
- **Cultural and ecological moderators are underexplored:** Real-world generalizability, heterogeneity in group context, and ecological validity remain open issues, as laboratory and theoretical assumptions may not match applied contexts.
- **Reward mechanisms not sufficiently contrasted with punishment:** Evidence for the substitution or synergistic effects of reward alongside punishment is thin.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature base provides **moderate, directionally informative evidence** for predicting how enabling punishment in PGG-like environments changes efficiency, especially when punishment is low-cost, effective, and well-institutionalized. Yet, limitations in empirical coverage, measurement focus, and design dimension breadth should temper predictions, particularly outside the theoretically well-characterized regions. For accurate predictive modeling, more direct, dimensionally explicit empirical studies of efficiency change with peer punishment are needed.
