# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is **broad and diverse**, encompassing 130 papers, with a substantial mix of **empirical lab experiments** and **theoretical models**. Importantly, a significant subset of the papers is **empirical and experimental** in _canonical public goods games (PGG)_ or very close variants, directly manipulating the presence or absence of punishment, and measuring payoff-based outcomes such as group profit, total earnings, or derived efficiency metrics. The set also includes numerous **theoretical and agent-based models** that explore the mechanisms and moderators affecting the relationship between punishment, cooperation, and efficiency.

However, a nontrivial proportion of papers use **adjacent paradigms** (e.g., trust games, prisoner's dilemma) or focus primarily on behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, norm adherence) rather than efficiency, and some examine **alternative sanctioning mechanisms** (exclusion, reward, gossip). A smaller share includes ethnographic, observational, or field-experimental work, which is contextually relevant but not always directly mappable to lab-based PGG predictions.

**Summary:**  
- **Empirical papers**: Robust presence in lab-experimental PGG settings, typically with well-documented design dimensions.
- **Theory papers**: Provide mechanistic and parametric insights, often with explicit attention to payoff/efficiency outcomes.
- **Adjacency**: Many papers are adjacent in payoff structure, mechanism, or measured outcomes (behavioral vs. efficiency).
- **Edge context**: Some contextual/ethnographic/field studies, but these rarely report efficiency in a way comparable to lab PGGs.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - **Exact or Close** relevance dominates empirical and theoretical work. Many studies use canonical linear PGGs or closely-matched variants (continuous or binary, fixed groups, lab settings). Several exceptions occur where adjacent paradigms are used (trust games, PD).
  - **Summary**: Majority of core evidence draws on exact or near-exact PGGs; substantial number of adjacent studies.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - Studies range from **exact** matches (peer punishment, institutional punishment, detailed punishment technology) to **adjacent** (ostracism, exclusion, rewarding as an alternative sanction, or reputation/gossip mechanisms). Punishment is typically operationalized as costly deduction, but implementation varies.
  - **Summary**: Most key contributions use or model exactly the type of peer or institutional punishment at issue, but important contextual evidence considers broader sanctioning.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - Direct **efficiency or group payoff** measures are present in a subset, especially lab experiments and modeling studies explicitly aimed at quantifying profit/welfare outcomes. **Many studies report only behavioral outcomes** (contribution levels, cooperation frequency, punishment rates), with efficiency discussed but not directly measured.
  - **Summary**: There is a reasonable number of papers assessing efficiency directly, but many important sources only provide indirect or adjacent evidence through behavioral proxies.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (most relevant):**
  - **Efficiency** explicitly measured (group payoff as a share of the social optimum): e.g., (Ozono et al., 2020; Nockur et al., 2021; Salahshour, 2021; Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2020; Dong et al., 2019; Liu & Chen, 2020; etc.)
  - **Total earnings** or **group profit/welfare** reported as absolute or relative values.
  - **Relative efficiency or changes in efficiency** across punishment-on versus off conditions.

- **Behavioral outcomes (less relevant for prediction, but informative):**
  - **Contribution rate/cooperation rate**
  - **Punishment frequency/amounts assigned**
  - **Inequality (Gini, variance in payoffs)**
  - **Norm compliance, conditional or coordinated punishment behavior, norm perceptions**

- Many studies reporting **behavioral outcomes do not translate directly into efficiency**, particularly when the cost of punishment is high relative to its impact, or when increased cooperation is offset by high expenditure on sanctions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Patterns**
- **Punishment can support higher cooperation, but its effect on efficiency is context-dependent:**
  - In some standard lab PGGs, **peer punishment increases contributions but reduces efficiency** due to high punishment costs (Nockur et al., 2021; Tsvetkova et al., 2021).
  - **Alternative punishment systems (democratic, central, institutionalized) sometimes sustain cooperation without a significant efficiency cost** (Nockur et al., 2021).
  - **Revealing information (endowments, incomes)** often increases the efficiency-enhancing effect of punishment/reward by aligning sanctioning with actual capacity/behavior (Hauser et al., 2021).

**Theoretical Patterns**
- **Efficiency gain from punishment is highest when punishment is cost-effective (high impact, low cost), anti-social punishment is minimized, and environment has high returns to cooperation (high mpcr):**
  - If punishment is too **cheap** or insufficiently targeted, anti-social punishment may dominate, reducing efficiency (Salahshour, 2021; García & Traulsen, 2019).
  - **Institutional reward often outperforms punishment** in error-prone environments (Dong et al., 2019).
  - **Observability, information structure, and the ability to coordinate punishment** (via voting, consensus, or reputation) strongly moderate efficiency outcomes (García & Traulsen, 2019; Gao et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2018).

**Boundary Conditions**
- The alignment of **local and global incentives** is crucial: if local efficiency is low, punishment has little or no effect on global (full-game) efficiency (Ozono et al., 2020).
- **Size, structure, and composition of the group** (player_count, network effects, conditional cooperators, leaders) are critical moderators (Podder et al., 2021; Gallier, 2020; Kosfeld, 2020).
- **Ecological/resource constraints** can limit the efficacy of punishment (Wang et al., 2021 [CPR]); in hard resource-constrained settings, punishment often fails to prevent collapse.

**Disagreement or Ambiguity**
- Some studies find **neutral or negative effects** of punishment on efficiency, especially in baseline-cooperative contexts (Nockur et al., 2021; Nair et al., 2018).
- Others argue **institutional design, reputation, or rewards** can substitute for punishment, sometimes more efficiently (Liu & Chen, 2020; Dong et al., 2019).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For the core prediction task ("given design dimensions and control efficiency, predict treatment efficiency with peer punishment enabled"):**
- **Direct empirical and theoretical evidence** suggests that the marginal effect of enabling punishment on efficiency is highly moderated by:
  - **Punishment cost and impact**: High punishment cost relative to its impact often means higher cooperation is achieved at net efficiency loss (Nockur et al., 2021; Dong et al., 2019). **Institutional, democratic, or coordinated punishment** (lower cost/broader base) can be less harmful or even neutral for efficiency.
  - **Returns to cooperation (mpcr)**: Greater efficiency gains from punishment in high-mpcr games, but little or negative effect if mpcr is low (Salahshour, 2021; Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2020).
  - **Group size and structure**: Effectiveness of punishment may decline in large groups if targeting is hard, but group heterogeneity or leader/pool-based punishment can overcome free-rider and bystander problems (Ozono et al., 2020; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2018).
  - **Type of punishment system**: Peer punishment can decrease efficiency, while institutional or democratic punishment may maintain or increase it (Nockur et al., 2021; Dong et al., 2019; Liu & Chen, 2020).
  - **Scope of application and observability**: Efficiency gains are more likely where punishment is observable and conditional strategies are facilitated (García & Traulsen, 2019).
- **Baseline (control) efficiency** informs the potential for efficiency gain:  
  - When **control efficiency is already high**, enabling punishment often reduces efficiency—the efficiency loss from costly punishment is not overcome by higher cooperation (Nockur et al., 2021; Nair et al., 2018).
  - When **control efficiency is low** (high temptation to defect), the marginal gain from punishment can be positive, depending on system parameters (Nair et al., 2018; Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2020).
- **Information and transparency** enhance the efficiency of punishment (Hauser et al., 2021; Podder et al., 2021).  
  - If players can accurately observe others' behavior and/or endowments, punishment is more effectively targeted, raising the likelihood of efficiency improvement.
- **Interactions with other mechanisms** (reward, reputation):  
  - When both reward and punishment are feasible, institutional reward may be preferable, especially under bounded rationality and error (Dong et al., 2019).
  - Reputation systems, especially when aligned with social norms, can substitute or complement punishment for efficiency gains (Podder et al., 2021; Okada, 2020).

**Summary Table of Typical Effects:**  
| Baseline Efficiency | Punishment Cost/Impact | System Type          | Predicted Marginal Effect of Punishment on Efficiency       |
|---------------------|-----------------------|----------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|
| High                | High                  | Peer/Decentralized   | Negative (efficiency loss from costly punishment)           |
| High                | Low                   | Institutional/Demo   | Neutral to moderately positive                              |
| Low                 | High                  | Peer                 | Weakly positive or neutral (may not recover full efficiency)|
| Low                 | Low                   | Institutional/Demo   | Positive (efficiency gains likely)                          |

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed**:
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `all_or_nothing`:
  - Most experiments and models specify these parameters, and key findings often provide explicit relationships with efficiency effects (e.g., larger groups, punishment cost, and mpcr as moderators).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`:
  - When present, often compared directly to punishment (Dong et al., 2019; Liu & Chen, 2020).

**Indirectly informed**:
- `show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries`, `chat` (communication), `default_contrib`, `show_n_rounds`:
  - Some studies manipulate information structure and visibility; evidence is indirect and often behavioral (e.g., chat/communication boosts cooperation, improves targeting and impact of punishment, but efficiency data inconsistent).

**Contextually discussed**:
- `show_n_rounds` (knowledge of game horizon): Some models/experiments discuss finite/infinite horizons as contextually important for sustaining cooperation and punishment; only a few report effects on efficiency.

**Effectively missing**:
- `default_contrib` (framing): Rarely manipulated as a main dimension; unclear direct effect on efficiency differential between punishment-on/off.
- `show_other_summaries`: Some information/communication manipulations, but rarely as a focal variable for efficiency moderators.
- Granularity of **implementation details** for peer vs. institutional punishment: Some mechanistic papers examine these in detail; many empirical studies use canonical peer punishment with little variation.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse direct efficiency measurement** in many behavioral studies: Numerous papers report only cooperation/contribution rates or punishment behavior, making inference about efficiency speculative.
- **Generalizability**:  
  - Many models and lab experiments use small, homogeneous groups and stylized settings; results may not transfer directly to field settings, larger groups, or different cultures.
  - Some theory papers assume infinite populations or specific updating rules, which may lack direct empirical analogs.
- **Punishment implementation heterogeneity**:  
  - Many studies use canonical peer punishment; fewer vary the technology (e.g., central, democratic, leader-driven, exclusion) and report efficiency outcomes.
- **Boundary cases underrepresented**:  
  - Little direct parametric evidence available for very large or very small punishment cost/impact regimes, rare network structures, or settings with complex reputation/punishment interactions.
- **Interaction effects**: Few studies systematically vary multiple dimensions (e.g., group size × mpcr × punishment cost) to quantify their interaction on efficiency.
- **Control efficiency dependence**: Marginal efficiency gains from punishment are conditional on baseline efficiency, but mapping this function empirically or theoretically is incomplete.
- **Ambiguity/disagreement**:  
  - Literature contains **both positive and negative findings** about the efficiency effects of punishment, especially for peer punishment in cooperative baselines and in the presence of anti-social punishment or retaliation.
  - Mechanism-specific limitations: Presence of corruption, implementation errors, or poor observability can reverse efficiency effects predicted by standard theory.

**Bottom Line:**  
The literature provides **robust, but context-dependent guidance** for predicting the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, especially as a function of design dimensions like `mpcr`, `player_count`, and `punishment_cost`. However, empirical evidence on direct efficiency outcomes (as opposed to behavioral proxies) is not universal, effects are often conditional on control (no-punishment) efficiency, and important moderators (e.g., information structure, reputation, anti-social punishment risk) can reverse or attenuate expected gains. Prediction efforts should exploit those studies that provide both control and punishment-enabled efficiency data under specified parameterizations, and extrapolate cautiously beyond their scope.
