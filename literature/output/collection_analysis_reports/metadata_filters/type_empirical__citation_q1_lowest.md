# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large (348 papers) and heavily empirical, composed almost entirely of laboratory and field experiments using economic games, primarily public goods games (PGGs) and close variants. The core evidence is empirical, with very limited theoretical or mechanism-only papers. The set is broad on payoff-based and cooperation-related outcomes and rich in direct tests of punishment interventions in PGGs, including a range of institutions (peer, leader, centralized, third-party, formal/informal, endogenous/exogenous), group sizes, time horizons, and punishment cost structures.

The coverage for the prediction task is substantial in the core domain: numerous studies report both control (no-punishment) and treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency outcomes, directly aligning with the prediction goal. There are also many studies analyzing moderators of punishment effects, such as group composition, heterogeneity, monitoring, information, communication, and the presence of rewards or alternative institutions.

However, a sizable minority of the evidence is adjacent—studying punishment in related games (e.g., threshold or common-pool resource dilemmas, coordination games), or focusing on norm enforcement, reputation, or psychological drivers of punishment, with only behavioral outcomes reported. Still others do not report efficiency or payoff outcomes at all, focusing instead on contribution rates, punishment behavior, or social evaluations.

# 2) Task Relevance

### a) `pgg_or_variant`
**Relevance:** The coverage is close to comprehensive for standard linear public goods games and well-covered for common variants (binary/all-or-nothing, threshold, CPR). About two-thirds of the set are exact or close PGGs, with most others being adjacent (Prisoner's Dilemma, trust games, allocation/ultimatum, team production). Purely non-game or context-only papers are rare.

- **Labels:** `exact` for standard linear/multi-round/continuous/binary PGGs; `close` for CPRs, threshold/voluntary contribution games, coordination games, or trust games with similar structures; `adjacent` for dyadic or allocation games where the central dilemma or punishment mechanism is not central.

### b) `punishment_or_sanctions`
**Relevance:** There is dense, direct evidence testing the enablement of punishment or sanctioning (peer, leader, third-party, exclusion, formal/informal). Forms include peer-to-peer deduction, centralized punishment, ostracism, costless/costly, binary/continuous, stratified authority, and endogenous institution choice. Many studies manipulate or compare punishment cost and technology.

- **Labels:** `exact` in most empirical PGG tests with direct punishment treatments; `close` in those with sanctions as part of CPR/contest/conflict games, or non-costly social 'punishment' (ostracism, exclusion, scolding); `adjacent` in studies focusing only on reputation, partner selection, or communication as norm enforcement.

### c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
**Relevance:** Group efficiency (total/group payoff relative to the cooperative benchmark) is a primary or secondary outcome in many studies, particularly those in top economics and behavioral science journals. Some focus on 'group payoff,' 'earnings,' or 'welfare.' Others report only contributions, cooperation/failure rates, or punishment frequency.

- **Labels:** `exact` when efficiency (total payoff or fraction of social optimum) is directly measured; `close` when total group earnings, welfare, or closely related surplus measures are provided; `adjacent` when only contributions, compliance, norm-congruent choices, or behavioral outcomes (not payoff-based) are reported.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:** These include efficiency (total group payoff relative to the full cooperation optimum), group/average payoff, total group earnings, welfare, and surplus. Many studies directly compare these across control (punishment-off) and treatment (punishment-on) conditions, with sufficient detail for quantitative prediction. A significant fraction also measure secondary outcomes like profit variance, equity, and the distribution of earnings (Gini coefficient).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:** These encompass individual and group contribution rates, cooperation frequency, norm compliance, punishment assigned/frequency, retaliation, exclusion, norm conformity, and willingness to sanction. Over a third of studies report only these outcomes; some conflate increased cooperation with implied efficiency, but this is not always justified (see below).

**Distinction:** Outcomes like increased contributions do not always map to higher efficiency because the costliness and targeting of punishment matter: costly or misdirected punishment can lower net payoffs even as cooperation rises. This distinction is made explicitly in numerous papers and is evident in mixed or negative efficiency findings despite higher contribution rates.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### General synthesis:

- **Mean effect:** Enabling peer or centralized punishment in standard (lab-type) PGGs increases group efficiency in many but not all studies. The average effect is positive but moderate; efficiency gains depend critically on institutional design and behavioral context.
- **Positive effects:** Efficiency gains from punishment are robustly found in standard, small-group, repeated PGGs with moderate punishment cost and effectiveness, especially when baseline (control) efficiency is low due to rampant free riding (e.g., (Zhang et al., 2024); (Gürdal et al., 2021); (Kamei, 2024); (Harrell, 2019); (Sparks et al., 2024); (Peng & Fan, 2023); (Hou et al., 2019); (Joseph et al., 2025)).
- **Mixed or negative effects:** In a substantial number of studies—particularly those with high punishment cost, short time horizons, high anti-social punishment, group heterogeneity/ inequality, or poor monitoring—enabling punishment does NOT yield efficiency gains, and may even lower efficiency (e.g., (Botelho et al., 2022); (Fehr et al., 2010); (Chen, 2022); (Robbett, 2019); (Milinski & Marotzke, 2022); (Casari & Tavoni, 2024)).
- **Moderators:** The design dimensions most strongly moderating the effect of punishment on efficiency are: number of rounds (game length), punishment cost/effectiveness, group size and structure, monitoring and feedback, the presence of reputation or communication, the possibility for counter-punishment or anti-social punishment, endowment heterogeneity/inequality, and the nature of institutional choice (exogenous vs. endogenous).
- **Control efficiency as predictor:** There is strong evidence that control (no-punishment) efficiency is predictive of the potential treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency, but only if key design moderators are matched (e.g., punishment tech, monitoring, anti-social punishment risk).
- **Role of rewards:** When included, rewards are often found to increase efficiency more reliably than punishment or to work synergistically with punishment. In some heterogeneous or high-antisocial-punishment environments, rewards outperform punishment for efficiency gains (e.g., (Chen, 2022); (Chugunova et al., 2020); (Hou et al., 2019); (Peng, 2022); (Heine & Strobel, 2020)).
- **Institutional competition and endogenous choice:** Efficiency gains from punishment are often largest when institutions are endogenously chosen, when institutional competition is possible, or when the threat of (rather than the use of) punishment is salient (e.g., (Gürdal et al., 2021); (Chugunova et al., 2020); (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022)).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Predict efficiency as a function of control efficiency plus moderators:** Use both the control (no-punishment) efficiency and explicit game design moderators (see section 6) to predict treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency. Control efficiency alone is insufficient if moderators diverge.
- **Expect positive efficiency gains from punishment when:** Group size is small (N ≤ 5), the number of rounds is moderate to high (≥ 10), punishment cost is moderate (e.g., 1:3 cost-to-impact ratio), anti-social punishment is rare, monitoring is full or high-quality, and feedback is detailed.
- **Be cautious when predicting positive efficiency effects if:** Punishment is high-cost or low-impact (inefficient), game is very short (≤ 10 rounds), group size is large or variable, anti-social punishment is common (as with heterogeneity or cross-cutting group identities), monitoring/feedback is poor, endowments or MPCRs are heterogeneous, or group composition is pluriform.
- **Adjust expectations for punishment institution type:** Centralized or leader punishment often yields higher efficiency than peer punishment, especially when the leader is prosocial, accountable, or selected endogenously (Harrell, 2019; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Lim & Zhang, 2020).
- **Incorporate contextual factors:** Transparency, communication, cultural context, reputation mechanisms, and institutional history can all modulate the efficiency effect of punishment, sometimes amplifying and sometimes reversing expected gains (Molenmaker et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Do NOT infer positive efficiency effects solely from increased contribution rates:** Contribution increases are common even when net payoffs decline (Fehr et al., 2010; Botelho et al., 2022; Chen, 2022; Casari & Tavoni, 2024; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).
- **In adjacent or non-exact PGG settings:** Be highly cautious when generalizing the efficiency impact of punishment if the game structure departs from canonical lab PGGs (e.g., collective threshold, CPR, contest/competition, trust games, or institutional settings with complex real-world features). Efficiency effects may become heavily context-dependent or even negative (Milinski & Marotzke, 2022; Robbett, 2019; Heine & Strobel, 2020; Del Ponte et al., 2025).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**
- `player_count`: Well covered; efficiency effects often reported separately for N=2–8. Larger groups sometimes face greater coordination/monitoring problems.
- `num_rounds`: Strongly directly covered; longer games tend to see greater efficiency gains from punishment, all else equal.
- `mpcr`: Marginal per-capita return is a primary manipulation and moderator; lower MPCR tends to make punishment less effective or efficiency-gains harder.
- `punishment_cost` & `punishment_tech`: Directly reported in most high-quality labs; cost-impact ratios are key in prediction (e.g., 1:3, 1:2, 1:1).
- `all_or_nothing`: Both binary ('all-or-nothing') and continuous ('linear') PGGs are well represented; effects of punishment can differ by discretization.
- `chat` (communication): Modulates effect of punishment; often tested as an orthogonal or joint treatment.
- `show_other_summaries`/`show_n_rounds`: Feedback and information structure (including round horizon) are directly manipulated in a subset of studies, with effects on efficiency and behavior.

**Indirectly informed/contextually discussed:**
- `default_contrib`: Opt-in vs. opt-out is less frequently a primary manipulation but sometimes discussed (see, e.g., Liu et al., 2022).
- `show_punishment_id`: Public vs. anonymous punishment is discussed in some studies; identity revelation can alter efficiency outcomes (anti-social punishment, retaliation).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Present in a minority of studies; when reward is included, its net efficiency effect is often compared to punishment.

**Effectively missing/rarely discussed:**
- Some summary/evaluation dimensions are only mentioned tangentially or are sparsely covered (e.g., co-presence of reward and punishment, very large group sizes [N>10], or automatic/algorithmic vs. voluntary punishment).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency outcomes are often context-dependent:** Many studies highlight that the net effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive and depends on game design, the costliness and frequency of punishment, group composition, and behavioral/psychological moderators.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes are not reliable proxies:** Many studies report only contribution or cooperation rates, which do not always predict efficiency directionally or in magnitude; costly punishment can increase cooperation but reduce overall payoff.
- **Anti-social punishment and heterogeneity limit generalizability:** In settings with endowment or benefit heterogeneity, anti-social punishment, group conflict, or discrimination can negate or reverse efficiency gains (Molenmaker et al., 2023; Chen, 2022; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021; Botelho et al., 2022).
- **Real-world and institutionally complex settings are underrepresented:** While lab evidence is strong for canonical PGGs, field or complex real-world settings with overlapping institutions, endogenous group formation, or informal sanctions present more mixed and less predictable effects.
- **Incomplete or poorly specified design dimension reporting:** Some studies lack full details on all 14 relevant dimensions, especially legacy or adjacent studies, which may limit the precision of dimension-level prediction.
- **Limited evidence for certain design extremes:** Very high or low punishment cost, extremely large groups, or hybrid settings (overlapping peer and central punishment, or dynamic group composition) are less directly tested.

**Summary:**  
The literature covered provides a rich, detailed, and nuanced empirical base for predicting the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, especially when mapping design dimensions and control efficiency to treatment outcomes. The net effect of enabling punishment is positive in many standard settings but varies significantly with cost structure, monitoring, group composition, and institutional context. Control (no-punishment) efficiency is informative but should always be adjusted for key game moderators. In settings with high anti-social punishment, heterogeneity, or poor monitoring, efficiency gains may be absent or negative. Contribution or cooperation rate increases do not guarantee efficiency gains. Prediction models must account for design-level moderators and institutional complexity as documented in the literature.
