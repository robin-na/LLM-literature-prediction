# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set contains a mix of empirical (primarily laboratory experiments) and theoretical studies, with the largest concentration of direct evidence on standard public goods games (PGGs), their close variants (e.g., voluntary participation, generalized exchange, minimum effort, delegation games), and adjacent social dilemmas (e.g., Prisoner's Dilemma, stag-hunt, ultimatum bargaining). Among 36 papers, approximately one-third provide *exact* empirical evidence on the prediction target—how enabling peer (or institutional) punishment alters efficiency in a public-goods-game-like environment compared to a no-punishment control (see, e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).

Most empirical studies clearly report actual group efficiency, total payoffs, or closely related welfare metrics. Theory papers complement by formally analyzing equilibrium efficiency under various punishment and design parameterizations. There is moderate breadth in punishment mechanisms (peer vs. centralized, costly vs. costless, coordinated vs. uncoordinated), game design features (group size, rounds, MPCR, optional participation, etc.), and settings (culture, openness, matching rules, communication, etc.).

A substantial subset of papers are adjacent (addressing closely related environments such as coordination games, trust games, prisoner’s dilemmas) or emphasize behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, norm compliance) rather than efficiency per se. However, almost all papers specifying payoff metrics do so in a way that is at least *close* to the prediction task.

Overall, the evidence base is strong for empirical PGGs and their direct variants, moderate for theoretical support, and supplemented by adjacent evidence on related structures. Some key design dimensions (e.g., chat, identity visibility, advanced punishment/reward tech) are less frequently varied or parsed.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:  
- *exact*: Majority of core studies use standard PGGs or direct extensions (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Pancotto et al., 2023; Botta et al., 2021).
- *close*: Some studies use settings that maintain aggregation/comparability (e.g., minimum effort, generalized exchange, optional participation).
- *adjacent/weak*: Many relevant adjacent papers use coordination, trust, or prisoners' dilemma games to model similar incentive structures, but mapping to PGG predictions requires care.

**punishment_or_sanctions**:
- *exact*: Majority of both empirical and theory papers feature explicit costly or costless peer or centralized punishment. Some also compare to reward treatments.
- *close/adjacent*: Several papers implement punishment analogously via exclusion, refusal to cooperate, voting, or replacement; others focus on monitoring, communication, or norms in lieu of sanctions.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
- *exact*: Many papers report actual efficiency (ratio of payoff to social optimum), total group payoff, or economic surplus (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Kanitsar, 2021).
- *adjacent*: Some use related metrics (receiver earnings, welfare, productivity) that are directly interpretable as efficiency for prediction.
- *weak/none*: Some focus on contributions, norm compliance, or psychological motives rather than efficiency.

**Summary**:  
Coverage is strong along all three task-relevance dimensions for standard PGGs with explicit punishment and efficiency outcomes; substantial but more interpretive for adjacent games and indirect or qualitative mechanisms. Direct evidence on the precise quantitative relationship between design dimensions and efficiency improvement due to punishment is less common.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes (Direct for Prediction)**:
  - Group efficiency: Ratio of total group earnings to social optimum (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Botta et al., 2021)
  - Average/total payoff per player or group
  - Economic surplus, welfare, or group productivity (Kamei & Tabero, 2025)
  - Aggregate coins generated

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Relevant but Not Proxies)**:  
  - Contribution rate, cooperation frequency (Pancotto et al., 2023; Grund et al., 2020)
  - Punishment frequency and assignment (Kanitsar, 2021)
  - Norm compliance, trust, pro-social choices (Golman, 2016; Dato & Friehe, 2025)
  - Preference/voting for mechanisms

Most empirical studies distinguish (or at least specify) both outcome types. A minority report only behavioral or attitudinal data, which should not be conflated with efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**1. Enabling Punishment Tends to Increase Efficiency in Standard PGGs, but Effect Sizes and Robustness Vary:**

- **Empirical and theoretical work consistently find a positive average effect of punishment on efficiency** relative to no-punishment controls in standard PGGs, provided that punishment is not prohibitively costly and is at least moderately effective (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
- **Magnitude of gains is highly variable:** Moderated by group composition (fraction of strong reciprocators vs norm-keepers), culture, openness of groups, punishment effectiveness, and baseline efficiency of control treatment (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
- **Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) is rare in standard designs**, and does not explain low efficiency; rather, its presence can *reduce* the gain from punishment.
- **Anticipation of punishment (even before it is used) increases contributions, and thereby efficiency.**

**2. Moderators and Design Factors Affecting the Efficiency Gain from Punishment:**

- **Group Structure:**  
  - **Network density:** Effectiveness is diminished or absent in sparse interaction structures (Kanitsar, 2021).
  - **Group openness & migration:** Formal/centralized punishment is especially effective in open/migratory groups, but not in closed ones (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
  - **Centralized vs. peer punishment:** Centralized tends to outperform informal/peer sanctions in sustaining efficiency, particularly as migration or heterogeneity increases.

- **Punishment Technology & Cost:**  
  - Efficiency gains are most reliable when punishment is costless or low-cost to the punisher and has a substantial bite for the punished (Kanitsar, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
  - If punishment is too costly, efficiency gains disappear or become negative (Kanitsar, 2021; Herne et al., 2022).
  - Fractional or randomized punishment of defectors can be highly effective at lower cost (Botta et al., 2021).

- **Game Parameters:**  
  - **MPCR, player count, number of rounds:** Most studies use standardized settings (e.g., 4–5 players, 6–30 rounds, MPCR ~0.4–0.5), and provide evidence that the positive effect of punishment generalizes conditional on these design factors.
  - **Option to abstain/opt out:** Voluntary participation interacts with punishment but primarily strengthens the punishment effect when the baseline is low (Pancotto et al., 2023).
  - **Communication/chat:** Not directly isolated as a moderator; evidence is limited.

**3. Boundary and Special Cases:**

- **Punishment is not always, and not universally, efficiency-enhancing:**
  - In trust-game or delegation settings, the cost of punishment may exceed its benefits, sometimes reducing efficiency (Herne et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).
  - Coordination and discriminatory punishment can sometimes enable efficient equilibria that are otherwise unreachable (Gueth & Otsubo, 2023; Lec et al., 2023).

- **Psychological and Cognitive mediators can amplify or dampen the effect of punishment** (Uchida et al., 2024).

- **Reward mechanisms**: Papers focusing solely on rewards or comparing reward to punishment find mixed effects; in general, peer rewards are less effective than majority-vote or formal reward mechanisms, and may sometimes reduce efficiency (Peng, 2022).

- **Control efficiency matters:** The lower the efficiency in the control condition (without punishment), the larger the typical efficiency gain from enabling punishment (Botta et al., 2021; Suleiman & Samid, 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **The general prediction, across papers, is that—conditional on punishment being not too costly, of sufficient effectiveness, and absent severe antisocial use—enabling peer or formal punishment in public-goods-like environments increases average efficiency relative to control.** The strength of this prediction is high for standard PGGs using typical lab parameters (player count 4–5, moderate rounds, MPCR 0.4–0.5, basic peer punishment, no communication), and becomes conditional elsewhere.
- **The effect is not uniform:** The improvement depends on several key design dimensions, especially:
  - **Punishment cost and magnitude:** Low cost/high impact yields larger efficiency gains. Costly punishment can eliminate gains.
  - **Group structure and openness:** Migratory or open environments, or those prone to group-level instability, benefit most from formal punishment (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
  - **Player composition:** Groups with a higher share of strong reciprocators benefit more (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
  - **Network density and interaction pattern:** Denser interaction structures support punishment-driven efficiency; sparse or generalized exchange structures do not (Kanitsar, 2021).

- **For prediction models:** Empirically, the best-supported predictors are the presence/absence of punishment, punishment cost/effectiveness, group structure (fixed vs. open), and the efficiency of the control game. In standard designs, enabling punishment typically raises normalized efficiency. If the baseline control efficiency is already high, the marginal impact of punishment is smaller.

- **Caution is warranted:**
  - If punishment is costly, antisocial, or misdirected, or if group structure undermines coordinated sanctioning, the efficiency gains can be zero or negative.
  - In adjacent environments (trust, coordination, bargaining, etc.), outcomes are less predictable and sometimes divergent.
  - Contextual factors not always controlled (e.g., wealth heterogeneity, communication, prior group history, cognitive biases) can moderate outcomes; prediction should account for such moderating variables where possible.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (contributions, norm compliance) are sometimes used as proxies, but predictions must be anchored on actual efficiency data.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed (frequent, clear empirical/theoretical guidance):**
- `player_count`
- `num_rounds`
- `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return)
- `punishment_cost`
- `punishment_tech` (including effectiveness/magnitude)
- `all_or_nothing` (binary vs. continuous contributions in some papers)
- `reward_exists` (often as comparison)
- `default_contrib` (framing; opt-in/out is sometimes specified)
- `show_n_rounds` (game horizon known vs. unknown)

**Indirectly Informed (sometimes specified, less frequently varied/tested as moderators):**
- `chat` (communication; inclusion is specified but rarely isolated as a moderator)
- `show_other_summaries` (information transparency/public feedback)
- `show_punishment_id` (identity of punishment assigner rarely varied)
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `reward_magnitude` (mainly in reward-focused papers)

**Only Contextually Discussed (rarely or never experimentally manipulated):**
- Variability of participant types (e.g., strong reciprocators, norm-keepers)
- Social/cultural context
- Existence or salience of constitutional/institutional choice stages

**Effectively Missing:**
- Simultaneous manipulations of more subtle design factors (e.g., identity visibility, nuanced punishment technologies, or interfaces)
- Integrated model of chat, information transparency, and identity features

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse Moderator Evidence:** Empirical papers rarely conduct multivariate tests of interaction effects across all 14 prediction dimensions; most test a subset (e.g., only punishment cost/tech and MPCR).
- **Generalizability to Non-Standard Designs:** Several adjacent or mechanism-variant studies demonstrate that effects of punishment can reverse or disappear in non-standard settings (generalized exchange, team investment, trust game, etc.).
- **Group Composition/Type Effects Not Observable in Prediction:** The influence of unobserved participant attributes (e.g., proportion of strong reciprocators or norm-keepers) is strong but hard to parameterize for ex ante predictions—the presence or absence of such types is rarely known in advance.
- **Ambiguity in Punishment Use:** Efficiency improvements from punishment rely on "prosocial" application—when punishment is widely misapplied or antisocial, gains are lower.
- **Uncertainty About the Effect of Communication, Chat, and Information Structure:** Although chat/communication is frequently mentioned, its precise impact (e.g., whether chat substitutes for or amplifies punishment) is unresolved.
- **Cultural/Contextual Specificity:** Some studies document substantial cross-cultural differences; results may not extrapolate cleanly to untested contexts.
- **Reward Effects:** When reward and punishment are combined or compared, relative effects change; literature predicting joint effects is less mature.
- **Limited Evidence on Certain Dimensions:** Identity visibility, sophisticated punishment/reward tech, framing, and some display conditions are rarely tested.
- **Adjacent and Non-Payoff Outcome Papers:** Caution is required not to over-interpret non-payoff behavioral findings as evidence for efficiency.

---
**In summary:** The literature directly supports the prediction that enabling sufficiently effective, well-targeted punishment in standard PGGs increases group efficiency relative to a no-punishment control, but the magnitude and robustness of this gain are conditional on punishment cost, group and network structure, openness, and group composition. For accurate prediction, models should prioritize direct efficiency evidence, control for structural moderators, and avoid conflating contribution or cooperation rates with efficiency. Gaps remain in specifying context interactions and design features beyond standard lab implementations.
