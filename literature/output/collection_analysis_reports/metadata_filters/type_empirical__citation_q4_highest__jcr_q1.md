# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set is extraordinarily broad and comprehensive for laboratory and some field contexts, with a predominant focus on **empirical, experimental studies** (lab and field experiments) directly testing public goods games (PGGs), their close variants (e.g., common-pool resource games, trust games, contest games), and an array of institutional designs including **peer punishment, centralized punishment, rewards, ostracism/exclusion, communication, partner choice, reputation mechanisms, group competition**, and various hybrids. The vast majority of primary studies are **empirical**, with only a minority being observational field studies or theory-focused reviews. There is extensive **direct evidence** for the canonical prediction task: predicting treatment group efficiency (as defined: group payoff relative to all-cooperate benchmark) with punishment enabled, given control game efficiency with punishment disabled and detailed design dimensions.

For public goods games with peer punishment, there are dozens of directly relevant studies measuring efficiency, group earnings, or closely related payoff-based outcomes. These are complemented by meta-analyses and cross-cultural studies that add robustness, moderation analysis, and identification of key moderators, but do not always report efficiency explicitly. Some adjacent evidence comes from related games (Prisoner’s Dilemma, Trust Game, Dictator/Ultimatum Games, contest frameworks), which are methodologically and mechanism-adjacent but not structurally identical to repeated PGGs.

Supporting this empirical core, a substantial literature studies **non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (contribution rates, cooperation, norm compliance, punishment frequencies, emotions, trust), social/psychological mechanisms, moderators (culture, identity, group composition, monitoring accuracy), and longer-run dynamics (spillovers, removal of punishment, reputation formation), as well as extensive attention to the limits and unintended consequences of punishment—such as **antisocial punishment, retaliation, and crowding out of prosocial motivation**.

Overall, this evidence base is **exceptionally rich** for standard lab PGGs, reasonably broad for field and variant environments, and moderately thin for some specific institutional or contextual moderators (e.g., extremely large groups, persistent field settings, cross-cultural/real-world generalizability).

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact:** The majority of studies are exact matches—repeated, multi-player, linear public goods games. Numerous studies closely match the prediction task in both structure and measured outcomes.
- **Close:** Some studies use CPR (common-pool resource) games, contest games, threshold public goods, or partner-choice frameworks—sharing core elements but not fully isomorphic to standard PGGs.
- **Adjacent/Weak:** A subset uses dyadic games or one-shot interactions, which offer less precise mapping but still illuminate key mechanisms.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact:** The predominant intervention is peer punishment—enabled via costly, targeted monetary deduction from others' payoffs. Several direct comparisons exist for alternate punishment forms (centralized/leader/third-party punishment, ostracism, expulsion) and for reward.
- **Close:** Exclusion, gossip, and institutional formation/choice fit closely, even if not always labeled “punishment.”
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Some studies focus on informal sanctions, reputational mechanisms, or mere observation, which are only analogously related to formal punishment.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact:** Many studies measure group efficiency (earnings as a percentage of maximum possible, surplus, welfare, or average earnings), often explicitly as the primary outcome.
- **Close:** Numerous provide group earnings, welfare, or surplus, which can be mapped onto efficiency.
- **Adjacent/Weak:** A significant group measures only behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, punishment frequencies), not payoffs, which weakens their direct relevance. Many meta-analyses and mechanism-focused papers report only cooperation rates.

Thus, **the literature is densely populated with studies of exact relevance** on all three dimensions, especially for lab PGGs with peer punishment and payoff-based efficiency outcomes, but with notable gaps for some design variations (see below).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Efficiency):**
- **Direct measures:** Group efficiency (payoff as a % of the cooperative optimum), group earnings, average net income, surplus, welfare—**explicitly reported in numerous key lab studies** (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Masclet et al., 2003; Gürerk et al., 2006; Sutter et al., 2010; Putterman et al., 2011; Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Cardenas, 2004).
- **Related measures:** Aggregate tokens/coins generated, average individual earnings, or explicit reporting of control vs. treatment group payoffs.
- **Indirect measures:** Changes in group earnings compared to control, or reporting final-round/group payoffs as an approximation.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- **Contribution/cooperation rates:** Frequently measured; often higher under punishment, but not always corresponding to increased efficiency.
- **Punishment behavior:** Frequency, intensity, targeting (antisocial vs. prosocial), and sometimes emotional or motivational correlates.
- **Norm compliance:** Perceptions, judgments, intentions, and psychological mediators (anger, fairness, trust, moral suasion, norm salience).
- **Reputation and partner selection:** Consequences of punishment or cooperation for subsequent trust/partner choice.
- **Institutional participation:** Voting for punishment/reward regimes, institution choice, migration/exit.

**Distinctions:**  
While increased cooperation/contribution rates often, but not always, translate to increased efficiency, **several studies highlight a dissociation:** punishment may raise contributions but, due to punishment costs (or antisocial punishment), total group payoffs—and thus efficiency—do not necessarily rise, and can even fall relative to control.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## General Patterns

1. **Standard Lab PGGs:**
   - *Enabling peer punishment almost always increases group efficiency over the no-punishment control*, typically by 10–40% (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Gintis et al., 2003; Fudenberg & Pathak, 2010; Putterman et al., 2011; Andreoni & Gee, 2012). In standard games (n=3–5, 10–30 rounds, MPCR 0.3–0.5, punishment 1:3), repeated play with peer punishment supports high, stable cooperation and higher average payoffs, offsetting the decay and drop to low efficiency in controls.
   - **Costs of punishment matter:** *The efficiency gain is reduced or sometimes negated by punishment costs,* especially if punishment is frequent, misdirected, or antisocial (Nikiforakis, 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Egas & Riedl, 2008).
   - **Targeting matters:** *Punishment aimed exclusively at defectors/free riders is efficiency-enhancing*; permission for antisocial or counter-punishment can destroy or reverse efficiency gains (Ertan et al., 2009; Herrmann et al., 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).
   - **Information accuracy is critical:** *With noisy/inaccurate feedback, punishment can lower efficiency below control, even as cooperation rises* (Ambrus & Greiner, 2012; Grechenig et al., 2010).

2. **Institutional Choice and Variation:**
   - *Endogenously chosen punishment institutions, delegated/centralized punishment, or pooled (voted) punishment yield higher efficiency than imposed/centralized or uncoordinated punishment* (Sutter et al., 2010; Gurerk et al., 2006; Putterman et al., 2011; Kosfeld et al., 2009; Markussen et al., 2014).
   - *Reward (when equally or more effective than punishment) tends to yield even higher efficiency, as it lacks a direct cost to others* (Rand et al., 2009; Sutter et al., 2010; Andreoni et al., 2003; Yang et al., 2018).
   - The *presence of norm-signaling or communication can multiply the efficiency gains of punishment* (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Bochet & Putterman, 2009; Cardenas, 2004).

3. **Contextual Moderators:**
   - *Antisocial punishment is the chief context where efficiency gains are absent or reversed* (Nikiforakis, 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011; Herrmann et al., 2008; Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
   - *Group composition and culture matter:* Ethnic, cultural, and cross-national patterns strongly moderate efficiency effects; some societies (e.g., Russia, all-Mongolian groups) see no efficiency gains or actual losses (Barclay, 2004; Gächter et al., 2010; Herrmann et al., 2008; Gächter & Herrmann, 2011).

4. **Variants and Boundary Cases:**
   - In *dynamic/CPR games, threshold games, or with complex resource/wealth dynamics,* punishment often **fails to raise efficiency** unless paired with communication or institution-building (Janssen et al., 2010; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Gächter et al., 2017; Cardenas, 2004; OSTROM et al., 1992).
   - *Exclusion/ostracism, when enabled, is typically strongly efficiency-enhancing (even more than costly monetary punishment)* (Masclet, 2003; Feinberg et al., 2014; Güth et al., 2007).
   - *Centralized/delegated/judicial punishment* can outperform peer punishment if targeted and legitimate (Andreoni & Gee, 2012; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015; Baldassarri & Grossman, 2011).
   - *Intergroup competition amplifies the efficiency benefits of punishment;* in its absence, the effect is much weaker or zero (Sääksvuori et al., 2011; Abbink et al., 2010).

5. **Design Sensitivities:**
   - *Matching protocol:* Fixed partner matching allows for norm and efficiency stabilization; random/stranger matching reduces the efficiency effect (Fehr & Gächter, 2000; Balliet et al., 2011).
   - *Group size:* Most findings generalize across n=3–5; very large groups or networked structures are under-studied or less robust.
   - *Number of rounds:* Longer games permit emergence of efficiency improvements; short games may see the cost of punishment exceed gains.

6. **Control Efficiency as a Baseline:**
   - *In almost all designs, control group efficiency (no punishment) declines over time in repeated games, sometimes to near zero,* due to conditional cooperation and learning (Fischbacher & Gächter, 2010; Burton-Chellew & West, 2013). *Punishment reverses this decay, raising stable cooperation and group earnings*—but only if punishment is not misdirected, too costly, or applied in contexts rife with antisocial/misdirected sanctions.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Given control game efficiency, the best-supported prediction is:**

- **Enabling peer punishment will usually, but not always, increase group efficiency above control in repeated lab public goods games**, with a typical gain of 10–40% (sometimes reaching near-optimal efficiency in well-functioning, pro-social, low-antisocial, accurately monitored settings).
- **Magnitude of the effect depends very strongly on:**
    - The *cost-to-impact ratio* of punishment (punishment_cost, punishment_tech): higher cost and less effectiveness reduce efficiency or can even make punishment efficiency-reducing.
    - The *accuracy and salience of monitoring* (not always parameterized, but tied to show_other_summaries): *imperfect/noisy monitoring can lead to punishment lowering efficiency below control*.
    - The *prevalence of antisocial/counter-punishment* (design, social context, culture): *high antisocial punishment can wipe out or reverse efficiency gains*.
    - Whether *punishment is targeted exclusively at defectors* (enabled by rules restricting punishment, or group choice): *targeted punishment maximizes efficiency*.
    - The *availability of alternative pro-social institutions* (reward, exclusion, institution choice, communication): *when these are enabled and well designed, they may outperform punishment in efficiency gains*.
    - The *nature of matching* (fixed vs. random/stranger): *fixed matching increases the efficiency effect of punishment*.
    - *Group size and rounds*: Marginal effect of punishment is robust for n=3–5, 10–30 rounds; effect in very large groups or single-shot games is less reliable.

- **Predict with caution whenever:**
    - Monitoring is imperfect/noisy,
    - Antisocial punishment is prevalent (either expected due to context or evidenced in the specific experimental population),
    - Game context allows for substantial counter-punishment or misdirected punishment,
    - The punishment technology is high-cost/low-impact,
    - The social context is unlike Western/WEIRD lab settings,
    - The baseline/control efficiency is already near-optimal (small room for improvement),
    - There are group asymmetries (MPCR, endowment, identity) that may create normative conflict.

- **Control group efficiency is a *necessary but not sufficient* predictor for treatment efficiency.** The effect of punishment is heavily conditional on the above moderators and **cannot be imputed solely from control efficiency without attention to the detailed game design and likely contextual moderators**.

**Sparsely or contextually covered:** Network structure, extremely large groups (n>8), field-population diversity, and rich real-world institution mixes.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Contextual Moderators Not Encoded in Design Dimensions:** Culture, social norms, prior history, and the prevalence of antisocial punishment are critical moderators that are **often not captured by the 14 design parameters**, but are essential for quantitative prediction (Herrmann et al., 2008; Gächter et al., 2010; Barclay, 2004).
- **Imperfect Mapping for Complex/Field/Network Environments:** For common-pool resource games, networked games, or field environments, **payoff-based effects of punishment are less consistent and sometimes negative**—especially where implementation, context, or monitoring do not fit lab idealizations.
- **Efficiency Not Always Measured:** While many classic studies measure efficiency or group earnings directly, **some studies and especially meta-analyses report only behavioral outcomes**, requiring cautious translation to efficiency predictions.
- **Unmodeled Interactions:** Many **interactions among design features** (e.g., the joint effect of monitoring accuracy, group composition, and matching protocol) are documented anecdotally or in multi-arm studies but are rarely systematically mapped.
- **Time Profile:** *Short-run vs long-run effects* of punishment differ; most studies focus on steady-state or final rounds, with few examining efficiency trajectories.
- **Understudied Cases:** **Very large groups, threshold settings, unstable group compositions, and field populations outside Western lab conventions** have sparser and more ambiguous evidence.
- **Estimation Uncertainty:** Even in well-studied designs, **point estimates vary across samples, and in some contexts, enabling punishment provides no efficiency gain or causes a loss**; **prediction intervals are wide**.
- **No Mechanistic Prediction from Non-Payoff Outcomes:** While increased cooperation usually predicts higher efficiency, **there are numerous exceptions** due to punishment cost, antisocial punishment, misdirected sanctions, or offsetting behavioral changes.

---

**In summary:** The literature provides robust, nuanced quantitative and qualitative evidence for predicting the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in public goods and closely related games, but only **when design dimensions, context (especially antisocial punishment, monitoring, institutional choice, and cultural background), and control efficiency are jointly considered**. Extrapolation to unstudied cases, field environments, or settings with high potential for antisocial sanctions should be done with considerable caution.
