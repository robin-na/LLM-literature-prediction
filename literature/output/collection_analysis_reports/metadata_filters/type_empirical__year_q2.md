# 1) Evidence Base

The supplied literature base comprises a large number (386) of papers, almost all empirical, and overwhelmingly based on laboratory experiments involving human subjects. The set is both broad and deep for public-goods-game (PGG) and close variants, particularly regarding experimental manipulations of punishment/sanctioning systems. It contains nearly all conceivable lab PGG manipulation combinations, with substantial representation of field experiments, common-pool resource games, trust games, and threshold/coordination variants. The evidence base is especially strong on the direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes of enabling peer punishment in canonical PGGs under varied institutional forms.

Most studies report actual payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, earnings), though a sizeable subset stop at reporting behavioral outcomes (contributions, cooperation rates, punishment assignment). Several high-quality meta-analyses and multi-paper syntheses are present. Only a minority of studies are purely theory papers; almost all are experimental and empirical.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**: The majority of studies are of **exact** relevance—canonical linear PGGs and their direct extensions. Some are *close* variants (e.g., weakest-link/threshold, trust games, public bads, commons dilemmas) and a robust additional set are *adjacent* (e.g., prisoner's dilemma, trust games with punishment, exclusion/ostracism, or partner choice). A very small number are *weak* (animal behavior, observational, or causal mechanisms only). The coverage for standard lab PGGs with typical parameters is exceptionally strong.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**: The literature is *exactly* relevant—peer punishment is present as the main manipulator in most studies. There is extensive coverage of peer punishment, centralized punishment, costless and costly punishment, counter-punishment (second/third order), legitimacy/moderation of punishment, and variations such as exclusion, communication, threat, and reward. *Close* and *adjacent* variants include exclusion as sanction, reward, partner choice, and conditional participation.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: There is a high density of **exact** and *close* outcome reporting—many studies report group efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation), earnings, surplus, or welfare. A smaller (but still substantial) subset report only behavioral results (contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency), requiring inference to efficiency. A limited number are *adjacent* or *weak*, reporting only on attitudes, emotions, or mechanisms.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**: Efficiency, group total payoff (expressed as % of maximum possible), net group earnings, surplus, and welfare are widely reported for both control (punishment disabled) and treatment (punishment enabled) games. Some studies provide direct statistical comparisons and effect sizes for the change in efficiency. Several report both pre-punishment and post-punishment (net of costs) group payoffs, allowing disaggregation of the benefit and cost of punishment actions.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**: Contribution rates, cooperation rates, frequency and form of punishment (prosocial vs. antisocial), norm compliance, preference for institutions, willingness to pay for punishment/reward, trust/trustworthiness, and emotional or cognitive responses are commonly measured and sometimes (but not always) correlated with efficiency.

Many papers explicitly distinguish between behavioral and payoff effects; numerous studies show increased cooperation with punishment without improved efficiency (or even reduced efficiency).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Key synthesis across the paper set (empirical focus):**

- **Enabling peer punishment often increases cooperation, but its effect on efficiency depends critically on implementation details.** In canonical linear PGGs, enabling standard peer punishment generally reduces or at best modestly increases group efficiency compared to control, because increased cooperation is often more than offset by the direct costs of costly punishment (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Simpson et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016; Abbink et al., 2017).

- **Punishment institutions’ structural features strongly moderate the efficiency effect:**  
    - *Punishment cost/technology*: Lower-cost (to punisher) or higher-impact punishment is more likely to increase efficiency (Dickinson et al., 2015; Page et al., 2013).  
    - *Deterrence*: If punishment is sufficiently deterrent (i.e., expected cost of defection/punishment is high), efficiency effects are more positive (Kamijo et al., 2014; Markussen et al., 2014).
    - *Reward mechanisms*: Pure reward (or redistributive punishment) more reliably increases efficiency than punishment mechanisms, especially when both are cheap or costless (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Dugar, 2013; Page et al., 2013).
    - *Legitimacy, targeting, and anti-social punishment*: Restricting punishment to legitimate targets and contexts (higher contributors punishing lower, or known justified free rider punishment) increases efficiency, while anti-social or ambiguous punishment reduces or eliminates efficiency gains (Faillo et al., 2013; Pfattheicher et al., 2017).
    - *Institutional features*: Centralized, democratically chosen, or legitimacy-affirming punishment is more likely to improve efficiency (Hilbe et al., 2014; Kamijo et al., 2014).  
    - *Network structure and monitoring*: Incomplete, asymmetric, or noisy punishment networks can eliminate or reverse efficiency gains (Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
    - *Information*: High informational noise (as in noisy monitoring of contributions) can make punishment destructive to efficiency (Markussen et al., 2016; van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Nicklisch et al., 2016).
    - *Communication and norm signaling*: Chat, explicit norm communication, and pre-punishment threats can enhance both efficiency and targeting of punishment, especially in long-term or repeated settings (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Dannenberg, 2016).

- **Negative, ambiguous, and mixed effects:**
    - In the presence of strong anti-social punishment, large or unstable power imbalances, endowment heterogeneity, or cultural mismatch, enabling punishment can *reduce* efficiency (Dorrough et al., 2017; Kingsley, 2016; Abbink et al., 2017; Gächter et al., 2017).
    - In well-structured or homogeneous groups with high baseline efficiency, punishment often delivers little or no additional efficiency, and sometimes creates new coordination or conflict problems (Javaid & Falk, 2015; Masclet et al., 2013; Javaid & Falk, 2015).
    - When information is poor or monitoring is incomplete, punishment can misfire, targeting cooperators or failing to deter defectors, reducing efficiency (Fischer et al., 2016; Markussen et al., 2016).

- **Positive/conditional effects:**
    - *Properly designed/legitimate/centralized punishment*—or when backed by communication/norms, cost-effectiveness, or voluntary centralization of punishment power—can yield large efficiency gains over control (Gross et al., 2016; Kamijo et al., 2014; Gürerk, 2013).
    - *Combining punishment with norm-signaling, communication, or explicit agreements* consistently enhances efficiency effects (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Dannenberg, 2016).
    - Exclusion-based sanctions, ostracism, and well-structured threat mechanisms can sometimes outperform peer punishment (Charness & Yang, 2014; Croson et al., 2015).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should this literature inform prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions and control efficiency?**

- **Controls are necessary but not sufficient:** The baseline efficiency of the control (no-punishment) game forms a weak to moderate predictor for the efficiency of the punishment-enabled game, but institutional and design details about punishment are essential to adjust predictions.

- **Game design dimensions matter:**
    - *Punishment cost and effectiveness*: Lower-cost, higher-impact, more deterrent punishment is more likely to yield efficiency gains.
    - *Network and monitoring completeness*: Full monitoring with symmetric networks enables effective and efficient punishment; incompleteness, noise, and asymmetry reduce or reverse efficiency effects.
    - *Type and legitimacy of punishment*: Mechanisms that restrict punishment to clear free riders and are perceived as legitimate improve efficiency; anti-social, revenge, or ambiguous punishment (especially in diverse or large groups) can make efficiency outcomes negative.
    - *Presence of reward, communication, or norm-signaling*: These can complement or substitute for punishment, driving higher efficiency when present.
    - *Group size*: Varies—punishment can be more effective (or inefficient) in small vs. large groups depending on the network, social norms, and legitimacy.
    - *Endogeneity*: Voluntary and democratically-chosen punishment regimes are more consistently efficient than imposed ones.
    - *Partner/exclusion mechanisms*: Exclusion/ostracism and partner choice can yield large efficiency gains, often exceeding peer punishment.
    - *Production function*: Complementary/weakest-link games respond positively to punishment; linear games' efficiency effect is mixed/moderated.

- **Specific prediction rules from the literature:**
    - If the game design matches canonical lab PGGs (e.g., 3-5 players, 10-30 rounds, standard punishment cost/impact, full feedback, no reward), enabling standard peer punishment is likely to slightly reduce or at best modestly increase efficiency relative to control.
    - If the punishment is low cost/high impact, closely targeted, legitimate, and embedded in a complete and symmetric network, the efficiency gain may be substantial.
    - If anti-social punishment, high heterogeneity, or unstable power is present, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency below control.
    - If the reward mechanism is available or punishment fines are redistributed, expect larger and more reliable efficiency gains than from punishment alone.
    - If punishment is combined with communication, norm-signaling, or explicit agreement, expect larger efficiency gains (potentially approaching full cooperation efficiency).
    - In real-world or field settings with strong local norms or high baseline efficiency, enabling *external* punishment may reduce efficiency (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
    - When using control (no-punishment) efficiency as a baseline, the *delta* to treatment efficiency should be adjusted up or down principally according to: punishment cost/technology, legitimacy, norm alignment, network completeness, monitoring accuracy, and presence of communication or exclusion.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed design dimensions:**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `all_or_nothing`, `chat`, `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `default_contrib`, and (to some degree) `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`.

**Indirectly informed/contextually discussed:**
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, and `show_punishment_id` appear in studies discussing partner feedback, transparency, and identifiability, which are found to moderate antisocial punishment and legitimacy.

**Sparse or missing:**
- No systematic evidence directly mapping the effect of `default_contrib` framing on punishment efficiency effects.
- The form and interface details of `punishment_tech` are usually limited to cost-to-impact ratio, network structure, and whether centralized or peer.
- No broad consensus on how chat/communication interacts with every punishment variant, though many studies show chat augments or substitutes for costly punishment.

**Particularly well-studied moderators:**
- Costliness/effectiveness of punishment (`punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`)
- Symmetry, coverage, and stability of the punishment network
- Legitimacy, targeting rules, and exclusion options
- Presence and form of rewards
- Type of group matching (fixed/stranger), information sharing, endowment homogeneity vs. heterogeneity

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Payoff vs. behavioral outcome conflation**: Many studies report only behavioral effects (cooperation/contribution rates) while using them as proxies for efficiency. These can mislead predictions if the cost of punishment is not accounted for.
- **Edge cases and contested settings**: Where heterogeneity (endowments, valuations), unstable punishment power, high anti-social punishment, or noisy information exist, effect direction is ambiguous or often negative.
- **Context-specific findings**: Some results are highly sensitive to local context (e.g., field experiments, cultural settings, unusual group sizes or nonlinear payoff structures), which may limit generalizability.
- **Sparse evidence for some design features**: There is limited empirical coverage of certain dimensions, especially around the precise effect of default contribution framing and the nuanced impacts of summary information displays.
- **Limited data on large, dynamic, or networked real-world groups**: Most evidence is in fixed, small-N, lab-based settings.
- **Unmeasured long-term or macro-level effects**: Few studies capture the durability of efficiency gains or losses from punishment once the institution or intervention is removed.
- **High control efficiency cases**: When baseline efficiency is already high (due to local norms, communication, endogenous group selection, etc.), punishment may at best have small or negative effects, but the number of such data points is limited.

---

Overall, the literature provides a rich, nuanced, and empirically-grounded foundation for predicting the efficiency effects of enabling peer punishment in PGG and closely related games, provided that key design moderators are recognized and accounted for. The effect of punishment should be understood as conditional, not universal: institutional, informational, and cultural context, along with the structural details of the punishment system, are decisive for the sign and magnitude of the efficiency impact.
