# 1) Evidence Base

This literature base is both broad and deep for the task of predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. It includes a substantial number of empirical laboratory experiments (notably Nikiforakis, 2010; Kube & Traxler, 2011; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Anderson & Putterman, 2006) and a large body of theoretical and simulation-based work (e.g., Gintis, 2000; Henrich & Boyd, 2001; Cressman et al., 2012; Eldakar et al., 2007). These sources focus primarily on standard or variant PGGs, their close analogues in related games, and on a suite of design dimensions highly relevant to the downstream prediction task.

Empirical studies are detailed in parameterization (group size, rounds, feedback, punishment mechanics), with several offering within-study variation of key dimensions and direct measurement of efficiency or group payoff. Theoretical work complements this with analytical results and simulation-based parameter sweeps, allowing for robust expectations about parameter-moderated impacts. While the majority of the literature focuses on the core question, some papers stretch into adjacent domains (e.g., trust games, CPR dilemmas, partner selection), providing context but less direct transferability.

A sizable subset of the corpus (but not all) directly reports efficiency or equivalent payoff-based group outcomes; others primarily report behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates, norm compliance) or conceptual arguments about mechanism but lack direct payoff data. In sum, the evidence set is strong, with both empirical and theoretical coverage, but attention to specific moderators and translation of findings to efficiency in PGGs with peer punishment is required.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`):**  
- The core of the literature consists of *exact* matches: studies with standard, linear public goods games. Both empirical and theoretical works commonly use canonical PGG frameworks (e.g., 4–6 players, fixed or random groups, continuous/all-or-nothing contributions). Many "close" papers study CPR games, trust games, iterated dilemmas, or threshold goods—each sharing essential features but sometimes lacking full generalizability.
- Overall, *task relevance on game structure is high*, with a well-represented focus on linear PGGs or direct extensions.

**Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`):**  
- A large portion of papers focus on *exact* peer punishment (costly, voluntary, typically anonymous). Others study institutional punishment (externally imposed), legal sanctions, and variant mechanisms (ostracism, reputation-based exclusion). Several theory papers treat punishment more abstractly or focus on related mechanisms (partner choice, indirect reciprocity).
- The relevance for *peer punishment enabled vs. disabled* (prediction task) is *generally exact or very close*, but some models mix peer/institutional forms or consider additional sanctions/reward mechanisms.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`):**  
- About half of the empirical literature and a substantial fraction of theory papers report *exact* efficiency or total payoff outcomes. Others focus on proximate behavioral outcomes (contribution, cooperation) and infer likely efficiency effects, but sometimes this inference is ambiguous due to punishment costs or side effects.
- Some studies clearly find that increased cooperation does *not* necessarily imply higher efficiency, especially in the presence of costly or counterproductive punishment (e.g., Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Colombier et al., 2011; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
- *Relevance is moderate to high*, but predictions using solely non-payoff outcomes should be flagged.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Key for Prediction):**
- *Efficiency*: Most directly, group efficiency is measured as actual group earnings divided by the maximum possible (full cooperation).
- *Total group earnings, mean payoff, surplus, total welfare/coins*: Used interchangeably with efficiency in many works.
- Papers with laboratory PGGs often present explicit quantitative efficiency values or group earnings across treatments.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- *Contribution rate, cooperation rate*: Common outcome, especially in theory and behaviorally oriented papers.
- *Punishment frequency/assigned, norm compliance*: How often and in what pattern punishment is used.
- *Punishment targeting (prosocial/antisocial/counterpunishment)*: Important moderator but not a direct proxy for efficiency.

**Distinction in Reporting:**
- Several studies caution that *increases in contribution* do not guarantee higher efficiency: the cost of punishment may offset gains.
- Many theoretical works provide comparative statics or equilibrium welfare but may not report realized efficiency in simulated play.
- Some papers focus *entirely* on behavioral or evolutionary stability outcomes, with payoff-based group efficiency discussed only indirectly.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Punishment Can Increase, Reduce, or Leave Efficiency Unchanged—Context Is Critical**
- *Punishment frequently increases efficiency* relative to control by sustaining higher contribution (Kube & Traxler, 2011; Gintis, 2000; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Cressman et al., 2012; Kranz, 2010; Milinski & Rockenbach, 2012).
- *Direct costs of punishment are often substantial*: Cheap punishment leads to more of it, higher contribution, but lower efficiency due to resource destruction (Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007). Expensive punishment can raise or, paradoxically, *recover* efficiency despite lower cooperation (less punishment expenditure).
- *Punishment effectiveness varies by design features*: 
  - *Feedback structure* (Nikiforakis, 2010): Contribution-focused feedback supports efficiency, earnings feedback or indiscriminate feedback often reduces efficiency as punishment becomes less targeted.
  - *Possibility of counterpunishment or retaliation* (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Janssen & Bushman, 2008): Enabling counterpunishment can result in cycles of mutual retaliation, reducing group efficiency below the no-punishment baseline.
  - *Type of punishment allowed*: Prosocial punishment (targeting defectors) enhances efficiency, while antisocial punishment (defectors punishing cooperators) undermines or even reverses the efficiency benefit (Rand et al., 2010; Powers et al., 2012).
- *Implementation details matter*: Bystander approval, identification of punishers, endogeneity of the punishment regime (voted or imposed), and network or group structure all moderate the outcome direction and magnitude (Tyran & Feld, 2006; Eckel et al., 2010; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).
- *Empirical support is robust but not unconditional*: Cases exist where allowing punishment fails to increase efficiency or actively reduces it, especially with cheap or multi-stage/counterpunishment (e.g., Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Colombier et al., 2011).
- *Theoretical models robustly predict positive punishment effects* on efficiency when punishment is moderately effective, not too costly, well-targeted, and not subject to antisocial/counterpunishment (Gintis, 2000; Cressman et al., 2012; Eldakar et al., 2007; Kranz, 2010).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Efficiency with Punishment is a Complex Function of Control Efficiency and Game Design Dimensions**

1. **Baseline control efficiency remains a strong predictor**: When baseline (no-punishment) games yield low efficiency (due to low contributions), enabling peer punishment often increases efficiency—provided the costs of punishment do not overwhelm the contribution gains.
2. **Key moderators for prediction:**
   - *Punishment cost and effectiveness*: Cheap, highly effective punishment can lead to overuse and efficiency losses due to high resource burn; expensive but effective punishment is rarely used, yields lower increases in contribution, but can sometimes increase overall efficiency by saving on punishment costs.
   - *Number of rounds*: Short games limit punishment’s deterrence effect and thus the efficiency gain. Longer or indefinite games allow the threat of future punishment to support cooperation and hence efficiency.
   - *Player count*: Larger groups often see diminished effectiveness of punishment due to diluted impact and monitoring difficulties (theoretical and empirical results caution scaling up directly from small-group results).
   - *Feedback/information structure*: Showing others’ contributions versus earnings, degree of anonymity of punishment, and possibility of chat or signals strongly moderate both punishment targeting and efficiency effects.
   - *Punishment technology*: The mapping of punishment expenditure to impact (i.e., cost/benefit ratio) is fundamental—if punishment is highly effective per unit cost, efficiency gains are more likely.
   - *Possibility of antisocial or counterpunishment*: If design allows punishment of cooperators (antisocial) or easy cycles of mutual punishment (counterpunishment), efficiency benefits may disappear or reverse.
   - *Motivation and norm structure*: Endogenous (group-voted) punishment regimes, or punishment supported by social norms and identification, may be more effective at raising efficiency than exogenously imposed ones.
3. **Information for missing/sparse dimensions:**
   - Some dimensions—e.g., chat, default contribution propensities, reward mechanisms, show_n_rounds—are less systematically studied and often only contextually discussed.
   - Peer punishment identity visibility (show_punishment_id), all-or-nothing vs continuous contributions, and the presence of rewards are less consistently manipulated, with evidence mainly indirect or theoretical.

**Overall,** to predict efficiency with punishment enabled, condition expectation on (a) control efficiency, (b) punishment cost/effectiveness, (c) group size, (d) information/feedback structure, and (e) possibility of retaliation/antisocial punishment. For parameter regimes and designs matching core PGG experiments with moderate-cost, effective, pro-socially targeted punishment, expect a marked efficiency increase. For designs with cheap punishment, multi-stage/counterpunishment, or antisocial punishment, expect neutral or even negative efficiency effects.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions (many studies with explicit evidence):**
- `player_count`
- `num_rounds`
- `mpcr` (Marginal per-capita return)
- `punishment_cost`
- `punishment_tech` (effectiveness per cost)
- `show_other_summaries` (type and detail of feedback)
- `all_or_nothing` (to a lesser extent—continuous vs. discrete actions)

**Indirectly or Contextually Discussed:**
- `chat` (rarely manipulated, sometimes controlled for)
- `default_contrib` (very rarely manipulated)
- `show_n_rounds` (sometimes discussed in one-shot vs. repeated games)
- `show_punishment_id` (manipulated in some retaliation/counterpunishment studies)

**Sparse or Missing:**
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Some evidence on reward/punishment co-presence, but pure reward mechanisms much less studied; most efficiency prediction evidence focuses on punishment alone.
- `default_contrib` and contribution framing: Not systematically studied in relation to efficiency outcomes.
- Detailed manipulation of feedback granularity and framing beyond overall feedback, with few studies distinguishing contribution vs. earnings vs. both (Nikiforakis, 2010 is a strong exception).

**Special Cases:**
- *Voluntary participation, group formation/voting, endogenous punishment regimes*: Available in some theoretical and particular experimental studies (e.g., Tyran & Feld, 2006; Bednar, 2006), not general across the set.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Punishment cost/benefit tradeoff is fundamental**: Many empirical studies do not report both contribution and efficiency (payoff) outcomes, making it difficult to infer whether observed increases in cooperation actually increase group welfare after accounting for resource destruction from punishment. 
- **Sample coverage of design dimensions is uneven**: Some critical moderators—such as identification of punisher, nature of feedback, chat, or mixed sanction/reward regimes—are rarely systematically manipulated.
- **Sensitivity to antisocial or counterpunishment is high**: Even small design details enabling antisocial punishment or easy retaliation can strongly reduce or reverse efficiency gains.
- **External validity and scaling**: Most evidence is from small groups in the lab. Prediction for larger groups or in the field may not generalize, and theoretical models often highlight diminishing effectiveness with increased group size or complexity.
- **Indirect inferences from contribution/cooperation rates**: Many studies equate high cooperation with high efficiency, but this can be misleading when punishment costs are high or misdirected.
- **Variation in feedback structure and visibility**: Subtle differences (e.g., contribution vs. earnings feedback) can dramatically change outcomes. These are not always well reported or considered in combined analyses.
- **Institutional vs. peer punishment generalizability**: Some theory results are for institutional punishment; empirical findings for peer punishment may not transfer to institutional settings (and vice versa).
- **Non-standard or adjacent games**: Some evidence is from adjacent but structurally different games (IPD, trust, CPR), which may not transfer cleanly to standard PGG predictions.

**In summary:** The literature is rich and offers strong support for evidence-based prediction of efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games, especially when game design and control efficiency are known. However, critical moderators must be attended to, and prediction should be cautious in cases with missing dimension-level evidence, potential for antisocial/counterpunishment, or extrapolation beyond small-group standard PGG lab settings.
