# 1) Evidence Base

**Mix of Papers:**  
The paper set is broad in scope, but for the prediction task at hand—estimating the efficiency effect of enabling punishment in public-goods-game-like (PGG) environments—it is composed almost entirely of theoretical and simulation-based works. There are virtually no empirical laboratory or field studies reporting observed efficiency changes upon enabling punishment, but the set includes a range of sophisticated theoretical models, agent-based simulations, evolutionary dynamics, and extensive literature syntheses.

**Coverage:**  
The majority of highly relevant papers are exact for PGGs, punishment or sanctions, and efficiency or related payoff outcomes (marked as `exact` or `close`). There is also a substantial body of adjacent work—such as those on Prisoner's Dilemma (PD), threshold games, and other social dilemmas—that inform mechanisms and moderators, but often rely on non-payoff/behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation rates). A minority cover contexts (trust games, animal cooperation, institutional environments) that are further afield.

**Breadth and Depth:**  
For the prediction task, the set is **theoretically broad, covering a wide parameter space and many design dimensions** (e.g., group size, rounds, monitoring, network structure, punishment cost/effectiveness, reward, exclusion, communication) and **deep in mechanism-level analysis**. However, it is **not balanced between theory and empirical evidence**; direct empirical calibration is sparse.


# 2) Task Relevance

**a. Public Goods Games or Variants (`pgg_or_variant`):**  
The relevance of the core evidence for the PGG structure is overwhelmingly high (`exact` for most papers in the central set), with a significant number of works exploring direct PGGs or canonical variants (e.g., threshold PGGs, linear and nonlinear forms). Adjacent papers use related social dilemmas (e.g., PD, trust games), which add mechanistic context.

**b. Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`):**  
Extensive attention is given to punishment: peer and pool punishment, social exclusion/ostracism, centralized (institutional) and decentralized approaches, reward, hybrid punishment/reward, second-order sanctions, antisocial punishment, and conditional punishment. The majority of central works manipulate or model the punishment dimension directly (`exact` relevance), while adjacent research explores punishment analogs in related game structures.

**c. Efficiency or Payoff Outcomes (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`):**  
Coverage of efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes is generally strong in the core set (`exact` or `close`), especially in the major theoretical works (e.g., Perc et al., Szolnoki & Perc, Hetzer & Sornette). Many papers provide explicit efficiency (group payoff as a share of max), while some adjacent works report average payoffs or stationary welfare. However, a nontrivial share only report behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, cooperation rates), making it necessary to distinguish inferred from directly measured efficiency.

**Summary:**  
*Task relevance is high for most of the central theory/simulation-based papers, moderate for behavioral/evolutionary dynamics, and lower for empirical literature (which is sparse).*


# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff/efficiency outcomes (`exact` or `close`):**
- **Direct:** Many theoretical/simulation studies report group efficiency explicitly (normalized total payoff), or provide formulas linking design parameters and equilibrium payoff (e.g., Szolnoki & Perc, Hetzer & Sornette, Chen et al., Roberts).
- **Indirect/Related:** Some focuses on average group payoff, group wealth, or welfare (adjacent to efficiency). A few papers use group achievement (success in threshold games) as a proxy for efficiency, which is reasonably close in those contexts.
- **Empirical:** Rare; efficiency changes mostly inferred from contribution and punishment rates, or not reported.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (NOT efficiency):**
- **Common:** Contribution rates, cooperation frequency, frequency or patterns of punishment/reward, equilibrium shares of cooperators/defectors/punishers, norm adoption, and conditional strategies.
- **Usage:** Behavioral outcomes are explicitly separated as non-payoff-based, though often used to argue for likely efficiency impacts (e.g., high cooperation with low punishment presumed to translate to higher payoff).

**Distinction:**  
*The literature often treats increased cooperation as a proxy for efficiency, but the relationship is not always direct—especially when punishment is costly, antisocial, or misapplied. This is carefully noted in authoritative syntheses (e.g., Kurzban et al., Barrett, Kroupa).*


# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**1. Enabling punishment usually increases efficiency—** *but only if punishment is effective and not too costly:*
- Robust theoretical evidence shows that **peer or institutional punishment increases efficiency when the cost-to-impact ratio of punishment is favorable** (i.e., low cost, high deterrence) (Roberts 2013; Szolnoki & Perc 2013; Hetzer & Sornette 2013; Adami et al. 2016).
- If **punishment costs are high, or antisocial punishment is frequent, or retaliation is possible, efficiency gains are lost and efficiency can decline** (Barrett 2016; Kurzban et al. 2015; Kroupa 2014; Ezeigbo 2017; Handfield et al. 2016).
- Adaptive, targeted, or probabilistic punishment can enhance efficiency by avoiding wasteful sanctioning (Perc et al. 2017; Szolnoki & Perc 2017; Ohdaira 2016/2017).

**2. Moderate to high group size limits the effectiveness of peer punishment unless institutional coordination is present:**
- **Punishment effects are strongest in small, well-connected, or spatially structured groups;** as size increases, efficiency gains are harder to sustain unless punishment is coordinated or institutionalized (Boyd et al. 2014; Powers & Lehmann 2017; Gavrilets & Richerson 2017; Vasconcelos et al. 2015).
- Relatedness, monitoring, and information flow moderate the effect: high relatedness or network clustering support efficiency via punishment.

**3. Reward and hybrid strategies:**
- Pure punishment often outperforms reward in sustaining efficiency, but hybrid "first carrot, then stick" incentive policies are most efficient in achieving and sustaining cooperation with low cost (Chen et al. 2015).
- In some cases, enabling reward has little marginal benefit once punishment is present; in others, rewards enable the emergence of stable punishment by solving the second-order free rider problem (Sasaki et al. 2015).

**4. Exclusion/ostracism and alternative sanctioning:**
- Social exclusion mechanisms (ostracism) often outperform direct costly punishment for efficiency, provided costs are low and exclusion is effective (Sasaki & Uchida 2013; Liu et al. 2017).
- Permanent ostracism can be counterproductive; forgivable/temporal exclusion yields higher efficiency (Ali & Miller 2016).

**5. Influence of design dimensions:**
- **Punishment cost and effectiveness are paramount**: If cost is too high or punishment cannot be effectively targeted, efficiency does not improve or is negative (Roberts 2013; Barrett 2016; Farjam et al. 2015).
- **MPCR (marginal per-capita return) is critical**: At low MPCR, punishment is most useful; at high MPCR, gains are small or null (Farjam et al. 2015; Ye et al. 2016).
- **Network structure, monitoring, and information:** Local, decentralized punishment is more effective in small, clustered, or structured populations (Perc et al. 2017; Wolitzky 2013), while centralized punishment can be undermined by corruption (Abdallah et al. 2014).
- **Communication, reputation, and information feedback:** Enable more selective/efficient punishment, reducing costs and boosting efficiency, especially when combined with punishment (Kroupa 2014; Milinski 2016).

**6. Negative or null results:**
- If punishment is too harsh, antisocial, or is not well-calibrated, it can reduce total welfare even as cooperation increases (Handfield et al. 2016; Griffin & Belmonte 2017; Barrett 2016).
- The presence of antisocial punishment, 'perverse' punishment, or second-order free riders can undermine or reverse efficiency gains (Kurzban et al. 2015; Barrett 2016; Handfield et al. 2016).

**7. Special cases and moderators:**
- In threshold or intergroup competition environments, the context (risk, group structure, returns-to-scale) can dominate: punishment sometimes increases, sometimes decreases, sometimes has no effect on efficiency (Ye et al. 2016; Vasconcelos et al. 2015; Gavrilets & Richerson 2017).
- Forgiveness, status differentiation, and norm internalization can improve the net efficiency effect of punishment (Ali & Miller 2016; Vincent 2017; Villatoro et al. 2015).

**Empirical calibration is lacking:** These results are predominantly theoretical/simulation-based; lab and field data remain rare and often focus on behavioral outcomes.


# 5) Prediction Guidance

**A. Enabling Peer Punishment:**
- **Expect an increase in efficiency compared to the control only if:**
  - Punishment cost per unit is not high relative to its impact on defectors.
  - Antisocial punishment/retaliation is rare or effectively prevented.
  - The group is small/medium or institutionally coordinated; in larger, anonymous, or rapidly rematched groups, expect smaller or no efficiency gains.
  - Communication, reputation, or information on contributions is available; these amplify the positive effect of punishment by making it more selective and less costly.

- **If control efficiency is very low** (i.e., groups fail to attain substantial cooperation without punishment), a well-designed punishment regime with reasonable costs is likely to produce a substantial efficiency gain, possibly approaching full cooperation for certain parameter regimes (Perc et al. 2017; Hetzer & Sornette 2013; Adami et al. 2016).

- **If punishment is costly and/or antisocial, or group size is large and uncoordinated**, enabling punishment may decrease efficiency relative to the control; increases in cooperation come at too great a cost (Kurzban et al. 2015; Barrett 2016; Handfield et al. 2016).

**B. Use of Design Dimensions in Prediction:**
- Account for *player_count*, *mpcr*, *punishment_cost*, *punishment_tech*, *reward_exists*, *reward_cost*, *reward_tech*, *network structure*, and *information feedback* as key moderators.
- Enabling reward in tandem with punishment typically does not add much to efficiency except under narrow, harsh conditions (Szolnoki & Perc 2013; Sasaki et al. 2015).
- Exclusion-based punishment (ostracism) can result in larger efficiency gains than costly fine-based punishment, especially if it is forgivable/temporary (Sasaki & Uchida 2013; Liu et al. 2017; Ali & Miller 2016).

**C. Cautions and Adjustments:**
- Where evidence is based on behavioral outcomes (cooperation rates), recognize that these are not always predictive of increases in group efficiency, especially when punishment is costly or targets cooperators.
- Theory and simulation results can map parameter regions (costs, group size, mpcr) to predicted efficiency improvement, but real-world behavior (e.g., frequency of antisocial punishment, errors, retaliation) can moderate or even reverse these effects.
- For designs including communication, reputation tracking, or repeated interactions, adjust expected efficiency gains upward; for anonymous, random-matching, or one-shot designs, be more cautious.


# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Design dimensions that are directly informed (by efficiency/payoff data):**
- **player_count (group size):** Directly analyzed in payoff terms; effect modulated by group size, with diminishing returns in larger groups unless centralized punishment or structure is present.
- **mpcr (synergy of contributions):** Direct/parametric role; low MPCR environments benefit more from effective punishment.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech (cost of punishing per unit, severity/effectiveness):** Central in almost all results; optimal efficiency gains only when punishment is potent but not too costly; mapping provided in theoretical models.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Direct role discussed; reward alone often insufficient, hybrid designs can help solve second-order problems.
- **all_or_nothing:** Modeled in both continuous and binary contribution games; both forms are represented.
- **num_rounds (game repetition):** Direct role in models; more rounds support greater potential for efficiency gains from punishment.
- **show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds:** Occasionally modeled as information dimensions; transparency can improve punishment efficacy.
- **show_punishment_id:** Discussed in papers on reputation and monitoring; relevant but less commonly mapped to payoff.

**Indirectly or contextually covered:**
- **chat:** Communication is frequently discussed as a moderator (increasing efficiency of punishment), but direct quantitative links to efficiency change are rare.
- **default_contrib:** Framing effects mentioned contextually; less often parameterized with efficiency results.
- **network structure (spatial/lattice vs. well-mixed):** Technically distinct from the 14 listed, but richly explored; abundance and reach of information, clustering, and network mobility are strongly influential.

**Sparse or effectively missing:**
- Few works directly parameterize **default_contrib**, *chat*, *show_punishment_id*, or compare *reward_cost* and *reward_tech* at sufficient granularity for strong quantitative guidance.
- Empirical data on treatment vs. control efficiency for specific parameterizations are sparse absent simulation/theory.

**Important caveats:**  
*Dimensions are mostly informed for continuous (theory) or binary (simulation) outcomes; translation to real (messier) empirical settings requires careful attention to context and potential for unmodeled moderators (antisocial punishment, error, reputation, communication, group dynamics, etc.).*


# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical evidence is sparse:** Most results come from theory or agent-based simulation, not observed efficiency changes in real human groups. Predictive accuracy for real-world or experimental outcomes is thus uncertain.
- **Behavioral vs. payoff outcomes:** Many studies report only on cooperation or contribution rates, not net efficiency—when punishment is costly, these are not reliable proxies.
- **Parameter boundaries:** The literature supports that enabling punishment can both increase and decrease efficiency depending on parameter values (punishment cost/impact, group size, structure, etc.). There is no universal effect.
- **Contextual moderators often unreported:** Features such as chat, framing, identifiability, history, and real-time feedback are not always explicitly modeled, but can significantly alter outcomes.
- **Antisocial punishment and error not always modeled:** Many theoretical results assume prosocial, targeted, error-free punishment; real group behavior can include errors, retaliation, or antisocial sanctioning that degrade efficiency.
- **Sparse coverage of certain game dimensions:** Some relevant dimensions are underexplored, especially in combination (e.g., the interactions between chat, identifiability, and dynamic group formation).
- **Transfer to real-world settings:** Models often assume rationality, infinite populations, or perfect monitoring—assumptions that may not hold in practice.
- **Variation by outcome type:** Results from threshold, contest, or intergroup games sometimes diverge in predicted efficiency effects compared to canonical PGGs; careful attention to context is required.

*Summing up: The literature gives strong, nuanced theoretical guidance on when and how punishment increases efficiency in public-goods games, but direct empirical confirmation is limited and predictions should be adjusted for the specifics of the design and the known boundary conditions found in simulations and models.*
