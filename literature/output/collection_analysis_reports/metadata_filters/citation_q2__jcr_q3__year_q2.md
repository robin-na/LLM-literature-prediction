# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set is **broad and rich for the specific prediction task**, featuring a strong mix of empirical (mostly laboratory experiments) and theoretical papers. Empirical studies (e.g., Fischer et al., 2016; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Andrighetto et al., 2016) examine standard and variant public goods games (PGGs), often with direct measurement of group efficiency or related payoff-based outcomes, both with and without punishment. Multiple theory papers explore determinants of efficiency and the mechanisms through which punishment (and, in some studies, reward or exclusion) may affect outcomes. 

Several adjacent or close-variant studies address games structurally similar to PGGs (e.g., common pool resource, repeated prisoner’s dilemma) and consider evolutionary, informational, and institutional dynamics which are relevant for understanding broader moderators, though not always mapping directly to standard PGGs. There is also relevant research on informal or endogenous sanctioning mechanisms and the interplay of structure, legitimacy, information, and group size. 

There is **ample direct empirical evidence** on the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in standard linear and variant PGGs across a range of game designs, institutions, and group sizes. The coverage is less complete but still informative for reward, endogenous or informal sanctions, and exotic designs (e.g., dynamic networks, evolutionary games). Some gaps remain for certain dimensions (e.g., default contribution framing, full range of reward mechanisms, public visibility features).

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - **exact**: Many core papers use standard linear PGGs (Fischer et al., 2016; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Dannenberg, 2016).
  - **close**: Some use close variants (e.g., common pool resource [Cason & Gangadharan, 2016]; embezzlement or agency games [Lierl, 2016]; mutual insurance pools [Lévy-Garboua et al., 2017]; weakest-link games [Fatas & Mateu, 2015]).
  - **adjacent/weak**: A subset of theory and laboratory studies (e.g., repeated prisoner’s dilemmas, dynamic network models) are adjacent, informative for mechanism but not directly matching the classic PGG environment.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - **exact**: Multiple core studies (Fischer et al., 2016; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Dorrough et al., 2017; Dannenberg, 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016) involve experimental manipulation of peer or centralized punishment.
  - **close**: Informal, socially-endogenous, exclusionary, or reputational sanctions (Lierl, 2016; Lévy-Garboua et al., 2017) provide functional equivalents and key design contrasts.
  - **adjacent/none**: Some studies (e.g., those focusing on reward or reputation, or with no punishment mechanism) are only contextually relevant.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - **exact**: Many papers report or focus on group efficiency/payoff (ratio or percent of maximum) (e.g., Fischer et al., 2016; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Dannenberg, 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016).
  - **close**: Some measure welfare, yield, group earnings, aggregate coins, or mean coverage rate, all functionally equivalent for the prediction task.
  - **adjacent/weak**: Others report only behavioral metrics (contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency), or discuss efficiency only indirectly.

**Summary:**  
There is strong direct coverage of all three dimensions—PGG relevance, punishment manipulation, and efficiency or payoff outcomes—though the coverage is uneven for some exotic game designs or adjacent mechanisms (reward, exclusion). Some theoretical contributions extend predictive guidance beyond the directly observed parameter space.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, welfare, yield, aggregate group earnings/coins/coverage):**
  - Empirically measured in numerous lab PGG experiments with and without punishment (Fischer et al., 2016; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Dannenberg, 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Lierl, 2016).
  - Some theory and model-based papers derive equilibrium group payoff or efficiency as the primary outcome (Hwang, 2017; Chassang & Zehnder, 2016; Buchholz et al., 2014).
  - Adjacent games sometimes use functionally equivalent payoff measures such as mean insurance coverage or yield/regulation in CPR contexts (Lévy-Garboua et al., 2017; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, cooperation rates, punishment assigned, norm compliance):**
  - Often measured, and sometimes correlated with payoff, but not the same as efficiency (e.g., cases where increased contributions from punishment are offset by punishment costs, Dannenberg, 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
  - Some papers focus only on behavioral metrics (e.g., Gatiso & Vollan, 2017; Mieth et al., 2017).
- **Other outcomes:**
  - Some studies report norm evolution, evolutionary stable states, or frequency of strategies (Kaiping et al., 2016; Van Cleve, 2016), sometimes linking these to efficiency only indirectly.

**Distinction:** The literature strongly supports payoff-based assessment, but several studies explicitly note that higher contributions do not always translate into higher efficiency due to punishment (or reward) costs.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across empirical and theoretical studies, the following main findings emerge:

### A. Effect of Punishment on Efficiency is **Highly Conditional**

- **Punishment often increases contributions, but not always efficiency.** If the cost of punishment is high or there is substantial antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators), the net effect on efficiency can be zero or negative, despite higher cooperation rates (Fischer et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Dannenberg, 2016; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016).  
- **The structure/institution of punishment matters:** Punishment is most likely to increase efficiency when (a) it is legitimate and/or visible (Zheng & Nie, 2013), (b) it is centralized and less prone to antisocial use (Fischer et al., 2016; Gao et al., 2015), (c) it is democratically chosen (Gatiso & Vollan, 2017; Dannenberg, 2016), or (d) enhances group focal points (Dannenberg, 2016).  
- **Group size interacts with punishment institution:** The effectiveness of punishment varies with group size and whether the punishment is anonymous or the punisher is identified (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017; Buchholz et al., 2014). Larger groups may gain more from coordinated, legitimized punishment; without coordination, larger size can reduce efficiency gains or even generate losses (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017).

### B. Punishment Is Most Effective When Combined with Other Institutions

- **Communication, agreements, and norms:**  
  - Communication (chat or messaging) dramatically increases efficiency both with and without punishment, but especially when both are enabled (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
  - Non-binding agreements specifying full cooperation, when paired with punishment, yield the highest efficiency outcomes—often reaching near-optimal levels (Dannenberg, 2016).

### C. Game Structure Moderates Punishment's Effect

- **Production function/type of public good:**  
  - In standard linear PGGs, punishment does not always increase efficiency (may lead to waste through antisocial punishment or overuse), especially in cultures or groups with high antisocial punishment rates (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Fischer et al., 2016).
  - In weakest-link/threshold games, punishment can dramatically increase efficiency, as the marginal benefit of contributing is tied to the minimum level (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
  - In common pool resource games with nonlinear/prod concave payoffs, punishment often fails to improve efficiency (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).

### D. Information and Legitimacy

- **Legitimacy of punishment and visibility of punisher/punished:**  
  - When punishment is seen as legitimate and/or the identity of the punisher is known (enabling social enforcement or retaliation), efficiency is increased in larger groups (Zheng & Nie, 2013).
  - Legitimacy can be enhanced through democratic selection or clear group consent (Dannenberg, 2016; Gatiso & Vollan, 2017).

### E. Details of Punishment Technology Matter

- **Cost, magnitude, and endogenous power distribution:**  
  - High punishment cost (relative to effectiveness) reduces the net efficiency benefit (Fischer et al., 2016; Chassang & Zehnder, 2016; Yu et al., 2016).
  - Inequality or instability in punishment power can reduce efficiency (Dorrough et al., 2017).

### F. Indirect/Related Mechanisms

- **Reward mechanisms and endogenous exclusion:**  
  - Well-designed reward mechanisms and exclusionary rules can also increase efficiency, sometimes substituting for punishment (Greiff, 2013; Lévy-Garboua et al., 2017).
- **Evolutionary settings:**  
  - Effectiveness of punishment in raising and sustaining efficiency depends on population structure, mutation/migration, monitoring, and norm evolution (Kaiping et al., 2016; Van Cleve, 2016; Yu et al., 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**What the literature supports for the downstream prediction task:**

- **Direct prediction from design and control efficiency is best informed for standard, repeated linear PGGs with peer punishment.**
  - The empirical literature provides treatment-control differences (absolute or in efficiency percentage) for a wide range of game configurations (player count, rounds, mpcr, punishment cost, information structure), enabling quantitative prediction where the design matches.
  - Specific guides: For small groups (n=4), anonymous punishment increases efficiency over control; for larger groups, punishment with identification of the punisher is more effective, and anonymous punishment may even decrease efficiency (Zheng & Nie, 2013).
  - The marginal effect of enabling punishment needs to be adjusted downward when antisocial punishment is prevalent or when punishment is used inefficiently (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Fischer et al., 2016; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016), and is increased when paired with clear agreements/communication (Dannenberg, 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016).
  - For games with communication, almost all negative treatment effects of punishment are neutralized, and efficiency gains are large (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
  - For weakest-link/complementarity games, enabling punishment leads to large efficiency gains, offsetting antisocial punishment culture (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).

- **Prediction for non-standard or variant games:**  
  - For nonlinear games or games with endogenous power, the effect of punishment on efficiency is generally neutral or negative, and the absence of robust positive effects in these designs signals caution (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Dorrough et al., 2017).
  - For control games with low efficiency, the presence of coordination/communication/norms is often required for punishment to produce significant efficiency improvements.

- **For dimensions not directly informed (e.g., chat, reward, default contribution framing, information visibility):**  
  - Evidence is primarily indirect and must be interpreted cautiously. Where available, suggest stronger efficiency effects from punishment in environments with high transparency, opportunity for legitimate enforcement, and appropriate matching of punishment cost/effectiveness.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed:**
  - `player_count` (group size): Strong empirical and theoretical evidence, with non-monotonic and dimension-specific effects (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017; Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
  - `num_rounds`: Varies widely; effect of punishment on efficiency is typically measured over repeated games (10–50 rounds) (multiple papers).
  - `mpcr`: Key parameter, directly examined in many studies.
  - `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (peer, centralized, anonymous/public, stable/unstable): Critical; empirically and theoretically explored (Fischer et al., 2016; Dorrough et al., 2017; Chassang & Zehnder, 2016).
  - `chat`: Strong evidence that communication interacts robustly with punishment to drive efficiency (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
  - `all_or_nothing`: Both continuous and binary games studied; type interacts with punishment effects (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
  - `show_punishment_id`: Directly addressed in relation to legitimacy and efficiency (Zheng & Nie, 2013).
  - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Studied mainly in theory and select empirical papers; direct evidence less widespread.

- **Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**
  - `default_contrib`: Not directly manipulated or reported; implications about loss/gain framing are discussed but weakly informed.
  - `show_n_rounds`: Appears in game descriptions and is sometimes varied, but little direct evidence on impact for punishment effects.
  - `show_other_summaries`: Occasionally addressed as part of information transparency; indirectly connected.

- **Effectively Missing:**
  - Some dimensions—such as contextual framing (`default_contrib`), nuanced visibility structures, or subtle variations in summary presentation—are not systematically varied or analyzed.
  - Rare or absent: detailed analysis of reward/punishment magnitude symmetry, interactions with real-world heterogeneity factors.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Heterogeneity and Generalizability:**  
  - Much of the empirical evidence comes from small-group lab games under controlled conditions, typically with student participants; field evidence and real-world generalizability are not systematically tested.
  - Cross-cultural variation in antisocial punishment, legitimacy perception, and communication norms is acknowledged but not fully mapped (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).

- **Limited Direct Coverage for Some Dimensions:**  
  - Some predictor dimensions are under-explored or not systematically manipulated (e.g., `default_contrib`, detailed reward structuring, summary information features).
  - Many papers report aggregate efficiency across the game but do not analyze dynamic or round-by-round effects except in passing.

- **Efficiency vs. Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  - Some studies emphasize behavioral outcomes (contributions, cooperation) rather than efficiency/payoff. Extrapolating efficiency from non-payoff outcomes is only supported when actual payoff data are reported.

- **Variant and Adjacent Designs:**  
  - Several papers use close but not identical designs (e.g., nonlinear CPR, weakest-link games, mutual insurance, evolutionary models). Findings may not transfer without adjustment to standard PGGs.

- **Mechanism and Context Dependency:**  
  - Efficiency effects of punishment are frequently shown to be highly context-dependent (information, legitimacy, institution, norms, structure), challenging one-size-fits-all predictions. Mechanism substitution effects (sanctioning vs. communication vs. reward) are observable but not always generalizable.

**Conclusion:**  
The literature provides substantial, nuanced, and directly applicable support for predicting how enabling peer punishment will affect efficiency in repeated public goods game environments. Critical moderators include game structure (linear, complementarity, or nonlinear), group size, information and legitimacy, the presence of communication or agreements, and the institution and technology of punishment. Several design dimensions are directly informed; others are less so and require cautious extrapolation or supplementation from adjacent literature. Ambiguity and non-monotonic effects are noted, especially when antisocial punishment or high punishment costs are present, or when game structure dampens punishment’s effectiveness.
