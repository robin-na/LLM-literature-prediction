# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists entirely of *theoretical* works—there are no purely empirical or experimental studies. The majority use formal models (evolutionary game theory, agent-based models, mathematical economic analysis) to explore variation in public-goods-game (PGG) or PGG-like environments, sanctioning institutions, and group payoff outcomes. Most reviews synthesize previously published empirical or experimental findings rather than present new data. The coverage is both broad and deep for theoretical mechanisms and model-based scenario analysis but is notably narrow with respect to real-world or laboratory effect sizes or practical implementation details. The focus is predominantly on conditions and moderators of punishment's effects rather than direct, empirical measurement of efficiency changes across parameter settings.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *Exact relevance* is present in several papers directly modeling standard or optional public goods games (e.g., Tanimoto, 2018; Botta et al., 2021; Eldakar et al., 2018).
- *Close* or *adjacent* relevance is offered by papers modeling threshold or coalition games, repeated games, volunteer’s dilemmas, and variants with institutional features (e.g., Kol'veková et al., 2021; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Kosfeld, 2020).
- *None* or *weak* relevance in papers with little connection to PGG structure.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *Exact* focus in multiple key studies modeling peer or institutional punishment, endogenous punitive mechanisms, and sanctions parameters (e.g., Tanimoto, 2018; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
- *Close/adjacent* relevance where linked mechanisms are considered (reward, exclusion, norm enforcement, exploitation, commitment strategies).
- *None/weak* where punishment is not modeled (e.g., some papers focused on rewards or belief management).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *Exact* payoff-based outcomes are a primary or explicit focus in a minority of papers (e.g., Tanimoto, 2018; Botta et al., 2021; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
- *Close/adjacent* in many others, where group cooperation, welfare, or mean reward is conceptually related but not always directly mapped to efficiency as a fraction of maximal available payoff.
- Substantial portion of the literature, including high-impact syntheses, report *non-payoff behavioral measures* only (e.g., contribution or cooperation rates), lacking direct efficiency or group payoff outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**  
  - *Efficiency* (group payoff relative to full cooperation) is directly analyzed in several theoretical and simulation models (Tanimoto, 2018; Botta et al., 2021; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Kosfeld, 2020).
  - *Group/average payoff, welfare, surplus*—varied terminology is used but usually maps to the efficiency construct in a PGG context.
  - *Provision rate* and *weighted mean reward* (Kol'veková et al., 2021) are direct payoffs.
  - Some models quantify parameterized group welfare or social cost (Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Glover & Kim, 2021).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
  - *Cooperation rate, contribution levels, norm compliance, punishment frequency, prosocial preference frequencies* are dominant outcome measures in many theoretical treatments and reviews.
  - *Oscillation in cooperation*, *frequency of loners*, *antisocial punishment rates*, *group composition* are commonly reported.
  - Many papers explicitly state that payoff/efficiency was *not* measured, focusing instead on such behavioral markers (e.g., Moreno & Gutierrez-Garcia, 2018; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).

- **Missing outcome data:**  
  - Several high-level syntheses and conceptual reviews do not report or analyze any efficiency, group payoff, or welfare metrics, even when discussing PGGs and sanctioning (e.g., van Dijk & De Dreu, 2021; Redhead et al., 2021).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**On Punishment and Efficiency:**
- Punishment can *increase cooperation rates* in PGGs, but this does *not always lead to higher group efficiency* due to the cost of implementing sanctions (Tanimoto, 2018; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- The *efficiency of punishment itself* (that is, the cost-to-impact ratio) is a critical moderator: cheap and well-targeted punishment is more likely to result in net efficiency gains, while costly or misapplied punishment often reduces efficiency even when cooperation increases (Tanimoto, 2018; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
- *Context and design matter intensely*: The presence of social, institutional, or informational moderators (e.g., symmetry of power, group structure, ability to align punishment motives with group benefit) often determines whether efficiency gains are realized (Eldakar et al., 2018; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Kosfeld, 2020).
- *Antisocial punishment* (punishing cooperators) and *misapplication* can cause efficiency to stagnate or fall (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Kosfeld, 2020).
- Several models show that *institutional or leader punishment* (vs. peer punishment) can yield higher or more robust efficiency, particularly when the institution is adaptive or endogenously formed (Kol'veková et al., 2021; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Kosfeld, 2020).
- In some formulations (especially "optional PGG" or with "fractional punishment"), enabling punishment above a parameterized threshold yields *maximal or near-maximal efficiency* (Botta et al., 2021; Kol'veková et al., 2021).

**Caveats and Exceptions:**
- Costly punishment can sometimes even *reduce efficiency* relative to control (no-punishment) if the resource drain outweighs the cooperation benefit (Tanimoto, 2018; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- *Asymmetries of power and the possible dominance of selfish/antisocial punishers* can attenuate or reverse efficiency gains from punishment (Eldakar et al., 2018; Kosfeld, 2020).
- Design features such as *unstable environments, delays in punishment, or noise* in sanctioning efficacy can undermine both cooperation and efficiency (Allgaier et al., 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Directly Informative Models:**  
  - When *punishment is low-cost, effective, normatively constrained, and aligned with prosocial motives*, enabling it almost always increases efficiency over control (Kol'veková et al., 2021; Kosfeld, 2020; Botta et al., 2021).
  - Where *punishment is expensive, poorly targeted, or prone to antisocial misuse*, efficiency can remain unchanged or even decrease despite higher cooperation (Tanimoto, 2018; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
  - The *baseline (control) efficiency* is conceptually foundational: the higher the baseline, the less room there is for punishment to yield net gains; if the baseline is low due to rampant free-riding, well-designed punishment has higher potential to generate efficiency gains.
  - Certain design features—especially *player count*, *number of rounds*, *mpcr*, *punishment cost and technology*—are consistently important parameters in both positive and negative outcome regimes.

- **Predictive Implications for Key Design Dimensions:**  
  - *Player count, mpcr, punishment cost/tech*: Strong theoretical/parameterized links to efficiency change upon enabling punishment.
  - *Chat, identity visibility, reward existence*: Impacts are discussed, but evidence is less specific; generally, channels for communication, increased transparency, and reward options can further boost group efficiency, especially in concert with punishment, but direct payoff outcomes are rarely provided.
  - *Institutional context*: Whether punishment is peer-based or institutionalized—and especially whether it's endogenously designed or imposed—profoundly affects predicted efficiency.

- **Ambiguity and Disagreement:**  
  - Some models and reviews strike a pessimistic tone about unconditional efficiency gains from punishment, cautioning that *increased cooperation does not guarantee higher efficiency* due to the deadweight loss from costly sanctions (Tanimoto, 2018; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
  - Others, emphasizing design and parameterization, show *substantial or even maximal efficiency gains* from properly tailored punishment mechanisms (Kol'veková et al., 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Kosfeld, 2020).
  - There is consensus, however, that *punishment is not always beneficial*—the net effect is highly context-dependent.

- **Evidence Gap:**  
  - Most models are theoretical/simulation-based and do not provide empirical effect size estimates for efficiency changes as a function of all 14 design dimensions.
  - Several design features are only contextually or partially addressed.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- `player_count`: Frequently modeled and highlighted as a moderator (Tanimoto, 2018; Botta et al., 2021; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
- `num_rounds`: Strongly modeled for repeated interaction effects (Tanimoto, 2018; Eldakar et al., 2018; Kosfeld, 2020).
- `all_or_nothing`: Present in many formal models and volunteer’s dilemma analyses (Botta et al., 2021; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018).
- `mpcr`: Explicitly parameterized in theoretical models of cooperation thresholds and efficiency (Tanimoto, 2018; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Central to models—cost/impact ratio of punishment and how it is administered (peer, institutional) are almost always parameterized (Tanimoto, 2018; Kol'veková et al., 2021).

**Indirectly Informed:**
- `default_contrib`: Considered in some models as opt-in/opt-out, but rarely linked to efficiency empirically.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Sometimes addressed in the context of information availability, transparency, and social learning (Gavrilets, 2021; Kosfeld, 2020).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Analyzed mostly in relation to *adjacent mechanisms*; some models show synergy or trade-offs with punishment but rarely focus on these as core prediction variables (Toriumi et al., 2020; Skarzhinskaya & Tsurikov, 2021).
- `chat`, `show_punishment_id`: Mentioned in theoretical analyses regarding communication and transparency, but seldom with explicit payoff outcome mapping.

**Sparse/Missing:**
- Many papers do not model or systematically analyze the effects of `chat`, `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id` on efficiency—as such, evidence is sparse and inductive, not parameterized.
- The presence or absence of `reward` mechanisms is sometimes discussed but generally not measured alongside punishment in direct efficiency terms.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical Validation Is Absent:**  
  - The entire corpus is theoretical; there is no direct experimental or field data to confirm the magnitude or even the sign of efficiency changes across the full parameter space.
- **Outcome Measures Mismatch:**  
  - Many influential papers report only non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation/contribution rates) which are not reliably predictive of efficiency, especially under costly punishment regimes.
- **Parameter Coverage:**  
  - While primary PGG design features are well-modeled, several of the 14 structural dimensions are only discussed conceptually or are absent from formal models (e.g., chat, summary visibility, identity of punishers, opt-in framing).
- **Diversity of Game Environments:**  
  - Not all models are standard linear PGGs—there is diversity in structures (threshold PGG, coalition games, voluntary participation, leader/follower setups, group selection) that may not generalize.
- **Ambiguity in Mechanisms:**  
  - Persistent ambiguity/conflict about when punishment increases efficiency versus when it simply redistributes or squanders group resources.
- **No Calibration Guidance:**  
  - No paper in this set provides empirical calibration for mapping baseline (control) efficiency and design dimension settings to treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency; theoretical thresholds for effects are highly model-dependent.
- **Missing Real-world Factors:**  
  - Factors such as error, noise, social institutions, motivational crowding, and antisocial behavior are highlighted as crucial but are rarely parameterized with operational prediction rules.

---

**In summary:**
The literature provides *robust theoretical rationales* and *parameterized models* supporting both the beneficial and harmful possibilities of peer punishment for group efficiency in PGGs, with *context and game design* (especially punishment cost and targeting) as strong moderators. However, an empirical basis for calibrated predictions is missing, and the direct mapping from all design dimensions and control efficiency to treatment efficiency remains only partially specified in these works.
