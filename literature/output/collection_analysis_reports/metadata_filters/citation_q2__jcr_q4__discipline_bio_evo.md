# 1) Evidence Base

The examined literature consists exclusively of *theoretical* papers, with no empirical or experimental studies included. All three sources provide formal models relevant to public-goods or closely related social dilemma settings, but there are no direct, real-world effect size estimates or data-driven results. The papers differ slightly in the specific scenario modeled: one focuses on classic public goods and common-pool resource games (Lee & Iwasa, 2014), one addresses evolutionary dynamics in repeated multi-person prisoner’s dilemmas with voluntary participation (Castro & Toro, 2008), and one analyzes cooperation and punishment in networks with continuous contributions (Voelkl, 2015). Relative to the prediction task, this is a *narrow* evidence base regarding both empirical generalizability and full coverage of experimental manipulations.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *Lee & Iwasa (2014):* Close (models social dilemmas with PGG-like features).  
- *Castro & Toro (2008):* Close (repeated N-person Prisoner's Dilemma, structurally akin to PGGs).  
- *Voelkl (2015):* Adjacent (cooperative marketing with continuous contributions—structurally similar, but not canonical PGG).  

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *Lee & Iwasa (2014):* Exact (punishment mechanism is directly modeled).  
- *Castro & Toro (2008):* Adjacent (punishment discussed but not modeled).  
- *Voelkl (2015):* Exact (punishment as penalties for “unfaithfulness”).  

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- All three sources: Exact (focus directly on efficiency, total group payoff, or group welfare as primary outcomes).  

**Synthesis:**  
The paper set is most directly relevant for the *punishment* and *payoff/efficiency* aspects of the prediction task, while the relevance for canonical public goods games is “close” or “adjacent” but not always “exact”.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**  
All three papers prioritize efficiency or group welfare (ratio of achieved payoff to full-cooperation payoff, total earnings/welfare/surplus). This aligns directly with the prediction target.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
- *Lee & Iwasa (2014):* Also models norm compliance and heterogeneity, but always in terms of payoff consequences.
- *Castro & Toro (2008):* Examines prevalence of loner, cooperator, and defector strategies as mechanisms producing (or not) efficient payoffs.
- *Voelkl (2015):* Focuses on emergence and stability of cooperation; non-payoff behaviors discussed as means to efficiency, not as separate outcomes.

**Summary:**  
Behavioral markers (contribution rates, strategy frequencies) are discussed as mechanisms or preconditions, but the main findings hinge on *payoff-based efficiency measures*.

---

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Across the papers, key converging theoretical insights are:

- **Enabling punishment increases efficiency:**  
  Both Lee & Iwasa (2014) and Voelkl (2015) demonstrate that allowing agents to sanction defectors leads to maximized group efficiency, as long as punishment is neither excessively harsh (Lee & Iwasa, 2014) nor too weak to deter defection (Voelkl, 2015).

- **Graduated or proportionate punishment is optimal:**  
  Lee & Iwasa (2014) show that graduated punishment—scaling with the harm caused—yields higher efficiency than flat, severe punishment.

- **Redistribution of punishment boosts effects:**  
  Voelkl (2015) finds that recycling punishment costs/penalties among cooperators can further strengthen group welfare and stability.

- **Baseline efficiency can be high *without* punishment if alternative cooperation mechanisms exist:**  
  Castro & Toro (2008) highlights how voluntary participation (the loner strategy) allows cooperation to arise and persist, meaning that the *incremental value* of enabling punishment for efficiency may be reduced or context-dependent.

- **Empirical effect sizes are not provided or estimated.**  
  All insights are qualitative or comparative rather than quantitative: the models predict direction and mechanism, not percentage-point gains.

---

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given the literature, the strongest theoretical expectation is that **introducing peer punishment mechanisms in a PGG-like environment should increase average efficiency relative to no-punishment control**, especially when punishment is  
- *graduated/proportionate*,  
- *not excessively severe relative to cooperation gains*, and  
- *sufficient to outweigh the benefits of defection* (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015).

However, if the baseline setting already has mechanisms producing high efficiency (e.g., voluntary participation, high chances of repeated interaction), the incremental efficiency benefit of punishment may be smaller or ambiguous (Castro & Toro, 2008).

Because all evidence is theoretical, predictions should be regarded as **qualitative**: punishment is expected to raise efficiency, but the magnitude, sensitivity to parameter changes, and interaction effects with other design dimensions are not empirically grounded here.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**  
- **punishment_tech** (mode of punishment: graduated, flat) (Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Voelkl, 2015)  
- **punishment_cost** (Voelkl, 2015)  
- **player_count**, **num_rounds**, **mpcr** (Castro & Toro, 2008; Voelkl, 2015)  
- **all_or_nothing** (Voelkl, 2015)  
- **reward_exists** (Voelkl, 2015)  
- **show_n_rounds** (Castro & Toro, 2008)

**Indirectly informed/contextual:**  
- **show_other_summaries**, **show_punishment_id**, **default_contrib**: sometimes discussed with respect to information availability or contribution framing, but not modeled directly.
- **reward_cost**/**reward_tech**: only Voelkl (2015) mentions rewards, but focus is on punishment.

**Effectively missing:**  
- **chat**: Not modeled or discussed.
- **default_contrib** (explicitly): Not distinguished.
- **show_other_summaries**, **show_punishment_id**: Not modeled.

**Dimension-level implications:**  
The efficiency benefit from enabling punishment may depend on its *strength*, *graduation*, and *redistribution design*, as well as on *group size*, *game length*, and *payoff structure*. Some dimensions (like chat, identity visibility, and framing) are not covered, making predictions for these factors speculative.

---

# 7) Important Limitations

- **No empirical effect sizes:** All findings are from *theoretical models*; there are no data-driven or experimental results to calibrate quantitative predictions.

- **Limited dimensional coverage:** Several game-design dimensions relevant to the prediction task (e.g., chat, contribution framing, feedback visibility) are not directly addressed.

- **Papers model "PGG-like" rather than canonical PGG environments:** Insights are likely to transfer, but some structural and dynamic features may differ in real-world or experimental public goods games.

- **No assessment of punishment costs on overall net efficiency:** While both main theory papers report net gains in efficiency from punishment, the total effect may depend on the social cost of administering punishments, which may not always be fully offset by gains in contribution.

- **Potential interactions and heterogeneity not thoroughly explored:** While Lee & Iwasa (2014) briefly analyze heterogeneity, broader variation in player behavior, communication, or institutional context is not systematically modeled.

- **Directional, not magnitude, predictions:** The set supports the expectation that enabling or optimizing punishment increases efficiency, but offers no direct guidance on effect size or precise treatment-control predictions.

- **Assumes rational or simple evolutionary actors:** Human psychological complexities, potential errors, institutional constraints, or non-material motivations may moderate real-world punishment effects.

---

**Summary:**  
This theory-only literature set strongly suggests, in broad terms, that well-calibrated, sufficiently strong, and appropriately graduated punishment mechanisms should increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments relative to no-punishment baselines. However, *empirical magnitude*, *full design generalizability*, and *detailed interaction effects* with other game features remain underspecified for precise prediction.
