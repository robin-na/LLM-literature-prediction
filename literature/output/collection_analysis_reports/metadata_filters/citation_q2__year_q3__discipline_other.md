# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises one empirical (experimental) study and three theoretical or conceptual papers:

- **Empirical coverage is narrow**: Only Zhang et al. (2019) provides empirical findings, focusing on lab-based experimental data in a public-goods-dilemma game. However, this game excludes punishment or sanction mechanisms.
- **Theoretical papers dominate and are broad in scope**: Handfield & Thrasher (2019), Vlerick (2020), and Heath & Rioux (2018) are all theory papers, discussing the origins and mechanisms of cooperation, sanctions, and prosocial behavior at a conceptual or evolutionary level, with only indirect (if any) reference to specific payoff outcomes or public-goods-game-like experimental design.
- **Narrow evidence for the prediction task**: The overall mix is heavily weighted toward high-level discussion, with only limited empirical support directly related to payoff or efficiency effects of punishment in public goods games.

# 2) Task Relevance

For the three relevance dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - *Zhang et al. (2019)*: `close` (experimental step-level public-goods-dilemma)
  - *All others*: `adjacent` (discuss cooperation dilemmas in general, not directly focused on PGGs)
  - *Overall*: Literature has weak direct relevance—only one study aligns closely with PGG structure.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - *Zhang et al. (2019)*: `none` (no punishment or sanction mechanism studied)
  - *Other theory papers*: `adjacent` (discuss punishment mechanisms conceptually, but not in experimental PGGs)
  - *Overall*: No empirical or direct evidence specifically about punishment in PGGs—most discussion is conceptual or evolutionary.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - *Zhang et al. (2019)*: `exact` (measures group/individual earnings; efficiency outcome is central)
  - *Theory papers*: `adjacent` to `weak` (mention payoff enhancement as a general aim/benefit of cooperation, but with no direct outcome measurement)
  - *Overall*: Only one paper provides direct, quantitative efficiency outcomes.

**Summary**: The literature most closely addresses efficiency in PGGs without punishment, offers broad conceptual links to punishment and cooperation, but does not empirically address how punishment alters efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, earnings):**
  - *Directly measured only in Zhang et al. (2019)*, where efficiency and earnings are the primary outcomes, but in PGGs without punishment.
  - *Theoretical papers* consider the existence or promotion of group-beneficial outcomes as part of their arguments but do not provide direct or quantitative measures.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, cooperation rate, punishment acts, norm compliance):**
  - *Vlerick (2020)* and *Heath & Rioux (2018)* are primarily concerned with norm compliance, altruistic punishment, and social mechanisms, not efficiency.
  - *Handfield & Thrasher (2019)* discuss mechanisms (honor, moral, punitive) and their hypothesized effects on behavior, not on payoffs.

- **Distinction maintained**: Only Zhang et al. (2019) reports actual efficiency/payoff data; all other papers theorize about social/behavioral mechanisms or non-payoff outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Empirical findings (Zhang et al., 2019):**
  - In a step-level public-goods game **without punishment**, introducing a consistent contributor (role model) increases both cooperation and group efficiency/payoff. This effect is robust irrespective of social value orientation.

- **Theory and mechanism arguments:**
  - *Handfield & Thrasher (2019)*: Both honor and punishment norms may stabilize cooperation and deter defection, potentially leading to group-beneficial outcomes, but do not specify conditions or report outcomes.
  - *Vlerick (2020)*: Attributes the evolution of prosocial punishment to cultural group selection but measures only behavioral outcomes, not efficiency.
  - *Heath & Rioux (2018)*: Warn that models with payoff-improving punishment may rely on unrealistic mechanisms (greenbeards), casting doubt on their generalizability.

- **Lack of punishment effect evidence:** None of the papers provide direct empirical evidence on how enabling punishment will alter efficiency. The only experimentally supported efficiency result is for motivational interventions, not sanctions.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct prediction capability limited:**  
  The paper set does not empirically address the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. As a result, no direct guidance on the change in efficiency when enabling peer punishment can be derived from this literature set.

- **Control efficiency context available:**  
  The control condition (no punishment) efficiency in public-goods games is documented in Zhang et al. (2019), which may serve as a baseline for environments without punitive interventions.

- **No parameter-level guidance for punishment-enabled design:**  
  Theoretical papers collectively support the plausibility of punishment as a cooperation-stabilizing mechanism, but offer no quantitative frameworks, parameterizations, or direct predictions about the magnitude, direction, or conditions for efficiency improvement when peer punishment is introduced.

- **Implication:**  
  When using this literature set alone, one can make informed predictions only about efficiency in PGG-like environments without punishment or with motivation/role-model interventions (Zhang et al., 2019). The effect of peer punishment—especially as a function of game design parameters such as punishment cost, magnitude, or the presence of rewards—is not informed by empirical evidence in this set.

- **Directly informed (by empirical data):**
  - *player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds* (Zhang et al., 2019; in context of non-punishment games)
- **Indirectly or only contextually discussed:**
  - *punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id* (Mentioned in the theoretical literature only in broad terms; not empirically tested or parameterized)
- **Effectively missing:**
  - No empirical study in this set analyzes design dimensions specific to punishment or reward mechanisms (costs, magnitudes, visibility) or how they impact efficiency.

- **Implication:**  
  For predicting treatment efficiency (punishment-enabled), the set does not empirically inform how specific design parameters moderate the effect.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **No empirical coverage of punishment-enabled efficiency:**  
  The set contains **no empirical studies** measuring the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in public-goods or similar games.

- **Sparse outcome measurement:**  
  Only one paper measures efficiency, and only in games without punishment; the others do not report any payoff-based outcomes.

- **No game-dimension granularity:**  
  Important design features relevant to punishment (cost/magnitude/tracking/reward existence, etc.) are not evaluated empirically within this set.

- **Theory-practice gap:**  
  Theoretical arguments about the potential efficacy and caveats of punishment remain ungrounded in quantitative experimental evidence in these sources, leaving actionable prediction severely underspecified.

**Summary:**  
**This paper set provides no empirical basis to predict the impact of enabling peer punishment on group efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments.** It can only support (a) estimation of baseline efficiency without punishment and (b) a general theoretical expectation that punishment might stabilize cooperation. However, no quantitative predictions about efficiency change under different design conditions can be responsibly derived from this literature alone.
