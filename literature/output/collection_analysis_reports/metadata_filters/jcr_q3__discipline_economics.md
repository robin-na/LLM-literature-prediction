# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is large (120 items) and contains both a substantial body of empirical (especially laboratory experimental) and theoretical work. Most high-relevance empirical studies are experimental lab PGGs or close variants, with well-defined game parameters and measured control and treatment efficiency. A minority are field or artefactual field experiments, and others use non-PGG social dilemmas (e.g., trust games, repeated PDs, CPRs) with adjacent evidence. Theory papers range from narrowly calibrated models of lab PGGs to general game-theoretic treatments covering repeated games, monitoring, and community enforcement. Some studies focus on mechanism design, network effects, and the evolution of cooperation, providing context on broader institutional or cultural determinants of efficiency. Overall, the evidence base is relatively broad, but with a strong concentration of direct, high-quality, empirical evidence on standard PGGs with and without punishment.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessment across target-relevance dimensions:

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - Many papers are labeled as exact for PGGs: standard, linear, repeated, with variations in group size, feedback, and payoff structure (e.g., Chen 2022; Faillo et al., 2013; Nikiforakis, 2010).  
  - A considerable subset uses close variants—e.g., CPRs, trust games, networked PDs, all-or-nothing games, or public goods with minimum provision (adjacent).  
  - Some are only contextually related or discuss mechanisms/concepts relevant to PGGs.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - Many studies are exact; the central manipulation is peer or centralized punishment, varying by cost, magnitude, technology, legitimacy, feedback, identifiability, or structure (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
  - Several papers provide close or adjacent evidence, such as ostracism, endogenous coalition exclusion, or indirect sanctions (e.g., social approval/rating, gossip, information withholding).
  - Some discuss only rewards or other interventions, not punishment.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - A substantial fraction provides exact evidence: group efficiency, total earnings, or group welfare are reported as primary outcomes, with treatment vs. control comparisons (e.g., Chen, 2022; Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014).
  - Others use close or adjacent proxies: relative group payoffs, average earnings, surplus, or related welfare proxies.
  - A significant number primarily report non-payoff behavioral outcomes (cooperation/contribution rates, compliance, norm adherence) and sometimes explicitly note the lack of direct efficiency or payoff analysis.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**:  
  - Many key PGG experiments directly report group efficiency (earnings/max possible), group welfare, or average group earnings (e.g., Nikiforakis, 2010; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Dannenberg, 2016; Kamijo et al., 2014).  
  - Some studies report both contribution rates and payoffs, clarifying that contribution increases do not always translate into higher efficiency due to punishment costs (e.g., Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).  
  - Certain adjacent disciplines (CPR, trust games) report welfare or aggregate earnings, explicitly mapping to efficiency concepts.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:  
  - Many report contributions, cooperation, compliance, norm adherence, or punishment frequency as primary outcomes (e.g., Anderson & Stafford, 2003; Carpenter, 2007), sometimes as proxies for efficiency.
  - Some explicitly state that efficiency or earnings are not reported, focusing on behavioral dynamics (e.g., Kingsley & Smith-Walter, 2024).

- **Distinction maintained**:  
  - Several studies explicitly note when contribution increases due to punishment do not result in efficiency gains (due to incurred punishment costs or anti-social punishment), maintaining a strict separation (e.g., Chen, 2022; Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## **Empirical Patterns**

- **Punishment can increase or decrease efficiency**:  
  - In standard repeated linear PGGs, peer punishment frequently increases contributions, but the net effect on efficiency depends heavily on the costs and use of punishment. In many cases, efficiency increases relative to control (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Faillo et al., 2013; Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Kamei, 2019).
  - However, in PGGs with heterogeneous MPCRs or high antisocial punishment, enabling punishment can lower group efficiency versus control due to resource destruction (Chen, 2022; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Colombier et al., 2011; Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
  - Centralized or legal punishment (versus peer), or a combination, can yield higher, similar, or sometimes worse efficiency, with effects often conditional on legitimacy, institution selection, and monitoring regime (Kube & Traxler, 2011; Kamijo et al., 2014; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Tyran & Feld, 2006; DeAngelo & Gee, 2020).

- **Key moderators from design dimensions**:
  - **Player Count**: Effects can increase with group size under strong coordination, but efficiency may fall with poorly coordinated or overused punishment in larger groups (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017; Buchholz et al., 2014).
  - **Punishment Cost and Technology**: Cheap or highly effective punishment increases contributions but may reduce efficiency due to excessive use (Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Chen, 2022).
  - **Legitimacy/Structure**: Restricting punishment to 'legitimate' cases (e.g., only higher contributors punishing lower) and providing full feedback increase efficiency; unrestricted punishment or feedback can backfire due to antisocial punishment (Faillo et al., 2013; Zheng & Nie, 2013).
  - **Information/Feedback** (`show_other_summaries`): Feedback format (contributions vs. earnings vs. both) moderates the efficiency effect. Feedback on contributions sustains higher efficiency; earnings feedback can reduce it (Nikiforakis, 2010).
  - **Group Heterogeneity**: Heterogeneous groups (different MPCRs/endowments) can exacerbate antisocial punishment and hinder efficiency gains (Chen, 2022; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
  - **Monitoring institution**: Peer monitoring is robust to endogenization; group monitoring can collapse under endogenous rules, reducing efficiency (DeAngelo & Gee, 2020).
  - **Agreement/norms**: The presence of ambitious, clear, and non-binding group agreements, combined with punishment, can unlock substantial efficiency gains (Dannenberg, 2016).
  - **Legitimacy and identification** (`show_punishment_id`): Punisher identifiability can affect antisocial punishment rates and thus efficiency (Zheng & Nie, 2013).

## **Theoretical Patterns**

- **Theory generally supports the possibility of large efficiency gains from punishment** (Kranz, 2010; Dutta et al., 2021; Hwang, 2017; De Silva et al., 2010), but is careful to specify limits:
  - Efficiency gains require sufficient compliers and effective (strong enough) punishment.
  - Effectiveness is moderated by player patience (high discount factors), observability, and coordinated/targeted punishment.
  - Monitoring structure (e.g., breadth of information) and the risk of counter-punishment (retaliation cycles) are critical constraints.
  - Voluntary participation broadens the effectiveness of punishment (De Silva et al., 2010).
  - Ineffective punishment, excessive cost, or noisy identification can nullify or reverse gains.

## **Across Studies:**
- The net effect of peer punishment is highly contingent on design parameters—sometimes positive, sometimes negligible, sometimes negative for efficiency versus control.
- Reward-only or hybrid reward-punishment institutions are at least as effective, often more so for efficiency (Chen, 2022; Colombier et al., 2011).
- Social mechanisms (communication, agreement, ratings) can substitute for or moderate the impact of punishment.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **General expectation**: In standard, linear, repeated PGGs with moderate group sizes, continuous contributions, no chat, full contribution feedback, and well-calibrated punishment costs/effectiveness, enabling peer punishment typically increases efficiency compared to the control game with punishment disabled, often substantially so (Kranz, 2010; Dutta et al., 2021; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
- **When to expect no or negative effect**: If punishment is cheap and/or poorly targeted (allowing antisocial punishment), if group heterogeneity is high and the institution is not designed to mitigate antisocial punishment, if counterpunishment is possible and unconstrained, or if punishment is overused, enabling punishment can lower efficiency versus control (Chen, 2022; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
- **Crucial design moderators**:
  - Calibration to group size (`player_count`) and marginal per-capita return (`mpcr`).
  - Punishment cost relative to effectiveness (`punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`).
  - Information structure and feedback format (`show_other_summaries`).
  - Legitimacy or restrictiveness of punishment institution (ability to limit anti-social punishment).
  - Whether reward or hybrid mechanisms are present (`reward_exists`).
  - The presence of agreements, endogenous rule choice, and communication.

- **For prediction models**: Use control efficiency as a baseline, and adjust for the above design moderators based on empirical findings from games with closely-matching parameters. Peer punishment boosts efficiency when calibrated to avoid waste, focus on norm enforcement, and minimize antisocial use. In edge cases—large groups, highly heterogeneous groups, low-cost punishment, unconstrained counterpunishment—either no gain or efficiency loss is likely.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly and richly informed dimensions**:
  - `player_count`, `num_rounds`: Almost universally specified in empirical studies; group size and time horizon effects are well-researched.
  - `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return): Explicitly manipulated and reported in many studies; known to moderate the impact of punishment on efficiency.
  - `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Substantially analyzed (cost per point, magnitude, convexity, opportunity for counterpunishment).
  - `show_other_summaries`: Directly studied as a moderator (especially feedback about contributions vs. earnings).
  - `reward_exists`: Many studies include no-reward or hybrid (punishment and reward) treatments for direct comparison.
  - `chat`: Included in several studies as a critical co-intervention/moderator.
  - `all_or_nothing`: Manipulated explicitly in some comparative and network designs.
  - `show_punishment_id`: Explored in identifiability and legitimacy studies.
  - `default_contrib`: Discussed in framing, defaults, and 'opt-in/opt-out' designs in a few studies.

- **Indirectly or contextually informed**:
  - `punishment_tech`: Sometimes implied (e.g., by design of monitoring/assignment), but detailed technical structure is not the main focus in most studies.
  - `show_n_rounds`: Not a primary focus but described in experimental procedures; sometimes manipulated.
  - `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Analysis is richer in reward-centric designs; in punishment-centric designs, reward is usually absent.

- **Effectively missing or rare**:
  - Studies that manipulate or deeply analyze all 14 prediction dimensions simultaneously are rare; most focus on a subset relevant to their institutional manipulation.
  - Some dimensions (e.g., detailed implementation of `default_contrib`, nuanced forms of `reward_tech`) are under-explored across the literature.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Scope of laboratory settings**: Most direct findings are from short-horizon, incentivized laboratory experiments; field generalizability is always a caveat.
- **Heterogeneity in punishment implementation**: What qualifies as “punishment” varies (peer, centralized, ostracism, automatic fines, etc.), meaning that findings may not transfer cleanly if the punishment institution differs significantly.
- **Ambiguity and non-monotonicity**: Efficiency effects of enabling punishment are non-monotonic with respect to punishment cost, technology, legitimacy, and information structure. Contributions can increase but efficiency can fall, and sometimes enabling punishment reduces efficiency below control (e.g., in the presence of counterpunishment, antisocial use, or group-heterogeneity-driven targeting).
- **Design moderators often interact**: There is no universally “best” punishment design; the effect is highly sensitive to context and combinations of game design parameters. For example, group size interacts with monitoring, and feedback type interacts with outcome measures.
- **Behavioral vs. payoff outcomes**: A significant number of studies only report behavioral (contribution/cooperation) results, not efficiency or group payoffs, making them unreliable for direct efficiency prediction without adjustment or further analysis.
- **Absence of some design manipulations**: Some game design dimensions are rarely manipulated directly or in combination, limiting predictive coverage for those cases.
- **Legitimacy, feedback, and institution choice**: Legitimacy (endogenous selection, voting, agreement), feedback (contribution/earnings), and identifiability (public/private) critically moderate efficiency effects. Yet many studies vary only one at a time, leaving some interactions underexplored.
- **Conflicting findings**: There are direct empirical conflicts, especially regarding the net benefits of punishment in heterogeneous/large groups (Chen, 2022 vs. Reuben & Riedl, 2013) and when comparing centralized vs. peer punishment.

**In summary:**  
The paper set provides substantial, high-quality, directly relevant evidence mapping design dimensions and control efficiency to treatment efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments with punishment. The effect of punishment on efficiency is strongly context-dependent, varying with group size, group heterogeneity, punishment cost and effectiveness, institution legitimacy, information/feedback, and co-interventions (reward, agreement, chat). Many dimensions are richly studied, but important design interactions and field extensions remain open. The literature strongly supports the necessity of encoding key design moderators in any downstream efficiency prediction task; predictions outside well-studied parameter regimes should be made with caution.
