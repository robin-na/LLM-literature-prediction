# 1) Evidence Base

The reviewed paper set (7 papers) is mixed but leans strongly theoretical and review-based, with only one paper (Bradley et al., 2018) based on quantitative empirical (meta-analysis/observational) methodology, and several others (e.g., Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; West et al., 2021; Ågren et al., 2019) providing theoretical synthesis or evolutionary modeling. Most are broad in conceptual scope, covering both human and non-human cooperation, enforcement, and the role of structural and ecological moderators.

The papers span exact public goods games (PGG) (e.g., Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Alvarez-Rodriguez et al., 2021), PGG variants, and adjacent contexts such as repeated prisoner's dilemma, common-pool resource games, and general models of cooperation/enforcement. Across the set, direct empirical evidence for the prediction target (treatment efficiency with peer punishment in PGGs) is very limited. The handful of theoretical/simulation studies that do address payoff-based outcomes in PGG or analogous games are contextually constrained (e.g., focus on resource renewal, evolutionary parameters, or normative context).

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**
- **Exact relevance:** Fehr & Schurtenberger (2018), Alvarez-Rodriguez et al. (2021), and, with close variant, Chen & Szolnoki (2018) are most relevant for PGG design.
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Chowdhury et al. (2021) (repeated PD with policing), Ågren et al. (2019) (broad enforcement), and West et al. (2021) (general cooperation) are adjacent—structurally similar, but not direct PGG implementations.
- **Empirical paper (Bradley et al., 2018):** Evaluates several design features in social dilemmas, including PGGs, but does not focus on punishment.

**punishment_or_sanctions**
- **Exact relevance:** Fehr & Schurtenberger (2018), Chen & Szolnoki (2018), Ågren et al. (2019), Chowdhury et al. (2021) (theoretical).
- **Adjacent/weak:** West et al. (2021) and Bradley et al. (2018) discuss observability or enforcement generally, with little to no direct evidence on punishment mechanics or tech.
- **None:** Alvarez-Rodriguez et al. (2021) does not model or discuss punishment/rewards.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**
- **Exact/close:** Chen & Szolnoki (2018), Ågren et al. (2019), and Alvarez-Rodriguez et al. (2021) report or predict efficiency or directly related payoff outcomes.
- **Adjacent:** Fehr & Schurtenberger (2018) address group welfare but mostly via theoretical synthesis and emphasize the complexity of mapping increased cooperation to increased efficiency. Chowdhury et al. (2021) and West et al. (2021) focus mainly on cooperation rates or evolutionary stability, not efficiency. Bradley et al. (2018) reports on behavioral outcomes, not payoffs.

**Summary:** No empirical experimental paper directly reports treatment efficiency (with punishment enabled) in PGGs. The strongest relevance for the prediction task comes from theory papers and simulations, with direct quantitative payoff outcomes in exact PGGs scarce.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:** Group efficiency (total payoff as a ratio of ideal), welfare/surplus, total group payoff (Chen & Szolnoki, 2018; Alvarez-Rodriguez et al., 2021; Ågren et al., 2019; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018-adjacent).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:** Cooperation and contribution rates, strategy frequencies, norm compliance, prosocial actions/intentions (Bradley et al., 2018; Chowdhury et al., 2021; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; West et al., 2021).
- **Combined/mixed outcomes:** Several theory papers intermix behavioral and payoff-based discussion, but only a few clearly distinguish group efficiency versus behavioral metrics.

**Explicit warning:** Most papers that find robust increases in cooperation with punishment either do not translate this increase into efficiency/payoff improvements or find that the efficiency impact can be limited or negative (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Chen & Szolnoki, 2018).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment can increase cooperation—efficiency less certain:** There is broad theoretical support that punishment opportunities increase cooperation or contribution rates in PGG-like environments (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Chowdhury et al., 2021; Ågren et al., 2019; West et al., 2021).
- **Efficiency gains from punishment depend on context:** Increases in cooperation do not always translate into efficiency or payoff gains. Punishment incurs costs, and antisocial punishment (punishment of cooperators) can reduce group welfare, sometimes yielding neutral or negative net efficiency effects (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Chen & Szolnoki, 2018).
- **Role of design and ecological moderators:**
  - **Normative/institutional context critical:** Normative constraints that reduce antisocial punishment or make punishment targeted/pro-social can enable positive efficiency effects from punishment (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
  - **Effect of punishment cost/tech:** High punishment costs can outweigh the increases in contributions, reducing or negating efficiency gains (Chen & Szolnoki, 2018; Ågren et al., 2019).
  - **Resource/game dynamics:** In resource renewal variants, even maximal cooperation enforced by punishment may not improve efficiency if the resource cannot recover quickly (Chen & Szolnoki, 2018).
  - **Group size and structure:** Larger and more uniform groups, in the absence of punishment, can achieve higher baseline efficiency; however, direct evidence on how punishment effects scale with group size or structure is missing (Alvarez-Rodriguez et al., 2021).
- **Related interventions:** Observability (but not punishment) can increase prosocial behavior, which is a behavioral precursor to efficiency but not sufficient evidence for efficiency effects (Bradley et al., 2018).
- **No robust quantitative estimates:** There is a lack of direct empirical or even synthetic quantitative results that map game design plus control efficiency to treatment efficiency with punishment.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General inference:** The presence of punishment in PGGs is expected to increase cooperation rates, but efficiency (total group payoff relative to the social optimum) may increase, decrease, or remain unchanged depending on game and contextual moderators.

- **When to predict large efficiency gains:** When (a) punishment is normatively constrained (e.g., low antisocial punishment, prosocial targeting), (b) punishment is not prohibitively costly, and (c) the underlying context (such as resource renewability or system structure) allows for cooperation gains to translate into actual welfare improvements. This is supported by theoretical syntheses (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Ågren et al., 2019; Chen & Szolnoki, 2018).
- **When to predict weak or negative efficiency effects:** If punishment is costly, antisocial punishment is prevalent, or the system (e.g., slow resource renewal) does not allow cooperation to improve outcomes, enabling punishment may fail to improve or can even decrease group efficiency (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Chen & Szolnoki, 2018).

**Practical application:** Predictive models should condition on:
  - **Baseline (control) efficiency:** High baseline efficiency due to structural factors implies less room for punishment to improve outcomes (Alvarez-Rodriguez et al., 2021).
  - **Punishment features:** Cost, targeting/technology, and normative context. High cost and low targeting may blunt or reverse effects.
  - **Game design context:** Group size (some evidence for optimality), type of contribution (all-or-nothing vs. continuous), and resource dynamics (especially in PGG variants with resource renewal).
  
There is no direct support in the literature set to quantitatively predict the magnitude of treatment efficiency based on these variables, but strong guidance to avoid assuming a monotonic positive effect.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (theory/simulation or contextually):**
- `player_count`: Explicit in Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Alvarez-Rodriguez et al., 2021 (group size).
- `num_rounds`: Considered in both empirical and theoretical designs (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Chen & Szolnoki, 2018; Bradley et al., 2018).
- `chat`: Mentioned/contextual (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- `all_or_nothing`: Theory (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Chen & Szolnoki, 2018).
- `mpcr`: Explicitly modeled (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Chen & Szolnoki, 2018; Alvarez-Rodriguez et al., 2021).
- `punishment_cost`: Detailed modeling (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Chen & Szolnoki, 2018; Chowdhury et al., 2021; Ågren et al., 2019, West et al., 2021).
- `punishment_tech`: Mentioned/theorized as detection/inspection (Chen & Szolnoki, 2018; Chowdhury et al., 2021).
- `reward_exists`: Only high-level commentary (West et al., 2021).
- `show_n_rounds`: Simulation input (Alvarez-Rodriguez et al., 2021).
- `show_other_summaries`: Observability in empirical review (Bradley et al., 2018).
  
**Indirect/contextual coverage:**
- `default_contrib`: Not directly modeled.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `reward_magnitude`: Only mentioned abstractly (West et al., 2021).
- `show_punishment_id`: Not directly addressed.

**Effectively missing or only peripherally addressed:**
- Specific implementation details of `default_contrib`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `show_punishment_id`.
- Interaction effects between multiple intervention types (punishment × rewards × observability) generally not empirically analyzed.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of direct empirical results:** No paper in the set provides direct experimental evidence on the effect of enabling peer punishment on group efficiency or payoff in PGGs with quantified magnitudes across different design settings.
- **Emphasis on theory/simulations:** Most evidence is from theoretical models, simulations, or high-level synthesis. These may capture broad mechanisms but lack fine-grained, real-world quantitative mapping.
- **Payoff/efficiency vs. behavioral outcomes:** Many findings focus on behavioral outcomes rather than efficiency. There is evidence that increased cooperation (contributions) does not necessarily equate to improved efficiency in social dilemmas with costly punishment or antisocial punishment.
- **Sparse evidence on certain dimensions:** Critical moderators such as chat, reward/punishment visibility, default contribution framing, or design-driven norms are underspecified or missing.
- **Heterogeneity not addressed:** Variation across cultures, group composition, and institutional context—which may dramatically moderate punishment effects—is only indirectly discussed.
- **No guidance for combined interventions:** Very limited discussion of how the interplay of different dimensions (e.g., observability and punishment) affects efficiency.
- **No direct mapping from control efficiency to treatment effect:** The literature set does not offer a framework or data to precisely use control efficiency (no punishment) and design dimensions to predict efficiency with punishment enabled.

**Conclusion:** For downstream prediction of treatment efficiency based on game design and control outcomes, this literature set offers strong theoretical warnings and boundary conditions but insufficient direct empirical guidance. Predictions based on this set must remain cautious and highly conditional on identified moderators.
