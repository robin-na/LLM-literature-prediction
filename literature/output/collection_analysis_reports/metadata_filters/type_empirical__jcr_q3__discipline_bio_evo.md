# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base consists of 18 papers, overwhelmingly empirical and predominantly lab experiments, with only one observational (cross-cultural/anthropological) study and no purely theoretical work. Of these, a subset directly addresses standard or variant public goods games with manipulations of punishment and efficiency or payoffs, while others focus on adjacent or unrelated game types, neural/behavioral correlates, or social framing effects without measuring economic efficiency. The overall base is moderately broad in behavioral paradigms but is notably narrow in several key respects for the downstream prediction task: only a handful of studies provide direct, quantitative, empirical evidence on the impact of enabling punishment on group efficiency (welfare or total payoff) in PGGs with experimentally varied design dimensions. Most other studies offer only indirect, contextually adjacent, or missing relevance for payoff-based outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance:** About one-third of the papers use standard repeated public goods games or direct variants (e.g., Fischer et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Sparks et al., 2024; Espín et al., 2022; Micheli et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2020; Park et al., 2013; Gianotti et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2021).
- **Adjacent relevance:** A handful use other social dilemmas or allocation tasks but do not implement PGG structure (e.g., dyadic Dictator Games, Prisoner’s Dilemma, or Ultimatum Games).
- **Weak/none:** Several papers use designs that are not public goods or social dilemmas at all.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact relevance:** About half include explicit, costly punishment or counter-punishment mechanisms (Fischer et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Sparks et al., 2024; Espín et al., 2022).
- **Close:** Some use reward-only or social incentives, or only costless nonmaterial disapproval (Micheli et al., 2021; Sparks et al., 2024, for disapproval; other papers discuss punishment strategies behaviorally or reputationally).
- **Adjacent:** Others mention sanctions or punishment in framing, role switching, or theoretical discussion without implementing real economic sanctions (Townsend et al., 2020; Chen & Hauser, 2005).
- **None:** Many studies do not include punishment.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Only a handful explicitly report group efficiency, group earnings, or direct economic surplus as a main outcome (Fischer et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Sparks et al., 2024).
- **Adjacent:** Several others measure only cooperation/contribution behavior or psychological/neural outcomes (Micheli et al., 2021; Espín et al., 2022), sometimes assuming a link to payoff, but do not directly measure realized payoffs.
- **Weak/none:** Numerous papers measure behavioral, perceptual, cognitive, or neural correlates, with no payoff/efficiency analysis at all.

**Conclusion:** Only four papers (Fischer et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Sparks et al., 2024) are directly and exactly relevant to the core prediction task: the prospective effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency in PGG environments with specified design dimension parameters.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (exact or close):**
- *Group efficiency/earnings/welfare/surplus/total coins*: Explicit measures and comparison across punishment/no-punishment treatments in Fischer et al. (2016), Andrighetto et al. (2016), Fatas & Mateu (2015), and Sparks et al. (2024).
- *Adjacent payoff proxies*: Contribution rates (Micheli et al., 2021) when paired with standard PGG design could be loosely interpreted as indicative of payoff changes, but this is indirect.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
- *Contribution/cooperation rates* (most common, e.g., Espín et al., 2022; Micheli et al., 2021; Park et al., 2013; Gianotti et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2021)
- *Punishment frequency/targeting* (Espín et al., 2022; Ashlock et al., 1996)
- *Retaliation/selfishness* (Müller-Leinss et al., 2018)
- *Normative judgments, attitudes, beliefs* (Liu et al., 2020; Rilling et al., 2008; Garfield et al., 2023)
- *Neural or psychological responses* (Gianotti et al., 2019; Rilling et al., 2008)

**Distinction:** Only the first category (direct payoff/efficiency measurement) is relevant for the prediction task, whereas behavioral and neurocognitive outcomes are important for mechanisms but not for the payoff outcome prediction.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Synthesis Across Directly Relevant Papers:

- **Punishment does not always increase efficiency:**  
  In standard repeated PGGs, enabling punishment does not guarantee an increase in group efficiency; effects depend critically on game structure, punishment institution, and information environment. In Fischer et al. (2016), punishment—whether centralized or decentralized—did not reliably increase efficiency compared to the no-punishment condition, except under very specific circumstances (intermediate noise) and only transiently.

- **Key moderators:**  
  - *Production technology matters:* In weakest-link (complementary) PGG variants, punishment can dramatically increase efficiency, but in linear PGGs, this is much less likely or can even be negative if antisocial punishment is prevalent (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
  - *Communication can rescue efficiency:* When communication (normative or accountability-focused) is allowed in PGGs with punishment/counter-punishment, group efficiency increases substantially, even neutralizing negative effects of counter-punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016).
  - *Duration/exposure:* Longer games (e.g., 40 rounds vs. 20) may reveal a positive long-run efficiency effect of punishment, as the initial cost of punishment is later offset by higher cooperation and payoffs (Sparks et al., 2024).
  - *Antisocial punishment:* Cultures/groups with high antisocial punishment may experience little or no efficiency benefit from enabling punishment in standard linear PGGs (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).

- **Punishment mechanism details:**  
  Across all directly relevant studies, the cost and magnitude of punishment, who can punish, and the visibility of actions are controlled or held constant. There is little evidence in this set for a strong effect of variation in punishment parameters (cost, tech) *within* PGGs, given that these are often fixed.

- **Indirect or adjacent findings:**  
  Higher contribution rates are seen in the presence of financial punishment or reward in centralized systems (Micheli et al., 2021), suggesting (but not proving) a probable gain in efficiency. Antisocial punishment and group composition can moderate behavioral responses (Espín et al., 2022), but actual efficiency is not measured in these cases.

- **No evidence for costless disapproval:**  
  Costless signals of disapproval do not substitute for costly punishment in sustaining long-term group efficiency (Sparks et al., 2024).

## Areas of disagreement or ambiguity:
- **Centralization of punishment:** Centralizing the sanctioning authority removes perverse punishment but does not itself increase efficiency except under certain types of noisy information (Fischer et al., 2016).
- **Communication with punishment:** When communication is possible, even with counter-punishment, efficiency is higher across PGG designs (Andrighetto et al., 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Based exclusively on this evidence base:

- **Prediction of efficiency in PGGs with punishment enabled should be strongly conditioned on:**  
  - *Game structure (linear vs. complementary/weakest-link)*: Expect positive efficiency effects of punishment in weakest-link setups, less so in standard linear games—especially in environments prone to antisocial punishment.
  - *Communication availability*: If chat or message exchange is possible, expect a more positive effect of punishment on efficiency; otherwise, effects may be neutral or negative, particularly with counter-punishment enabled.
  - *Number of rounds*: Positive effects of costly punishment on efficiency are most observable in longer games (e.g., 40+ rounds), as initial costs are amortized over sustained cooperation.
  - *Punishment institution and information*: Centralization reduces perverse punishment, but efficiency gains require further conditions; noisy information can suppress the benefits of punishment.

- **Control game efficiency (with punishment disabled) is only partly predictive for treatment efficiency**:  
  Punishment’s effect is not just an incremental increase over control; in some setups, punishment can lower efficiency (via antisocial acts or excessive punishment), in others it can dramatically raise efficiency (weakest-link/complementary games).

- **In settings with costless/non-material punishment (“disapproval only”), do not expect meaningful efficiency gains** (Sparks et al., 2024).

- **Reward institution (when paired with punishment or alone) can also increase contributions, but direct efficiency evidence is missing** (Micheli et al., 2021).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions (with payoff/efficiency evidence):**
- `player_count`
- `num_rounds`
- `chat`
- `all_or_nothing`
- `mpcr`
- `punishment_cost`
- (by implication) `punishment_exists` (varied in most relevant studies)

**Indirectly informed/contextually discussed (mostly via behavioral measures or design details):**
- `show_n_rounds`
- `show_other_summaries` (some studies specify, but not as a manipulated variable)
- `show_punishment_id`
- `punishment_tech`
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (occasionally)
- `default_contrib` (framing discussed, but not as a focal manipulation)

**Sparse or missing for prediction purposes:**
- `default_contrib`
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `reward_exists` (no direct efficiency evidence with these as focal variables)
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id` (seldom manipulated as experimental conditions)

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Narrow coverage of relevant outcomes:** Only a minority of studies measure and report group efficiency/payoff as a primary outcome in treatments with and without punishment.
- **Limited manipulation of several design dimensions:** Most efficiency-relevant studies manipulate only a few dimensions (e.g., punishment presence, chat/communication, group size, rounds, MPCR) and hold others constant; many dimensions used for prediction (e.g., information visibility, default contribution, reward structures) are effectively missing or not analyzed in relation to efficiency.
- **Cultural and institutional context underrepresented:** Findings on punishment efficacy (e.g., the moderation by antisocial punishment or culture) are derived from single-country or single-lab contexts; generalization is difficult.
- **Insufficient theoretical breadth:** There are no pure mechanism/theory papers modeling how efficiency effects of punishment should depend on multiple design dimensions, making triangulation between empirical and theoretical sources impossible here.
- **Potential imprecision regarding adjacent/complementary treatments:** Some studies report only on behavior or neural activity, not on efficiency or payoffs, and these cannot substitute for direct efficiency data.
- **Measurement/definition alignment:** Contribution rate is often measured, but this does not always translate into proportional efficiency gains due to punishment costs, antisocial punishment, and other factors.

**In sum:** The literature set offers strong evidence for the payoff impact of punishment under certain PGG structures and moderator conditions (rounds, communication, game type), but offers little to no guidance regarding efficiency outcomes for many game design dimensions. Predictions outside this “directly covered” space require substantial extrapolation and should be made with caution.
