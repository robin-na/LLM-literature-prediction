# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of 38 papers, including a balanced mix of empirical experimental lab studies, theoretical/game-theoretic models, and a few observational or ethnographic works. The empirical studies primarily use economic game paradigms—including standard public goods games (PGGs), close variants (e.g., minimum effort or threshold games, delegation or trust games, surplus allocation, real-effort team games, etc.), and other related social dilemmas. Theoretical papers often generalize punishment mechanisms across repeated interaction or varying social structures.

For the precise downstream task—predicting the change in group efficiency from enabling peer punishment in PGG-like games, as a function of design dimensions and control efficiency—the evidence base is substantial for standard PGGs, moderate for adjacent domains (trust games, threshold games), and sparse for certain special contexts or when other intervention mechanisms predominate (e.g., meritocratic sorting, rewards alone).

Many papers target exactly the effect of punishment on payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group earnings); others prioritize behavioral outcomes or discuss payoff implications indirectly. A handful of works are only contextually relevant or lack empirical outcomes. The breadth is strong for PGGs and closely related games, but less comprehensive for environments combining multiple design features (e.g., chat, visibility, dynamic group formation).

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**  
- *Exact relevance*: A substantial portion of papers use standard or optional PGG designs (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Botta et al., 2021) or experimentally test PGG-like public goods and common-pool resource games (Kanitsar, 2021; Peng, 2022; Nax et al., 2018).
- *Close/adjacent*: Many other studies use games with similar incentive and interaction structures (e.g., minimum effort, team investment, generalized exchange, trust/ultimatum/delegation games), providing adjacent evidence.
- *Weak/none*: Several observational, ethnographic, or purely theoretical works address broader themes (tradition, collective action) but do not employ PGGs.

**Punishment or Sanctions:**  
- *Exact relevance*: The bulk of the included papers manipulate peer or centralized punishment as a treatment or institutional variable (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Kanitsar, 2021).
- *Close*: Where only reward, exclusion, or other forms of sanction are studied, relevance is classified as adjacent or weak.
- *None*: Some papers only discuss punishment in passing, or not at all.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**  
- *Exact*: Several empirical and theory papers measure efficiency as (normalized) group payoff, total earnings, or welfare (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Peng, 2022).
- *Close/adjacent*: Others infer efficiency from contribution rates, avoidance of negative outcomes (e.g., avoiding the tragedy of the commons), or discuss utility gains without explicit efficiency measures.
- *Weak/none*: Some studies report only behavioral outcomes (punishment frequency, norm compliance), not payoff-based results.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**  
- Direct measures: group efficiency (ratio of realized to maximum possible group payoffs), average group payoff/earnings per round, total group welfare, and net surplus (accounting for punishment/reward costs).
- Indirect/adjacent: success rates in avoiding group losses (e.g., threshold games); equilibrium payoffs in repeated game models; earnings of subgroups such as punishers or contributors.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
- Individual and group contribution rates, cooperation frequency, punishment frequency and intensity, norm compliance, and partner choice.
- Attitudinal measures (preference for partners, trust, perceived fairness, etc.), reported in some studies but not substitutive for efficiency outcomes.

**Distinctions:**  
- Papers sometimes infer likely efficiency gains from increased contributions, but actual efficiency can be moderated or reversed by the direct costs of punishment.
- Some coordination games show increased mutual cooperation but also increased sanctioning costs, potentially offsetting welfare gains.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment typically increases efficiency in standard PGGs:** Empirical studies focusing on standard or near-standard PGGs overwhelmingly find that enabling peer punishment increases group efficiency, albeit typically by a modest margin, and often with substantial variability between groups and societies (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Magnitude and direction depend on context and mechanism details:**
    - *Societal and group composition:* The effectiveness of punishment is moderated by group-level heterogeneity, prevalence of strong reciprocators, cultural context, and presence of antisocial punishment or norm-keepers (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
    - *Network structure:* Dense, fully connected (public goods) networks enable more effective punishment and efficiency gains than sparse or asymmetric (generalized exchange) structures (Kanitsar, 2021).
    - *Punishment cost/effectiveness:* Costless or highly effective punishment yields greater efficiency improvements; costly punishment may not increase, or may even decrease, efficiency due to the overhead of sanctioning (Kanitsar, 2021; Herne et al., 2022; Calabuig et al., 2024).
    - *Type of punishment:* Formal, coordinated, or precommitted punishment mechanisms can outperform informal or uncoordinated regimes in terms of promoting efficiency (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Gueth & Otsubo, 2023).
    - *Optional participation/loner option:* In PGG designs with optional participation, enabling punishment increases group efficiency and can stabilize full cooperation (Botta et al., 2021).
- **Reward mechanisms:** Empirical work on majority-vote and peer reward mechanisms (without punishment) shows that majority-vote rewards can also increase efficiency, sometimes more robustly than peer reward, especially in heterogeneous environments (Peng, 2022).
- **Ambiguity and negative findings:** Not all contexts show efficiency gains:
    - In some coordination/team investment games, costly punishment increases cooperation but not net efficiency due to extensive use of punishment (Calabuig et al., 2024).
    - In certain trust or delegation games, enabling costly punishment does not improve, and may even reduce, group efficiency relative to the control, as costs outweigh deterrence benefits (Herne et al., 2022).
    - Punishment's effectiveness is reduced or null in open groups with migration, high frequency of antisocial punishment, or where power asymmetry allows selfish punishers to dominate (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Eldakar et al., 2018).
- **Absence of punishment:** Papers examining transparency, monitoring, or communication (without punishment) suggest that these, alone, may fail to sustain cooperation and can even decrease efficiency depending on the context (Becchetti et al., 2015; Nax et al., 2018; Golman, 2016).
- **Theoretical support:** Formal models generally predict that enabling punishment can (in equilibrium) shift groups from inefficient to efficient cooperation, provided parameters (such as punishment severity and horizon/discount factor) are in the appropriate range (Botta et al., 2021; Gioffré & Tampieri, 2025; Annen, 2011; Jones, 1999). These models delineate clear parameter thresholds for when punishment is effective, but stress that design details (e.g., cost, punishment/benefit ratio, population size, continuation probability) are critical.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For prediction of treatment efficiency given design dimensions and control efficiency:**

- **Expect a modest increase in efficiency from enabling punishment in standard PGGs, holding other features constant.** The effect size is moderate and varies by group, but the direction is usually positive (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).

- **The size of the efficiency gain is sensitive to:**
    - *Punishment cost and effectiveness:* Low-cost, high-impact punishment (per unit cost) produces stronger gains; costly punishment may decrease net efficiency if sanctioning is frequent (Kanitsar, 2021; Herne et al., 2022).
    - *Network topology:* Fully connected public goods structures see larger effects than sparse or asymmetric networks (Kanitsar, 2021).
    - *Group heterogeneity and culture:* More strong reciprocators, fewer 'norm-keepers,' and higher baseline cooperation support greater gains (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
    - *Optionality of participation, open/closed group status:* Optional participation can amplify the effect, but migration/open groups may diminish informal punishment mechanisms' impact (Botta et al., 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
    - *Game horizon and knowledge thereof:* Repeated, known-horizon games (with sufficient length) allow for greater coordination via punishment (Jones, 1999).
- **Control efficiency is a useful predictor, but design dimension interactions are important.** For example, in very inefficient controls (low baseline cooperation), adding punishment is more likely to yield pronounced efficiency gains, provided other factors (such as cost, network) are favorable.

- **Non-PGG or non-payoff contexts do not provide clear quantitative estimates.** Findings from adjacent games, behavioral data, or observational studies need to be interpreted with caution and mainly inform qualitative predictions or mechanism selection.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed by evidence (often with multiple studies):**
- `player_count` (group size): almost universally reported; theory and experiments show its importance for threshold effects, group stability, and punishment effectiveness.
- `num_rounds` (game length): frequently manipulated; effects of repeated interaction and horizon on cooperation/punishment sustainability widely studied.
- `all_or_nothing` (binary vs. continuous): both types are studied, with different predictions for threshold/coordination versus standard provision.
- `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return): almost always specified; central to predictions about incentive structure and equilibrium.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (cost-effectiveness/severity of punishment): key focus of both theory and empirical work; direct moderator of efficiency effects.
- `chat` (communication): less frequently manipulated, but some studies systematically exclude or include it.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: informative studies on reward-only and reward-plus-punishment designs.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: reported in some lab designs but less systematically analyzed; some studies manipulate visibility of actions or identities.
- Design features like migration, group openness, membership handling, and exclusion are highly relevant in specific papers (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Grund et al., 2020), but not always mapped to the above list.

**Indirectly or sparsely addressed:**
- `default_contrib`: Only occasionally reported; frame effect studies (opt-in/opt-out) exist but are rare in this set.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Addressed primarily in studies on transparency/monitoring or on punishment identity effects; otherwise sparsely manipulated.

**Missing or only contextually discussed:**
- Some dimensions such as the precise structure of punishment/reward assignment (who can punish whom, identity visibility) are discussed only in passing or not systematically varied across the set.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Not all studies directly measure efficiency:** Many infer payoff implications from behavioral shifts, but, due to possible high punishment costs, increases in cooperation or contributions do not always yield efficiency gains.
- **Heterogeneity of contexts and implementation:** There is considerable variation in player count, rounds, game framing, and punishment technology across studies, making direct quantitative generalization challenging.
- **Adjacency, not identity, of some games:** Several papers use adjacent (not true PGG) designs such as coordination games, trust/ultimatum, or team output games. These may not transfer quantitatively to standard PGG outcomes.
- **Cultural and population-specific effects:** Strong evidence of heterogeneity in punishment effectiveness linked to group composition and societal context (Suleiman & Samid, 2021), which are not fully captured by design dimensions alone.
- **Sparse manipulation of some design dimensions:** Variables like chat, default contribution, and some transparency features are not consistently tested, limiting predictive specificity on those axes.
- **Predictive ambiguity in edge cases:** In games with high punishment cost, uncoordinated sanctioning, asymmetric power, or particular network topologies, enabling punishment may not increase, or may even reduce, efficiency.
- **Absence of field data:** Most evidence is ex ante laboratory or game-theoretic; real-world organizational, field, or natural experiment evidence is rare or only contextually addressed.
- **Missing data on parameter interactions:** Few papers systematically vary multiple design dimensions, making nonlinearities and interactions between parameters less observable.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature base offers strong and directly relevant evidence that enabling peer punishment in standard PGGs, all else equal, usually increases efficiency, but the magnitude is contingent on punishment cost-effectiveness, group composition, network structure, and the presence of other mechanisms (e.g., rewards, open groups, communication). Certain design dimensions (group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/effectiveness) are well-studied; others (contribution framing, identity visibility) are less so. Important limitations arise from generalizability across context, the indirect measurement of efficiency in some studies, and untested interactions among design features. For prediction, the direction of effect (efficiency increases with punishment) is robust for standard designs with reasonable cost-effectiveness; for less standard or high-cost settings, caution is advised, as efficiency gains are less reliable or may be reversed.
