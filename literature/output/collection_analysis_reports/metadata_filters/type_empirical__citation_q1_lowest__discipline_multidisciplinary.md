# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set comprises 49 papers with a broad empirical orientation, focused mainly on experimental (lab) studies in economics, psychology, and behavioral science. The majority of included papers are **empirical laboratory experiments**, with a much smaller number of observational and theoretical/conceptual works. The evidence base covers both **classic public goods games (PGGs)** and a variety of **related social dilemmas and coordination games**, as well as some adjacent paradigms (Prisoner's Dilemma, Dictator Game, Sender-Receiver, etc.).

Within the set, **direct, high-relevance studies of PGGs with explicit payoff outcomes and manipulated punishment institutions are present** but in the minority. Most studies focus on **behavioral outcomes** (e.g., contribution rates, punishment behavior, cooperation frequency) rather than direct measures of efficiency (group payoff as a proportion of maximum obtainable). Additionally, a subset directly reports **efficiency or welfare outcomes** (Harrell, 2019; Molenmaker et al., 2023; Heine & Strobel, 2020; DeCaro et al., 2024, etc.), while others provide only adjacent or indirect data.

This evidence set offers a reasonably **broad coverage of canonical lab designs**, but is **narrow for the prediction task** to the degree that direct efficiency results under different punishment conditions are few. Many studies are only **adjacent (or weaker) in their alignment with the central prediction problem**, examining other forms of social sanctions, different game structures, or using only behavioral proxies for efficiency.

# 2) Task Relevance

## pgg_or_variant
- **exact**: A substantial core of the evidence is from standard or near-standard PGGs or variants (e.g., threshold PGGs, variable MPCRs), matching the prediction environment.
- **close/adjacent**: Many studies use closely related designs—team contests, real effort tasks, dyadic dilemmas, sender-receiver, etc.—that share social dilemma features but may not generalize cleanly.
- **weak/none**: Some papers are observational, animal studies, or highly stylized one-shot games not involving contribution to a common pool.

## punishment_or_sanctions
- **exact**: A significant number of studies manipulate peer punishment, leader (centralized/third-party) punishment, or compare punishment with reward, addressing design parameters directly.
- **close/adjacent**: Several studies deal with related forms of sanctions (e.g., exclusion, rejection, partner choice), or discuss the potential for punishment but do not feature its presence or absence as a variable.
- **weak/none**: Some studies focus only on communication, trust, reputation mechanisms, or deception without enabling formal punishment.

## efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome
- **exact**: Only a minority reports treatment effects on group efficiency/welfare, total payoff, group earnings—**the core prediction target**.
- **close/adjacent**: Many studies emphasize contribution rates or cooperation frequency, which are correlated with, but not the same as, efficiency. Some report earnings only as descriptive statistics without direct treatment comparisons.
- **weak/none**: Some studies focus solely on behavioral or psychological responses, perceptions, or group formation dynamics without any payoff-based outcomes.

**Summary**: The set is **highly relevant for PGG structures and punishment interventions**, but only **moderately to weakly relevant for direct efficiency prediction** due to the predominance of non-payoff outcomes and varied experimental foci.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (efficiency, group earnings/payoff, welfare, surplus):**
  - **Directly measured and analyzed in a core subset:** Harrell (2019); Molenmaker et al. (2023); Heine & Strobel (2020); DeCaro et al. (2024) (all with experimental manipulation of punishment).
  - **Reported as secondary/descriptive or indirectly inferred in others:** Ozono & Nakama (2022); Liao et al. (2021, retracted); Riedl et al. (2021); Pulcu (2024); dos Santos et al. (2015); Lo Iacono et al. (2024).

- **Behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, cooperation/defection, punishment frequency, norm compliance):**
  - **Most common outcome type:** E.g., Nakagawa et al. (2022); Clark & Dickinson (2020); Dewey et al. (2024); Rodrigues et al. (2024); Makovi et al. (2025); Nielsen & Pfattheicher (2024).
  - **Often used as proxy for efficiency**, but do not capture the cost of punishment or welfare destruction inherent in the mechanism.

- **Other outcomes:**
  - **Belief updating, psychological processes, attitudes, perceptions** in adjacent studies (Radkani et al., 2025; Gächter et al., 2025).

**Distinction maintained:** Most studies **do not directly measure efficiency**; when only behavioral proxies are available, this is explicitly noted.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of empirical findings, focusing on direct evidence for treatment efficiency:**

- **Enabling punishment in standard (uniform group) PGGs generally increases efficiency over no-punishment, but only when punishment costs and structure do not destroy more value than they create** (Harrell, 2019; Molenmaker et al., 2023; DeCaro et al., 2024).
  - *Peer punishment* typically leads to higher contributions and sometimes higher group payoff, but the **efficiency gains are offset by punishment costs (welfare destruction)** due to uncoordinated, redundant, or even retaliatory punishment (Harrell, 2019; Heine & Strobel, 2020).
  - *Leader/centralized punishment* is generally **more efficient**, yielding higher payoff increases per unit of contribution boost, so **institution design is crucial** (Harrell, 2019; DeCaro et al., 2024).
  - **Facilitation, restorative justice, or communication that coordinates enforcement enhances the efficiency impact of punishment.** Without institutional or communication supports, punishment is less effective or dysfunctional (DeCaro et al., 2024).

- **Heterogeneity in group structure can moderate or reverse the effect:**
  - **Uniform groups benefit; pluriform (heterogeneous) groups may see no efficiency benefit or even net losses** due to discriminatory (out-group) punishment and breakdown in cooperation (Molenmaker et al., 2023).
  - **Profitable punishment schemes (where the punisher is rewarded) destabilize cooperation and reduce welfare**, even incentivizing antisocial punishment (Alam & Rai, 2025).

- **Punishment effectiveness depends on design specifics:**
  - **Punishment cost relative to impact (efficiency ratio)**—if too high, the **cost may outweigh any contribution gain** (Ozono & Nakama, 2022).
  - **Who bears the punishment cost matters:** Pool-funded or costless punishments can increase contributions but may drain shared resources or introduce new inefficiencies (Rodrigues et al., 2024).
  - **Feedback and information salience (e.g., showing IDs, showing summaries) can amplify the effects of punishment or lead to counterproductive behaviors (discriminatory or antisocial punishment).**

- **Reward mechanisms or the combined use of reward and punishment** can sometimes enhance contributions but may not always increase efficiency—particularly if both mechanisms are present and costs accumulate (Heine & Strobel, 2020; Makovi et al., 2025).

- **Contexts and moderators:**
  - **Enduring effects may not persist after the removal of punishment unless reinforced by social or authority-based trust communication** (Nakagawa et al., 2022; Posten et al., 2025).
  - **In some cultural contexts or sample populations, punishment has limited or no positive effect on cooperation or efficiency** (Ozono & Nakama, 2022).

**Theoretical and mechanism arguments (not directly tied to measured efficiency):**
- **Punishment effectiveness relies on its norm-signaling or reputation-communicating power**; when this is undermined (e.g., through profit motivation or targeted out-group punishment), benefits are lost or reversed (Alam & Rai, 2025; Molenmaker et al., 2023).
- **Punishment design must avoid providing incentives for antisocial use.**

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given this body of evidence, **predictions of the efficiency impact of enabling punishment in PGGs should be made with strong reference to the structure of the punishment institution, group homogeneity, and the specifics of the punishment mechanism (cost/impact, centralization, information structure)**:

- **If the baseline (control) efficiency is known and the group is uniform, peer punishment is enabled, cost-to-impact ratio is moderate to high, and no additional institutional supports or communication facilitation are provided—expect a modest efficiency increase, often limited by punishment costs.**  
  (Harrell, 2019; Molenmaker et al., 2023; DeCaro et al., 2024)

- **If punishment is centralized or coordinated (e.g., single leader, third-party punishment) and the leader is cooperative, efficiency gains are maximized.**  
  (Harrell, 2019; DeCaro et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2021 [retracted, but pattern noted])

- **If the group is heterogeneous in salient dimensions (pluriform), the effect of punishment may be absent or negative due to discriminatory punishment—do not expect efficiency gains.**  
  (Molenmaker et al., 2023)

- **If punishment design allows profitable punishment or antisocial punishment, expect efficiency losses or destabilization rather than gains.**  
  (Alam & Rai, 2025)

- **Reward mechanisms or the presence of both punishment and reward can, in some settings, fail to outperform either alone and may reduce efficiency.**  
  (Heine & Strobel, 2020; Makovi et al., 2025)

- **If punishment is costless for the punisher (e.g., common-pool funded), expect more generous use with possible boosts to contributions, but efficiency outcomes are ambiguous due to unmeasured impacts on the group pot.**  
  (Rodrigues et al., 2024)

- **When control efficiency is already high, scope for efficiency gains may be limited; when control efficiency is low and institutional supports are weak, gains from punishment may be small or negative.**

**Key caveat:**  
**The literature strongly supports moderation: punishment effects are context dependent and design conditional. Direct prediction should account for group structure, punishment centralization, effectiveness, cost, and information features. Baseline efficiency is necessary but not sufficient for precise prediction.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions** (empirically manipulated/measured and analyzed in multiple papers):

- `player_count` (group size): manipulated across classic PGGs and variants; small homogeneous groups generally exhibit stronger positive punishment effects (Harrell, 2019; Molenmaker et al., 2023; Nielsen & Pfattheicher, 2024).
- `num_rounds`: repeated games (typically 10-30 rounds) most studied; effect of punishment often cumulative or changing over time.
- `punishment_cost` (and impact ratio): a critical determinant of both behavior and efficiency (Harrell, 2019; Ozono & Nakama, 2022; Heine & Strobel, 2020).
- `punishment_tech` (mechanism details: peer vs. leader, third-party, cost source, allowable range): institution design matters for efficiency outcomes (Harrell, 2019; DeCaro et al., 2024; Rodrigues et al., 2024).
- `mpcr`: widely varied, directly manipulated; higher MPCRs foster greater cooperation and moderation of punishment effects.
- `all_or_nothing`, `chat` (communication): often manipulated; availability of communication and whether contributions are binary or continuous affect both cooperation and punishment dynamics.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`: less consistently, but sometimes studied; feedback and information affect behavioral responses to punishment.

**Indirectly or contextually discussed:**
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: analyzed in a subset of studies comparing reward to punishment or their combination.
- `default_contrib`: only occasionally manipulated; framing effects on default contribution can moderate baseline efficiency but are less studied in interaction with punishment.

**Sparsely covered or missing:**
- `show_punishment_id` (identity revealed): infrequently and usually as part of information feedback structures.
- `default_contrib`, aspects of *systematic framing* outside opt-in/opt-out.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Scarcity of direct efficiency results:** Many studies report only contributions/cooperation, not actual payoff or efficiency—**treatment effects on efficiency can diverge from contribution effects due to punishment costs**.
- **Underrepresentation of diverse institutional designs:** Most studies use lab-standard peer punishment and small group sizes; less is known about effects in very large groups or naturalistic, field settings.
- **Limited manipulation of full parameter space:** Systematic cross-paper variation is lacking for some dimensions (reward tech, communication details, identification, framing).
- **Group structure:** Key moderators such as group homogeneity/heterogeneity are only explored in a few studies—**predictions may be unreliable if group composition varies**.
- **Cultural and sample differences:** Not all effects generalize across participant pools (e.g., university students vs. online workers, national/cultural settings).
- **Institutional context and external validity:** Findings from controlled experiments may not transfer straightforwardly to applied or real-world environments with complex incentives and social structures.
- **Combination of punishment with other mechanisms:** The joint effect of punishment, reward, reputation, exclusion, and voluntary participation is under-studied in factorial designs.
- **Ambiguity in negative effects:** Some evidence of punishment reducing efficiency under certain conditions (e.g., profitable punishment, contest environments, heterogeneous groups) cautions against broad generalization.
- **Retracted, adjacent, or weakly relevant studies:** Caution must be used to discount or appropriately qualify evidence from papers that are retracted, only adjacent, or lack direct outcome data.

**Bottom line:**  
**Predictions about efficiency effects of punishment in PGG-like environments should be made conditionally, with explicit reference to group structure, institution design, cost/impact parameters, and baseline efficiency. Direct empirical evidence is available for some dimensions, but is missing or ambiguous for others, and non-behavioral outcomes must not be conflated with true efficiency or payoff measures.**
