# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises **105 studies**, encompassing a broad mix of empirical papers—primarily experimental lab and field studies, with a few observational analyses. The majority are **lab-based investigations directly manipulating punishment in public goods games (PGGs) or very close variants**. The field studies and adjacent-game experiments offer context but typically focus on norm violations or cooperation in broader social environments or with different game structures. Direct theoretical papers are rare, and most findings are empirical. Overall, for the task of predicting efficiency in PGGs with or without peer punishment, the evidence base is **broad, highly empirical, and relatively strong** for standard PGG environments, though narrower for more complex or non-standard designs.

# 2) Task Relevance

### pgg_or_variant
- **Exact relevance**: Many high-quality studies deploy standard linear PGGs with clear efficiency or payoff measures (e.g., Rand et al., 2009; Gürerk et al., 2006; Gächter et al., 2017; Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gross et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2013; Herrmann et al., 2008).
- **Close/adjacent relevance**: Several studies focus on repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma, indirect reciprocity, or common-pool resource (CPR) settings that are structurally similar but not identical (e.g., Janssen et al., 2010; Wu et al., 2009; Dreber et al., 2008).
- **Weak or none**: Some papers offer only conceptual or contextual relevance, or their primary games diverge too far from the PGG structure.

### punishment_or_sanctions
- **Exact relevance**: Many studies directly manipulate the presence/absence of peer punishment or institutional sanctions in PGGs, often with variation in implementation details (costs, technology, visibility).
- **Adjacent/weak**: Some explore similar mechanisms (e.g., ostracism, partner choice, reputation) or focus on punishment in other economic games, with only indirect relevance to PGGs.
- **None**: A subset of baseline or contextual studies omit any punishment mechanism.

### efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome
- **Exact or close**: A significant subset reports **group payoff, efficiency, welfare, or total earnings** as primary outcomes (e.g., Gürerk et al., 2006; Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006; Gächter et al., 2017; Lohse & Waichman, 2020).
- **Adjacent/weak**: Many studies focus on cooperation rates, contribution levels, or behavioral outcomes instead of efficiency.
- **None**: Surveys, mechanism studies, and motivational papers frequently omit payoff-based outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

### Payoff-Related Outcomes (directly relevant)
- **Efficiency**: Defined as group payoff divided by the social optimum (full cooperation), is the main outcome in a core set of PGG-punishment studies.
- **Group payoff/welfare/total earnings**: Frequently reported; increases/decreases are often directly linked to predictions about efficiency (e.g., Rand et al., 2009; Gürerk et al., 2006).
- **Surplus, aggregate coins, or welfare**: Used in a subset as primary or supplementary outcomes.

### Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (indirect)
- **Contribution/cooperation rate**: Ubiquitous, but insufficient as a proxy for efficiency, as punishment can increase cooperation but reduce efficiency if it is too costly.
- **Frequency/intensity of punishment**: Used to analyze mechanisms and responses but not directly linked to group welfare.
- **Norm compliance, reciprocity, ostracism, and partner choice**: Often used to study underlying micro-mechanisms, but must be distinguished from efficiency.
  
Some papers report both, but **any conclusion about efficiency must be based on actual payoff outcomes, not merely on increased cooperation or punishment use**.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### General Efficacy of Punishment on Efficiency in PGGs
- **Punishment often increases efficiency in repeated linear PGGs** (Gürerk et al., 2006; Gächter et al., 2017; Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gross et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2013).
    - In standard laboratory settings, introducing peer punishment raises efficiency from low (decaying cooperation) to near-optimal levels when baseline is low.
    - **Magnitude**: Gains can be dramatic but are reduced by the direct cost of punishment (Rand et al., 2009; Wu et al., 2016).
- **When punishment is costly and used frequently, efficiency gains are limited or even negative** (Wu et al., 2016; Lohse & Waichman, 2020; Janssen et al., 2010).
    - The net effect depends on whether gains from cooperation outweigh the direct efficiency losses from costly punishment.
    - Some experiments find that, **relative to costless alternatives (e.g., gossip, reward)**, punishment does not outperform and may underperform (Rand et al., 2009; Wu et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2018).
- **Contextual moderators**:
    - **Informational interventions** — Communication, reputation, and norm-signaling **boost the (cost-)efficiency of punishment** by reducing the frequency and severity of its actual use (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Rockenbach & Milinski, 2006; Gross et al., 2016).
    - **Societal context/antisocial punishment** — In some societies, punishment backfires due to 'antisocial punishment,' reducing efficiency or negating gains (Herrmann et al., 2008).
    - **Punishment structure** — Institutional design (centralization, legitimacy, voluntary power transfer, second-order punishment) mediates the ability of punishment to induce efficient cooperation (Gross et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014; Baldassarri & Grossman, 2011).
    - **Game environment** — In dynamic/resource-based (CPR) games or when punishment enables enforcement of destructive norms (low or negative MPCR), punishment can **reduce efficiency** (Abbink et al., 2017; Janssen et al., 2010).
    - **Bribery/corruption** — In some institutional punishment contexts, availability of side-payments or corruption can cause punishment to undermine efficiency (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).

### Scope and Boundaries
- **In dyadic/adjacent games (Prisoner’s Dilemma, trust, ultimatum)**: Most studies find punishment does not increase—and often reduces—group efficiency (Dreber et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2017; Fehr & Rockenbach, 2003).
- **With both punishment and reward**: Reward dominates punishment for efficiency; punishment is more effective at enforcing cooperation but undermines net payoffs due to cost (Rand et al., 2009; Yang et al., 2018).
- **No single universal effect size**: Effect magnitudes (and sometimes sign) depend on game design, parameterization, and context.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct predictor mapping**: The **best-supported prediction** is that, in canonical repeated PGGs with low baseline efficiency and punishment cost not too high, enabling punishment will **substantially increase efficiency above control**, approaching or achieving near-optimal group payoff (Gürerk et al., 2006; Gächter et al., 2017; Fehr & Gächter, 2002).
- **Moderators**:
    - *Punishment cost and technology*—Low (relative) cost and high impact of punishment increase the efficiency gains; high cost or inefficacy blunt gains or may cause net losses.
    - *Presence of communication or norm-signaling*—Channels for communication or explicit norm-signaling **increase the efficiency of punishment** by reducing its use and increasing norm adherence (Andrighetto et al., 2013).
    - *Social context*—High levels of antisocial punishment (whereby cooperators are punished) in some societies or groups **reverse or eliminate efficiency gains** (Herrmann et al., 2008).
    - *MPCR and all-or-nothing structure*—If the marginal return to cooperation is below the individual cost (MPCR < 1/n), punishment can enforce harmful norms, actively **reducing efficiency** (Abbink et al., 2017).
    - *Institutional punishment/centralization*—Voluntary centralization or legitimate authority can **amplify efficiency gains** relative to standard peer punishment (Gross et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014).
    - *Corruption opportunity*—Where punishment can be subverted (e.g., by bribery of institutional enforcers), its effect can become **negative** for efficiency (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
- **Control efficiency as baseline**: Empirical work suggests prediction should **use control efficiency (no punishment) as a baseline**, with **incremental shifts up or down** due to punishment modulated by the above factors.
- **Limits of generalization**: Caution is needed when generalizing to noncanonical designs, single-shot or one-round games, games with externalities, or games where social context diverges from standard lab protocols.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

## Directly Informed (empirically manipulated and analyzed)
- **player_count**: Strong support—studies use groups typically of 4-5, with some variation up to 24; efficiency effects consistent across standard sizes (Gürerk et al., 2006; Gächter et al., 2017).
- **num_rounds**: Well covered—repetition central to punishment’s efficacy; *longer* games produce greater efficiency gains via repeated enforcement.
- **mpcr**: Highly informative—outcomes depend on whether cooperation is individually costly/beneficial; studies directly vary this (Abbink et al., 2017; Gächter et al., 2017).
- **punishment_cost & punishment_tech**: Directly manipulated in numerous studies; critical for calibrating effect size and direction.
- **all_or_nothing**: Present but less variable—most canonical studies use continuous contributions; some all-or-nothing games exist, with similar trends.
- **chat/norm-signaling**: Several experiments show that communication channels or norm-signaling enhance punishment's efficiency effects.
- **reward_exists & reward_cost**: Frequently controlled; presence of reward mechanisms typically boosts efficiency more than punishment alone.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries**: Sometimes manipulated, especially in reputation or observability studies.

## Indirectly Informed or Often Held Constant
- **default_contrib**: Sometimes described (opt-in vs opt-out); rarely the focus of empirical variation for efficiency with punishment.
- **show_punishment_id**: Occasional manipulation (anonymous vs onymous punishment), less commonly central to prediction.
- **reward_tech**: Not always specified; more studies on punishment than nuanced reward structures.

## Only Contextually Discussed or Missing
- **chat** (beyond norm-signaling): Most PGGs prohibit real chat; when present, communication is a strong positive moderator.
- **show_other_summaries & player visibility**: Typically constant; direct studies on summary feedback are less common.
- **show_punishment_id**: Addressed in studies of anonymity/ononymity, but findings about efficiency are mostly collateral.
- **Designs allowing corruption/bribery or alternative solutions**: Covered in few, specialized papers (Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Gross & De Dreu, 2019).
- **Complex/dynamic CPR, asymmetric games**: A handful, relevant specifically to resource management (Janssen et al., 2010).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Boundary of generalizability**: Most high-quality evidence is for **standard, repeated, small-group, linear PGGs**. Effects may differ in dynamic CPRs, threshold/step-level PGGs, very large N, or games with partner choice, ostracism, or network effects as primary sanctioning modes.
- **Cultural and social context**: The impact of antisocial punishment or negative spillover via societal norms can defeat otherwise robust positive effects.
- **Payoff vs. contribution/behavioral outcome confusion**: As many papers only report contributions, **relying on non-payoff outcomes can yield misleading inferences**—higher contributions do not always translate into higher efficiency due to punishment costs.
- **Reward and combined interventions**: While punishment often increases efficiency relative to baseline, it is often **outperformed by reward-based mechanisms** or by the combination of reward with punishment.
- **Edge cases/negative results**: Some designs or parameterizations, especially with high punishment costs, allow for **negative or null efficiency effects** (Wu et al., 2016; Lohse & Waichman, 2020).
- **Sparse empirical manipulation of some dimensions**: Elements like `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id`, and fine-grained reward/feedback mechanics are under-explored in efficiency outcome terms.
- **Few formal prediction models**: While qualitative direction and effect size estimates are well supported, formal or statistical models linking all 14 design dimensions to efficiency under punishment are rare.
- **Indirectly relevant literature**: Many papers are adjacent in game form or outcome (trust, ultimatum, field norm enforcement); while mechanism insight is plentiful, their quantitative value for efficiency prediction is low.

---

**Summary**:  
The literature provides **strong empirical evidence** for the positive effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in canonical, repeated PGGs—**but only under certain design and contextual conditions**. Major effect moderators include punishment cost/technology, MPCR, communication or reputation channels, and social context (especially antisocial punishment or corruption). Prediction from design dimensions plus control efficiency is best anchored in these empirical findings, with corrective discounting in the presence of known negative moderators. **Evidence is sparse or only contextual for several less-often-manipulated design parameters and for highly nonstandard PGG variants.**
