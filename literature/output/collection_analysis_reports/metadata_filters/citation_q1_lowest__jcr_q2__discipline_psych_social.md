# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is relatively broad in disciplinary coverage, but only a small subset provides direct, high-quality empirical evidence for the effect of punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments. Among the 59 papers, roughly a dozen report empirical (experimental) findings on both punishment (or sanctions) and efficiency (group payoff, welfare, etc.) in PGG or direct variants, with several reporting only behavioral outcomes. A significant number are empirical or theoretical studies on adjacent or related games (e.g., CPRs, ultimatum games, trust games) or focus on behavioral mechanisms, psychological moderators, or contextual factors, often without measuring efficiency. Theoretical and review-based contributions contextualize the mechanisms by which punishment can (or cannot) enhance cooperation and payoff but generally lack quantification tied to game parameters. Overall, the direct evidence base for prediction is moderate in coverage and scope, with notable gaps in detail for some game design dimensions and cross-dimensional interactions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance**: A core set of papers uses classic laboratory PGGs or near-direct variants, with standard features (linear returns, 4–5 players, 1–32 rounds, typical MPCRs, optional punishment, no baseline communication, etc.) (e.g., Zhang et al., 2024; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Ye et al., 2023; Joseph et al., 2025; Botelho et al., 2022).  
- **Close/adjacent relevance**: Many others use games with overlapping structure/mechanism (e.g., CPR games, resource allocation, dyadic dilemmas, third-party punishment games), yielding **adjacent** relevance for PGGs.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact or close relevance**: Multiple studies directly manipulate peer or institutional punishment (costly/tokens deducted, targeted, leader-driven, democratic, etc.), closely matching the dimension of interest for the prediction task.
- **Adjacent/weak**: A substantial fraction of the literature examines fairness-based rejections, exclusion, or endogenous sanctions that are close behavioral analogues but do not always involve explicit, parameterized punishment stages as in PGGs.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact relevance**: Only a subset of the directly relevant PGG experiments measures and reports efficiency in the required sense (ratio of group payoff to theoretical maximum), or directly reports group payoff, average earnings, surplus, or welfare with and without punishment.  
- **Adjacent/weak**: Most other papers report cooperation/contribution rates, punishment frequencies, voting outcomes, or focus on mechanisms, with payoffs addressed only in passing (if at all).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**  
  - *Direct, quantified efficiency/group payoff* is measured and reported in a handful of studies (e.g., Zhang et al., 2024; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Ye et al., 2023; Joseph et al., 2025; Botelho et al., 2022; De Geest et al., 2022; Webb & Foddy, 2004).
  - *Total earnings* or *welfare* synonyms also sometimes appear (e.g., Billinger & Rosenbaum, 2019; Heller & Sieberg, 2008; Nasrallah & Cheaib, 2016).
- **Non-payoff (behavioral) outcomes:**  
  - *Cooperation rate*, *contribution rate*, *punishment frequency*, *norm compliance*, *emotional/motivational mechanisms*, and *fairness perceptions* are much more prevalent primary outcomes in the broader literature.
  - Many papers focus on when or why punishment occurs, or on moderators/motivators of sanctioning, rather than on output payoffs.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Empirical consensus:**  
  - **Punishment can increase group efficiency/payoff relative to control** in classic PGG settings, especially where parameters support effective norm enforcement, punishment is not overused, and the cost-to-impact ratio is moderate or favorable (Zhang et al., 2024; Benard & Barclay, 2020; Joseph et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2023 [for large punishment]).
  - **High punishment cost, frequent use, or low baseline cooperation can make punishment inefficient**, with group earnings (efficiency) dropping below the no-punishment baseline despite higher cooperation rates (Botelho et al., 2022; Billinger & Rosenbaum, 2019 for managerial appropriation cases; see also meta-analytic review evidence).
  - **Effect heterogeneity:** Efficiency gains from punishment depend on:
    - **Magnitude** of punishment (small penalties may increase costs without deterring free-riding; large penalties are more effective yet risk retaliation or antisocial usage) (Ye et al., 2023; Windrich et al., 2024).
    - **Punishment structure** (democratic vs. autocratic punishment, targeted vs. shared, peer vs. leader, etc.) (Benard & Barclay, 2020; Zhang et al., 2024; Webb & Foddy, 2004).
    - **Contextual moderators**: Communication, cognitive load, group selection methods, or information structure can change both the use and effectiveness of punishment (Zhao et al., 2024 [on antisocial punishment under cognitive load]; Pedrazzini et al., 2025).
  - **Adjacent environments (CPR, dyads, third-party)**: The mechanism is often similar, but the effect on efficiency is less predictable and sometimes negative, especially in small groups, repeated play, or where punishment is easily misapplied or triggers retaliation cycles (Rumble et al., 2022; Billinger & Rosenbaum, 2019; D'Exelle & Riedl, 2013).

- **Theory and mechanism arguments:**  
  - Multiple models show that **punishment can be a stable mechanism for sustaining cooperation and improving efficiency**, but only when costs are not prohibitive, application is nearly universal, and retaliation is limited (Heller & Sieberg, 2008; Nasrallah & Cheaib, 2016; Whitmeyer, 2004).
  - Nonlinearity and possible catastrophic equilibrium shifts (Whitmeyer, 2004): At intermediate cost or monitoring levels, punishment can sometimes destabilize cooperation, lowering efficiency.

- **Reward versus punishment:**  
  - Where direct comparison exists, **rewards often raise efficiency more than punishment** (Pedrazzini et al., 2025), though punishment can be more effective at sustaining cooperation (Zhang et al., 2024).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Use direct, dimension-matched empirical studies whenever possible**:  
  For PGGs with parameters close to Zhang et al. (2024), Benard & Barclay (2020), Joseph et al. (2025), rely primarily on their reported group payoffs and efficiency differences due to punishment.

- **Control efficiency informs bounds**:  
  The efficiency of the control (no-punishment) game provides a baseline. Where control efficiency is high, enabling punishment may yield modest or even negative effects on efficiency if punishment is overused or costly (Botelho et al., 2022). At intermediate baseline efficiency, punishment often increases efficiency if punishment is used judiciously.

- **Punishment technology matters**:  
  - *Low-to-moderate cost, high-impact punishment* (cost-to-impact ratio favorable) predicts higher likelihood of efficiency gains versus costly, low-impact schemes.
  - *Magnitude*: Small punishment may not suffice (Ye et al., 2023; Windrich et al., 2024).

- **Game structure and context can mediate or moderate effects**:  
  - *Leadership/democratic selection of punishers* can enhance efficiency impact (Benard & Barclay, 2020).
  - *Feedback, round structure, and communication* may support more effective/less costly punishment use.
  - *Small groups and repeated play* pose risk that punishment costs exceed benefits unless social norms or leader roles discipline usage.

- **Adjacent evidence**:  
  Where directly matched data are missing, theory supports a mechanism for efficiency gains from punishment under favorable cost structures, but empirical evidence from dyads or non-linear/complex settings (e.g., repeated CPR/dyadic games) suggests caution, as efficiency can sometimes decrease.

- **Dimension-level guidance**:  
  Only a subset of the 14 dimensions (see below) is robustly and directly tied to efficiency effects of punishment; inference for missing or context-only dimensions requires caution or theoretical reasoning.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed (multiple studies with quantified efficiency/payoff effects):**  
  - `player_count` (primarily 4–5 players, sometimes 2; effect size may change with group size),  
  - `num_rounds` (varies from 1 to 32; duration affects sustainability of cooperation),  
  - `mpcr` (commonly ~0.4–0.5; critical for sustaining contributions and punishment effectiveness),  
  - `punishment_cost` (ratio to impact key; moderate or low cost per impact supports positive efficiency effect),  
  - `punishment_tech` (peer, leader, democratic, targeted vs. shared)—the structure matters,
  - `all_or_nothing` (mostly continuous, but some threshold or step-level variants).  
- **Indirectly informed/contextually discussed:**  
  - `chat` and `show_other_summaries` (communication and feedback can affect coordination, but few direct efficiency findings),  
  - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (occasionally studied; reward often outperforms punishment for efficiency),  
  - `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id` (information structure and transparency are mentioned as moderators, but rarely as main treatments).  
- **Effectively missing or data-sparse:**  
  - `default_contrib` (contribution framing not systematically manipulated or reported),  
  - Reward dimensions are sparsely matched (when present, often reported as comparative mechanism rather than central variable).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Coverage gaps/iIncomplete design dimension data:**  
  - Not all dimensions are jointly manipulated or systematically reported, limiting cross-dimensional prediction (e.g., interaction of `punishment_cost` and `player_count` often implicit rather than explicitly studied).
  - `default_contrib`, reward dimensions, and information variables (`show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`) are infrequently or only indirectly addressed.

- **Behavioral versus efficiency outcomes:**  
  - Most studies prioritize cooperation/contribution rates, with only a minority reporting efficiency/payoff as a primary outcome. This creates ambiguity when attempting to infer payoff effects from behavioral effects.

- **Contextual and parameter heterogeneity:**  
  - Effects of punishment on efficiency are sensitive to subtle differences in player cognition (Ye et al., 2023), game framing, group selection, cost/impact ratio, and presence of communication or alternative enforcement mechanisms.
  - Laboratory findings may not generalize to field, natural, or high-stakes environments.

- **Ambiguity and disagreement:**  
  - Papers disagree, sometimes sharply, on whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency, with evidence of context-dependent reversals (e.g., Botelho et al., 2022 vs. Zhang et al., 2024). Retaliation, antisocial punishment, and overuse can negate or reverse expected gains.
  - Some theory papers warn of non-linearities and potential for "catastrophic" collapses tied to punishment dynamics (Whitmeyer, 2004).

- **Sparse data in key edge cases:**  
  - Large groups, very long games, designs with simultaneous reward and punishment, or institutionally implemented punishment (with minimal/central cost) are poorly covered.
  - Evidence is weakest for prediction when all or several parameters are at extremes or outside the standard lab PGG paradigm.

---

**Summary:**  
The empirical literature offers solid, directly relevant evidence that enabling peer punishment in small- to medium-sized PGGs with moderate MPCR and favorable punishment cost/impact typically increases efficiency relative to control, provided punishers do not overuse sanctions and baseline cooperation is not already high. However, when punishment is excessively costly, misapplied, or when group structure undermines cost-effectiveness, it can easily reduce efficiency—sometimes making group payoff worse than baseline. Most design dimensions are only partially or indirectly informed, making fine-grained prediction challenging outside well-studied parametric regions. Observed ambiguity across studies and settings suggests careful, context-matched extrapolation rather than mechanical application of punishment's average effect.
