# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base consists of a large and diverse set of 190 academic papers, with a substantial number providing **direct empirical, experimental laboratory evidence** on standard linear public goods games (PGG), costly peer punishment, and group efficiency. There are also a significant number of theoretical papers (modeling or agent-based simulations), field/lab-in-the-field experiments (often in CPR or adjacent settings), and papers focusing on variants or adjacent game paradigms (e.g., trust games, weakest-link games, snowdrift games, and other social dilemmas). 

- **Empirical vs. Theory**: The set captures a robust empirical core for standard linear PGGs with punishment (including efficiency measurements), complemented by extensive theory/simulation literature that generalizes results or explores moderators such as network structure, institutional design, or evolutionary dynamics.
- **Breadth/Narrowness**: For the **core prediction task**—predicting the effect of peer punishment on group efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments based on design parameters—coverage is **broad** but not universal across all possible design dimensions and institutional innovations. Some game features (e.g., endogenous institution formation, networked populations, nonlinear production functions, or complex monitoring regimes) are less densely covered with direct efficiency data and rely more on theory or adjacent paradigms.

# 2) Task Relevance

Relevance is assessed separately for each target dimension:

**a) PGG or Variant**:  
- **exact**: Substantial empirical and theoretical work is on standard linear PGGs or minor variants (e.g., threshold, weakest-link).  
- **close/adjacent/weak**: Many studies extend to CPR (common pool resource games), trust games, snowdrift games, or public good provision with extra features (e.g., expulsion or migration). Such studies are close/adjacent; generalization must be cautious.

**b) Punishment or Sanctions**:  
- **exact**: The majority of direct empirical and theoretical studies manipulate or model peer punishment or institutional sanctions explicitly.
- **close/adjacent/weak**: Some studies use ostracism, redistribution, or reputation loss (non-monetary 'punishment'), which are closely related but may differ in costs/outcomes.

**c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**:
- **exact**: A solid core of papers directly measures "efficiency" (group payoff relative to the cooperative optimum) or group total payoff/earnings.
- **close**: Many studies focus on contribution rates but also report total earnings, surplus, or (in simulation) long-run payoffs.
- **adjacent/weak**: Several studies only measure behavioral outcomes (contribution, cooperation rate, etc.) or present efficiency as a derived or secondary result. Caution must be taken when interpreting findings based on contribution rates for payoff-based predictions.

In summary, the set includes many **high-relevance (exact)** papers for all three focal dimensions, but there is considerable variation in how closely each paper matches the target outcomes and design features.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**a) Payoff-Related Outcomes:**  
- **Efficiency**: Explicit measurement of group efficiency (total payoff as a fraction of the cooperative optimum) is available in many lab PGG studies.
- **Total/group earnings or welfare**: Frequently used as exact or close proxies for efficiency across experimental and theoretical work.
- **Related**: Surplus, aggregate return, welfare, coverage rate, or income inequality when derived from group outcomes.

**b) Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
- **Contribution/cooperation rate**: Ubiquitous; often the primary or only measured outcome, sometimes used as a proxy for efficiency.
- **Punishment frequency, antisocial punishment, counter-punishment rates**: Common, especially in studies of mechanism design or cross-cultural variation.
- **Norm compliance, ostracism, social approval/disapproval**: Often reported in studies with non-monetary sanctions or informal mechanisms.

**Distinction Maintained**:  
- **Efficiency and payoff measures** are clearly distinct from non-payoff behavioral outcomes in the better lab evidence (e.g., Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Page et al., 2013), but some studies only report the latter or blur the distinction (must interpret with care).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the literature, the following main findings emerge for the **effect of peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments, conditioned on design dimensions**:

---

**A. Standard Linear PGG with Peer Punishment**
- *Punishment often increases average contributions but has an ambiguous or context-dependent effect on efficiency*: In many designs, the cost of punishment actions outweighs the gains from increased cooperation, resulting in **no improvement or even a reduction in group efficiency** compared to control (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016).
- *Key moderators*:
    - **Punishment cost/effectiveness ratio**: High-cost/low-impact punishment is typically net-negative for efficiency.
    - **Design of punishment mechanism**: Redistributive or tax-based punishment (where fines are redistributed, not 'burned') can reliably increase both contributions and efficiency (Page et al., 2013; Yao & Chen, 2014).
    - **Legitimacy and targeting**: Punishment increases efficiency only if perceived as legitimate and well-targeted (Zheng & Nie, 2013).
    - **Presence of antisocial punishment**: Substantial antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) can eliminate any efficiency gain (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Handfield et al., 2016).
    - **Availability and cost of punishment rights**: If acquiring the right to punish requires even a small cost, participation drops and efficiency gains disappear (Ramalingam et al., 2016).
    - **Communication**: Allowing communication can eliminate efficiency losses from counter-punishment and enhance the positive effect of punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016).
    - **Production function (technology)**: Complementarity/threshold games (e.g., weakest-link) see much larger efficiency gains from punishment than standard linear PGGs (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
    - **Group size**: In small groups, anonymous punishment can increase efficiency; in larger groups, public punishment (ID revealed) is more effective and can rescue efficiency (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017).
    - **Information environment**: Perfect or noisy monitoring shapes both the use and impact of punishment (Fischer et al., 2016; van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Goeschl & Jarke, 2016).
    - **Baseline (control) efficiency**: If control efficiency is already high due to strong norms or public information, adding punishment can **reduce** efficiency (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
    - **Institutional or endogenous enforcement**: Endogenous institution formation (e.g., jointly purchasing enforcement devices) can substantially increase efficiency if mechanisms are cheap and effective; if not, effects can be muted or negative (Reif et al., 2017; Kingsley & Brown, 2016).

---

**B. Design Innovations and Variants**
- *Selection/assignment of punishers*: Assigning punishment rights to top contributors (merit-based) increases efficiency (Grieco et al., 2017); random single-punisher assignment does not.
- *Sanction network structure*: Who can punish whom sharply moderates whether punishment increases efficiency (Lierl, 2016).
- *Centralized vs. decentralized punishment*: Centralization can eliminate perverse punishment but is not always more efficient overall (Fischer et al., 2016; Kingsley & Brown, 2016).
- *Reward vs. punishment*: Reward often produces higher efficiency than punishment; mixed systems (balanced or combined reward and punishment) can optimize efficiency (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Cong et al., 2016).

---

**C. Theoretical and Simulation Findings**
- *Punishment increases efficiency under favorable parameter conditions*: When cost/effectiveness and threshold criteria are met, punishment can move groups from low- to high-cooperation, high-efficiency equilibria (Roberts, 2013; Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Hwang, 2017).
- *Nonlinearities and network effects*: Coordination among punishers, network structure, and spatial features can amplify or attenuate the efficiency impact of punishment (Chung et al., 2013; Sui et al., 2017).
- *Optional participation and incentives*: With optional participation and institutional punishment, even weak punishment can ensure high efficiency; rewards alone are less effective in such designs (Sasaki, 2014).

---

**D. Conditions Leading to Negative or Null Efficiency Effects**
- *High baseline efficiency*: When pre-existing norms or transparency already create high efficiency, adding punishment often only adds costs (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
- *Complex or nonlinear payoff structures*: In nonlinear (e.g., concave) CPR settings, punishment may not increase efficiency or can even decrease it, while communication is a stronger positive moderator (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Robbett, 2016).
- *Noisy environments*: In the presence of monitoring noise, punishment frequently reduces efficiency below control (van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
- *Heterogeneous groups*: Punishment may be weak or ineffective in settings with private or diverse incentives (Robbett, 2016).

---

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Overall Guidance**:
- **Punishment is not universally efficiency-improving** in public goods games. While it often increases contribution rates, its effect on group efficiency (payoff relative to the optimum) depends crucially on design features beyond simply enabling punishment and control efficiency.

**For Downstream Prediction Using Game Design Dimensions and Control Efficiency:**

- **Direct prediction should consider**:
    - **Punishment Cost/Effectiveness** (`punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`): Higher costs and/or lower impact generally reduce net efficiency or even make it negative. Efficiency gain from punishment is possible only if the punishment efficacy is high relative to its cost (Roberts, 2013).
    - **Group Size (`player_count`)**: Smaller groups may more easily achieve positive efficiency effects via punishment, especially if punishment is anonymous; in larger groups, punishment tends to be more effective and efficient if its legitimacy and targeting are enhanced (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017).
    - **Number of Rounds (`num_rounds`)**: Longer games increase the likelihood that punishment's deterrent effect will outweigh its immediate costs (Eldakar et al., 2013).
    - **Communication (`chat`)**: Enabling pre-play chat or punishment-stage messaging amplifies the positive efficiency effect of punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Dannenberg, 2016).
    - **Contribution Modality (`all_or_nothing`, `default_contrib`)**: Binary choice or opt-out defaults may moderate the baseline efficiency, but direct effects via punishment are not yet well quantified.
    - **Baseline Efficiency**: If the baseline (no punishment) efficiency is moderate to high, the room for efficiency gain through punishment is limited, and efficiency may even decrease due to added punishment costs (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Javaid & Falk, 2015).
    - **Reward/Combo Mechanisms (`reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`)**: Availability of reward, especially in combination with punishment, generally increases efficiency above reward-only or punishment-only regimes (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Page et al., 2013).
    - **Information and Targeting (`show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries`)**: Public feedback and revealed identity increase both the legitimacy and effectiveness of punishment, especially in larger groups (Zheng & Nie, 2013).
    - **Production Technology (`mpcr`)**: In standard linear designs, the effect is sensitive but not monotonic; in threshold or complementary production functions, punishment more reliably increases efficiency (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Ye et al., 2016).

- **For features that are absent, unreported, or only contextually discussed** (e.g., `show_n_rounds`, idiosyncratic institutional features), treat prediction as uncertain and apply findings from similar studies cautiously.

- **Use quantitative evidence from control and punishment treatments, when available**. In high-relevance papers (e.g., Zheng & Nie, 2013; Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Page et al., 2013), explicit group efficiency data for both control and treatment allow direct parameterization.

**Summary Rule**:  
- *If the punishment regime is well-targeted, cost-effective, and the baseline efficiency is low, enabling punishment is more likely to increase group efficiency.*
- *If punishment is costly, misapplied (e.g., anti-social), or baseline efficiency is high due to strong norms, enabling punishment may have no effect or reduce efficiency.*
- *Design dimension interactions (e.g., size × monitoring, institution × matching/procedure) can flip the sign and magnitude of punishment's impact.*

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly and Densely Informed:**
- `player_count` (group size), `num_rounds` (game length), `mpcr` (incentive), `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (effectiveness), and `chat` (communication) receive substantial direct attention in both lab and theory studies.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` are well covered, at least for the question of punishment vs. reward vs. mixed modalities.
- `all_or_nothing` (binary vs. continuous contribution): some coverage, especially as moderators or in cross-design studies.
- `show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries`: covered as information feedback/identity features in several key papers (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Khadjavi et al., 2017).

**Indirectly or Occasionally Informed:**
- `default_contrib`: Framing is discussed in some studies; direct measurement of its moderating effect on efficiency is rare.
- `show_n_rounds`: Sometimes reported in experimental protocols, but very few studies systematically vary this as a treatment.
- Mechanism and institution-level design (`punishment_rights_acquisition`, endogenous institution formation): addressed in key papers (Ramalingam et al., 2016; Reif et al., 2017), but not common in every study.

**Sparse/Contextual/Missing:**
- Fine-grained features of network structure, institutional detail, or novel technologies (as opposed to standard peer punishment) are discussed in theory/simulation or adjacent studies, but are not fully parameterized in the most common lab experiments.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Contextual and Cultural Variability**: The efficiency effect of punishment can differ substantially across cultural or field settings (e.g., field CPR games vs. lab PGGs; Noussair & van Soest, 2014; Javaid & Falk, 2015).
- **Dependence on Non-Payoff Outcomes**: Many studies report only behavioral outcomes (contribution, cooperation rate) and do not distinguish (or do not report) efficiency or payoff, risking overextrapolation to welfare outcomes.
- **Conditionality of Results**: Effects of punishment are highly conditional on design parameters: misleading to extrapolate findings without matching key features such as punishment cost/efficacy, group size, information, communication, and baseline efficiency.
- **Sparse Data for Some Dimensions**: Not all 14 prediction dimensions are equally well covered; for dimensions such as defaults, round knowledge, and some information structures, direct quantitative effects on efficiency are less well established.
- **Lack of Generalizability for Some Mechanisms**: Some positive effects are shown for particular mechanisms (redistributive punishment, institutional design, or targeted selection of punishers), and may not generalize to all punishment regimes.
- **Over-Representation of Laboratory Contexts**: Despite increasing field and variant studies, lab-based linear PGGs (with undergraduate samples) comprise the empirical core; external validity for real-world public goods provision is still debated.
- **Ambiguity or Contradiction in Noisy/Heterogeneous/Complex Settings**: In the presence of noisy monitoring, heterogeneous incentives, or nonlinear payoffs, the efficiency effect of punishment is often null or negative, and evidence is more ambiguous (Robbett, 2016; Fischer et al., 2016; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).

---

**Summary**:  
The literature set provides strong, multi-study empirical and theoretical evidence directly supporting prediction of treatment efficiency in standard linear PGGs with punishment, and highlights multiple interacting design features that moderate this effect. For cases closely matching lab PGGs, quantitative effect sizes from control/treatment pairs are the gold standard. For innovative or contextually complex designs, prediction should reflect uncertainty, potential for null or negative effects, and be aware of important omitted variables and reporting gaps.
