# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set consists exclusively of theory papers (61 total) covering a broad range of models in public goods games (PGG), social dilemmas, and closely related environments. All are analytic or simulation-based (no empirical laboratory or field studies), with outcomes and mechanism arguments anchored in mathematical modeling rather than experimental observation.

Within this set, around one-third of the papers are highly focused on classic or variant public goods games with explicit punishment and payoff outcomes, while the rest analyze Prisoner's Dilemmas, trust games, common pool resources, and other social dilemmas with adjacent but not always directly mapped structures. Many papers offer formal results about cooperative equilibria, evolutionary stability, and efficiency bounds as a function of game parameters and design dimensions relevant to the prediction task. There is substantial diversity in the types of punishment analyzed (peer, pool, coordinated, reward, exclusion, second-order, anti-social, etc.), the game environments (well-mixed, structured, repeated, optional, with/without communication), and the specification of payoff functions and efficiency concepts.

Given the emphasis on theory, the evidence base is strong for deriving principled qualitative and mechanistic predictions, but direct empirical effect sizes and real-world variance are absent.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**  
- *Exact relevance:* ~9 papers are directly about (variants of) public goods games and explicitly model the standard or linear public goods structure (e.g., Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Hauser et al., 2014; Hwang, 2017; Zhang et al., 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).
- *Close relevance:* A further subset cover adjacent social dilemmas with group cooperation and similar free-rider problems (e.g., common-pool resource games, trust/team games), where mechanisms and results often translate but are not strictly standard PGGs.
- *Adjacent/weak relevance:* Many others examine related repeated/multiplayer games or networked dilemmas with transferable but structurally distinct features.

**Punishment or Sanctions:**  
- *Exact relevance:* Numerous papers explicitly analyze punishment (peer, pool, institution, sanction design, etc.), especially those focused on public goods games.
- *Close relevance:* Several model sanctioning, exclusion, or reward mechanisms as alternatives to punishment, and distinguish pro-social vs. anti-social punishment.
- *Adjacent/weak relevance:* Some discuss punishment conceptually or as a background mechanism but with analysis focused elsewhere.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**  
- *Exact relevance:* Several theory papers report group efficiency, welfare, or total payoff as an explicit primary outcome (notably the core PGG-theory papers).
- *Close/adjacent relevance:* Many others focus on outcome proxies (e.g., frequency of cooperation, prevalence of strategies) or conceptually link to payoff/efficiency but do not report it directly.
- *Weak/none:* A minority focus exclusively on behavioral or normative dynamics (contribution rates, punishment frequency) without connecting these to theoretical efficiency or group payoff.

Overall, a sizable core of the literature is exactly relevant to the prediction task, though much of the broader set provides only indirect or adjacent evidence.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**  
  *Direct group efficiency*: Ratio of actual group payoff to the full cooperation optimum (Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Hauser et al., 2014; Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Dercole et al., 2013; Buchholz et al., 2014).  
  *Group payoff / welfare / surplus*: Explicitly calculated in several model-based studies, usually as functions of equilibrium population composition and/or strategy mix (e.g., Zhang et al., 2013; Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).
  *Analytic efficiency thresholds*: Some models specify the parameter boundaries for reaching full cooperation and maximal efficiency, conditional on punishment technology or enforcement (e.g., Olcina & Calabuig, 2015).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
  *Cooperation/contribution rates*: Many models and simulations track frequencies of cooperation, punishment/investment behavior, or the probability of cooperative equilibrium, using these as proxies for efficiency (e.g., Chen & Perc, 2014; Hintze & Adami, 2015; Kaiping et al., 2016).
  *Strategy frequencies / evolutionary stability*: Outcomes are often strategy abundance or evolutionary stable states, used as indirect evidence for expected efficiency.
  *Norm compliance, exclusion, or punishment behavior*: Models sometimes focus on these as endpoints, only mapping loosely to payoff/efficiency.

Notably, some adjacent literature (e.g., reward, commitment devices, tagging, partner choice) reports outcomes relevant to comparison but not directly the group efficiency effect of enabling punishment versus control.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**(A) General mechanisms**  
- Punishment can increase efficiency in PGG-like environments if (a) it is efficient (high fine-to-cost ratio), (b) antisocial punishment is absent or suppressed, and (c) population structure, coordination, or monitoring allow for effective targeting of defectors (Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Hauser et al., 2014; Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Dercole et al., 2013; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015).
- The positive effect of punishment is much greater under structured populations or repeated interaction with sufficient monitoring/breadth of information (e.g., spatial models, public institution models, network knowledge: Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Larson, 2016; Camera & Gioffré, 2014).
- If anti-social punishment (punishing cooperators) is allowed, or if the enforcement mechanism is corrupt or honest enforcers are rare, the effect can be zero or negative—even with punishment enabled (Hauser et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2017; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).
- The absence of corruption/transparency and the visibility of punishment institutions are critical for sustaining high efficiency (Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2017).

**(B) Moderator dimensions**
- **Player count (group size):** Increasing group size can either reduce efficiency in the absence of punishment (Hilbe et al., 2015), or, if punishment is coordinated, make punishment more effective and increase treatment efficiency (Hwang, 2017; Dercole et al., 2013; Buchholz et al., 2014; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015).
- **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** Lower cost and higher fine (fine-to-cost ratio) enhance the efficiency effect of enabling punishment (Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Dercole et al., 2013; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015; Zhang & Hofbauer, 2016).
- **Punishment technology:** Pool (institutional) punishment is often more robust and effective for efficiency than peer punishment, especially when ant-social punishment is possible (Gao et al., 2015; Schoenmakers et al., 2014).
- **All-or-nothing vs. continuous action:** Most results robustly map to both, but parameter thresholds may shift.
- **Reward and combined treatments:** Adding explicit reward can either support efficiency or, if misallocated or excessive, undermine the effects of punishment (Antoci & Zarri, 2015; Shibayama, 2015).
- **Nonlinearity of benefit function (MPCR/production function):** Punishment is only necessary to reach high efficiency in linear PGGs; non-linear functions (e.g., sigmoid, step) can sustain high cooperation without it (Archetti & Scheuring, 2013).

**(C) Baseline control efficiency as moderator:** If the control (no-punishment) game exhibits already high efficiency (e.g., due to repeated interaction, effective direct reciprocity, easy monitoring), the marginal gain from enabling punishment is small or nil (Archetti & Scheuring, 2013; Han et al., 2017; Kurokawa & Ihara, 2017; Hilbe et al., 2015).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Strengths:**  
The theoretical literature provides robust qualitative and mechanistic support for predicting that, in standard (linear) PGGs, enabling (efficient, pro-social, non-corrupt) punishment increases average efficiency relative to control, especially when:
- The group is not too large *and/or* punishment is coordinated.
- Punishment cost is low relative to its effectiveness (high fine-to-cost).
- Anti-social punishment is prevented or unlikely.
- The enforcement institution is visible, honest, and accessible.

Explicit consideration of game design dimensions—player_count, mpcr, punishment_cost/magnitude/tech, information structure (visibility, monitoring), group structure, and public institution—allow theoretically informed adjustment of predicted effect sizes and thresholds.

The literature also highlights that prediction should *not* expect efficiency gains from punishment when:
- Anti-social punishment is present (Hauser et al., 2014).
- The punishment institution is corrupt or unidentifiable (Lee et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2017).
- Punishment is so costly or weak as to be ineffective (Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Zhang & Hofbauer, 2016).

When the control efficiency is high due to other mechanisms (e.g., repetition, strong direct reciprocity), the effect of enabling punishment diminishes.

**Limitations and caveats:**  
- Many papers only report contribution rates or strategy frequencies, not efficiency directly. Predictions must infer efficiency effects from these proxies, especially close to threshold dynamics.
- Theoretical models may not capture human behavioral errors, non-strategic play, or real-world variability (no empirical effect sizes).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed/explicitly modeled (theory with payoff/efficiency outcome):**
- `player_count` (group size): Robustly analyzed as a moderator; effect is context-dependent (Hwang, 2017; Hilbe et al., 2015; Dercole et al., 2013).
- `num_rounds` (repetition): Present in dynamic/repeated game models; structure matters for efficacy of punishment.
- `mpcr` (or public goods multiplier): Central to almost every model; higher mpcr generally makes efficiency via punishment easier to sustain (Schoenmakers et al., 2014; Dercole et al., 2013).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `punishment_exists`: Core to almost all relevant findings (effectiveness, cost, mode of punishment—peer vs. pool/institutional/coordinated).
- `reward_exists` and allied dimensions (in models with combined reward/punishment).
- `all_or_nothing`: Some discuss binary vs. continuous contributions—threshold effects and parameter boundaries sometimes differ (Hwang, 2017; Dercole et al., 2013; Archetti & Scheuring, 2013).
- `show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Less central, but a few papers model the effects of information visibility on the efficacy of punishment (Larson, 2016; Lee et al., 2015).

**Indirectly addressed/contextually discussed:**
- `chat`: Only discussed in the context of network or information structure models (Larson, 2016).
- `default_contrib`: Framing effects are not a focus; only a few models discuss initial contribution bias.
- Reward cost and technology: Informed but less central; a few models explicitly analyze combined mechanisms.
- `show_n_rounds`: Moderates knowledge of future play in repeated games; addressed by models investigating the effect of continuation probability (Jordan & Rand, 2017).

**Effectively missing or sparse:**
- Precise mapping of `chat`, `default_contrib`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, and to some extent `show_n_rounds` is often lacking empirical or even theoretical granularity for effect estimation in most papers.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavioral/empirical generalizability:** All evidence is from theory; actual human responses to punishment (emotional, strategic, or error-prone) might differ, so quantitative predictions based on this literature should be interpreted cautiously.
- **Efficiency vs. cooperation/contribution:** Many models use cooperation rates or strategy prevalence as a proxy for efficiency, but this can overstate the actual improvement in realized payoff—the cost of punishment itself sometimes reduces efficiency even if cooperation rises (Hauser et al., 2014; Dercole et al., 2013).
- **Antisocial punishment and corruption underrepresented in many models:** While anti-social punishment is a key negative moderator (Hauser et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2015), most classic PGG papers exclude or set aside this effect, potentially overstating the typical positive impact of punishment on efficiency.
- **Model scope and boundaries:** Some findings depend heavily on assumptions about monitoring, network structure, or institutional design that may not match all experimental PGGs.
- **Unmodeled dimensions:** Prize framing, communication (chat), and individual human differences are rarely modeled, leaving those prediction dimensions unsupported.
- **Nonlinear benefit functions:** Archetti & Scheuring (2013) make clear that strong efficiency gains require linear benefit functions; non-linear (threshold, sigmoid) cases often do not need punishment to sustain high efficiency.
- **Absence of effect size estimates:** No empirical effect size or variance information is available to calibrate quantitative predictions.

In summary, while the theoretical literature provides rich mechanistic predictions about punishment’s efficiency effects in PGG-like games linked to design dimensions, the absence of empirical data, the centrality of often-unmeasured moderators (anti-social punishment, corruption, monitoring structure), and the use of proxies (contribution rather than payoff) are key limitations for downstream quantitative prediction tasks.
