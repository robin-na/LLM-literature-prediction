# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base comprises a large and diverse set of 83 papers, with a heavy emphasis on empirical laboratory experiments using standard and variant public goods game (PGG) designs, as well as a substantial contingent of theoretical and simulation papers. Nearly all studies tagged as exact for the prediction task use experimental lab paradigms closely matching the canonical PGG (e.g., repeated or one-shot, continuous or discrete, n=4), often manipulating the presence or structure of punishment or sanctioning mechanisms.

A minority of papers employ common-pool resource, trust, or team production games as close analogs. Most outcome measures are behavioral (contribution, cooperation), but a substantial number directly report efficiency or group payoff, especially in the experimental PGG literature. Several theory/modeling papers provide explicit mappings from design parameters to efficiency, but not all include the full span of design dimensions needed for prediction. The evidence set is relatively broad in terms of game forms, participant pools, and punishment institutional structures, but less broad for contextual moderators like culture or long-run field settings.

Overall, the evidence base is rich and well-populated for lab PGGs with and without punishment, moderately populated for close variants, and sparser or less informative outside these environments or for non-payoff outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact:** The literature is strongest for exact PGGs (standard linear PGGs, interior-solution, n=4-6), with multiple direct empirical comparisons of efficiency/payoff outcomes with and without punishment (e.g., Kingsley, 2016; Gürerk, 2013; Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Qin & Wang, 2013; Kamei & Putterman, 2015).
- **close:** Studies using CPR games, trust games, snowdrift/dilemma games, and team production settings are adjacent but often retain key structural elements for relevance.
- **adjacent/weak/none:** Papers about individual trust, social network field studies, or non-game-theoretic settings provide only indirect or no relevance.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact:** Many studies directly manipulate the availability, cost, or structure of peer punishment as a treatment variable, providing exact relevance (e.g., Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015; Kamei & Putterman, 2015; Fu et al., 2017).
- **close:** Adjacent mechanisms include exclusion, reward, or institutionalized forms (judicial, reputation, group voting). Some studies test costless, non-monetary, or restricted punishment and examine communication or threats as alternatives.
- **weak/none:** Some papers discuss punishment normatively or in context without manipulating or modeling it.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact:** A sizable subset of studies report efficiency (group earnings relative to full cooperation) or very closely related measures (total payoff, surplus, welfare) as a primary outcome and provide direct pre/post-punishment comparison.
- **close/adjacent:** Many others focus on contributions or cooperation but do not directly report efficiency, requiring inference from behavioral data or lacking direct transferability (e.g., antisocial punishment studies).
- **weak/none:** Studies focusing exclusively on behavioral, neural, or psychological drivers without group payoff or efficiency measurement are less relevant.

**Summary:**  
Direct experimental evidence is abundant and highly relevant for exact and close matches to the prediction task, especially when focusing on repeated lab PGGs with controlled punishment manipulations and observable efficiency outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes (Relevant for Prediction):**
  - Efficiency (group payoff as a fraction of theoretical maximum): Directly measured in a substantial number of experimental PGG studies (e.g., Kingsley, 2016; Gürerk, 2013; Fu et al., 2017; Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Masclet et al., 2013).
  - Group earnings, welfare, surplus, total coins: These are used interchangeably or as close proxies for efficiency.
  - Some studies only report mean period earnings, which can be mapped to efficiency if design parameters are known.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Indirect or Not Relevant for Prediction):**
  - Contribution rate, cooperation rate, and frequency of full cooperation.
  - Frequency and target type of assigned punishment (pro-social vs. antisocial).
  - Punishment and reward decisions (who punishes/rewards whom, and when).
  - Norm compliance, perception, and framing effect measures.
  - Emotional, neural, or trait measures (anger, norm adherence, in-/out-group bias, Agreeableness).

**Explicit Distinction:**  
Many papers focus only on behavioral outcomes, which should not be directly equated to efficiency changes: higher contributions do not always translate to higher efficiency due to the possible offsetting cost of punishment (e.g., Kingsley, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015). Prediction efforts should rely primarily on studies reporting or allowing inference of actual group payoffs/efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## General Effect of Peer Punishment
- **Standard linear PGGs with homogeneous groups and well-structured punishment possibilities**: Enabling peer punishment robustly increases group contributions and often increases efficiency compared to the no-punishment baseline, particularly for moderate punishment cost/effectiveness ratios (Kingsley, 2016; Gürerk, 2013; Fu et al., 2017; Levine & Modica, 2016; Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017).
- **Magnitude of Effect**: The size of the efficiency boost depends on the balance between increased cooperation and the costliness of punishment—the effect can be positive, zero, or negative depending on parameter values.

## Important Moderators (from Design Dimensions)

- **Endowment/Wealth Heterogeneity**:  
  - **Homogeneous groups:** Peer punishment increases efficiency.
  - **Heterogeneous groups:** Effect is reduced or eliminated due to norm conflict; costs of punishment can outweigh contribution gains (Kingsley, 2016).

- **Punishment Network Structure:**  
  - **Complete/symmetric networks:** Support higher efficiency, as everyone can be punished and monitoring is even (Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
  - **Asymmetric/incomplete networks:** Efficiency gains are reduced or reversed, due to lack of monitoring, targeted retaliation, and anti-social punishment.
  - **Punishment capacity:** Simply increasing the ability to punish does not compensate for poor network coverage (Leibbrandt et al., 2015).

- **Punishment Cost and Technology:**  
  - **Moderate cost/effectiveness:** Support higher efficiency (Levine & Modica, 2016; Qin & Wang, 2013).
  - **High cost or low effectiveness:** Efficiency gains disappear or become negative due to wasted resources (Qin & Wang, 2013; Masclet et al., 2013; Leibbrandt et al., 2015).
  - **Punishment structure (probability/severity):** An inverse-U effect exists; extreme severity/low probability is less effective at increasing efficiency (Qin & Wang, 2013).

- **Information Structure and Identifiability:**  
  - **Full transparency** (punishment identity and history known): Allows effective counter-punishment of anti-social punishers and promotes pro-social discipline, increasing efficiency (Kamei & Putterman, 2015).
  - **Ego-centric or ambiguous feedback:** Encourages inefficient counter-punishment, can lower efficiency relative to punishment with transparency (Kamei & Putterman, 2015).

- **Communication and Threats:**  
  - **Structured communication or threat stages:** Combined with punishment, these can further increase efficiency, especially over repeated play (Masclet et al., 2013; Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013).
  - **Isolated communication or weak threats:** Effects are weaker; communication can substitute or complement punishment. The gain is often conditional on the threat or punishment being credible.

- **Subject Pool:**  
  - **Students vs. population:** Efficiency gains from punishment occur among student samples, but not general population participants, possibly due to differing use of anti-social punishment and sensitivity to norms (Bortolotti et al., 2015).

- **Centralized vs. Peer Punishment:**  
  - **Centralized punishment:** Robustly increases efficiency and is less prone to wasteful or anti-social punishment (Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017).
  - **Peer punishment:** Can generate anti-social punishment cycles, especially at high cost or low transparency.

- **History/Social Learning:**  
  - **Provision of history:** Facilitates higher acceptance and efficiency gains from punishment institutions (Gürerk, 2013).

## Counterexamples and Boundary Cases

- **Immaterial or costless social sanctions:** Often fail to raise efficiency (Peeters & Vorsatz, 2013; Krieg & Samek, 2017).
- **Non-linear or threshold production functions:** The effect of punishment/exclusion is strongly context-dependent (Croson et al., 2015).
- **Special forms of anti-social punishment:** In some contexts, antisocial punishment is prevalent and neutralizes efficiency gains (Bortolotti et al., 2015; Boosey & Isaac, 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given a game's design dimensions and observed control efficiency, **the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency is context-sensitive and depends strongly on several design factors** as documented in this literature:

**When to Expect a Positive Efficiency Effect:**
- Standard linear PGGs with homogeneous endowments, symmetric punishment networks, moderate punishment cost-effectiveness ratios, and full information about contributions and punishment identities (Kingsley, 2016; Fu et al., 2017; Levine & Modica, 2016).
- Addition of structured communication, history, or legal/normative framing can increase the efficiency gain (Gürerk, 2013; Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017; Masclet et al., 2013).

**When Efficiency Gains May Be Nullified or Reversed:**
- Heterogeneous endowments without transparency in punishment decisions (norm conflict), incomplete or asymmetric punishment networks, high punishment costs, low punishment effectiveness, or ambiguous information about punishment identity/behavior (Kingsley, 2016; Leibbrandt et al., 2015; Boosey & Isaac, 2016; Kamei & Putterman, 2015).
- Environments with prevalent anti-social punishment (Bortolotti et al., 2015).

**How to Use Control Efficiency for Prediction:**
- Control (no punishment) efficiency alone is **not a sufficient predictor**. Design dimensions must be considered jointly; with design features supporting effective, well-targeted, and not overly costly punishment, expect a significant efficiency boost proportional to the gap between control efficiency and full cooperation, but typically not reaching the social optimum.
- If the above moderators are adverse (heterogeneity, anti-social punishment sources, network asymmetry), enabling punishment may deliver little or even negative efficiency change, regardless of the control baseline.

**Important Notes:**
- Efficiency gains are generally largest early in repeated games and may diminish as punishment cycles become less effective or costly retaliation arises.
- Replacing peer punishment with centralized or automatic punishment alters the efficiency profile, usually in favor of higher efficiency due to fewer retaliation opportunities and lower anti-social punishment (Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017).
- Provision of reward or combined reward/punishment may further modulate efficiency gains, usually positively but with context dependency (Dugar, 2013).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Best Informed:**
- **player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech (effectiveness), all_or_nothing:** Direct empirical studies report results explicitly along these dimensions, allowing parameter-level prediction (Kingsley, 2016; Fu et al., 2017; Levine & Modica, 2016; Gürerk, 2013).
- **show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Several studies manipulate or fix observability of peer contributions and identities (Kamei & Putterman, 2015).
- **chat:** Emphasized as a moderator in several experiments (Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013; Janssen, 2015).
- **punishment_exists, reward_exists:** Nearly every relevant study manipulates or fixes this.

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**
- **default_contrib:** Framing is sometimes manipulated (Brekke et al., 2017), but rarely as a main dimension.
- **show_n_rounds:** Occasionally mentioned; more often fixed in experimental protocols.
- **reward_cost, reward_tech:** Less direct evidence; some studies test non-monetary reward, showing modest or absent efficiency effects compared to punishment (Dugar, 2013).
  
**Sparse or Missing:**
- **Structural features beyond symmetric fixed groups:** Dynamic networks, endogenous group formation, or real-world/field settings are much less tested.
- **Combined institutional features:** Few papers test layered interventions (e.g., communication *plus* punishment *plus* reward) in fully factorial designs.
- **Long-term effects, field sustainability, cultural context:** Some hints, but most evidence is from short-run lab environments with student subjects.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **External Validity:** Most evidence comes from lab experiments with small groups of students, short time frames, and artificial stakes. Extrapolation to field or policy settings requires caution, particularly regarding anti-social punishment or cultural moderators.
- **Efficiency Reporting:** Some studies measure only contributions/behavior, not actual efficiency or group payoff, requiring inference or limiting direct prediction utility.
- **Parameter Generality:** While many dimensions are addressed (player count, punishment cost, network structure, etc.), not all combinations are tested; important interactions could be missing.
- **Behavioral Resolution:** The literature is strongest for average group outcomes, with less insight into sub-group or individual-level heterogeneity in response to punishment.
- **Assumption of Rationality:** Some theory work assumes rational punishment and perfect monitoring, potentially overstating predicted efficiency gains or failing to capture anti-social punishment patterns.
- **Complexity and Realism of Punishment:** Real-world institutions may involve multi-level or collective enforcement, not just basic peer punishment tokens.
- **Sparse Data on Some Dimensions:** Key factors like chat, continuous vs. discrete contributions, and information about the number of rounds are less frequently varied.
- **Control Over Social Learning:** While history, legal framing, and social information are highlighted as important, most prediction tasks will be limited by the realism of these channels in practice.

**In summary:**  
The literature provides strong, nuanced, empirically grounded guidance for predicting the payoff effects of enabling punishment in PGG-like environments, particularly when the design dimensions align with tested settings. However, significant limitations remain regarding under-studied design features, long-term or field generalizability, and the precise mapping from behavioral interventions to efficiency under all combinations of parameters. For robust prediction, incorporate both control efficiency and the design dimensions most strongly supported by evidence—and expect context and interaction effects to drive variation in predicted efficiency gains.
