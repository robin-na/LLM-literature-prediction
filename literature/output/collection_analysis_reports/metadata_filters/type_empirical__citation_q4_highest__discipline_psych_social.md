# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is broad, encompassing 127 papers focused mainly on empirical, laboratory-based experiments, with some field, observational, and ethnographic studies. For the downstream prediction task—estimating how enabling peer punishment changes efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments—a substantial subset of papers provides *direct empirical evidence* with high design specificity. These include multi-round, multi-player public goods games (PGGs) that manipulate punishment and report explicit payoff/efficiency outcomes (e.g., Reuben & Riedl, 2009; Decker et al., 2003; Gächter et al., 2017; Fehr et al., 2002; Gintis et al., 2003; Masclet, 2003). 

The evidence base is strengthened by meta-analytic work and by studies examining key moderators (e.g., chat, group composition, severity of punishment, and game structure). However, the set is also broad in that it includes many studies adjacent in structure (dictator games, trust games, etc.) or focus (behavioral intentions, reputation, trust), many of which do *not* directly report efficiency or payoff-based outcomes. The inclusion of such studies adds theoretical and contextual nuance but also introduces heterogeneity and limitations for direct prediction tasks.

---

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) `pgg_or_variant`**
- **Exact relevance**: Multiple papers implement standard or close-variant linear PGGs, often with full reporting of key design dimensions (player count, rounds, MPCR, etc.).
- **Close/adjacent**: A subset uses resource dilemmas, step-level goods, or CPR-type games, which share strategic structure with PGGs but can differ in efficiency dynamics (e.g., Ostrom et al., 1992).
- **Weak/none**: Many papers, particularly in the observational or personality literature, are only contextually related to PGGs.

**b) `punishment_or_sanctions`**
- **Exact relevance**: Several core studies directly manipulate costly peer punishment or alternative punishment technologies (ostracism, exclusion) as experimental treatments.
- **Close/adjacent**: Some examine centralized or institutional punishment or compare reward and punishment.
- **Weak/none**: Many behavioral and personality studies discuss punishment or norm enforcement conceptually, but without manipulating these dimensions in game environments.

**c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`**
- **Exact relevance**: Fewer studies report efficiency or aggregate group payoff as defined for the prediction task. The most relevant directly compute efficiency as the ratio to the social optimum.
- **Close/adjacent**: Many studies focus on contribution and cooperation rates (behavioral), using them as proxies but not as final welfare outcomes. Some report total earnings, surplus, or similar payoff data.
- **Weak/none**: A substantial number focus solely on non-payoff outcomes: punishment frequency, trust, norm compliance, emotions, or reputation, with no connection to efficiency.

---

# 3) Outcomes Measured in the Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (directly relevant):**
  - *Efficiency* (aggregate group payoff relative to social optimum): Reuben & Riedl (2009), Masclet (2003), Gächter et al. (2017), Fehr et al. (2002), Gintis et al. (2003), Barclay (2004), Brown et al. (2004), Li & Xiao (2014):
    - These studies often directly compare group efficiency/payoff with and without punishment, sometimes across moderators.
  - *Total earnings/group payoff/welfare/surplus*: Alternate payoff-based outcomes used in a subset of other PGGs and adjacent game structures.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (adjacent/indirect):**
  - *Contribution/cooperation rates*: Most common. Many studies document increased contributions under punishment, but behavioral change does not always translate to higher net efficiency due to punishment costs.
  - *Punishment/reward frequency and allocation*: Used to document enforcement or norm dynamics, but not efficiency, e.g., Pfattheicher et al. (2017), Balliet & Van Lange (2013).
  - *Norm compliance, trust, prosocial attitudes, moral judgments, emotion*: Provide mechanism-focused insights but lack group payoff data.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant to Prediction

**A. Enabling Peer Punishment in Standard Linear PGGs:**
- *Empirical consensus*: Introducing peer punishment (with moderate cost-effectiveness ratios) in typical lab PGGs (3–5 players, 10–30 rounds, no chat, moderate MPCR) robustly increases efficiency, often dramatically, relative to a no-punishment baseline—*provided that* punishment is not excessively costly and does not trigger high rates of antisocial/emotional punishment (Gächter et al., 2017; Fehr et al., 2002; Gintis et al., 2003; Masclet, 2003; Barclay, 2004).
- *Magnitude*: Efficiency improvements can approach 40% or more over control (Fehr et al., 2002; Gintis et al., 2003), with most of the gain accruing in later rounds as punishment facilitates stable high contributions and punishment use declines.
- *Variability*: The efficiency improvement is less pronounced (or even negative) when:
  - Punishment is poorly targeted or applied emotionally (Decker et al., 2003; Reuben & Riedl, 2009).
  - Group heterogeneity or privilege reduces punishment's effectiveness (Reuben & Riedl, 2009).
  - Antisocial punishment is frequent, particularly in culturally or institutionally competitive contexts (Pleasant & Barclay, 2018; Goette et al., 2012).
  - The cost of punishment is high relative to its deterrent impact (Decker et al., 2003).

**B. Punishment Technology and Mechanism:**
- Both direct costly punishment and non-monetary exclusion/ostracism can increase efficiency (Masclet, 2003; Feinberg et al., 2014), sometimes even when exclusion has no direct cost.
- Centralized/institutional punishment yields mixed results; effectiveness depends on leader legitimacy, lack of corruption opportunities (Grossman & Baldassarri, 2012; Muthukrishna et al., 2017).

**C. Moderators and Design Factors:**
- *Player count*: Direct effects best documented for small groups (3–5); threat of exclusion is less effective as group size grows (Kerr et al., 2009).
- *Number of rounds*: Positive efficiency effects of punishment emerge or strengthen in longer games (Balliet & Van Lange, 2013; Gintis et al., 2003).
- *Group/ethnic composition*: Punishment efficiency can depend on homogeneity, social identity, and cultural context (Barclay, 2004).
- *Communication/chat*: When present, chat can often match or exceed the positive efficiency effect of punishment, sometimes making punishment redundant (Ostrom et al., 1992; Mak et al., 2015).

**D. Efficiency vs. Cooperation:**
- *Non-monotonic relationship*: Increased contributions under punishment do not *guarantee* increased efficiency—punishment costs can outweigh gains if punishment is used frequently or badly targeted (Decker et al., 2003; Ostrom et al., 1992).
- *Context dependency*: In baseline high-efficiency groups (e.g., from communication or high reciprocity), adding punishment may have little effect or even reduce efficiency (Lubell & Scholz, 2001).

**E. Control (Baseline) Efficiency:**
- High baseline efficiency reduces the marginal impact of punishment (since there is less room for improvement, and punishment costs may only subtract).
- Low baseline efficiency (e.g., due to free-riding decay over rounds) is where punishment's positive impact is most dramatic.

---

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**A. When Design and Baseline Match Lab PGGs with Proven Punishment Effects:**
- Expect a *substantial increase* in efficiency when peer punishment (cost/range similar to Fehr & Gächter) is introduced, especially in small groups, over multiple rounds, with continuous contributions, and moderate MPCR. If baseline efficiency is low (i.e., control game with pronounced cooperation decay), efficiency can approach the social optimum under punishment.

**B. Moderators to Adjust Expectation Up/Down:**
  - *High punishment cost, low impact, or poorly designed rules (e.g., collective or emotional punishment rules)*: Expect a smaller or even negative efficiency effect; costs may overwhelm the cooperative gains (Decker et al., 2003).
  - *Group heterogeneity or low norm salience*: Punishment is less effective (Reuben & Riedl, 2009).
  - *Presence of antisocial punishment or high competition*: May suppress efficiency gains (Pleasant & Barclay, 2018).
  - *Baseline efficiency already high (e.g., due to communication, reputation mechanisms, or high trust groups)*: Adding punishment yields minimal efficiency gain and may incur needless costs (Ostrom et al., 1992; Mak et al., 2015).

**C. Indirect Inference When Only Behavioral Outcomes Are Available:**
- Many studies show that increased cooperation *tends* to track with increased efficiency, but the relationship is softened by the (sometimes substantial) costs of punishment itself. If a study only reports increased contributions under punishment, the efficiency gain should be discounted unless it is explicitly shown that punishment use declines after establishing cooperation (as is typical in longer games with effective punishment deterrence).

**D. Generalizations Beyond Standard PGGs:**
- For larger groups or non-linear production functions, efficiency effects of punishment mechanisms are more ambiguous, and the empirical support from this evidence base is limited.
- When peer punishment is replaced with centralized or institutional punishment, or when opportunities for corruption/bribery exist, efficiency effects are context-dependent and may reverse (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).

---

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (i.e., impact per cost), `all_or_nothing` (contribution granularity): Well-reported in nearly all key empirical papers.
- `chat` (pre-play or inter-round communication): Directly tested in PGGs and step-level games.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Not the focus of most core studies, but sometimes tested as a comparison.

**Indirectly or Infrequently Informed**
- `default_contrib`: Contribution framing is generally opt-in; a minority examine opt-out or framing manipulations, but not systematically.
- `show_n_rounds` (round count information): Usually held constant; rarely analyzed as a treatment.
- `show_other_summaries` (feedback about others): Almost always present in standard PGGs, but its independent effect is seldom isolated.
- `show_punishment_id` (punisher identities): Sometimes varied, with anonymity generally default; its effects are occasionally explored, mainly in behavioral outcome studies.

**Effectively Missing**
- Some nuanced or operational details (e.g., precise framing of choices, cognitive load, identities shown only in certain rounds) are discussed for their effect on behavior, but robust efficiency outcomes for these manipulations are sparse.

---

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency Reporting Scarcity**: Although many studies examine PGGs with punishment, relatively few directly report efficiency or net group payoff as a percentage of the social optimum—most focus on contributions or behavioral measures, which can mislead if punishment costs are high.
- **Contextual and Mechanism Ambiguity**: Some studies document *when* and *how* punishment is used, but not *how much* it improves payoffs; others show that punishment increases cooperation, but the long-run efficiency gain may depend on declining punishment use over time (which only occurs in longer games).
- **External Validity**: The bulk of positive findings for punishment-driven efficiency come from small, homogeneous laboratory groups with strict controls. Results may not generalize to heterogeneous, larger groups, or real-world settings where punishers are corruptible or punishment is easily mis-targeted.
- **Complexity in Moderators**: The effect size and direction for punishment/efficiency depends on multiple interacting design dimensions. For instance, communication, group composition, and prior trust can all swamp or interact with punishment effects.
- **Mechanistic Uncertainty and Behavioral Spillovers**: Some evidence cautions that enabling punishment can undermine trust or prosociality when removed, or in some cases trigger strong antisocial punishment, reducing efficiency (Chen et al., 2009; Mulder et al., 2006; Pleasant & Barclay, 2018).
- **Gaps in Dimensions**: Many design features relevant to field or complex institutional settings (e.g., partial transparency, endogenous institution choice, multi-level sanctioning) are discussed in theory or contextually, but rarely studied with direct efficiency outcomes in controlled experimental setups.

---

### **Summary**

**The existing literature directly supports predictive modeling of the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in standard, small-group, multi-round PGGs, especially when control efficiency is low and punishment is moderate, observable, and well-targeted. Efficiency gains are not guaranteed and are contingent on design details such as punishment cost/impact ratio, group composition, and the presence of communication or trust. Extrapolating to larger, more complex, or field-like settings is risky, as empirical evidence is sparse or conflicting. Many design dimensions are empirically grounded, but others are only indirectly or contextually informed, creating risk in out-of-sample prediction.**
