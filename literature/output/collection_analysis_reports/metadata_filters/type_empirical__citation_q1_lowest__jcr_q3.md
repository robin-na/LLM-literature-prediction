# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is broad but unevenly focused on the downstream prediction question: “Given the game design dimensions and efficiency in control, predict efficiency with peer punishment enabled.” Of the 46 papers, the majority are empirical lab experiments focused on public goods games (PGGs) or close variants, with a smaller presence of field experiments, observational studies, and adjacent social dilemma or norm-enforcement contexts. The dominant mode is between-group laboratory experiments with standard economic game manipulations.

Empirical evidence on efficiency or closely-related payoff outcomes is present but patchy. Several papers report direct experimental evidence on efficiency effects of punishment in PGGs; others provide efficiency measures only for control or non-punishment institutional variants; many focus on behavioral outcomes like contribution rates without payoff analysis. A handful address closely related environments (IPD, CPR, contest, team production games) with sufficiently close design and reporting to be informative. Few papers are purely theoretical or mechanism-analytic (most arguments are empirically grounded).

Across the spectrum, there is heterogeneity in the specificity and detail with which design dimensions (the 14 “prediction dimensions”) are addressed, with core PGG features (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech) most often covered.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**  
- **exact**: Many papers use standard lab PGGs and repeated variants, directly matching the prediction task context (e.g., Chen, 2022; Sparks et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2019; Molenmaker et al., 2019).
- **close**: Several examine CPRs, IPDs, related social dilemmas, or market-like settings where individual and collective interests diverge similarly (e.g., van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Dasgupta & Musolesi, 2025; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022).
- **adjacent/weak**: A subset deal with trust games, dictator games, or broader collective action (e.g., Büyükboyaci et al., 2025; Stiff, 2008), offering conceptual links but less direct transfer.

**punishment_or_sanctions**  
- **exact**: Punishment as peer, third party, or formal sanctioning is explicitly manipulated in several key experiments (Chen, 2022; Sparks et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2019; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Nikias & Sy, 2021; Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020).
- **close/adjacent**: Other forms include centralized punishment, ostracism, reward mechanisms as alternatives, or penalty “costs” not strictly equivalent to peer punishment.
- **none**: Many studies do not manipulate or even mention punishment/sanctions, focusing instead on social preference, baseline cooperation, or alternative institutions.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**  
- **exact/close**: A moderate number of papers report group earnings, welfare, or surplus as primary outcomes, sometimes giving direct efficiency measures (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Lippert & Tremewan, 2021; Faillo et al., 2020; Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013).
- **adjacent/none**: Many focus only on contribution, cooperation rates, compliance, norm adherence, or behavioral intent—relevant but not directly mapping to efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes (directly informing prediction):**
    - *Efficiency* (group payoff as ratio to full cooperation)
    - *Total group earnings / payoffs* (absolute or relative surpluses)
    - *Resource sustainability / profit* in dynamic and resource games
    - (Sometimes) *Total coins or welfare*, when mapped against a clear cooperative benchmark

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (potentially misleading for prediction):**
    - *Contribution rate / average contributions*
    - *Cooperation rate, compliance rate* (often increased by punishment even if payoff falls)
    - *Frequency and targeting of punishment*
    - *Norm compliance, honesty, participation, group identity, reputation behaviors*
    - *Willingness to punish, timing, or sanctioning preferences*

The literature sometimes infers likely payoff consequences from increased cooperation, but direct efficiency calculations are essential for trustworthy prediction.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical findings—efficiency effects of enabling punishment:**

- **Positive/More Effective Punishment**
    - Enabling **costly peer punishment** in repeated PGGs can increase efficiency, particularly in longer games, when costs are moderate and payoff structures are clear (Sparks et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2019, for third-party/combined; Lippert & Tremewan, 2021 for a review-veto mechanism).
    - **Centralized (top-down) punishment** can also increase group payoffs, especially when financial incentives are provided and enforcement is not purely reputational (Micheli et al., 2021; Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020).
    - **Sanctioning in CPRs** (both strict and graduated forms) tends to increase efficiency initially, with graduated punishment being more sustainable over time (van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).
    - The impact of *punishment cost* is critical: efficiency gains are greater when punishment cost is lower (Nikias & Sy, 2021), and higher-magnitude punishment can further improve efficiency if costs are not excessive (Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020).

- **Mixed or Negative/Null Effects**
    - In **heterogeneous MPCR PGGs** (different player payoffs), punishment does not reliably increase efficiency and can even reduce it due to antisocial punishment or counterproductive use (Chen, 2022).
    - **Antisocial or poorly-targeted punishment** can lower group earnings (Chen, 2022; and partly implied in cases with norm confusion or malfunctioning institutions).
    - Some real-world or field-like settings see *punishment crowding out cooperation*, leading to lower investment or resilience (Amirova et al., 2022), showing context-specific costs of punishment.

- **Reward, Ostracism, and Communication as Alternatives / Moderators**
    - **Rewards** can be more effective than punishment in increasing efficiency (Chen, 2022; Hou et al., 2019).
    - **Social approval mechanisms** (ratings, costless peer approval) can boost efficiency, but adding costs eliminates their effect (Faillo et al., 2020).
    - **Communication** and **coalition mechanisms** bring large efficiency gains, often bigger than punishment alone (Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Ball et al., 2025).

**Mechanism/theory arguments:**
- **Timing and perception** of punishment implementation matter: ex post punishment is more effective at sustaining cooperation; announced/personalized punishment can crowd out intrinsic motivation (Molenmaker et al., 2019; Klempt & Pull, 2018).
- **Endogenous, credible commitment** to punishment or review stages is more effective than unstructured voluntary punishment (McEvoy, 2012; Lippert & Tremewan, 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Directly Informed Settings:**  
  For standard PGGs with clear design details (e.g., fixed player count, known rounds, no chat, explicit peer punishment with manageable cost and effective tech), *enabling punishment* should generally be predicted to increase efficiency over control, especially in longer games and when the control efficiency is not already near the cooperative maximum (Sparks et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2019; Lippert & Tremewan, 2021).

- **Context-Specific Modifiers:**
    - **High baseline efficiency:** Marginal gains may be small when cooperation is already high in control.
    - **Heterogeneous payoffs (e.g., MPCRs):** Punishment may be ineffective or even reduce efficiency due to antisocial motives (Chen, 2022).
    - **Punishment Cost/Tech:** Lower punishment cost, higher impact per unit, and easier monitoring favor stronger positive effects (Nikias & Sy, 2021; Mitzkewitz & Neugebauer, 2020).
    - **Game Duration:** Long repeated interactions allow for recovery from initial punishment costs and sustained efficiency (Sparks et al., 2024; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).
    - **Alternative/enhanced mechanisms:** Adding reward, communication, or coalition/pledge mechanisms can boost efficiency further, sometimes outperforming punishment alone.
    - **Malfunctioning/unenforced punishment:** If institutions are announced but not enforced, or if perceived as distrustful/intrusive, punishment can reduce efficiency (Amirova et al., 2022; Klempt & Pull, 2018; Hakimov & Kajackaite, 2024).
    - **Endogenous institutions:** When groups endogenously adopt punishment, effects are more positive and sustainable (van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024).

- **Control Efficiency as Moderator:**  
    - **Low control efficiency** predicts greater gains from punishment; **high control efficiency** reduces marginal improvement, and punishment costs may offset any behavioral gains.

- **When Only Behavioral Outcomes Are Reported:**  
    - Increased contributions/cooperation usually (but not always) correlate with higher efficiency, but if punishment costs are excessive or poorly targeted, payoff gains do not follow.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed Dimensions:**
    - `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return), `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`
    - Frequently, design features such as *continuous vs. all-or-nothing* (`all_or_nothing`), *feedback structure* (`show_other_summaries`), and *framing* (`default_contrib`) are well-documented.
    - Mechanism staging, ex post/ex ante sanction timing, and interaction with chat/communication are well-covered in several studies.

- **Indirect/Contextual Coverage:**
    - `chat` (takes the form of communication, which is often a key moderator)
    - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (in studies comparing reward versus punishment effectiveness, e.g., Chen, 2022; Hou et al., 2019)
    - `show_n_rounds` and `show_punishment_id` are documented in some experiments, but less frequently linked to outcome analysis.

- **Sparse/Missing Dimensions:**
    - `show_punishment_id` (identifiability of punishers/rewarders) is rarely isolated as a treatment variable.
    - Detailed manipulation of defaults (`default_contrib`), and specific interface cues are uncommon.
    - Direct analysis of interaction effects between multiple dimensions (e.g., how punishment cost moderates the effect of communication or chat) is rare.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Partial Measurement of Efficiency Outcomes:**  
  A significant fraction of the literature measures only behavioral proxies (contribution/compliance) and not group efficiency or payoffs—potentially leading to overprediction of punishment benefits if efficiency costs are ignored.

- **Variation in Game Complexity and External Validity:**  
  Results are more robust for standard lab PGGs than for field, market, or asymmetric resource settings. Efficiency effects of punishment can reverse or disappear in heterogeneous or real-world participant samples (e.g., antisocial punishment, field settings with intrinsic motivation).

- **Limited Analysis of Extreme or Boundary Conditions:**  
  There is relatively little coverage of settings with very high or low punishment cost, rare player types (e.g., strong antisocials), or nonstandard grouping and repetition structures.

- **Unmeasured Moderators:**  
  Some key dimensions, including identifiability (`show_punishment_id`), nuanced aspects of feedback, and participant connection (e.g., chat) are not consistently manipulated or reported.

- **Ambiguity in Adjacent/Variant Environments:**  
  Findings in adjacent games (CPRs, contests, MARL/IPD environments) are relevant but their transfer to standard PGGs is imperfect, and efficiency definitions are not always commensurate.

- **Selection on Reporting:**  
  There may be publication bias toward reporting positive or significant punishment effects, or user attention may bias toward more dramatic treatment effects.

---

**In summary**, the empirical literature provides strong evidence that enabling costly, peer (or third-party) punishment typically increases group efficiency in standard repeated PGGs, *conditional on moderate punishment cost and effective targeting*. Effects are stronger in longer games with lower baseline efficiency. However, important exceptions and boundary conditions exist: in heterogeneous groups, field contexts, or when punishment is poorly structured, efficiency can stagnate or even fall. Many studied design dimensions are well-covered, but some remain sparse. Predictions should reflect direct efficiency data where available, treat behavioral outcomes with caution, and recognize context-dependence and reporting gaps.
