# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is large (145 papers) and primarily empirical, with a strong emphasis on laboratory experiments and field experiments. A minority of the papers are purely observational or theoretical. The evidence base is broad and rich for the core domains of public goods games (PGG) and their close variants, especially for designs involving peer or institutional punishment, and efficiency or related payoff outcomes.

The set includes a substantial number of exact-match PGG studies examining the treatment-control effect of enabling punishment, as well as a sizeable subset on common-pool resource (CPR) dilemmas and other social dilemmas adjacent to PGGs. The majority of these studies focus on real monetary outcomes—efficiency (group payoff relative to optimum), group earnings, or social welfare—though a significant portion report only behavioral outcomes such as contributions, norm adherence, or punishment frequency.

Most papers provide explicit game parameterizations and permit mapping game design dimensions to observed outcomes, making this evidence base well-suited—though not exhaustive—for the downstream prediction task described.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant relevance:**  
- *Exact relevance* is high; many papers are exact public goods games or direct variants (linear VCMs, standard PGGs).
- *Close/adjacent relevance* found in CPR games, trust games, and repeated social dilemmas that differ mainly in framing or resource structure but share incentive properties with PGGs (Ramalingam et al., 2019; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; De Geest & Stranlund, 2019).
- *Weak/none* for purely observational or psychology-based norm studies, or games where the core social dilemma differs deeply (ultimatum games, PDs, trust games).

**punishment_or_sanctions relevance:**  
- *Exact relevance* in PGGs with peer or institutional punishment (costly tokens or deduction points, exclusion, automatic fines, centralized or democratic punishment).
- *Close relevance* in studies of exclusion, threat-based or non-monetary sanctions, or games with monitoring/aggregation mechanisms serving a similar function.
- *Adjacent/weak* where reward, gossip, social approval, or non-monetary mechanisms substitute for punishment—a minority focus specifically on rewards without any punishment for direct comparison.
- *None* for many communication, sorting, or network studies that feature neither punishment nor direct institutional deterrents.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome relevance:**  
- *Exact/close relevance* is common, with group total payoff (as a ratio to social optimum/maximum possible) often the primary dependent variable.
- *Adjacent/weak* in studies reporting only behavioral outcomes (contributions, cooperation rates, frequency of punishment), which necessitate caution if inferring efficiency.
- Some papers analyze payoff-based spillovers (welfare, surplus) rather than direct efficiency ratios but can still strongly inform the prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**
- **Efficiency** (group payoff / maximum possible): frequently reported (Gürerk et al., 2018; Arechar et al., 2018; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Waichman, 2020).
- **Group payoff/earnings/social welfare:** Common, sometimes as absolute totals or normalized ratios (Engl et al., 2021; Angelovski et al., 2018; Bruhin et al., 2020).
- **Surplus/total coins generated:** Variants of above; relevant in both PGG and CPR contexts.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (distinct but sometimes associated):**
- **Contribution rate, mean contribution levels, norm compliance:** Highly reported but not equivalent to efficiency due to possible punishment cost or strategic misapplication (Mantilla et al., 2021; Drouvelis et al., 2021).
- **Punishment frequency, anti-social vs. pro-social punishment:** Used to explain heterogeneity in payoff outcomes but not direct measures of efficiency (Bruhin et al., 2020).
- **Communication, sorting, partner choice, belief elicitation:** Inform baseline behavior and conditional cooperation but not efficiency unless mapped to payoff data.

Many studies strongly distinguish between behavioral and payoff outcomes; increases in contributions do *not* always correspond to increased efficiency, especially where punishment is costly or misapplied.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Enabling punishment generally increases efficiency in standard PGGs with typical parameters (moderate group size, MPCR<1, no excessive anti-social punishment, observable contributions) compared to control (no-punishment) conditions** (Gürerk et al., 2018; Engl et al., 2021; Arechar et al., 2018; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019).
- **Magnitude and direction of effect depend on moderators:**
    - **Punishment cost-effectiveness**: High punishment cost or inefficient punishment technology can negate efficiency gains (De Geest & Kingsley, 2019; Angelovski et al., 2018; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).
    - **Anti-social punishment prevalence:** High rates of anti-social punishment or norm-keepers can reduce or reverse the efficiency effect (Bruhin et al., 2020; Vollan et al., 2019; Mantilla et al., 2021).
    - **Information structure:** If contributors’ endowments or actions are not observable, punishment can become misdirected and reduce efficiency (De Geest & Kingsley, 2019; Nicklisch et al., 2021).
    - **Group composition:** Ethnic/motivational/skill composition and the presence of information about it moderate the effectiveness and targeting of punishment (Mantilla et al., 2021; Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).
    - **Network structure:** Dense (fully connected or hierarchical) networks support efficiency gains from punishment; sparse or circular networks (where punishment is less targeted) often do not (Fatas et al., 2020; Kanitsar, 2021).
    - **Institutional context:** The way punishment institutions are implemented (democratic vs. exogenous), whether multiple channels exist, and if selection is endogenous can all moderate effects (Marcin et al., 2019; Dannenberg & Gallier, 2020).
    - **Emotional context:** Emotional states (e.g., induced anger or happiness) can flip the effect of punishment on efficiency (Lee & Min, 2021).
    - **Presence of communication:** Combining punishment with chat/communication or early covenants can yield much larger efficiency gains (Koch et al., 2021).
    - **Reward or alternative incentive options:** Sometimes rewards exceed punishment in raising efficiency, or the presence of both modifies the effect size (Gürerk et al., 2018; Chugunova et al., 2020).

- **Punishment is not universally efficiency-enhancing:**  
    - In some contexts, costly or misapplied punishment reduces total group payoff (Vollan et al., 2019; Drouvelis et al., 2021; Robbett, 2019).
    - Large group size with uncoordinated punishment can produce over-punishment and efficiency losses (Kamei, 2020).

- **Control (no-punishment) efficiency is a strong but not sufficient baseline:**  
    - In groups with already-high control efficiency, adding punishment may reduce efficiency due to unnecessary punishment costs (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).
    - In groups with low baseline efficiency (free-riding prevalent), punishment reliably increases efficiency if well-targeted and not anti-social (Arechar et al., 2018; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019).
    - In mixed or asymmetric groups, effects can be neutral or negative depending on the matching of punishment capacities, endowment, and ability to observe contributions (Waichman, 2020; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **When the game is a standard linear PGG with observable contributions and typical parameters (moderate group size, moderate MPCR, no chat, punishment cost:impact 1:3 or similar, and limited heterogeneity), enabling peer punishment should be predicted to increase average efficiency over the control game with punishment disabled.**  
- **Use control (no-punishment) efficiency as a baseline, but adjust predicted efficiency upward, factoring in the following:**
    - *Increase is moderate to substantial* if anti-social punishment is rare, monitoring is accurate and not too costly, and endowments/contributions are observable (Gürerk et al., 2018; Engl et al., 2021; Arechar et al., 2018).
    - *Increase is minimal, null, or negative* if anti-social punishment is common, punishment is misapplied (due to group composition, private endowments, or lack of information), or if the cost of punishment is very high relative to its impact (Bruhin et al., 2020; De Geest & Kingsley, 2019; Vollan et al., 2019).
    - *Effect can be negative* when group composition is unfavorable (heterogeneous groups with strong in-group favoritism), or when group size is large and uncoordinated punishment produces costly over-punishment (Mantilla et al., 2021; Kamei, 2020).
- **Do not extrapolate from contribution increases to efficiency gains unless payoff data also indicate reduced punishment costs; rising cooperation may be offset by costly enforcement.**
- **Use design dimensions (especially those that drive moderators listed above) as covariates or interaction terms in any predictive model: reward existence, information provision, communication, group composition, punishment institution type, and cost-effectiveness are particularly crucial.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed:**  
- `player_count` (essential: varying effects by group size, with some evidence of over-punishment at large N)
- `num_rounds` (longer repeated games show stronger sustained punishment/cooperation effects)
- `mpcr` (marginal per capita return, critical for baseline efficiency; effects interact with punishment use)
- `all_or_nothing` (binary vs. continuous affects norm formation and punishment effectiveness)
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech` (cost-to-impact ratios are central moderators)
- `reward_exists` (can crowd in or out punishment effectiveness, can directly increase efficiency)
- `chat` (communication/feedback combined with punishment dramatically impacts efficiency)
- `show_other_summaries` / `show_n_rounds` (information provision is pivotal for norm enforcement)
- `show_punishment_id` (affects anti-social punishment and retaliation)
- `default_contrib` (framing has second-order effects through expectations and defaults)

**Indirectly informed/contextually discussed:**  
- `punishment_magnitude` (often varied in studies as a function of cost but rarely isolated)
- `reward_cost` and `reward_tech` (in reward studies, typically not present with punishment)
- `show_punishment_id` (visible identity moderates anti-social vs. pro-social punishment)
- `show_other_summaries` (summary info about peer outcomes supports targeted enforcement)

**Effectively missing:**  
- Few studies systematically vary all 14 dimensions at once, so higher-order interactions are rarely identified.
- Some, like `default_contrib`, `show_n_rounds`, or very detailed information treatments (e.g., full vs. partial visibility), are under-explored except in targeted communication or information structure studies.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Efficiency is not always the main or reported outcome; many studies emphasize contributions or punishment frequency, requiring caution when using them to infer payoff-based effects.**
- **External validity is limited, especially for large groups, field settings, or environments with real-world social heterogeneity and persistent relationships.**
- **Significant heterogeneity across cultures, group compositions, emotional states, and institution implementation orders; effects observed in one context (e.g., homogeneous, student-majority groups) may not generalize.**
- **Interaction effects (e.g., how chat and punishment combine, or punishment and reward co-exist) are explored in only a few papers, often with conflicting or context-dependent results.**
- **Some design dimensions (e.g., endogenous vs. exogenous institution choice, non-observable endowments) are studied in isolation, limiting generalizability to broader design space.**
- **Gaps in systematic exploration of all 14 prediction dimensions simultaneously; most studies select a manageable subset, so fully interactive statistical models are underpowered.**
- **Studies with only behavioral (not payoff) outcomes should not be relied on for direct efficiency prediction.**
- **Ambiguities and disagreements remain when antisocial punishment is prevalent, or when efficiency gains are offset by increased punishment cost (Bruhin et al., 2020; Vollan et al., 2019; Drouvelis et al., 2021)—predictions in these domains should reflect uncertainty.**
