# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set contains 16 studies, including a mix of empirical laboratory and field experiments (notably Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Drouvelis et al., 2015; Noussair et al., 2015; Becchetti et al., 2015) and several theoretical or mechanism-focused works (e.g., Thöni, 2014; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Forges et al., 2016). Most empirical studies are laboratory-based and focus on controlled public goods or closely related games, but there is some coverage of field, hypothetical, and adjacent game-theoretic contexts (e.g., coordination games, common-pool resource games, and two-party dilemmas). The base is moderately broad on institutional mechanisms and behavioral drivers of punishment, yet relatively narrow in the number of studies providing direct, quantitative data on efficiency or group payoff outcomes when peer punishment is enabled versus disabled in public goods games.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:  
- Relevance is mostly **exact** or **close** for papers focusing on linear or all-or-nothing repeated public goods games or closely related common-pool resource contexts (e.g., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015; Becchetti et al., 2015).  
- A few papers are **adjacent** (coordination games, stag hunt, or repeated prisoner's dilemma) or **none/weak** if they focus on hypothetical or helping tasks.

**punishment_or_sanctions**:  
- Only a handful of papers directly manipulate or analyze peer punishment in public goods or adjacent games (**exact**: Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Thöni, 2014; Noussair et al., 2015).
- Many others either use no punishment mechanism (**none**) or examine *intentions*/attitudes without actual punishment mechanisms (**weak**/**adjacent**).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- Only a small subset provides **exact** group payoff or efficiency outcomes (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Becchetti et al., 2015).
- Frequently, studies measure only contributions, cooperation, or attitudinal proxies (**adjacent**/**weak**), not efficiency or net surplus (e.g., Drouvelis et al., 2015; Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014).

**Summary**:  
Only a minority of the set directly informs the specific prediction task of forecasting efficiency change from enabling peer punishment in PGGs. Most papers touch on adjacent concepts (e.g., cooperation rates, willingness to punish, social norms) without reporting group payoff or efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes** (**efficiency, total earnings, group payoff, surplus**):  
  Only a few studies provide these directly as the primary variable of interest (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Becchetti et al., 2015; Forges et al., 2016 [theoretical]); some report them as secondary or via inference from contribution levels.

- **Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes** (**contribution rate, punishment frequency, norm compliance, punitive sentiment, willingness to contribute or punish, attitudinal measures**):  
  The majority of studies use these as their main outcomes (e.g., Drouvelis et al., 2015; Roberts et al., 2013; Lopez, 2017; Gordon & Lea, 2016).

- **Other**:  
  Several studies analyze the structure/mechanisms underlying cooperation, punishment, signaling, or equilibrium selection without testing impact on group welfare or efficiency (Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Golman, 2016).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Peer punishment can increase efficiency, but only under specific design parameters**:  
  In standard repeated linear PGGs, enabling (single-stage, anonymous) peer punishment leads to higher group earnings/efficiency relative to control (no punishment), improvements that grow in later rounds as cooperation ratchets up (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

- **Structural details of the punishment mechanism are critical moderators**:  
  When punishment opportunities are unconstrained—allowing multiple stages, fixed player IDs, and full information—retaliation and ongoing feuds can emerge, greatly increasing punishment costs and negating efficiency gains, even when cooperation levels rise (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015). Thus, *how* punishment is implemented is as crucial as *whether* it exists.

- **Antisocial punishment and its undermining effect**:  
  Behavioral and theoretical evidence suggest that antisocial punishment—where cooperators are punished or punishment is not targeted at defectors—can erode or even reverse the efficiency benefits of punishment, especially in heterogeneous groups or when driven by motives unrelated to inequality reduction (Thöni, 2014).

- **No efficiency gain from punishment in specific field or CPR contexts**:  
  In more applied/field environments with PGG-like structure, both peer punishment and peer rewards sometimes fail to increase behavioral cooperation or its payoff proxies (Noussair et al., 2015), and adding only monitoring (without sanctions) can actually lower efficiency (Becchetti et al., 2015).

- **Mechanism arguments and theory**:  
  The success of punishment in raising efficiency depends on limits to retaliation, credibility, and the riskiness of cooperation equilibria (Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Forges et al., 2016). Punishment's ability to enforce (jointly) rational and efficient outcomes requires both low enough costs and the ability to credibly threaten defectors. 

- **Contextual and behavioral moderators**:  
  Papers examining communication, transparency, status, or social preferences underscore that the effect of punishment is likely sensitive to game framing, salience of the punishment option, information flow, and social norms; yet, these are only indirectly linked to efficiency outcomes.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Prediction should explicitly condition on details of the punishment mechanism**:  
  Efficiency gains from enabling peer punishment are seen primarily in designs with limited, anonymous, non-retaliatory punishment (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015). Changing to a more “rich,” multi-stage, or identity-transparent system can nullify or reverse efficiency gains, even controlling for baseline efficiency.

- **Efficiency gains are not guaranteed, and can be fully offset by punishment costs or antisocial use**:  
  Even when cooperation rates rise, if the structure allows for costly feuding or antisocial punishment, net efficiency can match or fall below the control (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Thöni, 2014).

- **Generalization to field or more “real” contexts should be cautious**:  
  In field-like CPR or framed naturalistic games, punishment may have much weaker or no impact on behavioral cooperation or efficiency (Noussair et al., 2015; Becchetti et al., 2015). Predictors based solely on lab PGG evidence may overestimate field effects.

- **Quantitative calibration of prediction is only possible in a narrow set of cases**:  
  Reliable extrapolation is feasible when the prediction case closely matches the lab scenarios with single, anonymous punishment options and no special information structures. Most design dimensions are only indirectly or contextually supported in the literature.

- **When only behavioral, not efficiency, data is available, caution is warranted**:  
  Increased contributions or cooperation rates do not necessarily imply higher efficiency, if punishment is costly (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015). Predictors using only behavioral outcomes may misestimate net payoff effects.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions** (Papers provide explicit manipulation or evidence):  
- `player_count` (most studies report, occasionally varied)  
- `num_rounds` (almost always specified; sometimes tested for effects)  
- `mpcr` (Marginal per-capita return, carefully defined in lab studies)  
- `all_or_nothing` (some studies use binary, others continuous contributions)  
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (varied structure/cost, especially well-covered in Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015)

**Indirectly Informed Dimensions** (Occasionally manipulated or discussed):  
- `show_n_rounds` (sometimes shown/hidden, e.g., Drouvelis et al., 2015)  
- `chat` (studies examine communication but mostly outside the key payoff mechanisms)  
- `show_other_summaries` and `show_punishment_id` (public feedback/manipulation effects examined, e.g., Becchetti et al., 2015)

**Contextually Discussed or Effectively Missing**:  
- `default_contrib` (contribution framing rarely varied/explicit)  
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (separately discussed in a few studies but not connected to punishment effect on efficiency in PGGs)  
- `punishment_magnitude` (often coupled with cost, but not always separately manipulated or measured)

Overall, most predictors are best supported by information about group size, round structure, marginal returns, and type/cost of punishment options. Dimensions related to rewards, communication, and information structures are less systematically studied.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse direct evidence on efficiency outcomes with peer punishment enabled/disabled**:  
  Few papers provide the needed comparison of efficiency with and without punishment across multiple design dimensions; most outcomes are behavioral or attitudinal.

- **Limited coverage and granularity of some prediction dimensions**:  
  Key variables (e.g., identity disclosure, default contribution, reward structures) are only weakly, indirectly, or not at all addressed.

- **Mode heterogeneity (lab vs. field)**:  
  Lab-based results may not generalize to field-like contexts, where institutional legitimacy, noise, or motivation differ (Noussair et al., 2015).

- **Punishment structure complexity and real-world analogs**:  
  Most evidence concerns simple, single-stage, anonymous punishment. Richer, more plausible real-world mechanisms are less studied and can yield sharply different outcomes (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

- **Ambiguity about moderators**:  
  Substantial evidence suggests moderators like social capital, transparency, antisocial motivation, and context matter, but these are rarely linked systematically to outcomes.

- **Behavioral proxy/efficiency disconnect**:  
  Many papers report only contribution or cooperation rates; these are not reliable stand-ins for net payoff or group efficiency when punishment is costly.

- **No clear consensus on all design dimensions**:  
  In cases where evidence is adjacent, theory-based, or behavioral only, predictions of efficiency effects require substantial extrapolation or remain ambiguous.

---

**In summary:** The literature most directly relevant for predicting efficiency change from enabling peer punishment in PGG-like environments is limited to a subset of empirical laboratory studies, predominantly those with standard, constrained punishment mechanisms. Design dimensions such as player count, round number, MPCR, and punishment structure are directly addressed, while many other dimensions are weakly or not at all discussed. Prediction of treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions should be alert to the sharply moderated and sometimes null or negative effects of punishment, especially as the punishment environment becomes "richer" or more realistic. Most of the literature discusses behavioral outcomes or mechanisms, not group efficiency, so extrapolation requires caution.
