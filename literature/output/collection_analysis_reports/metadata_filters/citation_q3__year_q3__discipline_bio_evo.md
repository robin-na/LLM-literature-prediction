# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set includes 31 sources skewed toward theoretical modeling, with a modest set of empirical (mainly lab experimental) papers. Many papers are theoretical, either modeling strategic decision-making in PGGs or conceptualizing cooperation, reputation, and punishment mechanisms. Empirical coverage of payoff-based outcomes in classical or variant PGG designs is narrow, with only a handful of papers directly reporting efficiency (or closely related group payoff metrics) and the actual effect of enabling punishment. The remainder largely examine adjacent domains, mechanism arguments, or non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contributions, norm adherence, punishment/reward behavior, gossip, etc.). Thus, for the specific prediction task—estimating average efficiency under peer punishment, contingent on design dimensions and control efficiency—the evidence base is relatively broad in conceptual mechanisms but narrow in directly comparable empirical or quantitative treatment–control efficiency pairs.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *exact* match: Multiple theory papers (e.g., Dong et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2018; Podder et al., 2021; Flores et al., 2021; Molleman et al., 2019) work with standard or variant PGGs. Several other sources are *close* (e.g., threshold/risky/optional PGGs) or only *adjacent* (other social dilemmas, Dictator/Trust/Prisoner’s Dilemma games).
  
**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *exact* match: Several theory papers and a handful of experiments manipulate or model punishment dimensions as a variable (Dong et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2018; Podder et al., 2021; Couto et al., 2020).  
- *close* or *adjacent*: Numerous papers analyze normative sanctioning, indirect/soft punishment, or reputation-based mechanisms, but do not manipulate peer punishment directly as a switchable PGG design parameter.
  
**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *exact* match: Few papers (Dong et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2018; Bhui et al., 2019; Hooper et al., 2021) directly model or report efficiency or group payoff as the central outcome.  
- *close* or *adjacent*: Several others report on group fitness, achievement, or welfare as a near proxy, but many only analyze contribution rates or norm compliance, not group efficiency.

**Summary:** Only a select subset of the paper set directly matches all three target-relevance dimensions (“exact,” “exact,” “exact”), substantially limiting direct, empirically grounded guidance for the prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes:**  
- *Efficiency, mean/total group payoff or welfare, surplus, group fitness, achievement:*  
  Explicitly reported or theoretically derived in a small number of PGG-focused models (Dong et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2018; Hooper et al., 2021; Bhui et al., 2019; Couto et al., 2020). A minority of experimental studies track group earnings (most focus instead on behavior).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
- *Contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency, norm compliance, reputation scores, gossip valence, individual giving, choice data:*  
  Widely reported in experimental and theory papers. Most studies analyze mechanisms of cooperation, psychosocial drivers (e.g., coordination, punishment motivation), or the stability and emergence of cooperative behavior over time or in different social-ecological contexts.

**Distinction:**  
The majority of the literature uses behavioral proxies, not direct compensation/efficiency outcomes, to gauge intervention effects. Explicit caution is warranted to avoid conflating cooperation rates with efficiency (as punishment can increase cooperation but decrease efficiency due to its cost, or vice versa).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Punishment and Efficiency—Direct Evidence:**  
- *Institutional punishment often reduces efficiency compared to institutional reward, especially with decision errors, unless the game system is nearly error-free and incentive sizes are finely tuned (Dong et al., 2019).*  
- *Probabilistic (not always-on) punishment can raise efficiency in continuous PGGs, even at high cost, if execution probability and fine size are moderate; always-on or high-cost punishment collapses efficiency (Jiao et al., 2020).*  
- *Hierarchical settings with corruption: punishment with corruption control can increase efficiency, particularly with asymmetrical punishment (exclusion of corrupt punishers) (Huang et al., 2018).*  
- *Graduated punishment (punishment increasing in severity and cost with number of defectors) is more effective for group achievement and welfare than strict/fixed punishment, especially in collective-risk PGGs with risk (Couto et al., 2020).*  
- *Optional PGGs: Punishment alone does not reliably raise efficiency; synergy between reputation and punishment (especially with strong reputation norms) is required for robust gains (Podder et al., 2021).*
- *Negative indirect reciprocity (exploiting those with bad reputations) can increase group efficiency by stabilizing cooperation, especially when exploitation is inefficient and error rates in reputational assignment are low (Bhui et al., 2019).*
- *Punishment can be efficiency-increasing when it functions as exclusion or shunning in repeated, high-gain social dilemmas (Hooper et al., 2021).*

**Punishment, Cooperation, and Social Context—Indirect or Contextual Evidence:**  
- *Punishment may have counterproductive effects in settings with antisocial punishment or where power asymmetries make retaliation likely (Phillips, 2018; Raihani & Power, 2021).*  
- *Behavioral and neural studies highlight diverse motivations for punishment (altruistic, antisocial, coordinated), but do not directly link these to efficiency (Molleman et al., 2019; Lo Gerfo et al., 2019).*  
- *Communication and reputation mechanisms can substitute for or complement formal sanctions in raising cooperation, but do not guarantee efficiency unless aligned with actual impact (Jolly & Chang, 2021; Roberts, 2020; Romano et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2021; Fonseca & Peters, 2021).*

**General Patterns:**  
- Effects of enabling punishment depend heavily on game parameters: punishment cost, execution probability, punishment magnitude, group size, presence of corruption, synergy with reputation, and the baseline efficiency.
- There is substantial context dependence; enabling punishment does not consistently increase efficiency across all PGG-like environments.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct Lessons for Predicting Treatment Efficiency:**
- **If punishment is always-on, costly, or not tightly calibrated:** Expect little or negative effect on efficiency compared to control (unless error rates are low and incentive sizes appropriate) (Dong et al., 2019).
- **Probabilistic and moderate-cost punishment:** Can improve efficiency, especially where costs are not negligible and punishment is not used indiscriminately (Jiao et al., 2020).
- **Corruption or antisocial punishment:** Undermine the efficiency benefit; effectiveness rises with corruption control and mechanisms promoting altruistic over antisocial punishment (Huang et al., 2018; Podder et al., 2021; Raihani & Power, 2021).
- **Graduated punishment schemes:** More likely to produce efficiency gains than fixed sanctions in threshold/risk PGGs (Couto et al., 2020).
- **Baseline efficiency (control):** High initial efficiency reduces the potential for gains from punishment, and in some contexts, punishment may even reduce efficiency (Dong et al., 2019).
- **Reputation mechanisms present:** Synergy between punishment and reputation is key for robust efficiency improvements in optional or voluntary PGGs. When only punishment is available, effects are unpredictable or negative (Podder et al., 2021).

**Control Efficiency as a Predictor:**
- If control game (punishment off) already achieves high efficiency, the marginal gain from enabling punishment is likely to be negligible or negative.
- If control efficiency is low and punishment is well-calibrated, probabilistic, or built into a graduated system, expect potential for efficiency improvement.

**Uncertainties and Moderators:**
- Ambiguity remains in spatially structured games, in settings with antisocial punishment, and in games with complex or poorly aligned social/power structures.
- Many papers highlight the importance of calibration—punishment cost, technical implementation, and its tie-in with group outcome visibility and reputation tracking.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**  
- *player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, all_or_nothing, punishment_cost, punishment_tech (and magnitude), reward_exists, corruption structure* — Several theory models and a few experiments directly manipulate or parameterize these, linking them informatively to efficiency or group payoff (Dong et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2018; Couto et al., 2020; Podder et al., 2021).

**Indirectly/Contextually Discussed:**  
- *chat, show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds:* Most commonly analyzed as moderators of behavioral outcomes or mechanisms for cooperation, not efficiency per se (Jolly & Chang, 2021; Gianotti et al., 2019).
- *reward_cost, reward_tech, reward_magnitude:* Addressed only in the context of studies with both reward and punishment options (Dong et al., 2019; Jiao et al., 2020; Burum et al., 2020), with little direct efficiency data.
- *default_contrib (framing):* Discussed in experiment framing but rarely as a central variable linked to efficiency under punishment.

**Sparse or Effectively Missing:**  
- *show_punishment_id:* Touched on in indirect mechanism papers (Molho & Wu, 2021) but not directly manipulated in efficiency-focused studies.
- *Combinatorial effects of dimensions (e.g., chat × punishment, reward × punishment):* Largely unaddressed quantitatively.
- *Most other second-order design details:* Not systematically examined across papers for their impact on efficiency outcomes.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical Gaps:** Few lab experiments directly report efficiency (or group payoff) treatment–control comparisons under explicit peer punishment. The majority of the literature is theoretical or reports only on behavioral proxies.
- **Limited Coverage of Game Designs:** While key parameters like group size, rounds, mpcr, and punishment cost are modeled, many relevant design features (chat, identity display, framing, reward/punishment combinations) lack empirical payoff-based coverage.
- **Context Dependence and Mechanism Complexity:** Many findings are conditional: the effect of punishment depends strongly on cost, probability, corruption/antisocial punishment risk, synergy with reputation, and social context. This limits generalizability and precise quantitative prediction.
- **Payoff-Behavior Distinction:** High cooperation rates under punishment do *not* guarantee higher efficiency, due to the direct costs of punishment itself—many papers highlight this, but much of the empirical literature still focuses on contribution rates rather than efficiency.
- **Ambiguity/Disagreement:** Some models and settings find that punishment reduces efficiency (e.g., with high costs or errors, or in absence of supportive reputation/norm structures), while others find positive effects (with calibrated, low-cost, or cooperative-aligned punishment). Results are not universally consistent.
- **Indirectness:** Many empirical or conceptual papers address adjacent mechanisms (reputation, gossip, institutional structure) without isolating the payoff consequences of enabling punishment as a treatment difference in a PGG.
- **Parameter Combinations Underexplored:** Very few studies rigorously map efficiency as a function of *all* relevant design features in the prediction schema; most vary only a subset.

**Conclusion:**  
For downstream prediction, this literature gives qualitative and some quantitative guidance linking core game design parameters (especially punishment cost, probability, and structure) to expected changes in efficiency, but leaves important empirical and dimensional gaps. The theoretical consensus is that punishment must be well-calibrated (probabilistic, not overly costly, and synergistic with reputation mechanisms) to increase group efficiency, and that baseline efficiency in the control is a strong moderator. Quantitative estimates are extrapolated mainly from theory, with limited direct empirical treatment–control efficiency comparisons, leaving substantial scope for uncertainty.
