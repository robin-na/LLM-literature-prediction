# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is composed entirely of theoretical work—no empirical or experimental data is reported in any entry. The base is relatively broad in terms of theory models and conceptual domains, but exact, quantitative, or game-design-dimension-level findings about efficiency under punishment are scarce. Theoretical coverage includes standard public goods games (PGG), related collective/commons dilemmas, and variants such as repeated games, spatially-structured interactions, and agent-based models. Most papers focus on mechanisms, moderators, and conceptual frameworks for cooperation, punishment, reputation, and norm enforcement; several use explicit payoff or efficiency measures as primary outcomes, while many focus mainly on behavioral outcomes such as cooperation or contribution rates. Overall, while the set is rich in mechanism and contextual theory, direct empirical evidence for the prediction task is missing.

# 2) Task Relevance

## a) PGG or Variant
- **exact**: Several papers develop their findings explicitly in the context of public goods games or analyze dynamics that are direct extensions (e.g., Bednar, 2006; Ye et al., 2011; Baker & Rachlin, 2002; Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
- **close/adjacent**: Many others use closely related games (common-pool resource, repeated prisoner's dilemma, agent-based cooperation, or coalition games), so structural mapping to PGGs is often reasonable but imperfect (e.g., Tarui et al., 2008; Noailly et al., 2009; Kosfeld, 2020; Glover & Kim, 2021).
- **weak/none**: Some papers use models so removed from PGG structure (e.g., network formation, dyadic trust games) that inferences are only tangentially relevant.

## b) Punishment or Sanctions
- **exact**: Many papers model peer, pool, or institutional punishment explicitly as an available mechanism, sometimes distinguishing between types or conditional structures (e.g., peer vs. group punishment).
- **close**: Others focus on reputation-based exclusion, third-party punishment, or analogous mechanisms such as costly partner switching or exclusion, conceptualized as punishment-like.
- **adjacent/weak**: Several papers treat only social/disapproval/indirect sanctions or omit explicit punishment, instead modeling norm enforcement or endogenous commitment.

## c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome
- **exact/close**: A number of papers use group payoff, welfare, earnings, or explicit "efficiency" ratios to compare with the full-cooperation benchmark (e.g., Ye et al., 2011; Bednar, 2006; Tarui et al., 2008; Kosfeld, 2020), directly addressing the prediction target.
- **adjacent/weak**: Many report only cooperation rates, norm compliance, or contribution frequencies, with only indirect discussion of payoff consequences.
- **none**: A fraction focus entirely on behavioral, cognitive, neural, or evolutionary mechanisms with no explicit efficiency or payoff outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

## a) Payoff-Related Outcomes (Relevant for Prediction)
- **Directly Measured:**
  - **Group efficiency**: Ratio of group payoff to full-cooperation payoff (Ye et al., 2011; Bednar, 2006)
  - **Aggregate/group payoff or welfare**: (Kosfeld, 2020; Tarui et al., 2008; Noailly et al., 2009)
  - **Explicit formulas or simulation values for group earnings, surplus, or total returns**: (Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Glover & Kim, 2021)
- **Used in Prediction Guidance:** Surplus, social welfare, group earnings tied to efficiency predictions.

## b) Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Caution for Prediction)
- **Contribution or cooperation rate**: Increased under various punishment regimes (Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008; Strang & Park, 2017)
- **Frequency of punishers, defectors, or norm compliance**: Tracked as outcome proxies but not always mapped to efficiency (dos Santos & Wedekind, 2015; Jaffe & Zaballa, 2010)
- **Norm salience, psychological mechanisms (e.g., compliance, internalization, trust, revenge)**: Commonly discussed but not payoff-based.

## Distinction:**
Many behavioral improvements in cooperation do **not** necessarily translate into higher efficiency, particularly when punishment is costly or when antisocial punishment is prevalent.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## a) Punishment Can Increase or Decrease Efficiency, Conditional on Game Structure
- **Conditional Positive Impact:** The effect of punishment on efficiency is not universally positive. In standard PGGs **without compensation for punishers** (no "sympathy" or reward), punishment often fails to yield persistent cooperation or high efficiency (Ye et al., 2011).
- **Structural Moderators:**
  - **Punishment Cost/Effectiveness:** Lower cost and/or higher fine-to-fee ratio (punishment effectiveness) increases likelihood of efficiency gains (Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008; Friedman & Singh, 2004).
  - **Marginal Per Capita Return (MPCR):** High MPCR amplifies the positive effect of punishment; low MPCR may result in net losses even as cooperation increases (Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
  - **Population/Group Structure:** The presence of population structure (multiple groups, intergroup competition) enhances the effectiveness of punishment for efficiency; without structure, punishment is ineffective (POLLOCK, 1988; Dubreuil, 2008).
  - **Monitoring & Information:** More informative or extensive monitoring (perfect or high information about individual actions) increases the efficiency gains from punishment (Bednar, 2006; Boyd & Mathew, 2015).
  - **Type of Punishment:** Co-operative or group-based punishment outperforms purely individual punishment in sustaining high contribution and (implied) higher efficiency (Jaffe & Zaballa, 2010).

## b) Limitations Due to Second-Order Free Riding & Antisocial Punishment
- **Second-order free rider problem**: Uncompensated punishers bear cost; punitive strategies may not be evolutionarily stable and can fail to sustain cooperation and efficiency unless a "reward for punishment" mechanism is present (Ye et al., 2011).
- **Antisocial punishment**: The presence of punishment directed at cooperators (antisocial punishment) can nullify or reverse gains in efficiency (Thöni, 2014; Raihani & Power, 2021).

## c) Boundary Conditions Where Punishment Reduces Efficiency
- **Costly, Ineffective, or Mistargeted Punishment:** High cost or retaliatory potential limits efficiency. If punishment costs outweigh increased cooperation benefits, net earnings can fall (Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008; Wu et al., 2022).
- **No Pre-Existing Cooperation:** Where control efficiency is already high, adding punishment often reduces net efficiency due to costs exceeding benefits (Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).

## d) Mechanisms & Moderators Highlighted
- Realized effects depend on **punishment design (cost, magnitude, timing, sharing)**, **group structure**, **information availability**, and synergy or conflict with **reward/compensation mechanisms**.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Effect of Enabling Punishment:** Enabling peer or institutional punishment will, on average, **increase efficiency** compared to a control without punishment **if**:
  - Punishment is not excessively costly,
  - It is sufficiently effective (reasonably high fine-to-fee ratio),
  - Group structure, monitoring, and population size are supportive,
  - There is compensation or reward for punishers (or some built-in incentive),
  - Antisocial punishment is limited/minimized.
- **Magnitude of Efficiency Gains:** Cannot be precisely predicted from this set—few explicit calibrations or panel-level crossovers are present; expected gains are greater in low-control-efficiency, low-MPCR contexts and with well-designed punishment.
- **Risk of Efficiency Losses:** When control efficiency is high or punishment is misapplied, there is a notable risk that punishment's costs exceed benefits, reducing overall efficiency despite higher compliance or contribution (Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
- **Moderator Dimensions For Prediction:** For downstream prediction, give careful weight to MPCR, punishment cost/magnitude, group structure, information/monitoring, and any existence of reward/compensation for punishers.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- `player_count`: Effects on efficiency are moderated by group/population size; larger groups often need institutional or group-based punishment to scale efficiency gains (Ye et al., 2011; Dubreuil, 2008).
- `num_rounds`: Repetition supports equilibrium cooperation but diminishing returns when monitoring is imperfect (Bednar, 2006).
- `mpcr`: Central moderator—higher MPCR increases likelihood that punishment supports efficiency (Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech` (magnitude, collective vs. individual): Well-discussed—low cost and effective punishment more likely to improve efficiency; group punishment outperforms peer punishment (Jaffe & Zaballa, 2010).
- `reward_exists`: Reward or compensation for punishers greatly influences evolutionary stability/effect on efficiency (Ye et al., 2011).
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Information visibility (monitoring, reputation) is repeatedly discussed as moderating efficiency gains from punishment.

**Indirectly/Contextually Discussed:**
- `all_or_nothing`: Cited in relation to binary vs. continuous contributions, but less often tied directly to efficiency outcomes.
- `default_contrib`: Framing effects are rarely addressed directly.
- `chat`, `show_n_rounds`: Occasionally referenced (e.g., chat as supportive; information on rounds as related to strategic planning), not systematically analyzed for efficiency impact.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Only discussed in select models that include both reward and punishment (Baker & Rachlin, 2002).
- `show_punishment_id`: Reputational effects and visibility sometimes linked to efficiency predictions (Redhead et al., 2021).

**Effectively Missing:**
- No systematic evidence about `default_contrib`, `chat`, or detailed framing variables.
- Very limited quantitative calibration for multi-dimensional prediction (e.g., tradeoffs among more than two or three parameters at once).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of Empirical Data:** The set is composed of theoretical work with no direct empirical calibrations or effect sizes.
- **Sparse Coverage of Certain Dimensions:** Several game design features are only contextually addressed or omitted, limiting multidimensional predictive precision.
- **Payoff vs. Behavioral Outcome Distinction:** Many findings conflate or indirectly extrapolate from cooperation/contribution rates to efficiency; direct payoff-based outcomes are less common.
- **Limited Quantitative Guidance:** The literature provides qualitative and comparative predictions but does not offer reliable quantitative estimates for expected efficiency under punishment across arbitrary combinations of dimensions.
- **Boundary Effects Understudied:** Few papers address "in-between" cases where MPCR, group size, punishment cost, and pre-existing cooperation levels all interact.
- **Reliance on Model Assumptions:** Many results are model contingent (e.g., assumptions about monitoring, error rates, group structure) and may not generalize across all public-goods-like environments.
- **Uncertainty and Disagreement:** Some disagreement over whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency in realistic or diverse design spaces (notably when antisocial punishment or high punishment cost is present).
- **Ambiguity in Mechanism Interpretation:** Underlying motivations (altruism, self-interest, revenge) and their impact on efficiency are often not resolved (Thöni, 2014; Clavien & Klein, 2010).

---

**Summary**:  
The literature, though entirely theoretical and often not parameterized for out-of-sample predictive use, gives strong support to the conditional claim that punishment mechanisms in public goods games can (but do not always) increase efficiency compared to controls without punishment. Effects are strongly moderated by MPCR, punishment cost/effectiveness, group structure, monitoring/information, and the presence of revenge or antisocial punishment. Several important game design parameters are discussed directly, but not all are covered or mapped quantitatively to efficiency outcomes. The key caution is that increases in cooperation or compliance do not always translate to higher efficiency, especially where punishment is costly or misdirected. Any downstream prediction should treat findings as qualitative or at best comparative, not quantitative.
