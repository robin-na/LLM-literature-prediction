# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is broad and diverse, containing both **empirical (primarily laboratory experiment)** and **theoretical** studies, as well as a smaller number of field experiments and observational/ethnographic reports. It is heavily weighted toward experimental economics research on public goods games (PGGs) and close variants but also includes adjacent games (e.g., trust, coordination, common-pool resource, and bargaining games), evolutionary models, and mechanism design theory. Several studies provide direct empirical results on efficiency or group payoff effects of enabling punishment, while others focus on mechanisms, moderator variables (e.g., network topology, metanorms, power distribution), or non-payoff-related outcomes. Relative to the specific task of predicting the change in **group efficiency** after enabling **peer punishment** in standard or near-standard PGGs using detailed game design parameters, the evidence base is:

- **Strongest for standard linear PGGs and some close variants** with clear, quantitative reports on efficiency effects from enabling punishment.
- **Moderate for studies of centralized punishment, institutional or third-party sanctions, and for theory models parameterized by PGG-like features** but with differing structural details.
- **Sparse or only contextually relevant** for adjacent games, field/ethnographic studies, or research focusing solely on behavioral (contribution, cooperation) outcomes.

Very few papers are wholly theoretical without mapping to empirical or parameterized predictions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**
- **Exact Relevance:** Most core experimental and theory papers are on the standard PGG or canonical variants with continuous or discrete (all-or-nothing) contributions (e.g., Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Carpenter et al., 2012; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021). Some theory also directly models PGGs (e.g., Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Carpenter & Matthews, 2010).
- **Close/Adjacent/Weak:** Many additional studies use adjacent games (Prisoner's Dilemma, trust, minimum effort, CPR, etc.), which often share critical features with PGGs but have differences limiting direct comparability, especially where payoffs or feasible cooperation equilibria differ.
- **None:** A few observational or ethnographic studies do not model games at all.

**Punishment or Sanctions:**
- **Exact:** There is an abundance of direct manipulations: enabling/disabling peer or centralized punishment, varying punishment cost, effectiveness, anonymity, and network structure.
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some research explores institutional variants (formal vs. informal), third-party, metanorm-based, collective, or antisocial punishment, or mechanisms such as refusal to cooperate as de facto punishment.
- **Weak/None:** Papers focusing only on feedback, reputation, approval/disapproval, or indirect punishment are less relevant.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**
- **Exact:** Many experiments and theory explicitly report on **group efficiency**, **average payoff**, **total earnings**, or **welfare**—the core prediction target (e.g., Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Carpenter et al., 2012; Castillo et al., 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; theory in Botta et al., 2021).
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some measure only contributions or cooperation rates but infer likely efficiency impacts, or measure efficiency in adjacent but non-identical settings.
- **Weak/None:** Many papers are silent on payoff and focus primarily on behavioral, attitudinal, or norm-related outcomes.

**Summary:**  
Relevance is **substantial but not universal**. For the most standard PGGs with directly comparable punishment treatments, payoff-based efficiency outcomes are well represented. For more specialized design dimensions, network variants, or less canonical games, evidence becomes indirect or contextual.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Directly Relevant):**
- **Group efficiency**: Most central—group payoff/maximum possible if all fully cooperate (numerous, e.g., Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Carpenter et al., 2012).
- **Total group or average individual payoff/earnings/welfare/surplus.**
- **Empirical and theory studies frequently contrast treatment (punishment-enabled) and control (punishment-disabled) group payoffs.**

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Relevant but Distinct):**
- **Contribution rate**
- **Cooperation rate**
- **Norm compliance**
- **Punishment frequency/volume**
- **Acceptance, trust, and similar social attitudes**
- Many lab and field studies measure these primarily; some infer likely payoff effects, but such inferences are distinct from direct efficiency outcomes.

**Moderation Outcomes:**
- **Group variance/heterogeneity**
- **Who punishes/gets punished**
- **Stability/coordination of high-cooperation equilibria**

## Explicit Notes:
- Many papers report increases in cooperation from punishment, but **this does not guarantee increased efficiency**: punishment costs can, and often do, erode or even overturn the efficiency gains from increased cooperation.
- Some studies only report behavioral proxies, making it **essential not to conflate contribution/cooperation increases with efficiency improvements**.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Patterns (Synthesized Across Papers):**

- **Punishment increases cooperation and contributions in almost all PGG-like environments.**
  - But the impact on efficiency is **much more variable** and heavily dependent on cost-benefit structure, punishment design, social context, and group composition.

### (a) Peer Punishment in Standard PGG
- **Costs of punishment often outweigh the gains from increased cooperation**—punishment increases contributions, but efficiency may decline (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Kanitsar, 2021; Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- **Efficiency gains from punishment are possible when**:
    - **Punishment is costless or has a high impact-to-cost ratio**
    - **Punishment is rarely used (i.e., high deterrence)** or applied primarily early, allowing cooperation to stabilize with less ongoing cost (Carpenter et al., 2012; Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
    - **Network structure enables efficient monitoring/punishing** (Carpenter et al., 2012); **sparse or directed networks** lead to inefficiency via excessive or misdirected punishment.
    - **Antisocial punishment is rare**; where it is prevalent, punishment can decrease efficiency (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Thöni, 2014).

### (b) Centralized/Institutional Punishment
- **Centralized punishment (by a manager or institution) is more consistently associated with efficiency gains** relative to peer punishment, even in environments where peer punishment is ineffective or counterproductive (Castillo et al., 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Ishikawa & Fontanari, 2025).
- **Legitimacy or democratic selection of the punisher/central authority does not further increase efficiency** (Castillo et al., 2021).

### (c) Moderators and Mechanisms Identified
- **Punishment's efficiency effect is sensitive to:**
    - **Network architecture/connectedness**: Complete or well-connected networks allow punishment to increase efficiency; sparse/directed networks do not (Carpenter et al., 2012).
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness**: Low cost/impact ratios can support efficiency gains; high-cost or easily abused (e.g., antisocial, retaliatory) punishment can be net harmful (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Kanitsar, 2021).
    - **Baseline group culture and type mix**: Groups with more strong reciprocators (those who both cooperate and punish free riders) achieve higher efficiency; groups with norm-keepers (punish both high and low contributors) achieve lower efficiency (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
    - **Possibility for retaliation and feuding**: When punishment allows retaliation or cycles (multiple stages, IDs fixed, full info), efficiency gains are erased (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - **Migration/endogenous group formation**: Efficiency gains from punishment are higher in open societies with migration (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022).
    - **Power dynamics/symmetry**: Asymmetric power (centralized in one or few) can lead to gains but may also create corruption or selfish punishment at a cost to efficiency (Eldakar et al., 2018).

### (d) Reward, Metanorms, and Combined Mechanisms
- **Reward can outperform punishment in increasing efficiency**, especially in institutional settings; combining moderate punishments and rewards is often most effective (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Peng, 2022; Kendal et al., 2006).

### (e) Theory—Parameter Sensitivity
- Efficiency gains from punishment are **conditional**; there are clear thresholds for punishment cost, impact, population composition, and baseline cooperation level (Carpenter & Matthews, 2010; Deng et al., 2012; Botta et al., 2021). Effective, credible, and efficient enforcement mechanisms increase expected efficiency, particularly in larger groups and with well-calibrated punishment (not too severe or too weak).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How Should This Literature Inform Prediction of Treatment Efficiency Given Control Efficiency and Design Dimensions?**

- **In standard linear PGG with typical peer punishment (costly, 1:3 impact, anonymous, no communication):**
    - **Enabling punishment is more likely to _reduce efficiency_ compared to the control** (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Kanitsar, 2021), unless the control is at very low efficiency (where gains from increased cooperation may potentially compensate for punishment costs, but this is rare).
    - **Magnitude of efficiency change depends on punishment parameters:** High cost or frequent use of punishment tends to negate benefits. If punishment is costless or used sparingly to deter, efficiency gains are possible.
- **Centralized or third-party punishment, sufficiently effective and well-calibrated, tends to _increase efficiency_** compared to peer punishment or control (Castillo et al., 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021), but only if costs do not overwhelm gains or corruption/selfish punishment is avoided.
- **Reward mechanisms or combinations of reward and punishment often outperform pure punishment in generating efficiency gains** (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Peng, 2022; Kendal et al., 2006).
- **Network and information structure** (who can punish whom, observability, anonymity) can flip the sign of the efficiency effect; fully informed, anonymous, non-retaliatory contexts favor efficiency gains (Carpenter et al., 2012; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Societal context, group composition, and norm prevalence** (strong reciprocators vs norm-keepers) are significant moderators—models should be calibrated for contextual factors if possible (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Antisocial punishment, if present, is a major threat to efficiency**—design or subject pools prone to this should adjust predicted efficiency losses upward (Thöni, 2014).
- **Control game efficiency is a crucial baseline**—treatment effect should be modeled as a function of lost or gained efficiency from enabling punishment, considering punishment costs and expected change in contribution.

**For practical prediction:**
- **If the control game has high efficiency (≥70%) without punishment, do not expect further efficiency gains**—punishment likely reduces efficiency unless costless.
- **If the control game is at low efficiency (<30%), punishment may improve efficiency only where costs are low and antisocial/retaliatory punishment is minimized.**
- **Intermediate control efficiency requires careful modeling** based on punishment parameters, network, information, and population characteristics.
- **Reward or metanorm institutions should be considered as alternatives to maximize efficiency.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count**: Parametric effects discussed in theory and experiments (e.g., Deng et al., 2012; Kocher & Matzat, 2016).
- **num_rounds**: Most lab experiments specify rounds and examine dynamics over time (e.g., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **chat**: Communication is manipulated or reported in several studies; frequently found to moderate punishment's effect on efficiency (Carpenter et al., 2012; Brosig et al., 2004).
- **all_or_nothing**: Contribution modality is often specified; discrete vs. continuous nature sometimes alters efficiency dynamics (Botta et al., 2021).
- **mpcr**: Marginal Per Capita Return is nearly always specified and is core to both theory and experimental results.
- **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech** (including cost/impact, structure): Frequently manipulated, central moderators of punishment effects (Kanitsar, 2021; Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **reward_exists/cost/tech**: Directly included in some studies (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Peng, 2022).
- **network structure (monitoring/punishment topology)**: Explored in detail (Carpenter et al., 2012; Kanitsar, 2021).
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Occasionally manipulated; some evidence on the transparency of information affecting efficiency/retaliation/coordination (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Thöni, 2014).

**Indirect or Contextual Evidence:**
- **default_contrib**: Contribution framing (opt-in/out) is occasionally discussed but not always directly manipulated.
- **Migration/endogenous group formation**: Explored in Cobo-Reyes et al. (2022).

**Dimensions Largely Missing or Weakly Informed:**
- **Fine-grained manipulation of show_other_summaries and show_punishment_id** is rare, most studies either fully anonymous or fully transparent.
- **Reward parameters and structures** often underreported when not the focus.
- **Behavioral economic framing (present bias/other biases)**: Crossed with prospect-theoretic reasoning in Uchida et al. (2024), but not commonly parameterized.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lab environments and subject pools may not generalize to real-world collectives, especially in the presence of unmeasured contextual moderators (e.g., cultural norms, baseline trust, heterogeneity, repeated exposure) (Noussair et al., 2015).**
- **Extensive heterogeneity in the design of punishment mechanisms**—even minor variations (e.g., cost, targeting, anonymity, possibility of retaliation) can drastically alter efficiency effects, complicating meta-analyses and prediction modeling.
- **Much of the evidence on 'punishment increases efficiency' is conditional; several high-quality experiments and reviews show that peer punishment, especially when costly and subject to misuse, can easily reduce efficiency, even as cooperation rises.**
- **Direct evidence on the impact of reward mechanisms or the joint deployment of reward and punishment is more limited and fragmented, yet evidence suggests they can outperform punishment alone in promoting efficiency.**
- **Not all 14 design dimensions are equally represented or manipulated in the literature; especially underrepresented are systematic manipulations of information transparency and nuanced reward/punishment technologies.**
- **Theory papers often assume stylized, idealized conditions** (e.g., infinite/incomplete information, rationality, homogeneity), limiting their direct transferability to empirically observed settings.
- **Antisocial punishment and power asymmetries are not universally measured or controlled**, but have been shown to substantially moderate net efficiency outcomes.
- **Contribution or cooperation rates, while often reported, are not reliable proxies for efficiency, especially where punishment is frequent or costly.**
- **Potential for publication bias or overemphasis on parametric lab findings**—negative or null efficiency effects may be underreported.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature base provides nuanced and often conflicting evidence on the efficiency effects of enabling peer punishment in PGGs. Efficiency gains are not automatic; they depend critically on costs, the structure and observability of punishment, group composition, and the broader institutional or network context. The predictive model should be specified to these conditions, and strong caution is warranted in extrapolation to settings with untested parameter combinations or to environments far from the standard laboratory PGG paradigm.
