# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set is broad in theoretical scope and offers extensive empirical and theoretical coverage relevant to public goods games (PGGs), punishment/sanctioning, and efficiency or payoff-based outcomes. Emphasis falls on laboratory experiments and simulation studies examining linear PGGs, repeated games, pool and peer punishment, second-order punishment, and various institutional designs. Multiple studies provide direct, round-by-round efficiency and payoff results for standard lab PGGs with and without peer punishment. There is a strong mix of empirical (lab and field), simulation, and analytical theory papers. The literature also includes adjacent work in Prisoner’s Dilemma (PD), Ultimatum, Trust, and Snowdrift games, as well as resource allocation and networked variants—these offer mechanism insight but often lack direct efficiency outcome data for the prediction task.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact:** The core of the literature addresses standard linear PGGs or very close variants, covering a wide range of design features (e.g., player count, MPCR, rounds, punishment/reward, information, etc.).
- **Close:** Several key adjacent designs (threshold public goods, Snowdrift games, PDs with multi-person interaction, and networked/structured variants) provide mechanism-level insight where direct PGG efficiency outcomes aren't available.  
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Studies further afield (dyadic PDs, Dictator/Ultimatum/Trust games, resource sharing, or reputation-focused games) act primarily as mechanism or context background, with limited direct relevance.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact:** The majority of highly relevant papers experimentally enable or disable peer or institutional punishment (including second-order, collective, or hybrid punishment), providing clear contrasts between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions.
- **Close/Adjacent:** Related sanctioning mechanisms (ostracism, reward, partner choice, commitment, indirect punishment, or moral judgment) are well represented for mechanism comparison. Many are experimentally manipulated.  
- **Weak/None:** Studies in which punishment is not present or is only tangentially referenced serve as controls or provide information about baseline cooperation.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact:** Approximately half the studies report or compute group efficiency (total payoff as a fraction of social optimum) or directly related measures (welfare, group/total earnings, surplus, profit).  
- **Close/Adjacent:** A significant number report only cooperation/contribution rates, punishment/reward frequency, or norm compliance; some report "average payoff" without normalization, complicating direct efficiency mapping.  
- **None/Weak:** A few studies focus solely on behavioral or affective outcomes, not payoffs.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff/Efficiency:**  
  - Many experiments (e.g., Simpson et al., 2017; Andrighetto et al., 2013; Gächter et al., 2017; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017) report group or average individual earnings in control and punishment treatments, permitting direct computation of efficiency.  
  - Theoretical models often present stationary or mean payoffs under different sanctioning regimes (e.g., Hetzer & Sornette, 2013; Roberts, 2013).
- **Contribution/Cooperation Rates:**  
  - Common as primary or secondary outcomes. In linear PGGs, these generally track with efficiency but can be decoupled by the cost structure of punishment.
- **Punishment/Reward Frequency and Distribution:**  
  - Used to explain efficiency patterns, especially regarding antisocial or retaliatory punishment.
- **Norm Compliance, Trust, Reciprocity, and Behavioral/Motivational Outcomes:**  
  - Frequently measured, especially in studies on indirect sanctions, reputation, communication, or psychological framing, but not direct substitutes for efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Efficiency Effects of Peer Punishment:**
  - **Punishment increases contributions but does not always increase efficiency.** In standard linear PGGs with costly peer punishment, higher contributions are often offset by the material cost of punishing, resulting in no improvement—or sometimes a reduction—in group efficiency relative to control (Simpson et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016; Ozono et al., 2017; Barrett, 2016). 
  - **Effect depends on punishment cost-effectiveness.** When the punishment cost-to-impact ratio is favorable (low cost, high effect; e.g., 1:3), some studies report that efficiency can approach the social optimum (Gächter et al., 2017; Roberts, 2013; Gross et al., 2016). When cost is high or retaliation is common, efficiency can fall below control (Wu et al., 2016; Ezeigbo, 2017).
  - **Moral judgment and non-material sanctions can increase both cooperation and efficiency.** Costless signals (e.g., moral judgments or norm-signaling) achieve high efficiency by boosting contributions without the waste of material punishment (Simpson et al., 2017; Andrighetto et al., 2013).
  - **Communication and norm salience consistently interact with punishment:** Explicit norm communication or chat reduces the cost of sustaining cooperation, leading to higher efficiency when combined with punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017).
  - **Institutional design matters:** Voluntary centralization (e.g., power transfer to motivated punishers) can dramatically increase efficiency compared to standard peer punishment (Gross et al., 2016).
  - **Role of second-order punishment:** Enabling second-order punishment allows stable, high-efficiency regimes, especially when supported by appropriate institutional selection (Hilbe et al., 2014; Sasaki et al., 2015).
  - **Noise and antisocial punishment reduce efficiency:** When punishment is misdirected due to noisy signals (e.g., noisy monitoring) or when antisocial punishment is common, punishment can decrease group efficiency (van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Kubena et al., 2014).
  - **Corruption and leader exploitation can reverse efficiency gains.** In institutional punishment setups, if leaders can be bribed or use punishment power for extraction, stronger punishment may actually decrease efficiency, and anti-corruption interventions only help in some contexts (Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Abdallah et al., 2014).
  - **Competition/Rank-based payoffs alter punishment effects:** In competitive/tournament PGGs, punishment serves as a tool for rivalry and does not increase efficiency (Paál & Bereczkei, 2015).
  - **Pool (collective) punishment and resource abundance interact:** Pool punishment is more efficient if resources allow for credible second-order punishment and is aided by the addition of reward during the institutionalization phase (Sasaki et al., 2015).
  - **Efficiency gains are often context-dependent:** Variants like snowdrift games and maintenance vs. provision frames reveal that the effect of punishment depends on baseline efficiency and the inherent cooperation difficulty (Jiang et al., 2013; Gächter et al., 2017).
  - **Empirical and theoretical convergence:** Both lab experiments and evolutionary models generally agree that punishment can increase efficiency when targeted, cost-effective, and when retaliation and antisocial punishment are limited.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct prediction of treatment efficiency:**  
  - If the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low, and the punishment regime is *cost-effective* (low cost, high impact), enabling peer punishment is likely to increase average efficiency, sometimes close to the social optimum (Gächter et al., 2017; Roberts, 2013).
  - If the punishment is moderate or high-cost, and especially if punishment is subject to retaliation, antisocial application, or misdirected through noise, efficiency may not increase and may decrease relative to control (Simpson et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2016; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
  - The presence of norm-signaling (explicit message channels or communication about norms) typically enhances punishment's efficiency benefit by reducing the total cost needed to maintain cooperation (Andrighetto et al., 2013; Chaudhuri & Paichayontvijit, 2017).
  - Institutional mechanisms (leader selection, centralization) and design features (second-order punishment, voluntary power transfer, support-based punishment for leaders) are significant moderators. For games with voluntary centralization, higher efficiency is expected than with standard peer punishment (Gross et al., 2016; Hilbe et al., 2014).
  - In environments with *possibility of corruption* (including leader extraction or bribery), the effect of punishment becomes ambiguous or negative (Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Abdallah et al., 2014).
  - The baseline game structure moderates effects: when the game already sustains high efficiency without punishment, the marginal effect of adding costly punishment is likely to be zero or negative (Jiang et al., 2013).
  - In large groups or settings with high second-order free-riding, reward mechanisms can be a necessary precursor for effective pool punishment and efficiency gains (Sasaki et al., 2015).

- **Moderators to consider:**
  - Punishment cost and effectiveness (cost-to-impact ratio)
  - Player count (group size)
  - Round number and length (short games: less effect; long games: greater punishment impact)
  - Communication and feedback (norm signaling, chat visibility)
  - Targeting and information (noisy monitoring versus perfect knowledge)
  - The presence of rewards and the possibility for corruption or antisocial punishment
  - Whether punishment is peer-to-peer, institutional, or hybrid

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed dimensions**  
  - *player_count, num_rounds, mpcr*: Widely manipulated and reported. Impact of group size, repetition, and marginal return is well documented.
  - *punishment_cost, punishment_tech*: Carefully specified in most experimental and theoretical studies; crucial for predicting efficiency effects.
  - *reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech*: Included in many experiments for direct comparison or as hybrid interventions.
  - *chat, show_other_summaries, show_n_rounds*: Presence or absence, and degree of feedback, are often experimentally varied.
  - *all_or_nothing, default_contrib*: Examined in both continuous and binary contribution settings; less so for defaults.
  - *show_punishment_id*: Occasional manipulation or discussion, but less common as a primary dimension.

- **Indirectly informed/contextually discussed dimensions**  
  - *corruption/bribery, communication modes, institutional choice procedures*: Inferred as major moderators that interact with core dimensions.
  - *Second-order punishment*: Treated as a contextual feature or secondary manipulation in some studies.

- **Sparse/missing coverage**
  - *default_contrib*: Rarely manipulated or isolated as a key framing or default dimension outside of a few baseline behavior studies.
  - *show_punishment_id*: Discussed in a minority of studies and less often manipulated.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Ambiguity and context dependence remain:**  
  - Substantial variation in efficiency effects across experimental designs, with direct reversals possible under different punishment costs, information structures, or social contexts (e.g., presence of antisocial punishment, error-prone monitoring, corruption risk).
  - Several studies reporting "average payoff" or "group profit" do not normalize to efficiency as defined (ratio to full-cooperation maximum), introducing heterogeneity in reported effect sizes.
  - Many adjacent games (PD, Dictator, Trust, etc.) and mechanism studies report only contribution/cooperation rates, not efficiency, reducing their direct predictive value for the target outcome.
  - Game design features such as defaults, identifiability of punishers, and reward mechanisms are still underexplored as systematic moderators.
  - Large-scale, high-variance, or field environments are less covered than small-N, tightly controlled lab settings, limiting generalization to real-world policy contexts.
  - Direct head-to-head comparisons of multiple complex game design dimensions are rare; most studies manipulate only a subset of dimensions at a time.
  - The effects of punishment on long-run dynamics and welfare in environments with reputational, network, or evolutionary feedback are primarily theoretical, with sparse empirical confirmation.
  - Many behavioral outcome papers, while informative about mechanisms, cannot be simply mapped to efficiency unless the cost of punishment is also reported and factored in.

**Summary:**  
The literature offers robust, high-relevance evidence for direct prediction of average efficiency in PGGs with peer punishment enabled, conditional on careful specification of design features and control efficiency. Key moderators are punishment cost/effectiveness, norm communication, institutional structure, information quality (noise), and corruption potential. However, the efficiency impact of punishment is not universally positive—cost, targeting, and context all mediate the outcome—and prediction models must account for these interactions. Several important design dimensions (e.g., punishment visibility, defaults) are less well covered, and generalizability beyond lab-like linear PGGs requires caution.
