# 1) Evidence Base

**Empirical/Experimental vs. Theory:**  
The paper set is heavily empirical, with a strong backbone of experimental laboratory studies directly manipulating punishment availability in public-goods or closely related games. There are also supporting theory and simulation/modeling papers, especially for parameter sweeps or environments less tractable in the lab. The set includes both classic linear PGGs and structurally adjacent environments (threshold games, common-pool resource dilemmas, n-player prisoner's dilemmas), as well as several theoretical frameworks mapping the institutional or evolutionary logic of punishment.

**Breadth vs. Narrowness:**  
This is a broad and deep evidence set for downstream prediction of *efficiency effects* of enabling punishment in public goods or closely related games. Many studies provide direct, controlled empirical estimates of group efficiency under matched control (no punishment) and punishment-enabled conditions, with clear manipulation of game design features (e.g., group size, punishment effectiveness, cost, information structure). Several theoretical works examine parameter sensitivity (e.g., spatial structure, magnitude of punishment, norm learning), and some studies offer field or agent-based evidence for more complex/realistic settings.

However, a substantial minority of studies—especially where outcomes are labeled as “adjacent” or “weak”—focus exclusively on behavioral measures (contributions, compliance, punishment assigned), or are outside the strict PGG paradigm (e.g., dictator/ultimatum games, trust games, field/observational ethnography). Very few papers report null results, and some highlight that the generalizability across specific institutional details or population composition is limited.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Many studies (e.g., Fehr et al., 2002; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Joseph et al., 2025; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Reuben & Riedl, 2009) use classic linear/public-goods game structures with standard parameters. Others use close variants (threshold, CPR, n-player PD, top-contributor/leader punishment).  
- **Close/adjacent:** Studies with threshold dynamics, resource extraction, or spatial structure are considered close. Some highly cited theory/model papers use adjacent structures (Ultimatum Game, bargaining, agent-based CPR).  
- **Weak/none:** Some studies use only the language or logic of PGGs but do not manipulate relevant dimensions or outcomes.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact:** A large subset manipulates the presence/absence of *costly* punishment, varying technology (peer vs. exogenous, targeted vs. random, centralized vs. decentralized, severity/impact, information/disclosure). Some studies cover both punishment and reward.
- **Close/adjacent:** Some focus on only non-monetary/social sanctions, conditional threats, or use of “exit” rather than classic punishment.
- **Weak/none:** A minority mention punishment only contextually or as a background variable.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact:** Multiple papers measure group efficiency or total group payoff, sometimes reporting directly the ratio to possible social optimum (e.g., Fehr et al., 2002; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Wang & Qin, 2015; Decker et al., 2003).
- **Close:** Several use group earnings, welfare, or surplus as proxies, or can be reverse engineered given provided data.
- **Adjacent:** Many report only contribution rates/cooperation/frequency, not efficiency or payoff.
- **Weak/none:** Studies focusing on motivation, attitudes, neural correlates, or only behavioral allocation/type classification.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related Outcomes (Efficiency, Group Earnings, Welfare):**  
- Direct measurement of *efficiency* or group payoff is available in a substantial number of core studies (e.g., Fehr et al., 2002; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Wang & Qin, 2015; Reuben & Riedl, 2009; Joseph et al., 2025).
- Some studies provide efficiency as a percentage of the cooperative optimum or report enough data to compute it.
- Efficiency is sometimes measured as *group surplus* (payoff net of punishment costs), and sometimes as provision rate (in threshold games).

**Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
- Many studies focus on *contribution rate, cooperation rate, punishment frequency*, assignment of punishment, norm enforcement, compliance, or social evaluation—these are **not** payoff-based, though they often inform about intermediate mechanisms.
- The largest set of “adjacent”/“weak” papers covers only behavioral effects, sanctions motivation, or neural/emotional correlates, with no actual payoff outcomes.

**Distinction:**  
- A minority of studies risk conflating contribution with efficiency; in some punishment conditions, high cooperation may be offset by punishment costs (reducing efficiency even as contributions rise).
- Some theoretical works use agent-based or evolutionary models with aggregate payoffs, informing about efficiency in aggregate but not always linearly mapping to the lab PGG context.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Core (Exact PGG studies with Efficiency Outcomes):**
- *Classic result:* Enabling peer (or exogenous) costly punishment in repeated linear PGGs usually increases group efficiency (Fehr et al., 2002; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Wang & Qin, 2015; Joseph et al., 2025; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Reuben & Riedl, 2009), especially when punishment is not too expensive and is used in a targeted, norm-enforcing way.
- *Magnitude of Gain:* In several studies, enabling punishment nearly doubles efficiency, moving the group average payoff from well below the social optimum to ~70-90% of it, depending on design dimensions (Fehr et al., 2002; Schlösser et al., 2018).
- *Costly Punishment Caveat:* In some cases, while cooperation rises, efficiency gains are offset or erased if punishment is used heavily, for retaliatory or antisocial purposes, or if the cost/impact ratio is poor (Botelho et al., 2022; Decker et al., 2003; Vollan et al., 2019).
- *Moderators—Punishment Cost and Technology:* Low-cost, high-impact punishment is most likely to yield efficiency gains; high punishment costs or low impact can result in net loss, as cost of punishment exceeds gain from cooperation (Decker et al., 2003; Vollan et al., 2019; Botelho et al., 2022).
- *Role of Feedback & Information:* Punishment increases efficiency only when recipients can associate it with their prior behavior; delayed or ambiguous punishment reduces effectiveness (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).
- *Selection of Punishers:* Democratic or performance-based assignment of punisher roles (e.g., top contributor) can increase both cooperation and efficiency versus random or decentralized assignment (Benard & Barclay, 2020; Grieco et al., 2017).
- *Institutional Contexts:* Centralized, exogenous, or random punishment schemes can sometimes increase contributions but may reduce efficiency if not carefully structured, especially if penalties are not reallocated or as effectiveness is diluted across group size (Fatas et al., 2010; Decker et al., 2003).
- *Threshold/Public Good Structure:* In threshold games, punishment can increase provision rates but may not maximize efficiency if risk of “all lost” is high (Joseph et al., 2025; Kol'veková et al., 2021).

**Key Points of Disagreement/Ambiguity:**
- *Antisocial and Retaliatory Punishment:* In real groups—especially with heterogeneity or “status” asymmetries—punishment can increase inequality or actually reduce efficiency (Vollan et al., 2019; Botelho et al., 2022). Antisocial punishment is a major moderator and can undermine positive effects.
- *Effect Not Monotonic:* Increasing punishment severity or frequency does not guarantee increasing efficiency; too much punishment can be damaging (Decker et al., 2003; Botelho et al., 2022; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).
- *Population/Spatial Structure:* Theoretical models suggest strong spatial/group structure is often needed for punishment to reliably yield higher efficiency (Noailly et al., 2009; POLLOCK, 1988).
- *History and Social Learning:* Social learning/history boosts the acceptance and efficiency of punishment-enabled games (Gürerk, 2013).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Overall:**  
- The literature strongly supports the use of *control efficiency* (no-punishment) as a baseline, with the prediction that—when punishment is effective, targeted, and not prohibitively costly—*enabling peer or exogenous costly punishment will increase average group efficiency* toward the cooperative optimum for a wide range of standard PGG designs (Fehr et al., 2002; Schlösser et al., 2018).  
- However, *the strength and even the direction of the effect* is **highly sensitive** to game design dimensions:
    - Punishment cost/impact ratio.
    - Mechanism structure (peer vs. centralized, random vs. targeted).
    - Feedback and identification of norm violators.
    - Social learning/history availability.
    - Presence of heterogeneity, status hierarchy, or group identity divisions.
    - Group size and number of rounds.
    - Information displayed (e.g., identities, histories, group payoffs).
    - Context of reward or alternative sanction mechanisms.

**Dimension-Level:**
- *If baseline (control) efficiency is very low,* the *potential gain from punishment is higher*, unless punishment is used excessively or for antisocial purposes.
- *If the punishment mechanism is costly (high cost for punisher, low impact),* net efficiency may actually decrease even as contributions rise (Botelho et al., 2022; Vollan et al., 2019; Decker et al., 2003).
- *Punishment is more effective in repeated games and when norm enforcement is visible and targeted*, but can be ineffective in large, heterogeneous, or randomly assigned groups.
- *Exogenous, automatic, and well-calibrated punishment* tends to yield more efficient cooperation than decentralized, retaliatory, or emotional punishment (Wang & Qin, 2015; Fatas et al., 2010).
- *Combination with reward mechanisms* sometimes increases both cooperation and efficiency, but path-dependence and mechanism structure are critical (Gürerk et al., 2009).

**Behavioral vs. Payoff:**  
- Many studies find that *punishment increases contribution* but not necessarily *payoff/efficiency*—it is critical to estimate or model the net effect, not simply rely on behavioral compliance or cooperation rates.

*Ambiguity is greatest* where punishment is costly, ambiguous, or likely to be used for non-norm-enforcing reasons (e.g., status, antisocial motives, intergroup tension). In these environments, treatment efficiency may rise, fall, or remain unchanged relative to control.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed**
- *player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech*: Core experimental and theory studies vary these explicitly and show their quantitative/qualitative impact on efficiency and the success of punishment.
- *all_or_nothing*: Covered; the distinction is sometimes important for mechanism effectiveness.
- *show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries*: A number of studies manipulate or report feedback and disclosure, showing these affect targeting, timing, and effectiveness of punishment.
- *chat*: Some studies vary chat, finding that communication and chat typically support higher cooperation and moderate responses to punishment (Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013); but most classic PGGs use no-chat baselines.
- *reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech*: Directly varied in several studies; comparisons with reward highlight that reward mechanisms can sometimes outperform punishment in efficiency effects, especially when punishment costs are high or antisocial punishment is prevalent.

**Indirectly/Contextually Informed**
- *default_contrib*: While few studies systematically test default framing, theory and design notes suggest default contributions may affect baseline and the responsiveness to punishment.
- *show_punishment_id*: Occasionally manipulated; evidence points toward anonymity supporting more antisocial punishment and reducing efficiency (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Sylwester et al., 2013).
- *punishment_magnitude*: Punishment effectiveness (cost/impact ratio) is often discussed as critical; magnitude is manipulated, but often linearly with cost.
- *show_other_summaries*: Feedback and outcome summaries are highlighted as important for linking punishment to norm violation and for social learning.

**Less Informed/Effectively Missing**
- Some subtle distinctions (e.g., *identity vs. anonymous punishment*, *detailed summaries vs. aggregate feedback*) are less commonly manipulated as primary treatments, though multiple studies highlight the importance of feedback structures.

**Missing**
- Studies rarely manipulate *default_contrib* (contribution framing), and very few empirically vary *show_punishment_id* or *reward_tech* in detail. The contextual discussion is present, but systematic evidence is sparse.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Reliance on Lab Paradigms:** Most direct evidence comes from tightly controlled laboratory studies. Field/ethnographic/agent-based support is patchier, and generalizability to large, complex societies or organizational settings may be limited.
- **Behavioral–Payoff Disconnect:** Many papers rely on behavioral (contribution, compliance) rather than payoff/efficiency outcomes; behavioral increases do not always translate to higher efficiency.
- **Ambiguity in Mixed/Intermediate Results:** Where paper findings are mixed, the net effect of punishment is highly sensitive to design, group composition, history effects, information structure, and antisocial behavior.
- **Sparse Evidence for Some Dimensions:** Several design variables (default contribution framing, detailed punishment/reward visibility) are under-explored in terms of their quantitative effect on efficiency, making extrapolation hazardous outside of core manipulated axes.
- **Population/Structure Dependence:** Theoretical support for positive punishment effects often hinges on population structure, group replication, or availability of social learning.
- **Context-Specificity of Moderators:** The impact of cultural context, leadership selection, status differences, heterogeneity, and feedback structures varies widely and sometimes reverses directional predictions.
- **Reporting and Publication Bias:** Most empirical studies report significant or positive results for punishment-enabled increases in efficiency, while negative, null, or adverse-outcome papers are less frequent. Some literature reviews note this as a trend.
- **Estimation Gaps for Control/Treatment Mapping:** Although multiple studies report both control and treatment efficiency, not all report sufficient detail to precisely estimate the expected increment, especially at intermediate parameter values or in threshold/step-level games.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature base provides robust, multi-dimensional, and often directly relevant evidence for the prediction task. Predictive power is strongest where punishment cost, effectiveness, targeting, and information structure are specified and manipulation is cleanly between control (no-punishment) and punishment-enabled games. Where design deviates from classic lab PGGs or where process mechanisms (antisocial punishment, retaliation, feedback ambiguity) intervene, the predicted efficiency effects of punishment are more ambiguous, non-monotonic, or in some settings negative. The required 14 design dimensions are variably informed, with the core axes (group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/technology) best supported. For dimensions like default contribution, punishment/reward information, or subtle variants (visibility, communication, identity), evidence is thinner and contextual. Explicit distinction between behavioral and payoff outcomes is critical, particularly for prediction. Limitations hinge on reliance on lab settings, incomplete mapping of all design elements, and context-dependent outcome heterogeneity.
