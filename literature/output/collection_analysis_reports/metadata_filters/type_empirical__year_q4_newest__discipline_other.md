# Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 7 studies, a mix of empirical research mostly using experimental methods (field and laboratory), as well as several observational studies and one literature review. Of these, four papers are empirical lab/field experiments (Amirova et al., 2022; Herne et al., 2023; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Wei et al., 2025), while the other three are observational or review studies (Gao et al., 2024; Hirama et al., 2022; Holubcik et al., 2023). The empirical papers tend to focus on variants of public goods or common pool resource games, with several including direct manipulation of punishment or analogous sanction mechanisms. However, the set is broad in topic and outcome measures, and not all directly address the effect of punishment on efficiency in canonical public goods games. The literature does not include meta-analyses or theory-only papers; most evidence is based on primary data from experiments or survey/field observations.

# Task Relevance

### pgg_or_variant  
- **exact:** None of the papers use a textbook public goods game; several use close variants (common pool resource games, energy market games, investment scenarios).
- **close:** Amirova et al. (2022), Herne et al. (2023) (asymmetric CPR game), Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022), Wei et al. (2025) (principal-agent; adjacent structure).
- **adjacent/weak:** Gao et al. (2024), Hirama et al. (2022), Holubcik et al. (2023).

### punishment_or_sanctions  
- **exact:** Amirova et al. (2022) (explicit penalty/punishment treatments).
- **close:** Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022) (carbon tax as punishment), Wei et al. (2025) (job rotation as punishment/threat).
- **adjacent or none:** Others either discuss punishment contextually (Gao et al., 2024; Hirama et al., 2022), or do not include it at all.

### efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome  
- **exact:** Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022), Wei et al. (2025), Herne et al. (2023) (explicit group efficiency/payoff).
- **close:** Amirova et al. (2022) (behavioral proxy: collective investment).
- **adjacent or none:** Remaining papers address only behavioral/normative outcomes, not efficiency or payoff in an experimental game.

# Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (group payoff, efficiency, welfare):** Explicit in Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022), Wei et al. (2025), and Herne et al. (2023). Amirova et al. (2022) uses collective investment as a behavioral proxy for group efficiency, but does not provide explicit efficiency ratios.
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, cooperation, norm compliance, attitudes):** Primary focus in Amirova et al. (2022), Herne et al. (2023), and especially all observational studies (Gao et al., 2024; Hirama et al., 2022; Holubcik et al., 2023), where only attitudes, behavior, or motivation are measured—no explicit payoff/efficiency data.

# Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment can reduce efficiency:** In a repeated field setting similar to a public-good, introducing punishment (penalties) reduced collective investment and likely decreased group efficiency compared to a baseline with no punishment. Communication increased investment and likelihood of high-equilibrium but did not guarantee efficiency (Amirova et al., 2022). The main measured outcome is a behavioral proxy for efficiency, not payoff per se.
- **Punishment can (sometimes) increase efficiency:** In laboratory competitive or principal-agent environments (not canonical PGG), introducing a punishment mechanism—whether a carbon tax (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022) or job rotation (Wei et al., 2025)—led to higher total group profit (efficiency) or firm earnings, particularly after punishment was salient/enforced. However, in Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022), the efficiency increase was not statistically significant.
- **Chat/Communication increases efficiency:** Both in CPR games (Herne et al., 2023; Amirova et al., 2022), unstructured or structured communication reliably increased contributions and efficiency, irrespective of punishment. There is no direct evidence about interactions between chat and punishment effects.
- **Non-directly informative papers confirm the importance of design dimensions:** Gao et al. (2024) notes that punishment, reward, and communication are central research themes, even though the paper provides no quantitative evidence on efficiency.
- **Mechanism/ambiguity:** Punishment may crowd out intrinsic motivation (Amirova et al., 2022), or serve as a salient incentive (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Wei et al., 2025), producing either negative or positive efficiency impacts.

# Prediction Guidance

- **Context matters:** The impact of enabling punishment on efficiency depends on the game context and population. In field-like environments with strong intrinsic motivation, punishment can reduce efficiency by crowding out voluntary cooperation (Amirova et al., 2022). In more competitive or principal-agent settings, punishment tends to increase efficiency by deterring shirking or incentivizing optimal choices (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Wei et al., 2025).
- **No clear universal effect:** Results conflict: both positive and negative efficiency effects occur depending on context. There is no support for a simple, context-invariant prediction rule.
- **Dimension interactions:** Chat tends to increase efficiency in all cases with or without punishment, but evidence for chat-punishment interaction is limited.
- **Punishment effectiveness may depend on salience and implementation:** The evidence suggests that punishment must be actively implemented, not just announced, to impact outcomes (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022). The structure (exogenous vs. endogenous), cost, and threat credibility of punishment affect outcomes (Wei et al., 2025).
- **Payoff evidence is limited:** Only a subset of studies report direct group payoff or efficiency changes; others use behavioral proxies or only discuss mechanisms.

# Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

## Directly Informed Dimensions
- **player_count, num_rounds:** Directly manipulated in most experimental designs.
- **chat:** Directly manipulated in Amirova et al. (2022), Herne et al. (2023), Wei et al. (2025).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Directly manipulated in Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022), Wei et al. (2025).
- **all_or_nothing:** Reported in Herne et al. (2023), Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022), Wei et al. (2025).
- **mpcr:** Explicitly discussed in Herne et al. (2023).
- **show_n_rounds:** Explicitly reported in Herne et al. (2023), Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022).
- **show_other_summaries:** Contextually mentioned but not a primary focus.
  
## Indirectly or Contextually Informed Dimensions
- **default_contrib:** Not directly manipulated; only possibly inferred.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Punishment focus predominates; reward is only discussed contextually (Gao et al., 2024).
- **show_punishment_id:** Not directly manipulated.

## Effectively Missing
- **reward dimensions, show_punishment_id:** Not measured or manipulated in this set.
- **detailed manipulation of mpcr, all_or_nothing:** Only present in some designs.

# Important Limitations

- **No canonical PGGs:** No study uses the exact public goods game; results are mostly based on close or adjacent variants. Generalizability to canonical PGG is uncertain.
- **Payoff evidence is sparse:** Only a subset of experiments report group efficiency or payoff directly; some use behavioral proxies.
- **Contradictory results:** Punishment decreases efficiency in some settings (Amirova et al., 2022) and increases (or does not significantly change) it in others (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Wei et al., 2025).
- **Dimension interaction unclear:** The interaction between chat and punishment, and structural variation in punishment implementation, is not systematically explored.
- **No quantitative or meta-analytic effect sizes:** No paper provides generalizable, quantitative effect estimates for use in prediction algorithms.
- **Context specificity:** Several findings may be driven by particular settings (field vs. lab, real-world constraints, asymmetries), limiting direct prediction for other designs.
- **Reward mechanisms unaddressed:** The prediction task includes reward-related dimensions, but these are not empirically studied in this set.
- **Missing outcome granularity:** Many outcomes are group-level or binary, not allowing finer-grained prediction across the parameter space.
