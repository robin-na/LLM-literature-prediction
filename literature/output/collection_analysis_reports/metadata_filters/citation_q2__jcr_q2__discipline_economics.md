# 1) Evidence Base

**Nature and Breadth:**  
This paper set is broad and diverse, encompassing 101 papers with a predominant focus on empirical, laboratory experimental studies in economics, alongside some theory and simulation-based investigations. The literature covers both standard public goods games (PGGs) and numerous close variants (e.g., common-pool resources, threshold games, asymmetric and networked games, and adjacent social dilemmas like prisoner's dilemma and trust games). There is an abundance of empirical data directly assessing the impact of peer or institutional punishment mechanisms in PGGs (either enabling/disabling or comparing different punishment designs).

**Empirical vs. Non-empirical:**  
The vast majority of included studies are **empirical lab experiments**, providing rich data about contributions, group earnings, and efficiency with and without punishment. There are also a few **theory/simulation** papers offering mechanism-level and evolutionary arguments regarding punishment's role in supporting cooperation and efficiency. While some studies directly quantify group efficiency or welfare, others focus more on behavioral outcomes (e.g., contributions, cooperation rates, norm compliance).

**Coverage for Prediction Task:**  
The evidence base is strong for informing the prediction of efficiency outcomes in PGGs when enabling punishment, especially for canonical game designs. Several studies provide direct, quantitative treatment-control efficiency comparisons and analyze moderating effects of game design dimensions such as group size, MPCR, heterogeneity, information structure, and punishment technology/costs. Coverage is thinner for games with extreme design parameters (e.g., very large groups, rare or highly asymmetric punishment options) and for certain prediction dimensions (e.g., visibility of punishment, presence of rewards, default contribution framing).

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`):**
- **exact:** The majority of reviewed studies are direct experiments in standard linear public goods games (PGG), often using continuous contributions, 3–5 players, and repeated rounds. Many others involve **close** variants (e.g., CPR, threshold games), while a minority are **adjacent** (e.g., repeated PD, trust games, allocation games).
- **Coverage:** **High**—Exact and close variants dominate.

**Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`):**
- **exact:** Many papers implement canonical peer or centralized punishment mechanisms (costly punishment, institutional fines, redistributive sanctions, etc.).
- **close/adjacent:** Some employ endogenous/third-party, exclusion, whistleblowing, or reputation-based sanctions.
- **Coverage:** **Strong** for peer and exogenous punishment; moderate for more nuanced forms (e.g., ostracism, delayed or indirect sanctions).

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`):**
- **exact:** There is robust, direct analysis of group efficiency, total payoff, or surplus in many studies.
- **close:** Some present closely related outcomes (group earnings, welfare, surplus creation) that are directly convertible to efficiency measures.
- **adjacent/weak:** Others focus solely on behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, norm compliance), which are not always directly mapped to efficiency.
- **Coverage:** **High**, though a sizable minority stop short at contribution levels or compliance without translating to efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related Outcomes:**
- **Group Efficiency:** Direct measures (total group earnings/payoff as share of maximum possible) are frequently reported.
- **Related Payoff Outcomes:** Surplus, welfare, group income, and similar are also used, particularly in CPR/threshold and asymmetric games.
- **Group Earnings:** Often cited in baseline/treatment comparisons and as proxies for efficiency.

**Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Important but Distinct):**
- **Contribution and Cooperation Rates:** Almost universally measured; often primary in studies lacking clear efficiency reporting.
- **Punishment Frequency/Targeting:** Used to infer mechanism efficiency, antisocial punishment prevalence, and moderation by game design.
- **Norm Compliance, Retaliation, and Antisocial Punishment:** Behavioral moderators of efficiency in many settings.

**Explicit Distinction:**  
Many summaries and findings emphasize whether higher contributions translate into higher efficiency, especially where costly punishment may offset or negate welfare gains.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Direction:**
- **Punishment increases contributions reliably across diverse PGG and variant settings.**  
- **Punishment's effect on efficiency is conditional:** Frequently positive but sometimes neutral or negative, depending on game design, punishment technology, group composition, and other moderators.

**Key Moderators and Insights:**

- **Punishment Type/Structure:**  
    - **Peer punishment** increases contributions but may not always raise efficiency when costs/antisocial punishment are high (Page et al., 2013; Vollan et al., 2019).
    - **Centralized or rule-based punishment**: More effective and less prone to antisocial use if aligned with observable norms and enforceable at low cost (Kingsley & Brown, 2016; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
    - **Redistributive punishment** (fines benefit the group) increases both efficiency and contributions more robustly than 'burned' fines (Page et al., 2013).
    - **Endogenous/voting mechanisms**: Allowing groups to choose whether to implement punishment often increases efficiency and equity (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021).

- **Game Design Dimensions:**
    - **Group Size/Player Count:** Small groups (3–5 players) are common; evidence on larger groups is sparse, though theory suggests effectiveness of punishment may diminish with size unless coordinated (Kritikos & Bolle, 2004).
    - **Number of Rounds:** More rounds generally allow for stable efficiency improvements under punishment, as deterrence builds over time (many studies).
    - **Punishment Cost/Effectiveness:** High punishment cost or low effectiveness can erase or reverse efficiency gains (Peng, 2022; Kingsley & Brown, 2016; Ramalingam et al., 2016; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).
    - **Information Structure:** Complete information about endowments or actions is crucial; with incomplete information, mis-targeted punishment leads to lower (sometimes negative) efficiency effects (De Geest & Kingsley, 2019; Waichman, 2020).
    - **Social/Ethnic Composition:** In-group favoritism and ethnic heterogeneity can reduce the effectiveness of punishment, sometimes making it detrimental to efficiency (Mantilla et al., 2021; Drouvelis et al., 2021).
    - **Network Structure**: Hierarchical or complete networks see greater efficiency gains from punishment than line/circle structures due to lower antisocial punishment and better targeting (Fatas et al., 2020).

- **Context Moderators:**
    - **Chat/Communication:** The combination of punishment and communication (especially early, unstructured chat) yields higher efficiency than either alone (Koch et al., 2021).
    - **Emotional Context:** Emotional induction (e.g., anger vs. happiness) can moderate the efficiency effect of punishment (Lee & Min, 2021).
    - **Endowment/Ability Heterogeneity:** Punishment restores efficiency only if it can be targeted according to observation of ability/contributions; unobservable heterogeneity leads to poorer efficiency even with punishment (Waichman, 2020; De Geest, 2019).
    - **Antisocial Punishment:** High rates of antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) significantly erode any efficiency gains from the mechanism (Vollan et al., 2019; Peng, 2022).

- **Design of Punishment Institutions:**
    - **Universal vs. Costly Rights:** If acquiring the right to punish is costly, efficiency gains disappear; costless/universal rights yield large gains when two or more punishers participate (Ramalingam et al., 2016).
    - **Targeted/Role-based Punishment:** Allocating punishment power to 'top contributors' or rotating among contributors generates higher efficiency than random designation or unrestricted peer punishment (Grieco et al., 2017).

- **Special Cases:**
    - **Threshold/CPR Environments:** In threshold or resource-extraction games, punishment may reduce efficiency if antisocial punishment is high or if group coordination is weak (Vollan et al., 2019; Robbett, 2016).
    - **External Threats and Asymmetry:** The effect of punishment can depend on the presence of external threats (e.g., theft) or institutional asymmetries (Khadjavi et al., 2017; De Geest & Stranlund, 2019).

**Conflict/Ambiguity:**
- Some studies find **no significant effect** of punishment on efficiency (Peng, 2022; Robbett, 2016), especially in the presence of mis-targeted or antisocial punishment, or where punishment costs absorb cooperation gains.
- In mixed or heterogeneous groups, **punishment can sometimes decrease efficiency relative to control** (Mantilla et al., 2021), in contrast to the increases seen in more homogeneous settings.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Baseline Control Efficiency is Crucial:** The average efficiency in the control (no-punishment) game is a strong predictor for the efficiency potential of punishment-enabled games. Where control efficiency is already high, the marginal gains from punishment may be smaller or even negative if costs are high. Where control efficiency is low (especially due to free riding), the potential for improvement is greater if the punishment mechanism is well-designed and properly targeted.

- **Expect Positive Efficiency Effects When:**
    - Group size is small to moderate (≤5), rounds are repeated, and peer punishment is low cost and high impact (Page et al., 2013; Wang & Qin, 2015).
    - Punishment is **well-targeted** (information is complete, or identity/transparency mechanisms are enabled) (Khadjavi et al., 2017; Waichman, 2020).
    - Institutional design supports effective, **endogenous adoption** of punishment (voting, low entry cost for punishers) (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021).

- **Expect Neutral or Negative Effects When:**
    - **Punishment is costly or mis-targeted** (e.g., in the presence of information asymmetry, high heterogeneity, or costly participation in punishment) (Ramalingam et al., 2016; De Geest & Kingsley, 2019).
    - Antisocial punishment is common (e.g., in diverse or status-differentiated groups) (Vollan et al., 2019; Mantilla et al., 2021).
    - Social context undermines deterrence (e.g., in-group favoritism, emotional environments such as happiness induction) (Mantilla et al., 2021; Lee & Min, 2021).

- **Institutional Details Matter:**
    - Redistribution of fines to the group (rather than 'burning' them) improves the efficiency benefits of punishment (Page et al., 2013).
    - The **visibility and observability** of punishment actions can improve targeting and efficiency (Kamei, 2018).
    - Communication between group members, especially before punishment is possible, amplifies the efficiency effect (Koch et al., 2021).

- **For Heterogeneous and Real-world Settings:**
    - If group endowments or returns are **observable and symmetric**, peer punishment increases efficiency (Waichman, 2020; De Geest & Kingsley, 2021).
    - If not, punishment may not support efficient cooperation or could reduce efficiency via mis-targeted or antisocial punishment (De Geest & Kingsley, 2019).

**Quantitative Guidance:**  
In standard, well-functioning lab PGGs with efficient peer punishment and small groups, enabling punishment often increases efficiency by 10–30 percentage points over control (e.g., from .6 to .8, or .7 to .9), but sometimes only restores efficiency to near-control levels if punishment costs are high. In settings with very low control efficiency and effective punishment mechanisms, even larger gains are possible. However, in diverse, poorly targeted, or high-cost environments, expect zero or negative gains.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Best Informed Dimensions (Direct Empirical Evidence):**
- **player_count:** Commonly varied between 3–5; small-group effects well understood; diminishing effectiveness as groups grow.
- **num_rounds:** Repeated games (5–30 rounds typical); time horizon affects sustainability of efficiency gains.
- **mpcr:** Central moderator of incentives and efficiency potential.
- **punishment_cost / punishment_tech (impact, centralization, institution type):** Varied in numerous papers; cost-to-impact ratio a major determinant of efficiency gain.
- **all_or_nothing:** Both continuous and binary (all-or-nothing) contributions tested; effects generalize but may be larger in continuous games.
- **information variables (show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id):** Targeting of punishment shown to be critical for efficiency.

**Indirectly Informed or Sparsely Documented Dimensions:**
- **chat/communication:** Empirically tested; combined with punishment often increases efficiency more than either alone.
- **default_contrib:** Framing effects only sparsely studied; not directly analyzed for punishment effect.
- **punishment_magnitude, reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Less commonly varied in direct efficiency analyses; several studies consider presence/absence or design of rewards alongside punishment.
- **show_n_rounds:** Some evidence on effect of round transparency, but mostly as context rather than a direct moderator.

**Effectively Missing in This Paper Set:**
- **Visibility of punishment identity to others (show_punishment_id):** Only a few studies directly manipulate or measure this as a moderator of efficiency.

# 7) Important Limitations

**Reporting Gaps:**
- **Behavior vs. Payoff:** Substantial number of papers report only contribution/cooperation rates, not efficiency or payoff, making direct quantitative synthesis more challenging.
- **Sparse Data for Large Groups and Some Institutional Designs:** Most evidence centers on small, fixed groups, with thin evidence for scalability to larger populations or for fine-grained institutional dimensions (e.g., complex reward/punishment combinations, third-party or exclusion-based systems).

**Generalizability:**
- **External Validity:** Most studies are lab-based with students; evidence from diverse field settings is limited and often features additional complicating factors (e.g., ethnic division, real resource stakes).
- **Interaction Effects:** Combinations of design dimensions (e.g., communication plus punishment, endogenous institution choice, real-world diversity) are explored only in a subset of studies.

**Ambiguity and Disagreement:**
- **Efficiency Gains Not Uniform:** There is clear evidence that enabling punishment does not always increase efficiency—cost structure, antisocial behavior, and social context all play critical moderating roles.

**Parameter Coverage:**
- **Missing Dimensions:** Some prediction dimensions (e.g., default contribution framing, fine-grained punishment/reward tech, punishment identity visibility) are only indirectly addressed or not varied in available studies.

**Applicability for Prediction:**
- **Care With Extrapolation:** Predicting outside the well-studied range of game parameters or participant populations (e.g., very large groups, field vs. lab) is less certain due to limited direct empirical evidence.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature base is broad, empirically rich, and highly relevant for the prediction task of estimating the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, especially when grounded in canonical lab settings and using the specified game design dimensions. Most prediction dimensions are either directly or indirectly informed, with especially strong evidence for player count, number of rounds, punishment cost/tech, MPCR, and information structure as key moderators. However, efficiency benefits are not uniform: institutional quality, cost structure, targeting capability, group composition, communication, and context all critically shape whether punishment raises, dampens, or fails to affect group efficiency. Overall, the literature supports nuanced, design-sensitive predictions rather than one-size-fits-all estimates.
