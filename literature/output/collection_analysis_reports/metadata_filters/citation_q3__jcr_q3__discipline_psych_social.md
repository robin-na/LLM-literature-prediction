# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set comprises 27 sources, including a mix of empirical laboratory and field experiments, formal and simulation-based theory papers, and conceptual/theoretical reviews. The empirical base is relatively strong for laboratory experiments manipulating public goods games (PGGs) and punishment design, with several studies measuring cooperation, punishment assignment, and group payoff outcomes. There is also notable coverage of adjacent and closely related environments, including trust games, inspection games, CPR dilemmas, dictator/ultimatum games, and institution-building models.

However, the evidence base is narrower for direct, quantitative assessment of efficiency (i.e., group payoff as a proportion of the cooperative maximum) in PGGs with and without punishment. Many empirical and theoretical papers focus primarily on behavioral or psychological mechanisms (contributions, compliance, fairness, retaliation, norm enforcement) rather than efficiency or aggregate group payoff. Theory and review papers extend the scope to cultural, psychological, and institutional moderators but often without direct payoff data. Overall, the evidence is robust for some design dimensions (player count, punishment cost/technology, number of rounds, continuous contributions) and weaker or sparse for others (chat, player identification, reward mechanisms, detailed monitoring/reputation structures).

# 2) Task Relevance

Each dimension of task relevance:

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - *Exact*: Several papers (e.g., Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Bednar, 2006; Nelissen & Mulder, 2013; Irwin et al., 2014) use standard or slight variants of public goods games.
  - *Close/Adjacent*: Many others use trust games, CPR dilemmas, inspection games, or institution-building models (e.g., Bohnet & Baytelman, 2007; Bravo, 2011), which are structurally related but differ in key game mechanics (simultaneity, autonomy, institutions).
  - *Weak/None*: Some studies are not directly related to PGGs or their close variants.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:
  - *Exact*: A substantial subset manipulate explicit, costly peer or centralized punishment (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Nelissen & Mulder, 2013).
  - *Close/Adjacent*: Some consider reward, social sanctions, or inspect/policing mechanisms; others enable institution building (Bravo, 2011).
  - *Weak/None*: Others focus on reputation, communication, or non-costly/non-player-administered sanctions.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
  - *Exact or Close*: Fairly few lab papers directly report efficiency or total group earnings (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Bednar, 2006; Bohnet & Baytelman, 2007). Several others report only adjacent or non-payoff outcomes (contributions, compliance).
  - *Adjacent/Weak*: Many studies report on contribution rates or prosocial actions—related, but not equivalent, to efficiency, as the cost of punishment can reduce efficiency even if cooperation increases.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes**:
    - *Efficiency (primary)*: Ratio of realized to possible maximum group payoff. Directly measured in a limited set of papers (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Bednar, 2006; Bohnet & Baytelman, 2007; Bravo, 2011).
    - *Total group earnings / welfare*: Sometimes reported, often as aggregate coin/token payouts or average payoffs.
    - *Adjacently related*: Some trust games and CPR models report average payoffs as efficiency proxies.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**:
    - *Contribution/cooperation rate*: Most commonly measured; often used as a proxy for efficiency but does not account for costliness of punishment or efficiency losses from antisocial punishment (Irwin et al., 2014; Nelissen & Mulder, 2013).
    - *Punishment assigned, norm compliance, trust, fairness, satisfaction*: Frequently assessed at the individual or group level.
    - *Retaliation, enforcement rate, norm maintenance*: Important for mechanism understanding but not directly interpretable as efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the highest-relevance papers:

- **Enabling costly punishment in standard PGGs**:
  - Can increase group efficiency and earnings relative to no-punishment controls if the punishment system is appropriately designed—especially with centralized or democratic mechanisms that limit excessive or antisocial punishment (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
  - Costly *peer* punishment with weak constraints (no legitimacy/democracy/centralization) often leads to excessive punishment, which reduces efficiency: higher cooperation is counterbalanced or outweighed by increased costs of sanctions (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Bednar, 2006).
  - Democratic or authority-based punishment appears to reduce wasteful (antisocial) punishment and can achieve eventual efficiency improvements over time, sometimes even surpassing no-punishment controls by the end of repeated games (Pfattheicher et al., 2018).

- **Cost structure matters**:
  - Lower-cost punishment can make excessive punishment more likely, eroding or reversing efficiency gains (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017).
  - High-magnitude punishment can improve efficiency up to a point but will not achieve full efficiency except under idealized monitoring (Bednar, 2006).

- **Monitoring and information**:
  - Imperfect information about contributions or defectors reduces the effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency; efficiency remains below potential even with punishment enabled (Bednar, 2006).

- **Sanction strength and removal**:
  - Weak sanctions improve cooperation when present; strong sanctions may not outperform no-punishment conditions and can undermine trust (Irwin et al., 2014; Nelissen & Mulder, 2013). When sanctions are removed, cooperation and trust often fall (Irwin et al., 2014).

- **Reward mechanisms**:
  - Parallel reward options can have similar short-term effects as punishment on cooperation, but their long-term efficiency impact is less clear (Irwin et al., 2014).

- **Reputation, norms, and culture**:
  - Norm enforcement via punishment is more acceptable and frequent in collectivist contexts (Eriksson et al., 2017; Jung et al., 2014). Cultural variation may moderate the effectiveness and legitimacy of punishment, indirectly shaping efficiency outcomes.

- **Theoretical and adjacent findings**:
  - Institution-building and monitoring mechanisms, as well as indirect enforcement (reputation, rule-setting), reliably increase efficiency, sometimes dramatically (Bravo, 2011; Bravo & Tamburino, 2008)—though these are not always formal punishment.

- **Ambiguities and disagreements**:
  - Some papers show mixed efficiency trends (democratic peer punishment sometimes crosses over to outperform no-punishment only after several rounds; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
  - Not all increases in cooperation translate to increased efficiency, especially when sanctioning costs are high or punishment is misapplied.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **When the control game is already highly efficient** (i.e., contribution rates and group payoffs are near the cooperative maximum), *enabling peer punishment is unlikely to raise efficiency further* and may reduce it if sanctioning is costly and not well-targeted (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Bednar, 2006).
- **When control efficiency is low to moderate** (i.e., baseline cooperation is weak), *enabling punishment—especially with constraints such as cost, centralized authority, or democratic procedures—will likely increase efficiency* modestly to moderately, but not usually to the full-cooperation benchmark (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
- **The predicted effect size and direction strongly depend on**:
    - **Type of punishment mechanism**: Centralization/democracy/authority improve efficacy and reduce waste; unconstrained peer punishment is risky.
    - **Punishment cost and magnitude**: Middle-range punishment costs are optimal for efficiency; costless punishment leads to wasteful behavior, and excessive costs can backfire (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Bednar, 2006).
    - **Length of the game (num_rounds)**: Longer games allow positive effects of democratic punishment to accumulate; over short horizons, punishment may not "pay off" (Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
    - **Monitoring/information structure**: Imperfect monitoring suppresses the efficiency gains of punishment (Bednar, 2006).
    - **Baseline efficiency (control condition)**: If baseline is already high due to norms or other mechanisms, marginal benefit of punishment may be zero or negative.
    - **Cultural context**: Effectiveness and acceptance of punishment, and by extension its effect on efficiency, may be contingent on prevailing meta-norms and perceptions of legitimacy (Eriksson et al., 2017).

- **Direct mappings from design dimensions to efficiency under punishment are only well informed for**: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, and some aspects of punishment_tech (peer/authority/democracy).
- **Other dimensions (e.g., chat, default_contrib, reward_exists, show_punishment_id)** are sparsely covered or only discussed contextually.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed (by experimental data or theory relevant to PGG efficiency):**
    - **player_count**: Manipulated in most experimental PGGs (typically 3–4 players).
    - **num_rounds**: Varies across studies; longer games allow for reputation, late-round effects.
    - **mpcr**: MPCR effects discussed (Bednar, 2006); larger returns generally support higher efficiency.
    - **all_or_nothing, continuous contrib**: Both variants covered (Bednar, 2006; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017).
    - **punishment_cost, punishment_magnitude/tech**: Strongly manipulated and analyzed (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Bednar, 2006).
    - **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries**: Some discussion in theory (Bednar, 2006; Bravo & Tamburino, 2008).
    - **reward_exists**: Considered, but less systematically analyzed.
 
- **Indirectly informed/contextually discussed**:
    - **chat**: Often mentioned but rarely systematically varied.
    - **show_punishment_id**: Touched on in few studies.
    - **reward_cost/reward_tech**: Paralleled in some studies but usually as a secondary manipulation.
    - **default_contrib_prop**: Rarely manipulated directly.

- **Effectively missing**:  
  Detailed manipulations or analyses are generally absent for default_contrib, extensive reward structures, sophisticated reputation or player identity display (except as context), or complex communication/negotiation phases.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Reliance on behavioral proxies**: Many studies equate increased contribution rates with improved efficiency, but do not account for the costliness or targeting of punishment, which can reduce group payoff even as cooperation rises.
- **Sparse direct efficiency measurement**: Only a minority of experiments report aggregate group payoff as a proportion of the possible cooperative maximum; this limits the precision of predictions for treatment efficiency.
- **Generality across design dimensions**: Findings are strongest for standard PGGs with 3–4 players, 6–10 rounds, continuous contributions, explicit costly punishment, and no rewards or chat; out-of-sample designs (e.g., large groups, long games, dynamic networks, simultaneous reward and punishment, variable information) are less reliably predicted.
- **Limited coverage of certain dimensions**: Chat, default contributions, extensive rewards, display of punishment identity, and sophisticated monitoring/information structures are not systematically explored.
- **Contextual and cultural moderators under-studied**: Cross-cultural work and real-world field evidence offer only indirect guidance, with laboratory findings likely overestimating both the frequency and payoff impact of punishment in naturalistic settings (Berger & Hevenstone, 2016; Eriksson et al., 2017).
- **Ambiguities in mechanism and outcome**: Some theory and review papers highlight psychological, motivational, or institutional contingencies that are not generally measured or manipulated in empirical PGG treatments, introducing uncertainty about generalizability.
- **Baseline (control) efficiency dependence**: When control games are highly efficient, marginal benefit of punishment is minimal or negative—this is often overlooked in studies reporting only average treatment effects.

**In summary:** This literature set is moderately strong in addressing the core prediction task for standard PGGs with punishment, especially as a function of group size, rounds, contribution type, MPCR, and punishment design. Predictions for designs further from these empirical paradigms, or where efficiency is already high, are more speculative and should be made with caution. Distinguishing payoff gains from mere cooperation increases is essential; attention to design details (e.g., democratic punishment, authority, monitoring) is critical in leveraging the established findings.
