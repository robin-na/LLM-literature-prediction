# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set is extensive (50 papers) and predominantly empirical, focusing heavily on experimental studies using laboratory public goods games (PGGs) or closely related designs. About half of the studies directly use standard PGGs with clear efficiency/payoff outcomes; the remainder uses adjacent paradigms (e.g., Prisoner’s Dilemma, Dictator Games, third-party punishment, CPR games) or offers theoretical syntheses. Within the empirical subset, laboratory experiments are far more common than field or observational studies. Only a minority of theory/review papers appear, most offering mechanistic or evolutionary perspectives, not direct modeling of efficiency effects or dimension-specific effect sizes.

Overall, the evidence is broad in behavioral mechanisms but narrower for direct, quantitative support of **punishment’s effect on efficiency in PGG-like environments** as posed in the prediction task. There is strong coverage of game design dimensions affecting cooperation and punishment behavior, but only scattered, sometimes conflicting, direct evidence for efficiency (payoff-based) outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

Relevance is assessed separately on three dimensions—PGG or variant, punishment or sanctions, and efficiency (or related payoff):

- **pgg_or_variant**: The majority of studies (especially those with strong empirical evidence) are **exact** matches (lab-based linear or threshold PGGs). Some provide only a **close** or **adjacent** match via related social dilemmas (e.g., CPRs, third-party dictator games, tax evasion, repeated PDs).

- **punishment_or_sanctions**: Many studies directly manipulate **punishment** options as a key independent variable (**exact** or **close**). Some focus on **adjacent** forms (e.g., central penalties or peer reporting) or only discuss punishment contextually.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: Only a fraction of studies (**exact** or **close**) measure actual **efficiency**—total group payoff relative to the cooperative benchmark. Many instead report only **adjacent** behavioral outcomes (contribution, punishment frequency, norm compliance) with no direct efficiency or welfare data. Reviews and theory works more commonly speak to *mechanisms* affecting efficiency rather than observed effect sizes.

In sum, **highly task-relevant (i.e., exact-exact-exact) studies are in the minority but do exist** (see, e.g., Zhang et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2023; Joseph et al., 2025; Botelho et al., 2022). Much of the rest of the set supports only behavioral (non-payoff) or mechanism-level inferences.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

A sharp distinction exists in the outcomes measured:

- **Payoff-related outcomes**: Group efficiency, total earnings/payoff, welfare, surplus, and total coins generated (as defined in the prediction task) are measured directly in a subset of studies (Zhang et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2023; Joseph et al., 2025; Botelho et al., 2022; De Geest et al., 2022; some theoretical models).
    - Several studies report **absolute efficiency values under treatment vs. control**, permitting direct estimation of the marginal effect of punishment. Others report only relative or non-statistically-disaggregated figures.
- **Behavioral, non-payoff outcomes:** 
    - Most studies (including many with "PGG" in the title) report only contribution rates, punishment assigned, cooperation rates, norm compliance, or cognitive/emotional responses. 
    - These outcomes are important for understanding mechanisms but cannot be equated with efficiency unless paired with explicit analysis of payoff consequences (e.g., Zhao et al., 2024; Funk & Mischkowski, 2022; Bogdan et al., 2023, etc.).

- **Theory and reviews:** Synthesize mechanisms potentially affecting payoffs via cooperation/norm enforcement, or recount prior empirical findings with varying clarity as to whether efficiency was improved.

In summary, while behavioral and cognitive outcomes are richly covered, **efficiency and direct payoff results are reported in only a minority of highly relevant papers.**

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## General Patterns (Empirical Evidence)

- **Punishment increases contributions, but efficiency effects are mixed:**  
  - In multiple standard PGG lab studies, **enabling peer/institutional punishment increases group contributions** compared to the no-punishment control. However, the effect on group efficiency is *ambiguous* due to the costliness of punishment:
    - **Some studies find efficiency goes up** (i.e., net payoff increases more than the exclusion of the punishment costs) when punishment is sufficiently strong/effective (Zhang et al., 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2023 [large punishment], De Geest et al., 2022 [CPR variant]).
    - **Others find efficiency is reduced or unchanged** (Botelho et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2023 [small punishment]; Rumble et al., 2022 [dyadic games]) because the costs of punishment outweigh cooperation gains, especially with high punishment frequency or when punishment is used inefficiently or antisocially.

- **Magnitude and cost of punishment matter:**  
  - **Greater punishment magnitude** (holding cost fixed) is more likely to increase efficiency, as it deters free riding with less overall punishment expenditure (Ye et al., 2023; Windrich et al., 2024 [adjacent]; De Geest et al., 2022).
  - **Costly punishment** can wipe out gains from cooperation if used indiscriminately or too often (Botelho et al., 2022; Pedrazzini et al., 2025).

- **Cognitive ability, group context, and player characteristics** moderate the efficiency effect of punishment:
    - Ye et al. (2023) show that small punishment can actually *reduce* efficiency in repeated games with high cognitive ability groups.
    - Group identity and mechanisms for coordination (e.g., leaders, earned roles, communication) can enhance the effectiveness of punishment (Zhang et al., 2024; De Geest et al., 2022; Joseph et al., 2025).

- **Threshold/mechanism design interacts with efficiency:**  Joseph et al. (2025) show that threshold (all-or-nothing) mechanisms can boost contributions but may *reduce* efficiency due to risk of total loss, whereas punishment can provide a more reliable efficiency boost.

- **Antisocial punishment and poor targeting are efficiency risks:** Several behavioral studies report high rates of antisocial or mis-targeted punishment (i.e., punishing high contributors or out-group members), possibly leading to reduced efficiency (Zhao et al., 2024; Pedrazzini et al., 2025; Bogdan et al., 2023 [behavioral only]).

## Theory & Mechanistic Arguments

- Theory papers argue that punishment, by enabling credible threats, changes equilibria and allows groups to sustain higher efficiency (Melkonyan et al., 2022 [VBE]; Levy, 2022).
- However, **costly or retaliatory punishment**, especially in dyads or poorly regulated settings, can reduce efficiency relative to control (Rumble et al., 2022; Botelho et al., 2022).

## Indirect, Contextual, or Adjacent Evidence

- Punishment’s effectiveness is context-dependent: its efficiency effect is **amplified by enabling communication, group identity, accurate information, and low antisocial punishment** (De Geest et al., 2022; Pedrazzini et al., 2025; Frey & Mischkowski, 2022 [review]).
- Reward mechanisms often have *clearer positive efficiency effects* (Pedrazzini et al., 2025 [review]; Bruttel et al., 2022 [reward: exact]).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Given both direct and indirect evidence, **the following synthesis best guides predictions** for efficiency in PGG-like environments when enabling punishment, conditional on design dimensions and control efficiency:

- **If punishment is strong and cost-effective** (high punishment magnitude per cost), and the game is not prone to antisocial punishment or overuse (e.g., via clarity/accuracy of information, high MPCR, presence of a leader or coordination mechanisms), **enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency compared to control** (Zhang et al., 2024; Joseph et al., 2025; De Geest et al., 2022; Melkonyan et al., 2022 [mechanism]).
- **If punishment is weak or costly**, or is used frequently and indiscriminately (especially in the absence of high baseline cooperation, or when antisocial punishment is present), **enabling punishment may *reduce* efficiency relative to control**, as punishment expenditures offset the cooperation increase (Botelho et al., 2022; Rumble et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2023 for small punishment).
- **Magnitude of the efficiency change is context-sensitive:** use reported control efficiency as a baseline; expect *larger* positive shifts in environments where cooperation is initially low and punishment leads to robust coordination, but either *no shift* or a *negative shift* when baseline cooperation is already high or punishment is overused.
- **Specific design dimensions to attend to for prediction**: MPCR (higher MPCR tends to moderate the negative cost effect), punishment cost/magnitude ratio, group size (though most direct evidence is for small (4–5) groups), number of rounds (effect can grow in repeated settings, but over-punishment risk accumulates), communication (can enhance coordination and mitigate misdirected punishment), and whether information about others' actions is accurate and available.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions** (across multiple high-relevance papers):
- `player_count` (4–5 is most studied; larger groups are rare)
- `num_rounds` (ranges from one-shot to 32 rounds; repeated games' dynamics are nontrivial for efficiency effects)
- `mpcr` (frequently varied; higher MPCR moderates the efficiency cost of punishment)
- `punishment_cost` (critical; covered in nearly every relevant study)
- `punishment_tech` (cost-to-impact ratio varies; higher impact per cost generally favors efficiency gains)
- `chat` (communication is a moderator in some studies [De Geest et al., 2022; Joseph et al., 2025])
- `all_or_nothing` (both continuous and threshold mechanisms are manipulated; threshold can interact with risk and efficiency [Joseph et al., 2025])
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds` (feedback transparency is experimentally manipulated in a subset)
  
**Indirectly informed dimensions**:
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Discussed mainly as contrasts/experimental variants; direct evidence is sparse for rewards, but evidence suggests rewards often increase efficiency more reliably than punishment (Pedrazzini et al., 2025).
- `default_contrib`: Mentioned in framing studies or theory.
- `show_punishment_id`: Rarely the focus, but theoretically discussed.

**Only contextually discussed or largely missing:** 
- Most studies do not systematically manipulate or report `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id`, or full details of `reward_tech` and `reward_cost`.
- Large groups (>5), non-lab or high-stakes field environments, and structural communication networks are only lightly present.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse direct efficiency evidence:** Only a minority of studies directly measure group efficiency/payoff; most report behavioral outcomes that do *not* map cleanly into efficiency effects without additional calculation or assumptions.
- **Lab-centric, small-group focus:** Majority of evidence is from laboratory settings with small (often 4-5) groups; generalizability to larger groups, high-stakes field environments, or one-shot vs. repeated interactions is limited.
- **Conflicting results on efficiency:** Even in direct lab-PGG evidence, punishment sometimes raises, sometimes lowers, and sometimes has no significant effect on efficiency. The mechanism (positive via deterring free riding, negative via wasted punishment cost) is well supported, but net effect varies with design and context.
- **Limited parameter range:** Most studies use a limited range of MPCR, punishment cost/magnitude, group sizes, and communication protocols; detailed prediction outside these ranges is extrapolative.
- **Antisocial punishment and targeting rarely addressed in efficiency terms:** Many studies observe antisocial or mis-targeted punishment, but few quantify its impact on efficiency.
- **Dimension interactions underexplored:** Studies often vary one or two design dimensions at a time; high-dimensional predictions or strong interactions are not fully covered.
- **Reward and other positive mechanisms underexplored:** While some indirect evidence exists (Pedrazzini et al., 2025; Bruttel et al., 2022), the direct comparative efficiency effects of rewards versus punishment are not systematically established.

---

**Summary**:  
While a substantial body of experimental evidence directly addresses punishment in PGGs, the effect of enabling punishment on group efficiency is *highly context-dependent* and can be either positive or negative, with the cost-to-impact ratio and frequency of punishment being critical moderators. Prediction should rely most on studies measuring efficiency/payoff directly under matched design conditions, and treat behavioral results with caution as proxies. Many dimensions are well covered (group size, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost), but several (large groups, networked communication, detailed reward/punishment tech) are missing or underexplored. Generalizability beyond small-group, lab-centric environments is limited.
