# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists almost entirely of **experimental empirical lab studies** (with a small minority of observational or field studies), overwhelmingly focused on public goods games (PGGs) and their close variants, as well as coordination, bargaining, and trust games that share design features with PGGs. A subset of the studies explores institutional mechanisms, such as peer and centralized punishment, reward, partnership formation, and feedback transparency. Most studies report behavioral outcomes; a substantial minority provide **direct group efficiency or payoff measures**. The evidence base is **broad for standard linear PGGs with and without punishment**, including both peer and formal punishment, various network architectures, and institution-selection regimes. By contrast, coverage is more **sparse for PGGs with nonstandard features** (e.g., threshold/all-or-nothing, endogenous group formation, communication/chat, and nuanced feedback revelation).

There is a strong **empirical orientation**: the bulk of findings are data-driven, with clear reporting of controlled contrasts between punishment-enabled and punishment-disabled conditions. **Theoretical mechanism arguments** do appear, chiefly as interpretations of observed behaviors, but are rarely the sole basis for outcome claims.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:  
- **Relevance:** Majority of papers = `exact` (linear/repeated PGGs), some `close` (generalized exchange, CPR, team investment), several `adjacent` (trust, allocation, bargaining, coordination games), and a few `none` or `weak`.
- **Coverage:** Strong for standard PGGs; moderate for coordination or allocation games with comparable collective action elements.

**punishment_or_sanctions**:  
- **Relevance:** Large set = `exact` (direct manipulation of punishment/sanctions in treatment arms), some `adjacent` (exclusion, reputation/feedback, or implicit/indirect sanctions), and a few with only discussion or no active manipulation (`adjacent`/`weak`/`none`).
- **Coverage:** Peer punishment, centralized/formal sanctions, and endogenous/exogenous institution conditions are all well represented.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- **Relevance:** About half = `exact` or `close` (explicit efficiency or group payoff as primary outcome), remainder report only behavior (contributions/cooperation/punishment assigned) but not efficiency (`adjacent`/`weak`/`none`).  
- **Coverage:** Quantitative efficiency measures are available for many standard PGGs, less so for non-standard games or those emphasizing behavioral mechanisms.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**:  
    - *Efficiency* (group payoff as proportion of max possible, surplus, welfare): Directly reported in many PGG papers and variants—especially those contrasting punishment/no-punishment.
    - *Total group earnings/payoff*: Often reported as a primary outcome; in some studies, efficiency is derived from these.
    - *Clarity*: Some papers report only proxy outcomes (e.g., proportion of groups "successful" under a binary outcome) rather than continuous efficiency.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:  
    - *Contribution/cooperation rates*: Ubiquitously measured; directly tied to efficiency only when mapped through punishment/reward cost structure.
    - *Punishment frequency/intensity; assigned punishment*: Frequently analyzed, but interpretation requires attention to associated costs.
    - *Norm compliance, trustworthiness, fairness, partner choice*: Explored mainly in mechanism/psychological studies; only indirectly linked to group efficiency.
    - *Attitudinal and personality measures*: Occasionally addressed, typically not tied to economic outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Public Goods Games (PGGs) with Peer Punishment:**
- Peer punishment **often increases group contributions**, but its effect on efficiency is ambivalent—driven by the balance between higher contributions and the *direct costs* of punishment:
    - **Efficiency decreases** when punishment costs outweigh cooperation gains (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015 [RP treatment]; Abbink et al., 2004; Calabuig et al., 2024).
    - **Efficiency increases** when punishment is effective (strong deterrence, sparing use) and direct costs are low (Castillo et al., 2021; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022 [with migration]).
    - **Network structure**, *punishment technology*, and *identity visibility* moderate these effects strongly (Carpenter et al., 2012; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

**Centralized/Formal Punishment:**
- **Consistently increases efficiency** relative to no-punishment controls, especially when group composition is endogenous (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021), and when migration is possible/formal enforcement is available.

**Indirect/Adjacent Sanctions:**
- Exclusion or partner choice and other non-monetary social sanctions may increase cooperation, but **impact on efficiency is context-dependent** and, when measured, usually **less than that of monetary punishment/rewards** (Grund et al., 2020; Di Guida et al., 2021).

**Reward vs. Punishment:**
- *Reward schemes* typically **raise both contributions and efficiency better than punishment**; peer punishment may undermine efficiency due to cost, unless sparingly used (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Peng, 2022).

**Context and Moderators:**
- Cultural background (prevalence of “strong reciprocators”), group openness, and information structure (public feedback/visibility) modulate the efficiency effects of punishment (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Carpenter et al., 2012; Nax et al., 2018).

- *Game design matters*: Efficiency with punishment can be *higher, lower, or the same* as no-punishment control, depending on design parameters like cost-effectiveness of punishment, structure of punishment network, and presence of communication.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

### How Should the Literature Inform Prediction of Treatment Efficiency?

- **Control Efficiency Is Not Sufficient:** The average efficiency of the control (no-punishment) game is at best an *approximate base rate* for likely efficiency under punishment-enabled conditions; the literature shows that the *actual shift* (positive, negative, or null) depends heavily on several game design dimensions (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Carpenter et al., 2012; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
  
- **Critical Moderators Explicitly Supported**:
    - **Punishment Cost/Effectiveness Ratio:** Lower-cost, high-impact punishment technologies are most likely to yield efficiency gains (Kanitsar, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021).
    - **Network Structure of Punishment:** Dense and complete punishment networks (e.g., everyone can punish everyone) tend to support efficiency only if punishment expenditures are moderate; sparse or complex structures can lead to over-punishment or feuding, reducing efficiency (Carpenter et al., 2012; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - **Centralization:** Centralized/formal punishment mechanisms consistently result in higher efficiency gains than peer (decentralized) punishment, especially in dynamic/open groups (Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021).
    - **Communication & Visibility:** Lack of communication or feedback about others' contributions/punishments makes punishment less efficient and may increase costs (Carpenter et al., 2012). Public information structures can also backfire if not paired with sanctions (Becchetti et al., 2015).
    - **Reward Mechanisms:** Direct comparison shows reward is usually superior to punishment for efficiency (Kocher & Matzat, 2016; Peng, 2022).
  
- **Expected Patterns**:
    - *If control efficiency is moderate or low and punishment is costly*: enabling punishment often **decreases efficiency**.
    - *If punishment is cheap/effective or centralized/formal*: enabling punishment can **increase efficiency**, particularly if free-riding is pervasive in control.
    - *If the punishment network is incomplete, directed, or admits retaliation*: efficiency gains are likely to be null or even negative.
    - *Presence of strong reciprocators (“cooperative punishers”)* in the sample: positive effects of punishment are stronger (Suleiman & Samid, 2021).

### Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes  
- Many studies report strong effects on contributions but only **weak, null, or negative effects on efficiency/payoff** due to punishment costs. **Predictions should use only efficiency data, not contribution rates, when available.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Design Dimensions Directly Informed:**
    - `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `all_or_nothing`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Widely and systematically varied, with strong correspondence between design choices and reported efficiency effects.
    - `chat`: A few studies manipulate chat/communication, typically finding that chat can mitigate the need for punishment or increase its efficiency (Carpenter et al., 2012).

- **Indirectly or Moderately Informed:**
    - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Several papers include rewards, but usually only to contrast with punishment; findings indicate rewards generally increase efficiency more than punishment.
    - `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Some studies touch on feedback and transparency, with evidence that too much public information without sanctions can reduce efficiency (Becchetti et al., 2015; Nax et al., 2018), while visible punishment identity can heighten costs or retaliation (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - `default_contrib`: Framing (opt-in vs. opt-out) is rarely manipulated directly and impacts are unclear.

- **Contextually Discussed or Missing:**
    - Effects of communication are somewhat discussed, but *systematic comparisons* are rare.
    - *Nuanced features* such as belief elicitation, network formation mechanisms, endogenous group formation, or detailed assignment of punishment authority/legitimacy are only occasionally explored.
    - Some design choices (e.g., threshold goods, dynamic matching, stochastic shocks) are *underrepresented* in studies reporting efficiency as the main outcome.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Universal Generalizations Are Not Supported:** There is *substantial heterogeneity* in the effect of punishment on efficiency; simple enabling of peer punishment does not guarantee a direction of change.
- **Behavioral-Outcome Bias:** Many studies report only contribution/punishment behavior, requiring caution in translating these results to *efficiency-based* predictions. Behavioral improvement does not always mean efficiency gains.
- **Selective Reporting of Design Dimensions:** While common dimensions like `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, and `punishment_cost/tech` are well covered, other dimensions (such as the impact of feedback revelation, communication/chat, opt-in framing, and identity visibility) are sparsely addressed—limiting fine-grained prediction in less typical designs.
- **Limited Coverage of Extreme or Novel Designs:** Very high-cost punishment, large groups, dynamic or uncertain settings, and rich communication environments are underrepresented. Evidence is mostly from standard 4- to 5-player games with moderate punishment cost/impact.
- **Contextual Moderators:** The social composition (e.g., strong reciprocators, cultural context) and group openness are shown to moderate punishment efficacy, but these are not easily observed or included as prediction inputs.
- **Adjacent Evidence:** A substantial number of adjacent studies, while illuminating for mechanisms (e.g., coordination, trust, exclusion), do not provide direct quantitative evidence for efficiency outcomes of interest in standard PGGs.
- **Field Settings:** A minority of studies suggest lab findings may not fully generalize to real-world or field-like environments.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature base is broad, rich, and predominantly empirical for standard PGGs with and without punishment, with strong direct evidence connecting design dimensions (`player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, network structure) to efficiency outcomes. The effect of enabling peer punishment on group efficiency is *not uniform*: it depends heavily on design details and context. Control efficiency alone is not sufficient for prediction. Key moderators (punishment cost-effectiveness, network completeness, centralization, communication, and group composition) must be incorporated explicitly. Predictions made outside standard design ranges or in environments where only behavioral outcomes are reported should be qualified accordingly.
