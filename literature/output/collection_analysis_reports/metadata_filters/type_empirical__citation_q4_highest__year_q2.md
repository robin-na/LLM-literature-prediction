# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises a broad empirical literature of 115 studies, heavily weighted toward experimental laboratory public goods games (PGGs) and closely related social dilemmas. The bulk of the evidence is empirical, with almost all key findings coming from actual laboratory or field experiments, rather than theoretical arguments or simulations. Out of these, a substantial subset report direct payoff-based outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, welfare), while a large auxiliary literature focuses on behavioral (non-payoff) variables such as cooperation/contribution rates, punishment frequency, and norm compliance.

Most empirical studies manipulate common PGG design dimensions—group size, number of rounds, punishment and/or reward existence, costs of sanctioning, presence/absence of chat, and various feedback or reputation mechanisms. Several studies are field experiments or utilize context-relevant subjects, lending some external validity. The literature is both broad and deep regarding standard linear PGGs with and without punishment, but is thinner for nonstandard or highly parameterized settings (e.g., step-level public goods, institutional heterogeneity, dynamic endowments).

Across the evidence base, strong attention is given to variations in punishment presence, cost, technology (peer, centralized, exclusion/ostracism, second-order, etc.), and—less commonly—the interaction with communication or reputation mechanisms. Studies reporting direct treatment effects on payoff-based measures are the most relevant for downstream predictions about efficiency. However, many studies measure only behavioral or psychological outcomes, providing context but not direct quantitative guidance for efficiency prediction.

# 2) Task Relevance

Below we assess the literature on three relevance dimensions:

### A. PGG or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`)

- **Exact**: Most payoff-relevant studies use a standard linear PGG or a minimal structural variant (e.g., continuous contributions, VCM, threshold, repeated, step-level, dyads).
- **Close/Adjacent**: Some evidence comes from related social dilemmas (prisoner's dilemmas, trust/dictator games, weakest-link, or CPR games) or from games with related but not identical sanctioning institutions (ostracism, exclusion, bribery-enabled IPGGs).
- **Weak/None**: Literature focusing purely on one-shot, dyadic, or non-PGG games is generally of only indirect value.

### B. Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`)

- **Exact**: There is strong coverage of settings with peer punishment, formal/centralized punishment, and combinations with reward, gossip, or exclusion.
- **Close**: Many studies explore ostracism/exclusion, third-party punishment, or indirect sanctions.
- **Adjacent/Weak**: Some papers discuss reputation, indirect reciprocity, or non-costly sanctions; a large segment is purely control (no sanctions).

### C. Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcomes (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`)

- **Exact**: Roughly a third of the studies directly report efficiency (group payoff/share of social optimum), group welfare, or closely related outcomes.
- **Close/Adjacent**: Many report group contribution levels (often a proxy) but not always net of punishment/reward costs.
- **Weak/None**: A significant share reports only behavioral variables (cooperation rate, norm compliance, intent to punish, emotional responses, trust).

**In summary:**  
The literature is highly relevant to the core prediction problem—with a large base of exact matches for PGGs with peer punishment and efficiency outcomes—but the evidence for some marginal or compound design dimensions (e.g., second-order punishment, power transfer, endogenous group formation, bribery/corruption options, mood induction) is more limited or context-dependent.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes**:  
  - *Efficiency*: Ratio of group earnings to the maximum possible under full cooperation.  
  - *Group Payoff/Group Welfare/Earnings*: Total coins/tokens earned by all players.
  - *Surplus*: Net group benefit relative to no-contribution outcome.
  - *Monetary impact of sanctioning*: Netting out the cost of punishment/rewards.

  These are the primary outcomes used for direct prediction of treatment efficiency.

- **Behavioral (Non-Payoff) Outcomes**:  
  - *Contribution/Cooperation rates*: Fraction or amount contributed to the public good.
  - *Punishment Frequency and Magnitude*: How often and how severely players sanction others.
  - *Norm Compliance or Enforcement*: Whether contributions align with stated or inferred group norms.
  - *Trust, Reciprocity, Reputational Ratings*: Other-regarding beliefs and behaviors.
  - *Emotional or Psychological Measures*: Mood, intent, fairness perceptions, neural and affective correlates.

  While many studies report only these outcomes, they are not perfectly predictive of efficiency, especially when costly punishment reduces overall group earnings despite higher cooperation/contribution rates.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### General Pattern

- **Peer punishment (standard linear PGG, moderate cost):**  
  Enabling punishment typically *raises efficiency*—often very substantially—when control (no-punishment) groups display substantial free-riding (Gächter et al., 2017; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016; Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Markussen et al., 2014; Kölle, 2015; Dal Bó & Dal Bó, 2014).
- **Limits and Context-Dependence:**  
  - If punishment is too costly, used antisocially, or applied under conditions of very low MPCR (where cooperation is inefficient), it can *reduce efficiency* relative to control. This is due to costlier punishment and resource destruction (Wu et al., 2016; Gächter et al., 2017; Abbink et al., 2017; Gächter et al., 2017 [growth & inequality]).
  - In settings with high inequality, dynamic endowments, or opportunities for corruption/bribery, punishment can fail to improve or can worsen efficiency (Muthukrishna et al., 2017; Gächter et al., 2017 [growth & inequality]; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015).
  - Efficiency gains are often largest when punishment is relatively cheap and/or well-targeted (Markussen et al., 2014), when there are opportunities for communication or explicit norm-signaling (Andrighetto et al., 2013), or when power to punish is voluntarily centralized (Gross et al., 2016).

### Communication, Norm-Signaling, and Reputation

- Allowing chat or norm-signaling alongside punishment *amplifies efficiency gains* by reducing the need for frequent costly punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2013).
- Gossip and exclusion as indirect sanctions can also increase efficiency, sometimes outperforming punishment, especially when punishment is costly or used indiscriminately (Wu et al., 2016; Feinberg et al., 2014; Charness & Yang, 2014).

### Heterogeneity and Institutional Variants

- Effectiveness of punishment varies by group heterogeneity—capability homogeneity enhances, valuation heterogeneity diminishes efficiency gains (Kölle, 2015).
- Voluntary centralization of punishment power, endogenously chosen sanction regimes, and opportunities for democratic institution formation can enhance efficiency beyond exogenously imposed or purely peer-based punishment (Gross et al., 2016; Markussen et al., 2014; Hilbe et al., 2014).
- Anti-social punishment and emotion-induced retributive motives *reduce or erase efficiency gains* (Dickinson & Masclet, 2015; Pfattheicher et al., 2017; Kosfeld & Rustagi, 2015), and too much opportunity for venting can decrease deterrence (Dickinson & Masclet, 2015).

### Negative and Reversed Effects

- When the game is designed such that contributions are inefficient (MPCR < 1/n), punishment enforces destructive norms and *decreases efficiency* (Abbink et al., 2017).
- In some repeated games or dynamic wealth settings, punishment fails to raise—and may lower—group welfare due to resource destruction, particularly in lengthy games (Gächter et al., 2017 [growth & inequality]).

### Behavioral vs. Payoff Disconnect

- Many studies show that higher contribution rates induced by punishment do *not always* translate to higher efficiency, especially when punishment costs are high or punishment is used anti-socially (Wu et al., 2016; Gächter et al., 2017 [growth & inequality]).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **For standard lab PGGs with moderate group size, moderate MPCR (>1/n), moderate punishment cost, and no complex institutional structure:**  
  Enabling (peer) punishment can be predicted to increase average efficiency substantially over no-punishment controls (Gächter et al., 2017; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).

- **Magnitude of Effect:**  
  - If control efficiency is low, expect large gains (often bringing the group near or above 80–90% of the social optimum). If control efficiency is already high, the absolute gain is smaller.
  - The *delta* between control and punishment-enabled efficiency is generally positive and substantial if punishment is well-targeted, costs are moderate, and anti-social punishment is rare.

- **Moderators of the Effect:**
  - **Communication/Norm-Signaling (`chat`):** Strong positive moderator; presence amplifies punishment's efficient effect (Andrighetto et al., 2013).
  - **Punishment Cost/Efficiency (`punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`):** High cost or low fine-to-fee ratio reduces effect and can lead to efficiency losses (Wu et al., 2016; Markussen et al., 2014).
  - **MPCR/Payoff Structure (`mpcr`):** For MPCR < 1/n or negative social return, punishment can decrease efficiency by enforcing perverse norms (Abbink et al., 2017).
  - **Group Heterogeneity:** If group heterogeneity is in capability but not valuation, punishment is more effective; otherwise, heterogeneity can reduce efficiency gains (Kölle, 2015).
  - **Corruption/Bribery Opportunities:** If the institution can be corrupted (bribery), punishment's effect is negative or highly context-dependent (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
  - **Centralization or Power Transfer (`punishment_tech`):** Voluntary centralization increases efficiency above standard peer punishment (Gross et al., 2016).
  - **Emotion and Venting:** Excessive emotion or venting opportunities can decrease deterrence and net efficiency (Dickinson & Masclet, 2015; Drouvelis & Grosskopf, 2016).
  - **Democratic choice of regime:** Endogenously adopted punishment performs better than exogenously imposed punishment (Markussen et al., 2014; Hilbe et al., 2014).

- **Conditions Where Punishment May Reduce or Fail to Increase Efficiency:**
  - Costly or frequent anti-social punishment.
  - High punishment cost with low marginal deterrence.
  - Dynamic settings with endogenous inequality or wealth accumulation (Gächter et al., 2017 [growth & inequality]).
  - Settings where group norms support destructive behavior, or the payoff structure penalizes cooperation.
  - Presence of opportunities for institutional corruption or bribery (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).

- **Indirect Guidance / Cautions:**
  - Do not infer efficiency from increased contributions unless punishment costs are clearly netted out of group payoffs.
  - Behavioral findings (increased norm compliance, punishment frequency) do not guarantee improved efficiency.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count` (playerCount): Variably specified; direct comparisons show moderate effects of group size on punishment efficacy, but the effect is less than other dimensions (Balliet & Van Lange, 2013; Gächter et al., 2017).
- `num_rounds` (numRounds): Strong evidence that effect sizes grow with more rounds up to a point; repeated interaction generally amplifies punishment's benefits (Gächter et al., 2017; Markussen et al., 2014).
- `chat` (chat): Multiple studies show that allowing chat/norm-signaling, especially in conjunction with punishment, dramatically increases efficiency (Andrighetto et al., 2013).
- `mpcr` (mpcr): Strongly manipulated; effect of punishment is positive only when MPCR > 1/n; becomes negative or null otherwise (Abbink et al., 2017).
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Well-characterized in most studies; optimal punishment cost/fine ratio is context-dependent, but very high costs can flip the sign of the effect (Wu et al., 2016; Markussen et al., 2014).
- `all_or_nothing` (allOrNothing): Inclusion of both all-or-nothing and continuous settings, with similar qualitative findings.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Feedback about other's contributions and knowledge of game horizon both moderate effects, but are less systematically varied.

**Indirectly Informed / Contextually Discussed:**
- `default_contrib` (defaultContrib): Only a few studies manipulate framing of default action.
- `punishment_magnitude`: Most set at standard ratios (e.g., 1:3 cost:fine), varied in a few.
- `punishment_tech`: Some sophisticated institutional variants (voluntary centralization, second-order punishment, exogenous power assignment), but less data for these.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Only a subset of papers study reward in combination with punishment (Choi & Ahn, 2013; Wu et al., 2014).
- `show_punishment_id`: Only sporadically manipulated; visibility of punishers can impact reputational incentives.

**Effectively Missing:**
- Combined effects of multiple simultaneous dimensions (e.g., chat + endogenous group formation + power transfer).
- Robust field evidence for large, complex groups or real-world institutions (some field/lab studies exist, but less systematic).
- Long-term dynamic/institutional persistence (most studies use short-to-moderate time horizons).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Parameter Coverage and External Validity:** Most high-internal-validity findings are from laboratory settings with small groups over 10–30 rounds. Extrapolations to larger groups, real organizations, or societies are tenuous.
- **Missing Data for Certain Designs:** Sparse evidence for specific design dimensions or their interactions (e.g., simultaneous manipulation of chat, identity revelation, and endogenous regime choice).
- **Behavioral/Payoff Divergence:** Many studies report only behavioral outcomes or use contribution rates as proxies, which can mislead predictions if punishment is costly or misapplied.
- **Contextual Moderators and Non-Monotonic Effects:**  
  - Effects of punishment are not monotonic in cost, magnitude, or group structure; in some contexts, punishment reduces efficiency.
  - Heterogeneity in group composition (capability, valuation) and institutional context (corruption/bribery, anti-social punishment) can radically change effects, sometimes reversing expected gains.
- **Limited Long-Term/Institutional Evidence:** Most studies do not follow group dynamics long enough to study institutional persistence or unintended long-run consequences.
- **Theory/Mechanism Gaps:** While general mechanisms of deterrence, reciprocity, and norm enforcement are supported, direct mechanism mapping to design dimensions is incomplete.

---

**In summary**:  
The literature provides strong, direct empirical evidence for positive efficiency effects of punishment in standard repeated PGGs with moderate MPCR, moderate group size, and moderate punishment cost—especially in the absence of institutional or behavioral pathologies (anti-social punishment, corruption, destructive norms). However, the body of evidence also demonstrates important boundary conditions and potential reversals of this effect under certain payoffs, social, and institutional environments. Predictors should condition on the detailed game design and reported control efficiency and remain cautious in extrapolating beyond well-covered parameter regimes. Behavioral outcomes such as increased contribution rates must not be mistaken for improvements in efficiency when netting out the costs of punishment.
