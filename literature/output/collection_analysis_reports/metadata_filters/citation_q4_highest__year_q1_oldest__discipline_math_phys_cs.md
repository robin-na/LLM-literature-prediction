# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set (N=66) is predominately composed of theoretical investigations, with limited empirical and experimental evidence. The majority of papers model public goods games (PGGs), their close variants, or adjacent social dilemmas to explore the evolutionary and dynamic consequences of enabling punishment (and, occasionally, reward) on cooperation, norm compliance, and payoff-related outcomes. Most studies are not direct empirical tests with experimental data; a few laboratory experiments exist, but these are often in adjacent paradigms or lack direct efficiency outcome measurement.

The literature’s breadth is strong in modeling and mechanistic analysis of punishment effects in group social dilemmas, with some coverage (via simulation/theory) of key moderators (e.g., punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, group size, spatial structure, and norm assignment rules). However, direct empirical measurement of efficiency (as defined in the prediction task) is sparse, and many papers report behavioral outcomes (e.g., cooperation rates, punishment frequency) as proxies or correlates of efficiency.

Overall, the evidence base is **broad and deep in theory and simulation**, narrowly focused in empirical studies, and highly parameterized regarding game-theoretic design features.

# 2) Task Relevance

Task relevance—measured by (a) relation to PGG-like environments, (b) focus on punishment or sanctions, and (c) reporting of efficiency/payoff outcomes—is summarized below:

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - **Exact**: Many papers model or analyze explicit PGGs, covering both standard forms and spatial/networked/population-structured variants.
  - **Close**: Several others use games adjacent to the PGG, e.g., collective-risk, partnership, threshold, and repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma settings with public-goods-like payoff structures.
  - **Adjacent/Weak**: Some literature examines social dilemmas with comparable mechanisms but diverges further from the canonical PGG.
  - **Summary:** Majority of the papers have exact or close relevance.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - **Exact/Close**: A core subset focuses squarely on peer or institutional punishment, with careful attention to cost, effectiveness, and behavioral context.  
  - **Adjacent**: A large proportion discuss related mechanisms—reputation-based sanctions, ostracism, exclusion, reward, or norm-based 'soft' punishment—but not all operationalize punishment as in PGG experiments.
  - **None/Weak**: Some works focus on cooperation evolution, migration, or partner selection with no explicit sanctioning.
  - **Summary:** Direct focus on punishment is common, but the operationalization often varies.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - **Exact**: Some theory papers directly derive efficiency or total-group-payoff consequences of punishment in PGGs.
  - **Close/Adjacent**: Many others use cooperation rate or proportion of defectors/punishers/cooperators as proxies, inferring likely efficiency effects without direct calculation.  
  - **Weak/None**: Several papers stop at individual behavior or perceptions, without reporting group payoff or efficiency.
  - **Summary:** Exact measurement of efficiency is limited; most evidence is proxy-based or indirect.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (high task relevance):**
  - *Efficiency*: Ratio of observed payoff to full-cooperation payoff is directly analyzed in a subset of theory papers (e.g., Cressman et al., 2012; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Gintis, 2000).
  - *Explicit Group Payoff/Average Earnings/Welfare*: A core focus in several theoretical models and in some empirical lab studies (e.g., Brown et al., 2004).
  - *Surplus/Total Coins Generated*: Sometimes derived, but rarely as the primary outcome.
  - *Proxy measures inferred from strategy composition*: Many studies link the dominance of cooperative or punishing strategies to higher expected group payoffs, even when efficiency is not explicitly reported.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (moderate/low task relevance):**
  - *Cooperation/contribution rates*: Ubiquitous in both theory and empirical studies.
  - *Punishment/reward frequency*: Often measured but tells more about mechanism.
  - *Strategy frequencies, norm compliance*: Used as indicators of underlying game dynamics.
  - *Retaliation, spiteful/antisocial punishment*: Measured in behavioral studies, relevant mainly as moderators or explainers rather than outcomes.

- **Caveat:**  
  Behavioral outcomes often serve as *proxies* for efficiency but are not valid substitutes in strict prediction of group payoff.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of Cross-Paper Evidence:**

- **Punishment typically increases efficiency, but only under favorable conditions.**  
  Theoretical models consistently predict that enabling effective costly punishment in PGGs increases group efficiency compared to no-punishment (Cressman et al., 2012; Gintis, 2000; Bowles & Gintis, 2004; Gintis et al., 2001). The gain is greatest when punishment is inexpensive relative to its impact, and when group size and repeated interaction support the stability of cooperation (Henrich & Boyd, 2001; Gintis, 2000).

- **Critical moderators:**  
  - **Punishment cost and magnitude:** High-impact, low-cost punishment maximizes efficiency gains; if the cost to punishers is too high, or the effect on defectors is too weak, punishment may not support cooperation—or may even reduce efficiency (Janssen & Bushman, 2008; Szolnoki et al., 2011a).
  - **Type of punishment:** Anti-social or spiteful punishment (punishing cooperators or random punishment) can eliminate or reverse efficiency gains (Rand et al., 2010; Goette et al., 2012).
  - **Retaliation and anonymity:** When punishment is identifiable, and retaliation is possible, the efficiency effect is greatly reduced or negative (Janssen & Bushman, 2008).
  - **Population structure:** Spatial/networked games often show greater ability for punishment mechanisms to stabilize cooperation and efficiency than well-mixed populations, as cluster formation and adaptive deployment of punishment limit costs (Helbing et al., 2010; Perc & Szolnoki, 2012).
  - **Social norms and reputation:** Justified punishment rules (e.g., standing/judging) and reputation assignment that does not penalize justified punishers are vital for sustaining high efficiency (Ohtsuki & Iwasa, 2004; Chalub et al., 2006).

- **Reward versus punishment:** Adaptive punishment is generally predicted to be more effective than reward at eliminating defectors and maximizing efficiency, holding cost constant (Szolnoki & Perc, 2012).

- **Repeated interaction and information:** In repeated environments, the effectiveness of punishment for efficiency depends on the informativeness and timing of monitoring, patience (discount rate), and the ability to condition play on prior actions (Abreu et al., 1991; Brown et al., 2004).

- **Group size and MPCR:** Baseline efficiency in control conditions without punishment is often lower in large groups and low-MPCR environments, so the absolute gain from enabling punishment may be greater. However, very large groups or tiny MPCRs may make it difficult for punishment to sustain cooperation unless additional mechanisms are present (Suzuki & Akiyama, 2007).

**Disagreement and Ambiguity:**  
When anti-social punishment or revenge cycles are possible, net efficiency gains may be eliminated (Rand et al., 2010; Goette et al., 2012). Some environments with high punishment costs or easily triggered retaliation can see punishment reduce welfare relative to the control.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct literature implications:**  
  When predicting treatment (punishment-enabled) efficiency from game design and control efficiency, the core literature supports the following conditional guidance:
    - *If punishment is effective, low in cost, and mainly pro-social,* expect treatment efficiency to increase substantially relative to control, often approaching full-cooperation efficiency for moderate group sizes and MPCR.
    - *If punishment is costly, weak in impact, allows anti-social targeting or retaliation, or is visible and exposes punishers to revenge,* efficiency gains may be limited, eliminated, or reversed.
    - *Spatial structure, adaptive or context-sensitive punishment,* and justified punishment rules (not harming the punisher’s reputation) strongly support positive efficiency outcomes even as group size rises.
    - *If the baseline/control efficiency is already high (e.g., due to alternative mechanisms such as partner choice, reputation, or spatial clustering),* the marginal efficiency gain from enabling punishment may be smaller.

- **Dimension-to-outcome mappings:**
    - **player_count:** Larger groups generally reduce baseline efficiency without punishment, but punishment's impact can be more pronounced if other moderators are favorable. Relevant both directly (in models) and indirectly (via discussion).
    - **mpcr:** Higher MPCR = easier to sustain cooperation and efficiency with punishment.
    - **punishment_cost/punishment_tech:** Key determinants of whether punishment enhances or erodes efficiency. Directly addressed in many models.
    - **all_or_nothing:** Modeled in both threshold and continuous contribution games but with less direct evidence for fine quantitative predictions.
    - **chat/communication, show_other_summaries:** Communication can itself raise efficiency, but also interacts with punishment by enabling coordination or norm emergence.
    - **show_punishment_id:** Anonymity reduces retaliation risk and increases the net effect of punishment.
    - **reward_exists, reward_cost:** Reward generally less effective than punishment; optimal efficiency occurs when both are well-calibrated.
    - **show_n_rounds, num_rounds:** Repetition and known horizon affect equilibrium selection; more rounds and patience facilitate efficiency gains from punishment.

- **Cautions:**  
  Do not assume monotonic increase in efficiency with punishment enabled—account for moderators such as anti-social punishment rules, retaliation, or high cost structures.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Strong, directly informed:**
- *player_count*: Modeled in almost all theory papers with considered variation and discussion.
- *mpcr (Synergy Factor)*: Principally manipulated and deeply analyzed.
- *punishment_cost*, *punishment_tech / punishment_magnitude*: Nearly all core models analyze cost/benefit of punishment.
- *num_rounds*: Both one-shot and repeated game models feature heavily.
- *all_or_nothing*: Considered directly in several models, especially threshold or binary-action games.

**Indirectly informed / contextually discussed:**
- *chat*: Some coverage in context of communication effects but not central to most payoff models.
- *show_n_rounds*, *show_other_summaries*: Occasionally discussed as information dimensions or in empirical protocols.
- *show_punishment_id*: Modeled in context of retaliation/visibility effects.
- *reward_exists*, *reward_cost*, *reward_tech*: Addressed in several models, with comparison to punishment efficacy.
- *spatial structure / population structure*: Strong coverage, though not listed in the 14 dimensions.

**Sparse or missing:**
- *default_contrib*: Rarely modeled explicitly.
- *social learning/framing*: Discussed in mechanism papers, but not as formal dimensions in quantitative prediction.
- Some aspects of empirical implementation (e.g., demographic variation, context effects) are largely unaddressed in this model-heavy set.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical Gaps:**  
  Few studies provide direct experimental or field-based measurement of efficiency change with and without punishment using the full 14-dimension design space. Most findings are theoretical or simulation-based.

- **Payoff-Behavior Distinction:**  
  Many conclusions about efficiency are inferred from cooperation rates or strategic stability rather than measured group payoff. This introduces risk of overestimating efficiency gains, particularly in the presence of costly or misdirected punishment.

- **Complex Interactions and Scope Conditions:**  
  Crucial moderators such as anti-social punishment, retaliation potential, punishment visibility, social norm structure, and population structure are sometimes modeled in isolation; results may not generalize when these interact.

- **Parameter Space Coverage:**  
  Not all design dimensions are equally explored; especially sparse are empirical manipulations of chat, default contribution framing, and complex information treatments.

- **Quantitative Prediction Limits:**  
  Most theory papers specify qualitative or conditional relationships; exact effect sizes, especially for large or atypical parameter settings, are not generalizable.

- **Adjacent Literature Warning:**  
  Some highly cited findings are for adjacent games (repeated PD, ultimatum, ostracism, reputation), which may not transfer directly to PGGs with explicit costly punishment.

- **Framing and Context Effects Largely Absent:**  
  Subtle contextual factors (framing, cultural variation, implicit norm salience) identified as influential in adjacent work are not parameterized or are treated as background, so prediction accuracy is at risk in new settings.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature base provides strong, theoretically grounded, but mostly qualitative and conditional guidance on how enabling peer punishment is expected to affect group efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments, conditional on critical design dimensions. Most design parameters needed for prediction are directly or indirectly informed, but quantitative empirical data for model validation is limited. The risk of mispredicting efficiency due to unmodeled moderators or over-reliance on behavioral proxies should be explicitly acknowledged in any application of this literature to real-world or novel game designs.
