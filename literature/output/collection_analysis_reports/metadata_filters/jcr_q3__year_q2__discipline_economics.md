# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 33 papers, with a strong representation of **lab experimental studies** (empirical), supplemented by a substantial number of **theoretical and modeling papers**. The empirical papers predominantly use linear or variant **public goods games (PGGs)** and related repeated social dilemmas to analyze punishment, reward, and efficiency. The theoretical work explores efficiency, punishment design, equilibrium selection, network effects, and norm evolution in environments closely related to or including PGGs.

Coverage is **broad and multidisciplinary**, ranging from exact matches to standard PGGs with implemented punishment institutions and direct efficiency results, to adjacent games (CPRs, IPDs, duopolies, trust games) with transferable mechanisms, and theory papers offering general principles and explicit parameter boundaries. A **minority of papers** focus mainly on behavioral (not payoff) outcomes or on games where punishment is not a variable, thereby functioning mainly as controls or context.

**Empirical findings** provide the clearest actionable evidence for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency, while theory work identifies boundary conditions, moderators, and mechanism-level implications but less often gives direct quantitative payoff predictions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Relevance dimensions:**
- **pgg_or_variant:** Many papers are of **exact relevance** (standard PGGs or direct variants), others are **close** (CPR, insurance pool, repeated PD with punishment options), and a number are **adjacent** (trust games, networks, mechanism design, evolutionary models).
- **punishment_or_sanctions:** Several papers are of **exact** relevance (peer/imposed/costly punishment as explicit design variable), while some provide **close** or **adjacent** coverage (exclusion, gradual vulnerability, reversal of punishment, endogenous sanctioning, or empirical absence of punishment).
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** About half the set report **efficiency or group payoff** as a primary outcome (**exact/close**), while others focus on cooperation/contribution behavior (**adjacent**) or on non-payoff-based outcomes (**weak/none**).

## Matrix of Relevance:
- Studies like **Faillo et al. (2013)**, **Zheng & Nie (2013)**, **Dannenberg (2016)**, **Kamijo et al. (2014)**, **Reuben & Riedl (2013)**, and **Rockenbach & Wolff (2016)** are **exact** on all three dimensions.
- Theory papers (e.g., **Hwang (2017)**, **Olcina & Calabuig (2015)**, **Buchholz et al. (2014)**) are **exact/close** on payoff and punishment, and **exact/adjacent** on pgg-ness.
- Papers on related games without direct punishment variables (e.g., **Tan & Wei (2014)**, **Figuières et al. (2013)**) are primarily **adjacent/weak/none** for punishment/payoff outcomes.
- For papers addressing only behavioral (not payoff) outcomes or for environments not based on PGGs, relevance for the prediction task is **limited**.

**Summary:** The literature set is **highly relevant** for the prediction task, especially regarding exact/close analogs to PGGs and explicit punishment treatments, though some evidence is best interpreted as boundary-defining or explanatory rather than directly predictive.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based outcomes:** Many papers report **group payoff, efficiency (payoff as % of full coop), welfare, and average earnings**. These are consistently used as the metric by which the impact of punishment is assessed (**Faillo et al., 2013; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Dannenberg, 2016; Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016**).
- **Closely related:** Some theory and simulation papers give **explicit equilibrium payoffs, welfare bounds, and efficiency constraints** (**Olcina & Calabuig, 2015; Hwang, 2017; Buchholz et al., 2014; Laclau & Tomala, 2017; Chassang & Zehnder, 2016**).
- **Behavioral outcomes (not payoffs):** Contribution rate, cooperation/compliance rate, harvest ratio, frequency/intensity of punishment, emergence of contribution norms, and similar process measures are sometimes reported as proxies or correlated outcomes (**Gatiso & Vollan, 2017; Greiff & Paetzel, 2016; Hsu, 2013; Wilson & Wu, 2017**), but must be distinguished from efficiency itself.
- **Ambiguous cases:** A few studies (e.g., on insurance pools or CPR games) measure **efficiency or group payoff** alongside rich behavioral data, allowing partial inference of efficiency effects.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Cross-paper synthesis:**

- **Punishment can (but does not always) increase efficiency:** Direct PGG experiments demonstrate that enabling *unrestricted* peer punishment sometimes results in **no gain or even a decline in efficiency** due to antisocial punishment costs (**Faillo et al., 2013; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016**). When punishment is **legitimate, restricted to free-riders, and supported by transparency or feedback**, significant efficiency gains are observed (**Faillo et al., 2013; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Dannenberg, 2016**).
- **Structure of punishment matters:** **Legitimacy, targeting, identifiability of punishers, and institution type** (e.g., absolute vs. relative, centralized vs. peer) are usually necessary for efficiency improvements. Antisocial/retaliatory or ambiguous punishment regimes can reduce or reverse the benefits (**Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Dannenberg, 2016**).
- **Group size and feedback/information:** Efficiency gains from punishment **depend on group size** and the nature of feedback. In *larger groups*, identifiability of punishers and legitimacy can help maintain or even improve efficiency (**Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017; Buchholz et al., 2014**).
- **Role of agreements and communication:** The presence of a **shared agreement or strong communication channel** boosts the effectiveness of punishment by helping coordinate expectations and reducing antisocial punishment, leading to higher efficiency (**Dannenberg, 2016; Zhosan & Gardner, 2013**).
- **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** **High punishment costs** relative to deterrence value can cause net efficiency losses. Lower costs and/or higher effectiveness yield better outcomes, up to a point—if punishment is *too easy* or *not proportionate*, efficiency can again fall (**Olcina & Calabuig, 2015; Kamijo et al., 2014; Wilson & Wu, 2017; Chassang & Zehnder, 2016**).
- **Coordination among punishers:** Punishment is most effective when *coordinated* (either via explicit mechanisms or endogenous social learning/peer pressure) so antisocial punishment is minimized and signaling is possible (**Hwang, 2017; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015; Kopányi-Peuker et al., 2017**).
- **Nonlinear effects, context dependence, and tradeoffs:** The efficiency effect of punishment is *not* monotonic—overuse or inefficient application can reduce efficiency, and institutional details (such as who can punish, whether punishment is endogenous, and whether reputation is available) are critical for outcomes (**Wilson & Wu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016**).
- **Robustness in heterogenous populations:** Efficiency gains from punishment appear robust to endowment heterogeneity, but the specific norm enforced may differ (equal vs proportional contributions; **Reuben & Riedl, 2013**).
- **Absolute scale:** In well-designed and targeted punishment settings, efficiency with punishment can approach full cooperation (sometimes exceeding 90–95% of social optimum; **Dannenberg, 2016; Zheng & Nie, 2013**), whereas poorly designed enforcement institutions can leave efficiency unchanged or reduced compared to control (**Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016**).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Implications for predicting treatment efficiency:**

- If **punishment is enabled via a legitimate, well-targeted, and transparent peer mechanism**, and **the control efficiency is low**, *substantial positive impact* on efficiency is likely (potentially approaching full cooperation). **Full feedback** and **norm clarity** (e.g., via agreements) further increase this effect (**Faillo et al., 2013; Dannenberg, 2016; Reuben & Riedl, 2013**).
- If the punishment design is **unrestricted, anonymous, costly, or allows for antisocial punishment**, or if feedback is limited, **the efficiency effect is weakened or possibly negative** due to wasted resources or retaliation (**Faillo et al., 2013; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016**).
- The **relationship between control efficiency and punishment treatment efficiency is strongly moderated by context**: in games with already high control efficiency, introducing punishment yields limited further gains and may even decrease average payoffs due to the cost of unnecessary punishment (**Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Yu et al., 2016**).
- **Group size**: Efficiency gains from punishment can **increase, decrease, or remain stable with group size**, depending on whether coordination among punishers is feasible and whether legitimacy/feedback mechanisms scale effectively (**Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017; Buchholz et al., 2014**).
- **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** Reducing **punishment cost** and/or increasing **deterrence power** (punishment magnitude) increases efficiency up to a critical threshold; beyond that, risk of over-punishment/antisocial punishment rises (**Wilson & Wu, 2017; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015**).
- **Institutional and communication features:** The **presence of communication**, **visible agreements**, or **democratic institution choice** can moderate punishment effects (either reinforcing efficiency gains or in some cases substituting for punishment altogether; **Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Dannenberg, 2016; Gatiso & Vollan, 2017**).
- **Prediction procedure recommendation:** Use a **conditional mapping** approach: given the control efficiency and detailed design dimensions (as listed in the 14 predictors), assign a **higher predicted efficiency** for punishment-enabled treatments when:
    - Punishment is legitimate, proportionate, and visible;
    - Communication/feedback/norms are strong;
    - Punishment is cost-effective;
    - Antisocial use is likely to be low (e.g., via institutional constraints).
  Conversely, **adjust or lower the efficiency prediction** if design features suggest high risk of poor targeting, antisocial punishment, or inefficient cost structure.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed (Frequently analyzed with payoff impacts):**
- **player_count**: Varying group sizes (4, 8, 12, and larger), clear moderator of the punishment effect (**Zheng & Nie, 2013; Hwang, 2017; Buchholz et al., 2014; Faillo et al., 2013**).
- **num_rounds**: Ranges typically 10–20 rounds; length of play affects learning, sustainability, and institution choice (**Faillo et al., 2013; Dannenberg, 2016; Kamijo et al., 2014**).
- **mpcr**: Explicitly tied to incentive structure and efficiency outcomes (**Faillo et al., 2013; Kamijo et al., 2014; Dannenberg, 2016**).
- **punishment_cost**: A major moderator across empirical and theoretical work; efficiency gains only realized when costs are not prohibitive (**Faillo et al., 2013; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Olcina & Calabuig, 2015; Wilson & Wu, 2017**).
- **punishment_tech** (how punishment is implemented; e.g., peer, centralized): Specific focus in experimental and theory work (**Kamijo et al., 2014; Faillo et al., 2013; Chassang & Zehnder, 2016**).
- **all_or_nothing**: Binary vs. continuous contributions often discussed in effect moderation, especially in theory papers.

**Indirectly informed / contextually discussed:**
- **chat**: Presence of communication is a robust moderator; its effect on efficiency is consistently positive and interacts with punishment (**Zhosan & Gardner, 2013; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Gatiso & Vollan, 2017**).
- **show_n_rounds**/**show_other_summaries**/**show_punishment_id**: Feedback and information provision (transparency, identity visibility) are important for the legitimacy and effect of punishment, covered in some experimental designs (**Faillo et al., 2013; Zheng & Nie, 2013**).
- **reward_exists**, **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**: Direct empirical attention is rare (most evidence is on punishment), but some theoretical studies and a few experiments touch on combined punishment-reward regimes with payoff outcomes (**Greiff, 2013; Chassang & Zehnder, 2016**).
- **default_contrib**: Contribution framing (opt-in/opt-out) occasionally discussed, but effect on punishment-outcome link is not a main theme.

**Sparse or missing:**  
No papers explicitly analyze or vary **default_contrib_prop** in the context of the punishment/efficiency link. **Reward-related dimensions** are occasionally modeled/theorized but lack systematic empirical evidence in this set. The effect of **show_punishment_id** is present in some, but not explored broadly.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Antisocial punishment and context specificity:** Results depend critically on institutional design—if antisocial punishment is permitted, or if punishment legitimacy is weak, the effect on efficiency can be null or negative. Many design dimensions interact in non-additive ways, making simple extrapolation risky.
- **Control efficiency dependency:** Where control (no-punishment) efficiency is already high, the addition of punishment can be wasteful and reduce average payoff.
- **Heterogeneity of settings and external validity:** Laboratory PGGs, CPRs, and adjacent repeated games may not generalize to large-scale or field environments where social structure, non-monetary costs, institution design, and learning dynamics are more complex.
- **Limited variation on some predictors:** Some institutionally relevant variables (e.g., contribution framing, rich communication protocols, reward cost/technology) are under-explored empirically with direct payoff measurement.
- **Long-run vs. short-run outcomes:** Some theory suggests that dynamic or evolutionary welfare can be reduced by punishment even if short-run efficiency is higher; most lab experiments do not address long-run learning or endogenous social preference formation (**Povey, 2014; Yu et al., 2016**).
- **Aggregation ambiguity:** In heterogeneous populations, efficiency gains may coexist with increased inequality, or with different underlying contribution norms (**Reuben & Riedl, 2013; Lévy-Garboua et al., 2017**).
- **Ambiguity around first-order vs. second-order punishment effects:** Many models focus on primary punishment (punishing defectors), but real-world environments often see retaliatory, antisocial, or strategic punishment, which is less frequently analyzed with payoff impacts in this set.
- **Behavioral vs. payoff outcomes:** Some findings based on behavioral outcomes alone (contribution/compliance, norm adoption) should not be treated as if they will necessarily predict efficiency gains. Payoff-based results should be prioritized for prediction.

**In summary:** While the literature set is strongly relevant and informative for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in PGG environments—especially with careful attention to detailed game design dimensions—limitations arise from design complexity, incomplete empirical attention to some predictors, and nontrivial context interactions. Ambiguity is substantial in poorly specified or misaligned institutional designs.
