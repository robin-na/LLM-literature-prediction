# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set for this literature analysis is broad in scope, comprising 65 sources including a mix of empirical (laboratory and field experiments, observational studies) and theoretical (analytical and simulation modeling) works. About a third of the papers report direct, incentivized public goods games (PGGs) or close variants; many others deal with adjacent social dilemma games (prisoner’s dilemma, common pool resources, volunteer’s dilemma, etc.). Mechanisms studied range from peer punishment, reward, and exclusion, to institutional and group-level interventions, with substantial theoretical treatment of the mechanisms by which sanctions affect group behavior and payoff. The outcome focus is somewhat diverse: while several studies provide direct measures of efficiency or group payoff, many others center mainly on cooperation/contribution rates, punishment behavior, or norm compliance.

The set provides both high-relevance, direct evidence for the efficiency effects of punishment in PGGs, and a substantial body of adjacent or mechanistic evidence. While several dimensions of public-good game design are regularly documented and manipulated, evidence coverage is more patchy for certain nuanced design features. Overall, the evidence base is sufficiently large and diverse to allow cautious synthesis, but the direct relevance to the downstream prediction task varies considerably across included studies.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **Exact relevance:** A substantial subset directly studies economic PGGs (e.g., Chen, 2022; Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Karakostas et al., 2023; Oechssler et al., 2022; others).
- **Close/adjacent:** Many examine close variants (common pool resource games, volunteer’s dilemma, iterated PD, “helping” games, n-person PD, donation games, firm/organizational analogues). Some theoretical papers abstract the dilemma structure with modifications to group structure, benefit function, or sanctioning.
- **Weak/none:** A minority are only contextually related (e.g., social capital perceptions, strategic business cooperation).
  
**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **Exact relevance:** Core empirical and theoretical works directly manipulate punishment (costly peer punishment: Sparks et al., 2024; Chen, 2022; networked punishment; institutional or tax-based punishment: Prétôt et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023; others).
- **Close/adjacent:** Several study implicit or endogenous sanctions (allocation power, exclusion, tax-funded punishment, group-level sanctions, reputation-based or emotional punishment, carbon taxes as penalty, third-party or partner selection).
- **Weak/none:** Many control or baseline conditions lack punishment (or focus on alternative mechanisms such as communication, reward, coalition formation, normative or institutional framing).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **Exact/close relevance:** Direct measures of efficiency or group payoff are reported in a subset of studies (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Chen, 2022; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Karakostas et al., 2023; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Dughera, 2022; Vinayak, 2025; Cooney, 2025; Kristensen et al., 2025; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Dasgupta & Musolesi, 2025; others). Some provide adjacent outcomes interpretable as payoff (collective investment, resilience, equilibrium reward in simulations).
- **Adjacent:** Many studies report only cooperation rate, contribution, punishment frequency, behavioral change, or norm compliance as primary outcomes—important moderators, but not equivalent to efficiency.
- **Weak/none:** Papers solely reporting attitudinal outcomes, psychological correlates, or descriptive non-payoff behaviors.

**Synthesis:**  
There is sufficient high-relevance evidence to address all three dimensions for the core prediction task, though much of the behavioral/Psych-oriented material is only indirectly informative for efficiency prediction.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes (directly matching ‘efficiency’):**  
- **Efficiency/group payoff:** Total group earnings as a % of fully cooperative outcome (e.g., Sparks et al., 2024; Chen, 2022; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Karakostas et al., 2023; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Falvey et al., 2025; Dughera, 2022; Vinayak, 2025; Cooney, 2025; etc.).
- **Total coins, welfare, resource sustainability:** Functionally equivalent to efficiency in economic experiments and some theoretical models.
- **Explicit institutional cost to achieve efficiency:** In some models (Duong et al., 2024), optimal balance of cost and outcome is key.

**Non-payoff outcomes (behavioral/proxy measures):**  
- **Contribution rate, cooperation rate:** Frequently measured, often correlated with payoff, but not equivalent (e.g., Espín et al., 2022; Wu & Sun, 2022; Kingsley & Smith-Walter, 2024; Du et al., 2023).
- **Punishment frequency/severity, exclusion, norm compliance, emotional reactions:** Ongoing focus in behavioral experimental studies and cultural/mechanistic theory.
- **Network position/degrees, group structure, coalition formation rates, communication quality:** Often used as moderators or secondary outcomes, sometimes without associated efficiency analysis.
- **Psychological, normative, framing outcomes:** Attitudinal/psychological studies rarely report efficiency or group payoff directly.

**Distinction clarity:**  
- Several behavioral studies conflate high contribution rates with efficiency; only papers explicitly reporting group payoff or welfare allow inference about actual efficiency changes due to punishment.
- Many theoretical models clarify that high cooperation rates do not guarantee high efficiency if the costs of punishment or monitoring exceed benefits.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical Synthesis (Direct PGGs):**  
- **Peer punishment can sometimes increase efficiency, but only under certain conditions.** In lab PGGs with moderate to low MPCR, peer punishment often increases cooperation and—if punishment is not excessively costly—can raise efficiency or bring it in line with control (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; van Klingeren & Buskens, 2024; Falvey et al., 2025).
- **Punishment sometimes does *not* improve efficiency, and can lower it.** Especially in heterogeneous groups (Chen, 2022), when antisocial or misapplied punishment is frequent, or when punishment inflicts high deadweight losses. Field evidence with intrinsically motivated players (Amirova et al., 2022) can show crowding-out: penalties reduce investment and efficiency.
- **Institutional punishment often outperforms individual/peer punishment.** Theory and some experiments (Prétôt et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023; Duong et al., 2024; Vinayak, 2025) show that collectively funded, targeted, or threshold-based punishment is much more likely to boost efficiency, whereas peer punishment is often underfunded and subject to second-order free riding.
- **Punishment effect depends on cost/benefit structure, technology, and group size.** If punishment is too costly or misdirected (antisocial), efficiency falls (Cooney, 2025; Kurokawa, 2023; Murase, 2025). Large groups and low detectability reduce positive effects.
- **Alternative mechanisms (reward, coalition formation, allocation rules, communication) may match or outperform punishment for efficiency.** Several papers show that endogenous allocation (Falvey et al., 2025; Karakostas et al., 2023), coalition building (Ball et al., 2025), or strong communication (Herne et al., 2023; Adams et al., 2022) increase efficiency, sometimes more robustly than punishment.
- **Critical moderators:**
    - *Player count:* Positive effects from punishment typically stronger in small/moderate groups; large groups require targeted/institutional punishment (Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023).
    - *Num_rounds:* Positive efficiency effects of punishment generally accrue over longer games; short games rarely see benefits (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
    - *Punishment cost/tech:* Low-cost, highly effective or institutionally pooled punishments are most likely to yield net efficiency gains.
    - *MPCR:* Higher MPCR makes both punishment and alternative incentive schemes more effective at improving efficiency (Falvey et al., 2025).
    - *Heterogeneity (player endowments, benefits):* Increases risk of antisocial punishment, reducing efficiency payoff from peer punishment.

**Theoretical/Mechanistic Synthesis:**
- **Peer punishment is vulnerable to underprovision and antisocial use; institutionalization is key for predictable efficiency improvement (Prétôt et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023).**
- **Sharp nonlinearities exist:** There are threshold effects (punishment only works if above critical cost/strength; Duong et al., 2024; Vinayak, 2025).
- **Punishment is not always monotonic with efficiency:** Increasing punishment strength or implementation does not always increase efficiency, especially if costs are endogenous or if antisocial punishment emerges (Cooney, 2025; Kurokawa, 2023).
- **Monitoring/observability matters:** High error in detecting defection (or low transparency) can make punishment inefficient or even counterproductive (Murase, 2025; Gallo et al., 2022).
- **Alternative conditions:** Rewards and hybrid schemes can sometimes outperform punishment-only settings for efficiency, particularly where positive reinforcement is less likely to trigger destructive antisocial behavior (Duong et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023; Falvey et al., 2025).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Directly predictive relationships:**  
    - If the design matches lab PGGs with moderate size, repeated rounds, moderate MPCR, and reasonably cheap, targeted punishment, enabling peer punishment is likely to improve efficiency somewhat over control, particularly as rounds progress and if antisocial punishment is rare (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
    - In *heterogeneous* PGGs (unequal MPCR or endowments across players), peer punishment may not improve efficiency, and can even depress it below control, due to poorly targeted or antisocial sanctions (Chen, 2022).
    - When punishment is made institutional (tax-funded, externally administered, threshold-based), the likelihood of a positive efficiency effect increases, especially when the cost and technology are well calibrated (Prétôt et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023).
    - The magnitude of effect is moderated by the control efficiency: If baseline (control) efficiency is already high due to communication or alternative mechanisms, marginal gains from punishment may be limited or even absent (Oechssler et al., 2022; Ball et al., 2025; Herne et al., 2023).
    - High monitoring noise, lack of feedback, or opportunities for antisocial punishment reduce the efficiency benefit, while designs that limit these factors (clear feedback, targeted punishments, transparent misbehavior) increase net positive effects (Murase, 2025; Gallo et al., 2022).

- **Dimension-based predictions:**
    - **player_count:** Positive punishment effects diminish with group size absent institutionalization; in large groups, rewards or collective schemes often required.
    - **num_rounds:** Short games see little effect; positive efficiency impact grows with longer repeated interaction.
    - **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Lower relative cost and high effectiveness necessary for efficiency gains. Second-order free riding (on punishers) is important—solutions must address this (Prétôt et al., 2024).
    - **mpcr:** High MPCR aids any incentive mechanism (reward or punishment) in achieving efficiency gains.
    - **chat/communication:** Can substitute for or strengthen the need for punishment; often boosts efficiency more reliably in the absence of punishment (Herne et al., 2023; Adams et al., 2022; Amirova et al., 2022).
    - **all_or_nothing, reward_exists, etc.:** Evidence is sparser—see next section.
    
- **Baselines:** Empirical and theoretical studies suggest that the *direction* of efficiency change from enabling punishment is context-dependent:
    - Positive for standard, symmetric repeated PGGs with moderate group size, calibrated punishment (Sparks et al., 2024).
    - Zero or negative for heterogeneous groups or if punishment is costly and/or antisocial (Chen, 2022; Amirova et al., 2022).

- **Ambiguity/disagreement:** Some lab and field results show efficiency reduced by punishment (Amirova et al., 2022; Chen, 2022; theory shows non-monotonicity), so predictions cannot assume universal gains from enabling punishment.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count:** Widely reported and manipulated; strong evidence for moderation of punishment effect (Sparks et al., 2024; Harrell & Wolff, 2023; Sugaya & Wolitzky, 2023).
- **num_rounds:** Key moderator, most studies specify and analyze effects over time.
- **mpcr:** Frequently varied; clear evidence for its interactive effects (Falvey et al., 2025; Du et al., 2023; Prétôt et al., 2024).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Central to both experimental and theoretical analysis; cost structure and targeting are known to shape efficiency effects.
- **chat:** Studied as a mechanism sometimes substituting for or moderating the need for punishment (Herne et al., 2023; Adams et al., 2022).
- **all_or_nothing:** Manipulated in several studies, but analysis less focused on its specific moderation of punishment effect.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Addressed in several papers contrasting reward and punishment; reward often shown more effective or less damaging (Chen, 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Falvey et al., 2025).

**Indirectly Informed/Contextual Dimensions:**
- **default_contrib:** Framing/presentation of the default is mentioned in some studies, but rarely analyzed as a moderator of punishment effect.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries:** Occasionally varied, but rarely the focus in efficiency/punishment result reporting.
- **show_punishment_id:** Sometimes mentioned (Bruner & Smead, 2022), but little direct evidence for its effect on efficiency.

**Effectively Missing or Very Sparse:**
- None of the supplied papers systematically address all 14 prediction dimensions in one factorial design.
- Very limited direct evidence on the interactive effects of design nuances such as show_n_rounds, default_contrib, show_punishment_id, or detailed reward_tech on punishment/efficiency relationship.
- Some theorized mechanisms are only indirectly supported by adjacent, non-PGG literature.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Mismatch between behavioral outcomes and efficiency:** Many studies report changes in contribution/cooperation rate or punishment frequency without reporting efficiency or group payoff, which can obscure whether observed behavioral “improvements” actually translate into net welfare gains.
- **Heterogeneity in punishment form (peer, institutional, exclusion, endogenously chosen):** Not all mechanisms are directly comparable, and relative effectiveness depends heavily on context and implementation details.
- **Antisocial punishment and heterogeneous group settings:** Some findings (e.g., Chen, 2022) suggest that in real-world, heterogeneous groups, punishment may not only fail to improve but may even reduce efficiency.
- **Crowding-out of intrinsic motivation:** Some field/lab studies show that punishment undermines intrinsic motivation to cooperate or invest (Amirova et al., 2022; Hakimov & Kajackaite, 2024).
- **Sparse or missing evidence on design nuances:** Data on certain design features (punishment/reward technology, identification, information released) is thin, making extrapolation for those dimensions precarious.
- **Theoretical–empirical gap:** Some strong claims are based on theoretical models with restrictive or stylized assumptions; empirical confirmation, especially in field or high-stakes settings, is more limited.
- **Limit on generalizability:** Most evidence comes from small-group, high-control laboratory games. Extensions to real-world populations, large-scale public goods, or diverse cultural settings must emphasize potential differences in mechanisms and incentives.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature provides moderate to strong support for using game design dimensions—especially group size, round structure, MPCR, and punishment cost/technology—to predict the efficiency effect of enabling punishment, *conditional* on baseline efficiency. The direction and magnitude of the effect are context-dependent, with a consistent pattern that institutionalized or well-targeted, sufficiently strong, low-cost punishment in moderately sized, symmetric groups improves efficiency, but that in heterogeneous, large, or high-cost punishment settings, efficiency gains are uncertain or negative. The effect is further modulated by communication and availability of alternative mechanisms (reward, allocation, coalitions). Sparse evidence on several nuanced design features calls for cautious extrapolation for their specific moderating effect. Strong caution is warranted for applying laboratory findings wholesale to field contexts or to games with significant heterogeneity or intrinsic motivation.
