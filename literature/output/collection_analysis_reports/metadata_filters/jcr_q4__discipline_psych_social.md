# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises a broad and well-developed body of literature, with a strong mix of **empirical experimental studies** (lab and field) and **theoretical models**. Empirical coverage is skewed toward laboratory experiments with standard public goods game (PGG) designs, but also includes adjacent and variant games (minimum effort, trust, bargaining, generalized exchange, CPR, and delegation/ultimatum games). Several theoretical papers provide mechanism analyses (e.g., repeated games, trigger strategies, network structure, institutional versus peer punishment). Payoff-based outcomes are well represented in the core PGG studies, but evidence is notably thinner in adjacent or less standard environments. The paper set is relatively rich for the **prediction task** as defined, but there are design dimensions and moderating factors (such as communication, institutional variation, and culture/society) that are not universally or systematically addressed.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact:** Substantial portion of the set consists of standard or linear PGGs and direct variants (e.g., Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Cobo-Reyes et al., 2022; Castillo et al., 2021; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **close or adjacent:** Several studies examine closely related environments—generalized exchange, team/threshold games, CPRs, repeated PDs, coordination, and delegation/bargaining games—offering supporting mechanism insights but with differences in structure and payoff mapping.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact:** Core PGG studies directly manipulate peer or centralized punishment (Suleiman & Samid, Cobo-Reyes et al., Castillo et al., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, Botta et al.).
- **close/adjacent:** Many discuss adjacent forms (e.g., exclusion, replacement, reward, redistribution, social feedback, or implicit/voluntary punishment).
- **weak/none:** A minority focus only on reward, reputation, voluntary participation, monitoring, or partner choice, offering only indirect or contextual evidence.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact/close:** Several studies report explicit group earnings, efficiency, welfare, or surplus. Some provide only adjacent evidence—contribution rates, cooperation, or subjective welfare as proxies—cautioning against treating behavioral outcomes as direct stand-ins for payoff-based efficiency.

**Summary:** Overall, there are multiple **exactly relevant** papers on all three axes (game structure, punishment, and payoff-based efficiency), especially for standard PGG environments. Evidence is **less direct** for adjacent or structurally different games and for some context variables (e.g., society/culture).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes:**  
  - Group efficiency (average group earnings as a share of the full cooperation optimum; e.g., Suleiman & Samid, Cobo-Reyes et al., Castillo et al., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, Botta et al., Kanitsar, Zhang & Pei).
  - Average total or individual payoff, welfare, surplus, or coins generated.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  - Contribution and cooperation rates, behavioral compliance, frequency or intensity of punishment/reward given (e.g., Windmann et al., Thöni).
  - Norm compliance, sanctioning/anti-social punishment rates, attitudes toward punishment.
  - Replacement and exclusion rates, partner choice, willingness to punish, reported sentiment or expectations.

- **Distinction in Use:**  
  Many studies report both but emphasize that increases in contribution or cooperation do **not** automatically guarantee higher efficiency, especially when punishment is costly or antisocial punishment occurs.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Enabling Peer or Centralized Punishment Increases Efficiency in Standard PGGs:**  
  - Most core experimental PGG studies (Suleiman & Samid, Cobo-Reyes et al., Castillo et al., Botta et al.) find that adding punishment increases both contributions and group efficiency, though the effect varies with punishment's cost/tech, group composition, and broader social context. In centralized punishment (manager/authority), efficiency gains are robust; peer punishment effectiveness depends more on group norms and the ability to avoid costly retaliation/antisocial punishment.

- **Magnitude, Moderators, and Design Sensitivity:**  
  - Efficiency gains from punishment can be **dampened or nullified** if (a) punishment cost is high, (b) punishment permits retaliation/feuding (Engelmann & Nikiforakis), (c) norm-keepers/antisocial punishers are prevalent, or (d) punishment network/density is weak (Kanitsar).
  - The **structure of the punishment institution** (single vs. multiple stages, anonymous IDs, retaliation possibility, formal vs. informal, peer vs. institution) critically moderates the efficiency effect.
  - **Open vs. Closed Groups:** Efficiency gains from punishment are larger in open societies with endogenous group dynamics (Cobo-Reyes et al.).
  - **Power Asymmetries:** If punishment is controlled by selfish or powerful minorities, efficiency may rise only slightly or even decline (Eldakar et al.).

- **Adjacent Games and Mechanisms:**  
  - In coordination and team investment games, punishment sometimes fails to increase net efficiency, often due to high/direct punishment costs (Calabuig et al., Abbink et al.).
  - Minimum-effort (weakest link) and stag-hunt-like games often see **positive net efficiency effects** from punishment, but only if punishment aligns with efficiency goals (Lec et al., Gueth & Otsubo).

- **Theoretical Results:**  
  - Most theoretical models confirm the **conditionality** of punishment's efficiency effect: its efficacy depends on parameter thresholds—punishment cost/effectiveness, cooperation cost/benefit ratio, frequency, and opportunity for retaliation.
  - Some models warn that under standard peer punishment (costly, decentralized), efficiency may **not** always rise; costs can offset gains from higher contributions (Zhang & Pei).

- **Reward and Monitoring:**  
  - Peer/majority-vote rewards can also increase efficiency, but less robustly and mostly in homogeneous groups (Peng).
  - Monitoring and transparency alone can **reduce** efficiency when punishment is not available and conformity drives down cooperation (Becchetti et al.).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Direct Implication:**  
  Where the **control game (no punishment)** is a standard PGG, and when adding **peer or institutional punishment** with reasonable cost and effectiveness (and when design avoids features that promote retaliation, feuds, or antisocial punishment), enabling punishment should **increase average efficiency** relative to control. The size of the gain depends on:
    - **Punishment Cost/Effectiveness:** Lower-cost, higher-effectiveness punishment promotes greater efficiency gains; high-cost, low-impact punishment can offset contribution gains.
    - **Punishment Tech and Institutional Design:** Limiting punishment to one anonymous stage, disabling or hiding identity, and preventing retaliation/supporting formal mechanisms all favor efficiency gains (Engelmann & Nikiforakis; Cobo-Reyes et al.).
    - **Social/Norm Composition:** Cultural or group variation in the prevalence of strong reciprocators vs. norm-keepers modulates the size of the effect; more reciprocators → larger efficiency gains (Suleiman & Samid).
    - **Openness and Group Formation:** Efficiency gains from punishment are higher in open societies/migration-allowed environments.
    - **Other Moderators:** Density of punishment networks, symmetry of power, and psychological factors (probability weighting, prospect theory) also matter.

- **Caveats and Exceptions:**  
  - **Adjacent/Non-Standard Games:** Some environments (e.g., team investment, high punishment cost, dense information/retaliation opportunities, or uncoordinated settings) see **no efficiency gain or even losses** from enabling punishment.
  - **Design Dimension Interactions:** Efficiency increases may be absent if the interplay between design features allows for persistent antisocial punishment, frequent feuds, or weak deterrence.

- **Use of Control Efficiency:**  
  - The **magnitude of the efficiency boost** from punishment depends on baseline (control) efficiency: when the control is high (nearly full cooperation), the marginal gain from punishment is likely small; when control efficiency is low (due to persistent free-riding), punishment produces more pronounced efficiency gains, if costs are not excessive.

- **Indirect/Inferential Prediction:**  
  - In cases where payoff-based outcomes are missing and only contribution rates/cooperation are reported, **cautious upward adjustment** of efficiency can be inferred but with uncertainty, especially if punishment is costly or antisocial.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Most Directly Informed:**
- **player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Regularly specified in empirical PGG studies and theoretical models. Effects of these variables on efficiency gains are relatively well supported.
- **all_or_nothing, centralized vs. peer punishment:** Several papers compare binary vs. continuous contributions, and centralized vs. peer punishment.
- **chat:** Some papers include/no chat, but most do not manipulate or analyze its role in conjunction with punishment.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** Present in some studies (especially reward vs. punishment comparisons), but less central.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Occasionally addressed (e.g., information/monitoring studies), but less often manipulated specifically to inform treatment efficiency.

**Indirect/Contextually Discussed:**
- **default_contrib:** Rarely explicitly manipulated.
- **Group openness/migration (societal openness):** Covered (Cobo-Reyes et al.) but not as a standard design dimension.

**Effectively Missing:**
- Some nuances of **communication structures**, **framing effects**, detailed **identity revelation settings** (beyond a few studies), and **population heterogeneity in punishment preferences** are sparsely covered or only indirectly addressed.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **External Validity:** Results are strongest for standard lab PGGs; generalizability to field or broader social environments (e.g., with diverse populations or real stakes) is more limited. Field evidence sometimes contradicts lab results (e.g., Noussair et al.).
- **Coverage of Design Interactions:** Most studies manipulate a small subset of design dimensions at a time; systematic variation across high-dimensional design space is absent. This limits precision in predicting combinatorial effects.
- **Ambiguity in Mechanisms:** Empirical findings sometimes disagree with theoretical predictions, especially in adjacent games or environments with complex incentives. The net effect of punishment is contingent on nuanced interaction of group composition, institution, and design features.
- **Payoff vs Behavior:** Proxy outcomes (contribution rates, cooperation) do **not** guarantee efficiency gains; costs of punishment and the prevalence of antisocial punishment can lead to net efficiency losses, even when behavioral indicators improve.
- **Societal and Cultural Moderators:** The effect of group/cultural composition (reciprocators, norm-keepers, antisocial punishers) is important but often unobserved and hard to map from game design alone.
- **Sparse Dimensions:** Some relevant design parameters (default contributions, detailed feedback, or the role of identity and chat) are not well covered, constraining prediction accuracy for those settings.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature provides strong, direct evidence for prediction of treatment efficiency in **standard PGG environments** when enabling punishment, with the effect broadly positive but heavily moderated by **punishment cost/tech, institution design, network structure, group composition, and social context**. For less standard/adjoining game environments, evidence is more mixed or conditional. Several design dimensions are well supported, but not all; predictions outside these empirically-studied parameter ranges require greater caution. Efficient prediction further demands attention to not conflate increases in cooperation rate with improvements in group payoff, particularly when punishment is costly.
