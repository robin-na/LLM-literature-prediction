# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of 12 empirical papers, predominantly laboratory experiments, with one field experiment and one agent-based simulation supplementing empirical findings. The set is somewhat broad in theme—covering various social dilemma games and interventions—but narrow in direct relevance to peer punishment effects in standard public goods games (PGG). Only a subset of the studies reports direct efficiency or payoff-based outcomes for PGGs with or without peer punishment. Several papers investigate variants (e.g., common-pool resource games, trust games, allocation games), or focus on behavioral or normative changes rather than on efficiency as an outcome. The evidence base is thus strong for behavioral mechanisms and broader social dilemma contexts, but relatively limited in tightly matching the downstream prediction task requirements, especially regarding payoff-based treatment effects of peer punishment in standard PGGs.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Dimension 1: Public Goods Game or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`)**

- **exact relevance:** 6/12 papers employ standard or very close variants of PGGs (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016; Villatoro et al., 2014; Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015; Noussair et al., 2015).
- **close/adjacent:** Several others use related dilemmas (common-pool resource games, trust/reputation games, allocation games), which are adjacent in structure or incentive.
- **none:** Only a minority are unrelated.

**Dimension 2: Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`)**

- **exact relevance:** Only a few studies explicitly implement standard peer punishment in PGG or a close variant (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016; Villatoro et al., 2014; Noussair et al., 2015).
- **adjacent/indirect:** Some examine third-party/exogenous punishment or monetary vs. non-monetary approval (Hashim et al., 2014; Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014).
- **none:** Several do not incorporate any form of punishment (Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015; Becchetti et al., 2015; Li & Xiao, 2014; Schram & Charness, 2015).

**Dimension 3: Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`)**

- **exact relevance:** Only two papers report efficiency or group payoff directly and specifically for PGGs or very close variants with and without punishment (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Becchetti et al., 2015).
- **close/adjacent:** Efficiency or earnings is measured in related game forms (Hashim et al., 2014; Li & Xiao, 2014).
- **weak/none:** Most others focus on behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, punishment rates) and do not report group payoff or efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

## Payoff-Related Outcomes
- **Efficiency (group payoff relative to maximum possible):** Only Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) and Becchetti et al. (2015) report this directly, with the former comparing treatments with/without punishment.
- **Group earnings, welfare, total profit:** Reported directly in some adjacent or variant settings (e.g., Hashim et al., 2014; Li & Xiao, 2014).

## Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes
- **Contribution rate / cooperation rate:** Most commonly reported (e.g., Villatoro et al., 2014; Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016; Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015).
- **Punishment frequency, assignment, antisocial punishment:** Detailed in some studies (e.g., Peysakhovich & Rand, 2016; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Norm compliance / social approval-disapproval:** Often the main focus in non-payoff studies (e.g., Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014; Schram & Charness, 2015; Villatoro et al., 2014).

Most studies focus on behavioral shifts rather than direct group welfare or efficiency outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesis of cross-paper findings (distinguishing payoff-based from behavioral):

- **Peer punishment can increase efficiency, but only under constrained conditions**: In standard PGGs with single-stage, anonymous, non-retaliatory punishment, efficiency (group earnings as a percent of the cooperative optimum) rises, especially over time, as seen by Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015, SP treatment). However, when the punishment institution is unconstrained (multiple stages, fixed IDs, full information about punishers), efficiency gains disappear due to high punishment costs and feuding (RP treatment), despite increased contributions.
- **Efficiency impacts are contingent on punishment structure**: Structural aspects such as the number of punishment stages, anonymity, and information conditions critically moderate the effect-size and even sign of punishment on efficiency.
- **Evidence from adjacent domains is consistent but less precise**: Agent-based and behavioral lab studies confirm that coordinated, group-level or highly salient punishment signals increase cooperation (Villatoro et al., 2014), and sometimes, by extension, might improve efficiency, but they do not measure this directly.
- **Field evidence is more mixed**: In real-life common-pool resource contexts, peer punishment and reward do not increase cooperation or behavioral proxies for payoff (Noussair et al., 2015), suggesting limited or context-specific transfer from lab to field.
- **Role of monitoring/public feedback (without punishment)**: Without sanctions, increasing transparency can reduce efficiency (Becchetti et al., 2015), undermining the notion that monitoring is always beneficial. Monitoring alone is not a substitute for punishment in sustaining cooperation.
- **Non-payoff interventions (priming, advice, observability) matter for behavior, but not for direct payoff**: A range of studies show that prosocial behavior (e.g., contributions, cooperation) can be shifted by priming, advice, or observability (Schram & Charness, 2015; Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015), but these do not map directly to changes in group efficiency.
- **Punishment costs can offset contribution increases**: Even when contributions rise, efficiency may stagnate or fall if punishment costs are substantial or targeted ineffectively (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Evidence-Informed Predictions**:

- **Cautious expectation of treatment efficiency gains from peer punishment in standard, simple PGGs:** When the basic design is a standard linear PGG (small group, moderate MPCR, no chat, one punishment stage, anonymous punisher, single-stage, moderate punishment cost), the average efficiency with peer punishment enabled is likely to exceed that of an identical game with punishment disabled, particularly over repeated rounds (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Key moderating design dimensions:** Prediction models need to account for (i) number and anonymity of punishment stages, (ii) punishment cost/tech, (iii) possibility of retaliation (e.g., fixed IDs, full information), (iv) group size, and (v) baseline control efficiency. Where design enables feuds or retaliation, predicted efficiency gain should be reduced or set to zero.
- **No generalization to field settings or strong variants:** Efficiency gains from peer punishment in the lab may not generalize to field environments, real-world common pool resource problems, or games with strong framing/social capital differences (Noussair et al., 2015; Becchetti et al., 2015), so predicted treatment gains should be muted unless the task closely matches the evidence base.
- **Non-payoff behavioral shifts do not guarantee efficiency gains:** Increases in cooperation/contribution rates due to punishment or other interventions (priming, advice, visible monitoring) cannot be taken as reliable proxies for efficiency increases, since costs of punishment or negative peer effects on earnings may offset gains. Unless efficiency or welfare is measured, inference about payoff outcomes from behavioral results must remain cautious and qualified.

**Dimension-Specific Guidance**:

- **Multiple punishment stages, fixed IDs, or full information**: Predict little to no efficiency benefit from introducing punishment due to likely feuding/high punishment costs (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Reward mechanisms**: Evidence is sparse, but rebate and reward mechanisms can increase efficiency in trust/reputation games, less is known for PGGs (Li & Xiao, 2014).
- **Control game efficiency**: Where the control (punishment-off) game shows low baseline efficiency, the scope for efficiency improvement from punishment is higher—but only if the punishment system design does not facilitate antisocial punishment or escalation.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count**: Most studies report 4-person groups; effects of larger groups less well tested.
- **num_rounds**: Repeated games (10–30 rounds) most common; longer duration can shift dynamics.
- **mpcr**: Always specified, usually in the classic PGG range (e.g., 0.4, 0.25–0.5).
- **all_or_nothing**: Generally continuous, sometimes specified as all-or-nothing.
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Directly addressed in several studies, especially Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015).
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries**: Occasionally specified, with direct measurement in monitoring studies (Becchetti et al., 2015).

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**
- **chat**: Sometimes included; effect on efficiency not measured in relation to punishment.
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Present mainly in adjacent studies (Li & Xiao, 2014), not in PGGs.
- **punishment_magnitude**: Occasionally set or implied, but rarely varied experimentally or reported with nuance relevant to prediction.
- **default_contrib**: Framing is specified in design, but rarely tested for impact.
- **show_punishment_id**: Only critically discussed in Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015), where making identifiability possible sharply reduces efficiency through retaliation.

**Effectively Missing:**
- No studies systematically manipulate or report the prediction dimension of **show_punishment_id** (except Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015), **reward_cost/magnitude** (for PGGs), or **default_contrib** as a framing variable directly linked to efficiency under punishment.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse direct measurement of efficiency under punishment:** Only one paper (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015) across the set reports direct, payoff-based efficiency effects when toggling standard peer punishment, in a PGG, across more than one design. Most other studies either lack punishment, efficiency, or both.
- **Reliance on behavioral proxies in much of the literature:** Many studies focus on contributions or behavioral change, not on group-level payoff or efficiency outcomes. This restricts translation into accurate efficiency predictions.
- **Limited coverage of design space:** Few studies systematically vary key game design dimensions (e.g., multiple versus single punishment stages, identifiability, group size, MPCR, reward mechanisms) necessary for robust regression or model-based prediction.
- **Poor generalizability to field or more complex environments:** The few field experiments suggest divergence from lab findings, with punishment less effective or even counterproductive in some real-world settings (Noussair et al., 2015).
- **Ambiguity in the role of monitoring and transparency:** Some evidence indicates that public monitoring alone, absent punishment, can harm efficiency (Becchetti et al., 2015), complicating efforts to predict combined effects of transparency and punishment.
- **Heterogeneity in implementation and measurement of punishment:** Studies differ in their operationalization (peer, third-party, exogenous), timing, and salience of punishment, making pooled prediction across studies challenging.
- **Absence of systematic reporting of all 14 prediction-relevant dimensions:** Many studies lack detailed information about every relevant game design feature, so predictions must often extrapolate beyond directly measured variants.

**In sum:** The literature directly covering efficiency effects of peer punishment in standard PGGs, analytically mapped to the prediction dimensions supplied, is limited. While some robust qualitative principles emerge (notably the risk of efficiency collapse due to retaliation and the conditionality of positive treatment effects on punishment design), quantitative prediction of treatment efficiency from these studies is only strongly supported in the simplest, lab-standard conditions. Extrapolation to other designs or field settings should be made cautiously and attentively to the specific design dimensions flagged in the few informative papers.
