# 1) Evidence Base

The supplied literature set is comprehensive in scope, containing 48 papers with a strong tilt toward empirical (laboratory/field experimental) studies, several directly focused on payoff-based outcomes, and a subset grounded in public-goods-game (PGG) or close variant environments. The set is broad for the prediction task, encompassing studies on standard and non-standard PGGs, common-pool resource (CPR) games, and several adjacent paradigms (e.g., ultimatum games, helping games, evolutionary models). Most papers are empirical, offering direct behavioral and payoff data, though several only provide theoretical or mechanistic/psychological context. The empirical studies most relevant to the downstream efficiency prediction task are detailed investigations of repeated linear/step PGGs with or without punishment, often with well-documented design parameters. However, many others report only behavioral outcomes, psychological mechanisms, or observations in adjacent (non-payoff) domains.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. Public Goods Game or Variant (`pgg_or_variant`):**
- **Exact relevance:** Multiple studies examine the classic linear repeated PGG (e.g., Gächter et al., 2017; Fischer et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015), while others use clear variants like weakest-link, voluntary contribution, or punishment-enabled CPR designs.
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** Numerous studies investigate CPR games or social dilemmas with similar structure but differing feedback, extraction, or group formation dynamics.
- **Weak/none:** Some studies, especially those using ultimatum, dictator, trust, or dyadic helping games, are only contextually relevant.

**b. Punishment or Sanctions (`punishment_or_sanctions`):**
- **Exact relevance:** Several papers manipulate the presence or absence of peer or institutional punishment, varying punishment costs, technologies, and structures (centralized, decentralized, counter-punishment, ostracism).
- **Close/adjacent relevance:** A number consider social sanctioning (ostracism, exclusion), or focus on third-party punishment, which partially maps to peer sanctions in PGGs.
- **Weak/none:** Papers purely about cooperation or social learning, with no punishment treatment, are only of indirect relevance.

**c. Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome (`efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`):**
- **Exact:** About half the relevant experimental papers report efficiency, group earnings, welfare, or related payoff outcomes as primary dependent variables.
- **Close/adjacent:** Many behavioral studies report proxies (e.g., mean contribution, extraction, defection rate) and infer implications for efficiency, but do not measure group payoff directly.
- **Weak/none:** Studies focused only on individual contributions, punishment behavior, neural responses, or mechanisms have little to no direct bearing on efficiency outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Direct Payoff-based Outcomes:** Group efficiency (payoff relative to social optimum), total group earnings, welfare, and resource surplus are reported in well-aligned studies (e.g., Fischer et al., 2016; Gächter et al., 2017; Gelcich et al., 2013; Javaid & Falk, 2015).
    - Some studies provide detailed comparisons of pre- and post-punishment conditions or across variants (e.g., linear vs. weakest-link, Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
- **Indirect/Proxy Outcomes:** Mean contribution rates, extraction levels, compliance rates, or behavioral proxies for cooperation frequently appear, especially where efficiency is not computed or reported.
- **Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:** Frequency of punishment, types of punishment (antisocial/prosocial), norm compliance, ostracism, switching partners, neurophysiological or psychological correlates—these are abundant in the literature but are not direct measures of treatment efficiency and require careful mapping if used as indirect evidence.
- **Mechanistic/Contextual Outcomes:** Motivations for punishment, the effect of individual differences or social context on punishment/cooperation, neural substrates, and evolutionary or organizational mechanisms are explored often but are not suitable substitutes for group efficiency data.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment often increases efficiency, but context matters:** 
    - Standard lab PGGs show that introducing peer punishment can substantially increase efficiency, especially where baseline (control) efficiency is low due to decay in cooperation (Gächter et al., 2017; Gelcich et al., 2013). The effect is robust under both provision and maintenance frames.
    - However, punishment can fail to improve or even reduce efficiency under certain conditions:
        - **High baseline efficiency:** If cooperation/control efficiency is already high (e.g., due to strong social norms or transparency), enabling punishment can create coordination problems and decrease efficiency due to over-punishment or wasted resources (Javaid & Falk, 2015; Fischer et al., 2016 under certain conditions).
        - **Antisocial punishment:** Cultures with a tendency toward antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) suffer from reduced or negative efficiency effects (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; dos Santos et al., 2014).
        - **Information environment:** Imperfect or noisy information can reduce or reverse the efficiency benefits of punishment; centralization (assigning punishment authority) can moderate these effects, but does not guarantee improvement (Fischer et al., 2016).
        - **Corruption or exploitation of institutional punishment:** When institutional punishment mechanisms are vulnerable to bribery/corruption, punishment can significantly reduce efficiency, even below control levels (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
        - **Production technology:** The effect of punishment varies with the public good technology; in weakest-link or complementary settings, punishment can have strong positive effects (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
    - **Communication is a powerful moderator:** Adding chat or communication—especially at the punishment or accountability stage—amplifies the efficiency benefits of punishment and can neutralize the negative effects of counter-punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016).
    - **Sanction mechanism specification:** Peer punishment, centralization, and ostracism each have differing effects. Ostracism (as exclusion) reliably boosts cooperation and efficiency in dynamic or migratory group settings (Sääksvuori, 2014). The threat, rather than the frequent actual use, of sanctioning can be sufficient in many cases (Schroeder et al., 2015).
    - **Group composition/social capital:** The positive effect of punishment is exacerbated in groups with high social capital; in weak-norm or heterogeneous groups, gains are limited or short-lived (Gelcich et al., 2013; de Melo & Piaggio, 2015).
- **Absence or weakness of reward:** Peer or institutional rewards are rarely present in the most direct studies; where available, reward mechanisms (even in conjunction with punishment) are often less effective than punishment at raising efficiency.
- **Impact of explicit design dimensions:**
    - **Punishment cost:** A lower cost-to-impact ratio (e.g., 1:3) is typical in studies showing positive effects; high-cost punishment can negate benefits.
    - **Punishment institution:** Centralized sanctions are not necessarily more efficient than decentralized ones (Fischer et al., 2016).
    - **Visibility/identification:** Fixed identifiers and public summary feedback enable more effective use of punishment.
    - **Game horizon (rounds):** Effects are typically measured over multiple rounds, allowing for learning and the emergence of norm-enforcing behaviors.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **General Principle:** Punishment, when introduced into a PGG with low/moderate baseline (control) efficiency, often dramatically boosts group efficiency, by sustaining cooperation and deterring free-riding, *provided* that punishment is not subverted by antisocial motives, coordination failures, corruption, or excessive cost.
- **Key moderators:** 
    - **Chat/communication:** Presence sharply increases efficiency gains from punishment; enabling communication is typically more important than counter-punishment structure (Andrighetto et al., 2016).
    - **Production/technology (all-or-nothing):** Complementary or weakest-link designs produce greater efficiency improvement from punishment than linear VCMs (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
    - **Baseline control efficiency:** If control-game efficiency is already high (e.g., due to social information transparency), enabling punishment may reduce efficiency (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
    - **Information environment:** High noise/uncertainty about others’ actions, or misaligned punishment authority, generally weakens or reverses efficiency benefits (Fischer et al., 2016).
    - **Corruption risk:** If punishers/institutions can be bribed or co-opted, punishment reduces efficiency (Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
    - **Antisocial punishment propensity:** Cultures/settings with high antisocial punishment may see no gain or even reduced efficiency with punishment enabled.
- **Dimension-to-outcome mapping:** The most robust predictors, based on this literature, are *communication, production technology, punishment cost/efficacy, information transparency, and control efficiency*.
- **Parameter transfer:** Where parameterization matches (player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, etc.), empirical studies provide strong quantitative benchmarks for the efficiency gain or loss due to enabling punishment.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed (robust empirical grounding):**
    - `player_count` – Most studies specify (often 4 or 5).
    - `num_rounds` – Typically 10–30 rounds in repeated game studies.
    - `mpcr` – Often specified; high or low MPCR moderates effect size.
    - `punishment_cost` & `punishment_tech` – Detailed mechanisms described (usually cost-per-point and deduction multiplier).
    - `chat` – Effects of enabling/disabling systematically studied.
    - `all_or_nothing` – Linear vs. weakest-link/complementary production technologies explicitly tested for moderation effect.
    - `show_other_summaries`/`show_n_rounds` – Transparency and group feedback effects are routinely manipulated/studied.
- **Indirectly informed/contextual:**
    - `default_contrib` – Sometimes noted; opt-in/out framing less often the treatment focus.
    - `show_punishment_id` – Only occasionally manipulated (e.g., anonymity vs. fixed IDs).
    - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` – Rarely a focus in papers reporting efficiency.
- **Sparse/missing:**
    - Impact of `reward_exists`/`reward_cost`/`reward_tech` on efficiency in direct PGG environments is rarely measured in this set.
    - Effects of detailed identity display (`show_punishment_id`) or more nuanced information architectures are underexplored.
    - Interactions between multiple dimensions (such as reward and punishment joint effects, or nuanced default framing) receive little direct attention.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited generalizability outside standard lab PGGs:** Most direct efficiency results come from Western student samples in lab settings; only a handful of field or cross-cultural studies exist, and results can differ sharply in settings with strong social norms or distinct punishment beliefs.
- **Sparse data on efficiency where reward is present, or where punishment and reward are combined.**
- **Behavioral outcomes ≠ payoff:** Many papers report only on contributions, cooperation rates, or punishment frequency, not efficiency or earnings. Mapping from cooperation to efficiency is not always straightforward, especially where punishment is costly or antisocial.
- **Corruption and institutional design:** Only a few studies model the possibility of institutional failure, bribery, or perverse incentive structures, but these can be decisive for real-world prediction (cf. Muthukrishna et al., 2017).
- **Heterogeneity in punishment effects:** Strong sensitivity to culture, group composition, pre-existing norms, baseline efficiency, and even short communication windows is apparent; a single well-powered study is not alone determinative.
- **Underexplored design dimensions:** Critical game dimensions like nuanced information structures, punishment identity visibility, and reward mechanics are not well covered in studies that report direct efficiency effects.
- **Absence of long-run or real-world data:** Lab timeframes are short; effects observed over 10–30 rounds may not extrapolate to sustained, large-scale collective action contexts.

**In summary:** The literature base is strong and directly relevant for several core design dimensions (punishment, communication, MPCR, production function, information display), and provides clear guidance that prediction of efficiency effects from punishment must be sharply conditioned on the interaction of these variables and especially on the control (no-punishment) baseline efficiency. However, limitations remain regarding the effect of reward-based interventions, potential for perverse institutional dynamics, cross-cultural heterogeneity, and longer-term extrapolation. Assumptions that punishment is always efficiency-enhancing are not robust: context, punishment design, and social conditions can produce null, negative, or positive effects.
