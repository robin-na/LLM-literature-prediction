# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set comprises 55 papers, overwhelmingly theoretical in nature, with virtually all works classified as theory (no pure empirical or new experimental studies). The scope directly encompasses classic public goods games (PGGs) and their close variants, as well as adjacent social dilemma and bargaining environments, including threshold PGGs (TPGGs), repeated games, common-pool resource (CPR) games, and Prisoner’s Dilemma (PD) scenarios. Although much evidence is centered on mechanism or theory, some agent-based simulations directly measure efficiency or group payoff, while many more report only on behavioral outcomes. Overall, the evidence base for payoff/efficiency effects of punishment is broad in conceptual coverage but narrow in new quantitative data. The set provides substantial insight into the mechanisms, moderators, and design features shaping the impact of punishment on group outcomes, but with limited directly empirical or experimental evidence targeting the efficiency metric most relevant for prediction.

# 2) Task Relevance

Assessed by three core dimensions (`pgg_or_variant`, `punishment_or_sanctions`, `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`):

- **PGG or Variant:** Most papers are `exact` or `close` in relevance to public goods games or threshold variants. Papers consistently discuss classic PGG or environments that are direct theoretical extensions or laboratory analogues (TPGG, CPR, or repeated PD).
- **Punishment or Sanctions:** Roughly half of the papers provide `exact` coverage of peer or institutional punishment in the PGG context; others discuss sanctions or punishment mechanisms in adjacent games (e.g., repeated PD, social dilemmas, or bargaining) (`adjacent`). Some works address only trait-level or conceptual punishment (`weak`), while a few are contextually focused with little relevance (`none`).
- **Efficiency or Payoff:** There is a clear gradient; only a minority provide `exact` or `close` relevance—i.e., direct modeling or simulation of group efficiency or welfare. Most only offer `adjacent` or `weak` evidence, reporting on behavioral outcomes (cooperation, contribution rates, norm compliance) rather than explicit payoff-based metrics.

Overall, the literature is highly relevant to PGG and punishment mechanisms, moderatley well-informed on efficiency outcomes in theory and simulation, but sparse in direct, experimental efficiency data for PGGs with and without punishment.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes (Relevant for Efficiency):**
  - *Efficiency/group payoff/total earnings/welfare/surplus/mean reward*: Some theoretical/simulation papers explicitly measure or report these outcomes (e.g., Boyd et al., 2014; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Farjam et al., 2015; Langlois & Langlois, 2004; Nakao, 2009; Libois, 2022; Brandt & Svendsen, 2019; Heller & Sieberg, 2010; Zschache, 2012).
  - *Classically, control and treatment efficiency are compared (with and without punishment)* in threshold PGGs, PD, CPR, and repeated games.
- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (NOT Efficiency):**
  - *Contribution/cooperation rates, norm compliance, punishment/defection frequencies*: Most synthesis and review papers focus on contribution rates as the outcome of interest (e.g., Brandts & Fatas, 2012; Lazarus, 2023; Moreno & Gutierrez-Garcia, 2018; Prietula & Conway, 2009).
  - *Mechanistic/trait-level outcomes*: Many papers focus on the emergence or stability of mechanisms (e.g., strong reciprocity, reputation, or punishment propensity) or trait/dispositional correlates.

A critical distinction: only studies explicitly reporting mean group payoff, efficiency, or total earnings can directly support the prediction of treatment efficiency; most other outcomes (contribution, norm adherence) are indirect or contextually related, even if they are positively correlated under some parameter regimes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesizing evidence with the above distinctions yields these cross-paper conclusions:**

- **Punishment can increase efficiency, but conditions matter:**
  - *Small group size, high relatedness or assortment, and low punishment threshold*: Strong positive effects on efficiency (Boyd et al., 2014; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Heller & Sieberg, 2010).
  - *Large groups, low relatedness, high punishment cost, or noisy environments*: Efficiency gains from punishment are harder to realize and may not occur (Boyd et al., 2014; Farjam et al., 2015; Nakao, 2009; Handfield et al., 2016).
- **The design and cost structure of punishment are critical:**
  - *Efficient, targeted, low-cost punishment* (especially shared or endogenous): Strong efficiency gains (Kol'veková et al., 2021; Farjam et al., 2015; Langlois & Langlois, 2004).
  - *Unrestricted or antisocial punishment*: Often reduces or even reverses efficiency gains (Handfield et al., 2016; Brandts & Fatas, 2012).
  - *Institutional/centralized punishment*: Can overcome second-order free-riding and increase efficiency, but at a cost (Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- **Moderators include:**
  - *MPCR/resource abundance*: Punishment is most effective at raising efficiency when the marginal per-capita return is low (i.e., high temptation to defect, Farjam et al., 2015; Libois, 2022).
  - *Noise, monitoring efficiency, asymmetry, retaliation risk*: These can undermine or even reverse the efficiency impact of punishment mechanisms (Langlois & Langlois, 2004; Nakao, 2009; Handfield et al., 2016).
- **Behavioral mechanisms are not guarantees of efficiency increases:**
  - *Higher contributions/cooperation rates* commonly observed with punishment enabled, but net payoff/efficiency can decline due to the costliness of punishing (many reviews: Goette & Huffman, 2007; Brandts & Fatas, 2012; Sell & Reese, 2014).
- **Threshold and Endogenous Designs:**
  - *Threshold PGGs and endogenous shared punishment* are particularly effective at raising efficiency at minimal punishment cost (Kol'veková et al., 2021).
- **Adjacent evidence from repeated PD/CPR:**
  - *Contagious punishment and credible commitment* (grim triggers, VBE) can support maximal efficiency if punishment is credible and monitoring broad (Camera & Gioffré, 2014; Melkonyan et al., 2022), but not otherwise.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Implications for the prediction of treatment efficiency (punishment enabled):**

- **Simply enabling punishment does not guarantee an increase in efficiency.** Much depends on the interaction between design dimensions and the underlying control efficiency:
  - In small groups, with low punishment cost, effective monitoring, and a high temptation to defect (low MPCR), adding punishment typically boosts efficiency, often substantially, especially if the control condition exhibits low efficiency.
  - In large groups, with high punishment cost, significant antisocial punishment, poor monitoring, or noisy (error-prone) environments, enabling punishment may have a negligible or even negative impact on efficiency, even if it raises cooperation rates (Boyd et al., 2014; Farjam et al., 2015; Handfield et al., 2016).
- **Efficiency impact is conditional on system context:**
  - *Design features such as endogenous or shared punishment, visibility of punishment identity, and information feedback* (e.g., who punished, round totals displayed) can all moderate the effect, usually via adjustments to norm adherence, targeting accuracy, or reputation effects.
  - *The baseline (control) efficiency* is vital: effect sizes are larger (and more positive) in low-efficiency baseline environments—i.e., punishment "rescues" failed cooperation, but has little effect where cooperation is already high (Kol'veková et al., 2021; Farjam et al., 2015).
  - *The net effect of punishment on efficiency reflects both increased cooperation and the explicit cost deducted from group payoffs by punishment expenditures* (so in some designs, heightened punishment frequency can drive efficiency below the baseline).
- **Prediction should explicitly model key moderators:**
  - *Player count, MPCR, punishment cost, punishment magnitude/technology, threshold for cooperation/punishment, endogenous vs. exogenous punishment, monitoring/feedback features, and initial network/relatedness parameters* are all repeatedly identified as critical for anticipating the sign and magnitude of the punishment effect.
  - *Reward or bonus mechanisms* sometimes co-vary with punishment effects—they are generally less effective in shifting population states from defecting to cooperating, unless reward magnitude is high (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Toriumi et al., 2020).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- `player_count` — Strongly discussed; small groups respond much more to punishment (Boyd et al., 2014; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Kritikos & Bolle, 2004).
- `num_rounds` — Most models/theories assume repeated interactions; essential for sustaining punishment threats (Boyd et al., 2014; Camera & Gioffré, 2014).
- `mpcr` — Explicit moderator; punishment especially effective when MPCR is low (Farjam et al., 2015; Zschache, 2012).
- `punishment_cost` — Strong consensus that lower costs (relative to effect) increase likelihood of efficiency gains (Kol'veková et al., 2021; Farjam et al., 2015; Heller & Sieberg, 2010).
- `punishment_tech` — Variants (shared, targeted, endogenous, institutional) strongly impact outcomes (Kol'veková et al., 2021; Farjam et al., 2015; Brandts & Fatas, 2012).
- `all_or_nothing` — Threshold PGGs (all-or-nothing provision) inform several key findings (Kol'veková et al., 2021).

**Indirectly Informed or Contextually Discussed:**
- `chat` — Role of communication as moderator; increases cooperation (Brandts & Fatas, 2012; van Aaken, 2019), but efficiency impact is context-specific.
- `show_punishment_id`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds` — Effects tied to information/reputation and transparency, which moderate punishment effectiveness (De Silva & Sigmund, 2009; Barrett, 2020).
- `default_contrib` — Not directly manipulated, minor reference only.

**Sparse or Missing:**
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` — Less central, but a few papers on reward vs. punishment effects; rarely parameterized (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Toriumi et al., 2020).
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id` — Discussed in relation to observability/monitoring, but not always linked to treatment efficiency.
- **No papers experimentally manipulate all 14 dimensions in tandem or provide meta-analytic evidence for full parametric mapping.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of direct experimental/empirical evidence:** Most evidence is theoretical or simulation-based, with very few works reporting new, experimental, payoff-based efficiency data in PGGs with and without punishment. This sharply limits statistical modeling and effect-size calibration for prediction.
- **Overreliance on behavioral proxies:** The majority of findings pertain to contribution/cooperation rates and norm compliance, not group efficiency.
- **Parameter regimes may not map clearly:** Simulations often explore narrow parameter ranges or stylized cases, which may not extrapolate across the full design space (Kol'veková et al., 2021 explores group size 4 specifically; effects may differ at scale).
- **Potential for conflicting effects:** Some settings report efficiency losses from punishment (e.g., when antisocial punishment or high punishment cost occurs), others strong efficiency gains—often for different parameter values, leaving the direction of the marginal effect of punishment ambiguous outside well-characterized domains (Handfield et al., 2016 vs. Kol'veková et al., 2021).
- **Missing dimensions/variables:** Some predictors (e.g., default contribution framing, specific feedback or observability structures) are mostly contextually discussed, not parameterized, reducing the predictive value for those dimensions.
- **External validity cautions:** The generalizability of theoretical or agent-based results to laboratory or field PGGs is sometimes questioned; context/framing effects are robust (Naar, 2020).
- **Interaction effects not always tested:** Papers often treat moderators additively; potential high-order interactions (e.g., group size × punishment cost × network structure) are underexplored.
- **Ambiguous mapping from behavioral to efficiency outcomes:** Higher cooperation/contributions do not always guarantee efficiency gains, especially with costly or misapplied punishment.
- **Absence of effect size estimates:** Theoretical work provides sharp directional hypotheses and mechanism mapping, but does not permit quantitative estimation of treatment vs. control efficiency.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature base offers robust theoretical guidance on the structural determinants and directionality of punishment effects in PGG-like environments. It strongly supports modeling the effect of punishment on efficiency as highly dependent on group size, MPCR, punishment cost and technology, and reward structure, but offers little in the way of fine-grained, data-driven prediction of treatment efficiency across the full 14-dimensional space. Any application to prediction should carefully consider these limitations, rely on structural moderation and mechanism mapping, and avoid unqualified generalization from behavioral outcomes to efficiency.
