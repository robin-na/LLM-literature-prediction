# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set includes a mix of empirical laboratory experiments (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015; Brosig et al., 2004) and theory papers (Thöni, 2014; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Huang, 2007). Out of the seven papers, only one (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015) provides direct empirical evidence on **efficiency outcomes in public goods games (PGG)** with and without punishment. Several others (Thöni, 2014; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Huang, 2007) provide theoretical and conceptual arguments related to punishment, cooperation, and equilibrium selection, but do not report efficiency or payoff data. Two empirical studies (Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015) investigate PGGs without punishment or efficiency outcomes. Brosig et al. (2004) studies a sequential bargaining context with punishment and communication—adjacent but not identical to standard PGG. As a whole, the set is **narrow with respect to direct, empirical, payoff-based predictions for PGGs with punishment**, but broader in terms of conceptual coverage and adjacent paradigms.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **PGG or Variant:**  
  - *Exact relevance:* 4 papers directly examine or theorize standard public goods games (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Thöni, 2014; Drouvelis et al., 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015).
  - *Adjacent relevance:* 3 papers discuss structurally similar games (Prisoner’s Dilemma, sequential bargaining, trust games; Brosig et al., 2004; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Huang, 2007).

- **Punishment or Sanctions:**  
  - *Exact relevance:* Most papers include or theorize explicit peer punishment or punishment mechanisms (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Thöni, 2014; Brosig et al., 2004; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Huang, 2007).
  - *None/Weak relevance:* Drouvelis et al. (2015) and Martinsson et al. (2015) do not include punishment or sanctions.

- **Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**  
  - *Exact/close relevance:* Only Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) reports group efficiency outcomes in repeated PGGs with and without punishment.
  - *Adjacent relevance:* Brosig et al. (2004) discusses efficiency in bargaining, inferred through “equal splits,” but not in PGG.
  - *Adjacent/weak relevance:* Theory papers argue about conditions for cooperation that may, indirectly, affect efficiency, but do not report payoff data.
  - *None/weak:* Most others measure non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., contribution rates).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related Outcomes:**  
  - *Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015)*: Direct measurement of group earnings/efficiency.
  - *Brosig et al. (2004)*: Behaviorally inferred efficiency (equal splits) in sequential bargaining, not standard PGG.

- **Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  - *Thöni (2014); Drouvelis et al. (2015); Martinsson et al. (2015); Blonski & Spagnolo (2015); Huang (2007):*  
    These papers primarily report on cooperation rates, contribution behavior, punishment assignment, and/or theoretical determinants of cooperation, rather than group efficiency or total payoff.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across papers, several themes and findings relate to predicting the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency:

- **Design of Punishment Mechanism Critically Moderates Efficiency Gain:**  
  Empirical evidence from Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) shows that enabling standard, single-stage, anonymous punishment **increases efficiency** relative to no-punishment controls in repeated PGGs, but only when the punishment mechanism prevents costly feuds and retaliation. When punishment is unconstrained (multiple stages, fixed IDs, full transparency), **efficiency gains disappear** due to offsetting punishment costs, even though contributions remain high.

- **Antisocial Punishment and Its Detrimental Role:**  
  Thöni (2014) argues, via theory and empirical review, that environments fostering high antisocial punishment undermine the efficiency of peer sanctioning. While not providing payoff evidence, the paper stresses that such environments can negate efficiency gains, making the effect of punishment highly context-sensitive.

- **Communication as a Moderator (in Bargaining):**  
  Brosig et al. (2004) demonstrate, in a related bargaining game (not PGG), that allowing (especially face-to-face) communication drastically boosts efficient outcomes, regardless of the punishment mechanism. In the absence of communication, punishment parameters play a larger role, but cooperation/efficiency outcomes are generally poor.

- **Theory on Cooperation, Punishment, and Equilibrium Selection (Adjacent, Not Payoff-Direct):**  
  Theoretical work (Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015; Huang, 2007) suggests that strong, effective punishment mechanisms can, in principle, support higher cooperation; however, the relationship between design (harshness, cost, information) and actual group payoff is not directly modeled. Huang (2007) cautions that punishment can crowd out intrinsic motivation, complicating the relationship to sustained efficiency.

- **Baseline Control Game Outcomes:**  
  Drouvelis et al. (2015) and Martinsson et al. (2015) provide detailed baselines for contribution dynamics in PGGs without punishment. Their findings support the necessity of a well-specified control for accurate counterfactual efficiency estimation when punishment is introduced.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For the prediction task—given game design dimensions and control (no-punishment) efficiency, predict average efficiency with peer punishment enabled—this literature set provides the following guidance:**

- **Punishment can increase efficiency, but only under restrictive conditions:**  
  Empirical evidence clearly demonstrates that **standard, anonymous, single-stage punishment** can produce efficiency gains in repeated PGGs with control for retaliation and feuding (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015). If the implemented punishment technology is richer (multiple rounds, fixed IDs, transparent punisher identities), efficiency gains are eliminated by social conflict and increased punishment costs, even if contribution rates are higher.

- **Control game efficiency is necessary but not sufficient for prediction:**  
  High baseline efficiency in the control (no-punishment) game limits the potential improvement from enabling punishment, while low baseline efficiency makes an increase more likely—**but conditional on punishment design preventing counterproductive dynamics**.

- **Design dimensions with the largest impact:**  
  Dimensions directly related to punishment structure—especially the presence of multiple punishment stages, punisher anonymity, and information about punisher identity—can **fully moderate** (even reverse) efficiency impacts. Communication (if present), though not directly studied in PGGs, is suggested to potentially boost efficiency far beyond punishment alone (Brosig et al., 2004).

- **Indirect guidance from theory and adjacent games:**  
  Theory supports the expectation that **strong, well-targeted punishment can sustain cooperation and thus increase efficiency**, but also warns (Thöni, 2014; Huang, 2007) about antisocial punishment and crowd-out of intrinsic motivation, which can dampen or reverse gains depending on context.

- **Key risk factors for prediction errors:**  
  - Failing to account for *punishment-induced conflicts (retaliation)* wipes out gains (empirical evidence, Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
  - *Antisocial punishment* prevalence or design features that facilitate it reduce efficiency (theory and empirical review, Thöni, 2014).
  - *Communication* is a powerful efficiency booster, but its effect is only inferred from non-PGG contexts.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
  - `player_count` (all studies report this; effect generally assumed or held constant)  
  - `num_rounds` (critical for repeated game effects; frequently manipulated)  
  - `mpcr` (Marginal Per Capita Return; a key parameter, always specified)  
  - `all_or_nothing` (most use continuous; a few specify)  
  - `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech` (heavily discussed; empirically shown to moderate effects)  
  - `show_punishment_id` (via discussion of anonymity, fixed IDs, and information, especially Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015)

- **Indirectly/Contextually Informed or Sparse:**  
  - `chat` (only discussed in Brosig et al., 2004, not PGGs)  
  - `show_n_rounds` and `show_other_summaries` (reported as experimental conditions, not major predictors)
  - `default_contrib` (rarely manipulated or theorized)
  - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (not present in most included empirical PGG studies)

- **Effectively Missing for Prediction:**  
  - How `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, and `reward_tech` interact with punishment for predicting efficiency is not evaluated.  
  - The joint effects of `chat` and explicit punishment in PGGs are implied from adjacent games, not tested.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited Direct Evidence:**  
  Only one paper (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015) provides experimental, quantitative results on **efficiency changes** in repeated PGGs from enabling punishment. Most others examine behavior or theory, not group payoff.

- **Sparse Dimensional Coverage:**  
  Of 14 key predictive dimensions, only a subset—primarily those tied to punishment structure and round/repetition—are directly addressed with efficiency outcomes.

- **Generalizability Beyond Studied Designs is Uncertain:**  
  Results from adjacent games (Prisoner’s Dilemma, bargaining) and theoretical models may not transfer quantitatively to PGGs. Outcomes in settings with different `player_count`, `MPCR`, or with communication or reward mechanisms enabled are not well represented.

- **Non-payoff Outcomes vs. Efficiency:**  
  Most of the broader literature in this set measures behavioral responses (contributions, punishment assigned, norm compliance), **not efficiency or group payoff**. Efficiency impacts may diverge, especially if increased punishment costs offset gains in cooperation.

- **Ambiguity Around Antisocial Punishment:**  
  While its role in undermining efficiency is acknowledged (Thöni, 2014), conditions that foster or suppress antisocial punishment in specific experimental environments are not quantitatively mapped.

- **Absence of Data on Several Game Design Features:**  
  Critical prediction dimensions like reward mechanisms, communication within PGGs, visibility of other’s payoffs, and default contribution framing are not empirically linked to efficiency with punishment in the included studies.

- **No Evidence on One-shot PGGs:**  
  Effects of punishment on efficiency are only empirically established for repeated PGGs; implications for one-shot games remain unsupported in this paper set.

**In summary:**  
This literature set provides *qualitative and some quantitative guidance* on how and when peer punishment changes efficiency in public goods games, especially under repeated interaction with carefully controlled punishment design. However, it is not wide-ranging or comprehensive enough for high-confidence, nuanced prediction across the full span of possible game designs. Direct evidence is especially lacking for alternative mechanisms (reward, communication), more complex information structures, and many of the 14 design dimensions. Predictive models should thus be conservative and remain alert to contexts where core findings (especially regarding retaliation and punishment efficiency) might not generalize.
