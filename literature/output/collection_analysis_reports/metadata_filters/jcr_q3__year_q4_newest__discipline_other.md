# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of six studies with a mix of empirical (field, lab, and observational) and theoretical papers, though the overall set is relatively narrow for the specific prediction task of estimating efficiency changes when enabling punishment in PGG-like environments. Of these, three papers provide empirical experimental evidence (Amirova et al., 2022; Herne et al., 2023; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022), two are theory papers (Wu & Sun, 2022; Suratin et al., 2023), and one is an empirical observational paper examining real-world organizations (Holubcik et al., 2023). The empirical studies span both lab and field contexts with varying degrees of proximity to PGG constructs. Only a subset of the literature addresses all three focal areas—public goods games or close variants, explicit punishment or sanctioning interventions, and payoff-based efficiency outcomes.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- **exact:** Wu & Sun (2022) directly models PGGs;  
- **close:** Amirova et al. (2022) and Herne et al. (2023) use water governance and common pool resource games, respectively, which are close variants of PGG;  
- **adjacent:** Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022) and Suratin et al. (2023) use PGG-adjacent setups with market or policy interventions;  
- **weak/none:** Holubcik et al. (2023) is only weakly related, focusing on strategic organizational cooperation outside of a game-theoretic or experimental context.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- **exact:** Wu & Sun (2022) and Amirova et al. (2022) include explicit punishment/penalty treatments;  
- **close:** Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022) uses a tax (framed as punishment for fossil fuel use), which is close but not classic peer punishment. Suratin et al. (2023) theorizes about social and financial punishment;  
- **adjacent:** Suratin et al. (2023) (focus on social sanctions);  
- **none:** Herne et al. (2023) and Holubcik et al. (2023) do not include punishment mechanisms.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- **exact:** Herne et al. (2023) and Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022) report on efficiency or group payoff;  
- **close/adjacent:** Amirova et al. (2022) reports on investment behavior as a proxy for group efficiency; Wu & Sun (2022) and Suratin et al. (2023) focus on behavioral outcomes or theorize about payoff mechanisms;  
- **none:** Holubcik et al. (2023) does not report efficiency or payoff outcomes in group game terms.

In summary, only a minority of papers, primarily Wu & Sun (2022), Amirova et al. (2022), and Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022), have direct or close relevance across all three dimensions. The remainder contribute context but are often missing key links (especially direct measurement of payoff outcomes or inclusion of punishment).

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (efficiency, group profit, welfare):**
  - **Directly measured:** Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022) ("total group profit (efficiency)"). Herne et al. (2023) ("efficiency of resource generation").
  - **Close Proxy:** Amirova et al. (2022) ("collective investment" as a proxy for efficiency).
- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rates, norm compliance, cooperation):**
  - **Primary outcomes:** Wu & Sun (2022) measure “cooperation rates,” “total contributions”; Amirova et al. (2022) (investment); Suratin et al. (2023) (theorized norm compliance and cooperation); Holubcik et al. (2023) (synergy, innovation, knowledge-sharing).

Importantly, only Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022) provides direct, unequivocal measurement of efficiency as required for the prediction task. Wu & Sun (2022) and Amirova et al. (2022) provide non-payoff or proxy measures, which can only indirectly support efficiency predictions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment Mechanisms:**
  - **Positive effects:**  
    - Wu & Sun (2022): Punishment (particularly combined with monetary compensation for false accusations) increases cooperation rates and contributions in theory models, but direct efficiency is not measured.
    - Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022): A carbon tax ("punishment") led to increased renewable energy use and higher group profit (efficiency), but the efficiency gain was not always statistically significant.
    - Suratin et al. (2023): Theoretically argue social punishment may be more effective than financial sanctions, but provide no outcome data.
  - **Negative/Null effects:**  
    - Amirova et al. (2022): In a field experiment, penalty treatments reduced collective investment compared to no-punishment baseline, contrary to theoretical predictions—suggesting punishment can undermine collective efficiency when intrinsic motivation matters.
  - **Communication and context:**  
    - Herne et al. (2023): Communication increases efficiency, but does not study punishment.
    - Amirova et al. (2022): Communication (chat) increased investment and likelihood of high-efficiency equilibria, independent of punishment.

- **Design Features and Moderators:**  
  - The impact of punishment is heterogeneous; effects vary with communication (chat), the salience and implementation of punishment, and possibly the motivational context (e.g., intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation).

- **Efficiency Effects:**
  - Empirical evidence is mixed:  
    - Positive or neutral in game-theoretic, lab, or competitive settings (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022).
    - Negative in field settings with strong intrinsic motivation effects (Amirova et al., 2022).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For predicting efficiency when enabling punishment in PGG-like games, given design dimensions and baseline efficiency:**

- **Positive impact is possible** when punishment is salient, directly affects payoffs, and when intrinsic motivation is not central to cooperation, as in competitive or policy-driven lab environments (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022).
- **Negative impact is possible** in repeated field settings where intrinsic motivation is important—here, introducing punishment can crowd out voluntary collective action (Amirova et al., 2022).
- **Communication (chat) acts independently** to increase efficiency, but its interaction with punishment is not fully resolved.
- **Magnitude and type of punishment (e.g., financial vs. social)**, as well as design features like monetary compensation for wrongful sanctioning, may moderate effects on cooperation and, indirectly, efficiency, but direct payoff evidence for these nuances is missing in the set.
- Because explicit measurements of efficiency under punishment are rare and findings vary by context, **predictions should be conservative and context-sensitive**, relying on baseline efficiency and the motivational and communicative context of the game (lab vs. field, real vs. artificial incentives).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly informed:**  
  - **player_count, num_rounds:** These are varied in multiple experiments (Wu & Sun, 2022; Amirova et al., 2022; Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022; Herne et al., 2023).
  - **punishment_cost, punishment_tech:** Discussed explicitly in Wu & Sun (2022) and Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022).
  - **mpcr, all_or_nothing:** Used as manipulation or description in Wu & Sun (2022), Herne et al. (2023), and Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022).
  - **chat:** Directly manipulated and analyzed in Amirova et al. (2022) and Herne et al. (2023).
  - **show_n_rounds:** Mentioned as a game attribute in Suzuki & Ishiwata (2022) and Herne et al. (2023).

- **Indirectly or contextually discussed:**  
  - **reward_exists, show_other_summaries:** Contextually described in Suratin et al. (2023), but not tested experimentally.
  - **default_contrib:** Framing discussed in Wu & Sun (2022), but not empirically tested.
  - **show_punishment_id:** Not directly manipulated or discussed except as part of broader punishment mechanisms in Wu & Sun (2022).

- **Effectively missing:**  
  - **reward_cost, reward_tech, reward_magnitude:** Not empirically addressed in this set.
  - **show_punishment_id:** Largely absent in practical evidence.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Scarcity of direct efficiency measurements:** Only one experimental study (Suzuki & Ishiwata, 2022) directly measures group efficiency with and without punishment. Most evidence is based on proxies or non-payoff behavioral outcomes.
- **Context-dependency of effects:** Findings on whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency depend critically on game context (lab vs. field), incentive scheme, and the importance of intrinsic motivation—so results may not generalize.
- **Limited coverage of design dimensions:** Several key prediction input dimensions (e.g., reward mechanisms, information visibility, framing) are missing or unaddressed.
- **Heterogeneity of punishment implementations:** Not all forms of punishment (peer, social, institutional, financial) are distinguished or compared in empirical studies.
- **No studies test all design factors jointly:** The interaction effects between punishment and other design dimensions (e.g., chat, framing, group size) are underexplored.
- **Theory papers lack empirical validation:** Some mechanism arguments (e.g., optimal punishment/compensation design) are not corroborated by direct outcome data.
- **Adjacent (not exact) game settings:** Several studies use PGG variants or policy-relevant settings, making translation to canonical PGGs imperfect.
- **No large-scale statistical synthesis:** Effect sizes, variance, and confidence in predicted efficiency changes are not quantified across studies.

**In sum:** While the literature includes important cases where punishment either improves or worsens efficiency (sometimes dramatically), the direction and magnitude of effect for the prediction task is highly context-dependent; several key design dimensions remain empirically understudied. Direct, quantitative predictions from control efficiency to punishment-enabled efficiency can only be made cautiously and with strong caveats.
