# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set is composed entirely of theoretical (modeling and conceptual) papers; there are no empirical or experimental studies included. The set is relatively broad in its theoretical range: a minority of the papers analyze standard public goods games (PGGs) directly, while others address adjacent or related social dilemma environments, including volunteer’s dilemmas, repeated Prisoner's Dilemma, network games, evolutionary models, and high-level conceptual frameworks. While several papers focus on explicit punishment or sanctioning mechanisms, many others concentrate on endogenous incentives (e.g., coalition formation, emotional dynamics, norm compliance) or institutional context (law, constitutionalism) without direct manipulation of punishment variables. Only a subset of the papers analyzes group payoff or efficiency outcomes as primary dependent variables. Overall, the evidence base is mainly theoretical, with strong analytical rigor but limited empirical or experimental verification, providing diverse but occasionally indirect relevance to the prediction problem.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - **exact**: ~2 papers (e.g., Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021) directly model standard or optional PGGs.  
  - **close/adjacent**: Several others (Eldakar et al., 2018; Bolle, 2021; Skarzhinskaya & Tsurikov, 2021; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Okada, 2020; Andersson, 2020) consider games closely related to PGGs (volunteer’s dilemma, repeated PD, network games, coalition/provision models).
  - **weak/none**: Three papers (de Almeida, 2021; Hernández, 2021; McCannon, 2018) are mainly conceptual or abstract and do not specify PGGs.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - **exact**: Explicit peer- or centralized punishment is the central focus of several papers (Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Eldakar et al., 2018; Bolle, 2021; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018).
  - **close/adjacent/weak**: Some papers analyze related mechanisms (e.g., emotional states as punishment-like, coalition contracts as incentives, indirect reciprocity via reputation and justified punishment).
  - **none**: At least one paper (McCannon, 2018) omits punishment entirely.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - **exact**: Most directly: Alventosa & Olcina (2021), Botta et al. (2021), Skarzhinskaya & Tsurikov (2021), Friehe & Tabbach (2018), Andersson (2020), Madeo & Mocenni (2021).
  - **close/adjacent**: Others where payoff is discussed, but not as a primary outcome or not exactly in PGGs.
  - **weak/none**: Several papers (especially more conceptual ones) focus primarily on behavioral, evolutionary, or norm-based mechanisms, not on efficiency or group payoff.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (efficiency, group payoff, welfare, surplus):**
  - Explicit and central in a handful of theoretical models (Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Skarzhinskaya & Tsurikov, 2021; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Andersson, 2020; Madeo & Mocenni, 2021).
  - Reported as average group payoff, efficiency ratio (relative to the social optimum), or welfare/social cost.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, cooperation rate, punishment frequency, norm compliance):**
  - Primary outcomes in several papers (Eldakar et al., 2018; Bolle, 2021; Okada, 2020; de Almeida, 2021; McCannon, 2018; Hernández, 2021).
  - While these outcomes often relate to efficiency, their measurement is theoretically and operationally distinct from payoff-based outcomes.
  - In some theory papers, behavioral outcomes are linked to payoff, but sometimes only qualitatively or as a mechanism rather than a prediction.

- **Ambiguity:**  
  - Some models (especially formal or abstract theory) discuss “cooperation” or “altruism” without quantifying group welfare, making direct efficiency prediction more difficult.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Enabling punishment (especially strong or well-designed) reliably increases efficiency in PGGs compared to punishment-disabled controls, conditional on operational details:**
  - Both centralized (institutional) and decentralized (peer) punishment, if strong enough to induce nearly full contribution, can achieve or approach efficient group outcomes, provided their cost does not outweigh the gain (Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).
  - The positive efficiency effect is amplified when punishment parameters—effectiveness (punishment magnitude per cost), coverage fraction of defectors, or technological ability—surpass critical thresholds (Botta et al., 2021).
  - Partial or weak punishment can still improve efficiency, but effects are moderated by implementation cost and design details (Alventosa & Olcina, 2021).
  - Institutional context (who implements or controls the punishment mechanism) and power distribution (Eldakar et al., 2018) can reduce, amplify, or sometimes reverse expected efficiency gains.

- **Game structure matters:**
  - Settings not matching standard PGGs (e.g., threshold goods, volunteer dilemmas, network/cyclic games) can exhibit qualitatively different responses to punishment (Bolle, 2021; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Madeo & Mocenni, 2021).
  - Collective punishment can be ineffective or harmful in “public good” settings where resistance is individually costly (Bolle, 2021), even if it is effective in "public bad" settings; thus, effect direction may depend on the game’s fine structure.

- **Punishment effectiveness is sensitive to design dimensions:**
  - Efficiency gains depend critically on punishment's marginal impact, cost, technology, the ability to coordinate, and the fraction of punished defectors (Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018).
  - Reward mechanisms, coalition contracts, and endogenous norms can also raise efficiency, sometimes replicating the effect of punishment (Skarzhinskaya & Tsurikov, 2021; Okada, 2020).
  - Population structure and emotional or normative dynamics can mediate whether punishment increases or decreases efficiency (Andersson, 2020; Eldakar et al., 2018).

- **Payoff versus behavior:**
  - Several models find that punishment increases cooperation/norm compliance but do not always translate this into efficiency gains, especially where punishment costs are high or power is unequally distributed.
  - Where measured, efficiency typically increases with punishment but may not reach the social optimum due to costs, imperfect coverage, or implementation frictions.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Synthesis of the theoretical literature provides moderately strong support for the expectation that enabling peer (or institutional) punishment in public-goods-game-like environments increases efficiency, with the magnitude of improvement contingent on design dimensions and control-group baseline. The following guidance is supported:

- **Directionality:** Enabling punishment nearly always increases group efficiency relative to no-punishment controls, as long as punishment is effective and costs to the group do not outweigh contributions rescued by compliance (Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).

- **Effect size modulation:** The incremental efficiency gain is larger when:
  - The punishment is sufficiently strong, widespread, and not overly costly.
  - MPCR is neither too low (unmotivating) nor so high that voluntary cooperation already prevails.
  - Institutional or peer mechanisms are designed to minimize power asymmetry and prevent selfish punishment (Eldakar et al., 2018).

- **Critical thresholds:** There exist thresholds in punishment magnitude, coverage, and cost. Below these, punishment may be ineffective, leaving efficiency near the baseline; above, efficiency may approach the full-contribution optimum (Botta et al., 2021).

- **Game-structure dependencies:** Some variants (threshold goods, network games) may see muted, null, or even negative efficiency effects of punishment— especially if the game's logic makes punishment counterproductive (Bolle, 2021; Madeo & Mocenni, 2021).

- **Transferability:** Predictions are most reliable for standard or optional PGGs; less so for structurally different games or deeply institutionalized environments.

- **Use in model calibration:** The literature supports incorporating interaction effects between MPCR, punishment cost/tech, and player count, rather than assuming a uniform effect size across games.

- **Behavioral outcomes matter but are secondary for efficiency prediction:** In cases where only norm compliance or cooperation is modeled, infer only qualitatively that efficiency may improve if the cost of punishment does not offset rescued cooperation.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed:**
- `player_count`: Multiple papers (Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021; Eldakar et al., 2018; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018, etc.) analyze effect of group size on punishment and efficiency.
- `num_rounds`: Highlighted in repeated/iterated models and theory of repeated cooperation (Eldakar et al., 2018; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018; Andersson, 2020).
- `all_or_nothing`: Frequently modeled, especially in optional PGGs, volunteer’s dilemmas, and binary participation games.
- `mpcr`: Central in nearly all PGG-focused models. Determines threshold for cooperation/punishment effectiveness.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Explicitly parameterized and crucial in most punishment-centric models (Alventosa & Olcina, 2021; Botta et al., 2021).

**Indirectly Informed:**
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Discussed as alternative or complementary to punishment (Skarzhinskaya & Tsurikov, 2021; Friehe & Tabbach, 2018).
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`: Discussed in models of repeated play or information but not always specified as experimental manipulations.
- `chat`: Sometimes mentioned as a coordination/enhancement channel (McCannon, 2018) but not a primary focus.
- `default_contrib`: Only contextually or implicitly discussed (framing of contribution, e.g., opt-in/opt-out).

**Effectively Missing:**
- `show_punishment_id`: Not addressed as distinct variable; anonymity versus transparency of punishers is only very occasionally referenced and not analyzed as a direct design dimension.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Lack of empirical evidence:** All findings are theoretical; there is no direct experimental or field data. Mechanism effects may diverge in real settings with bounded rationality, learning, or preference heterogeneity.

- **Limited coverage of all design dimensions:** Some dimensions (especially `show_punishment_id`, explicit reward parameters, detailed framing variables) are un- or under-explored in this literature.

- **Ambiguity and heterogeneity in games studied:** While public goods games are often the referent, several studies analyze adjacent (but structurally distinct) games (e.g., network games, volunteer’s dilemmas, repeated PD), making direct transfer of predictions to classic PGGs less certain.

- **Outcome confounding:** Many models focus on cooperation/contribution rate outcomes rather than efficiency per se; translation from behavioral to payoff outcomes sometimes involves unmodeled assumptions about punishment cost and implementation.

- **Static versus dynamic effects:** Most models are static or equilibrium-focused. Transient effects, learning, and path dependence in real groups remain unaddressed.

- **Institutional complexity and context:** Models focusing on institutional punishment highlight that power asymmetries, government identity, and coalition formation can produce divergent or even perverse outcomes (Eldakar et al., 2018; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021), limiting generalizability.

- **No direct evidence for some key prediction variables:** Design features such as information visibility (`showOtherSummaries`, `showPunishmentId`), communication (`chat`), and default framing are at best discussed indirectly and are not subject to theory-based quantitative prediction in this set. 

**In summary:** While theoretical evidence supports the expectation that enabling punishment generally increases efficiency in PGG-like settings, the magnitude and conditions for these effects are strongly design-dependent and often only qualitatively supported. The absence of experimental evidence and patchy coverage of specific dimensions caution against overconfidence in quantitative forecasts for new environments.
