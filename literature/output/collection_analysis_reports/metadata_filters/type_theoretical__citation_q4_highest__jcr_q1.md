# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base consists entirely of theoretical and simulation-based literature (no direct laboratory or field experiments) with a very large number of items (189 papers). It is broad for the prediction task: virtually all major theoretical traditions and mechanisms relating to public goods games (PGGs), punishment, reward, institution design, norm enforcement, and efficiency are covered in detail, from core linear PGGs to variants involving spatial/network structure, exclusion, reputation, collective risk, and institutional incentives. Most findings are derived from analytical models or agent-based simulations rather than direct empirical measurement; even so, multiple items synthesize or generalize from the classic experimental findings (notably from Fehr & Gächter and others). The coverage for core PGGs with efficiency or payoff as a primary outcome is strong, but a substantial part of the literature focuses primarily on behavioral or evolutionary outcomes (e.g., cooperation frequency), which must be distinguished from efficiency.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**: The paper set’s relevance to the standard linear public goods game (PGG) is high. Most papers are labeled as either `exact` PGG or `close` variants: they either directly model the linear (or, in some cases, threshold or spatial/network) public goods game, or study adjacent settings with clearly comparable structure.

**punishment_or_sanctions**: Coverage of punishment (peer, pool, institutional, exclusion-based) is excellent, with near-universal theoretical discussion of punishment as a main variable (again, mostly as a modeled parameter rather than as an empirically manipulated intervention). Sanctions include both standard peer punishment and broader mechanisms such as pool/institutional punishment, exclusion, ostracism, and commitment/fine arrangements. Some papers also address reward.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**: The coverage here is more variable. A substantial number of the most relevant papers (labeled `exact` for payoff/efficiency) do model and report group efficiency, total welfare, or group payoff explicitly and use these as key outcomes (e.g., Perc et al., 2017; Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Duong & Han, 2021; etc.). Another segment of the literature uses behavioral or evolutionary endpoints (cooperation rate, abundance of strategies), inferring efficiency indirectly, or reports group achievement or success in threshold games, which are closely related but not strictly ratio-based efficiency. A large tail of the literature is labeled as adjacent or close, analyzing non-PGG games or focusing on behavior rather than payoff.

**Summary:** Task relevance is highest for exact PGG, explicit punishment mechanisms, and studies reporting efficiency or group payoff. However, a notable subset uses only close or adjacent game types and/or focuses on non-payoff behavioral outcomes, which must be interpreted accordingly.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (“efficiency” and variants):**
- *Efficiency* (group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum) is modeled directly in many core theoretical studies (e.g., Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Perc et al., 2017; Duong & Han, 2021; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; etc.).
- *Group payoff*, *average group earnings*, *welfare*, *total coins*, or *surplus* are also widely reported, either directly or as analytical expressions mapped to efficiency.
- *Institutional/implementer cost* is measured in studies of optimal incentive schemes (Duong & Han, 2021; Wang et al., 2023), where efficiency is linked to the cost required to induce cooperation.
- *Close proxies*: In threshold/collective-risk games, “group achievement” (fraction of groups reaching the collective target) is commonly measured and often interpreted as a payoff/efficiency proxy.

**Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- *Cooperation or contribution rate* (fraction of cooperators) is extremely widely reported and is the primary outcome in many theoretical and agent-based studies.
- *Strategy abundance* (frequency of punishers, excluders, defectors, etc.), *norm compliance*, and *punishment frequency* are often modeled, sometimes linked to payoffs only in equilibrium.
- *Oscillatory or phase regime dynamics* (e.g., alternation and coexistence of strategies, cyclic dominance) are emphasized in spatial/network models.

**Distinction**: Many papers observe that high cooperation is *necessary* for high efficiency, but costly punishment can lead to high cooperation without corresponding efficiency gains due to wasted resources, retaliation, or perverse effects (see, e.g., Guala, 2012; Sigmund et al., 2010; Kurzban et al., 2015).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Effects of Punishment:**
- *Punishment increases efficiency in PGGs* mainly when (a) the cost to punishers is not too high, (b) punishment is effective (large enough fine), and (c) design features allow punishment to be focused and not wasteful (Perc et al., 2017; Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).
- *Costly punishment can backfire*—increasing cooperation but reducing or failing to improve efficiency—if punishment is inefficient, if antisocial punishment is prevalent, or if retaliatory/counter-punishment escalates (Sigmund et al., 2010; Guala, 2012; Kurzban et al., 2015; Barrett, 2016; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).

**Moderators and Design Dimension Interactions:**
- *Punishment cost and effectiveness*: When punishment cost is moderate-to-low and the magnitude (impact) is high, efficiency gains from punishment are robust. Very costly punishment can destroy more resources than it saves even if cooperation rises (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Lee et al., 2022; MACY, 1993).
- *Presence of reward*: Adding reward often does not increase efficiency further beyond what is achieved by effective punishment, except in narrow parameter regions or when cooperation is hard to establish (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013; Chen et al., 2015). Reward can be more efficient at initiating cooperation with low baseline cooperation (Duong & Han, 2021).
- *Repetition and observed histories*: Repeated games, especially those with transparent histories or reputation, allow for trigger strategies and stable efficiency with punishment. Short, one-shot, or highly anonymous games usually see smaller or no efficiency effects (Sigmund et al., 2001; Kandori, 1992; ABREU et al., 1991).
- *Anonymity and reputation*: Without reputation or information (e.g., anonymous, unobservable punishment), efficiency gains from punishment are minimal or negative. With even minimal reputation or information, punishment is much more effective at sustaining cooperation and efficiency (Sigmund et al., 2001; dos Santos et al., 2011).
- *Institutional vs. peer punishment*: Pool punishment is more stable but less efficient due to overhead; peer punishment is often more efficient but can be undermined by second-order free-riding unless additional mechanisms are present (Sigmund et al., 2010; Perc, 2012; Sasaki et al., 2015).
- *Voluntary participation/optional exit*: When voluntary participation is allowed, punishment produces much higher efficiency gains with smaller required fines. Compulsory games need much steeper fines to stabilize cooperation (Sasaki et al., 2012; Hauert et al., 2007; Mathew & Boyd, 2009).
- *Group size/player count*: Effects are robust in small to moderate groups; in large groups, the marginal benefit of punishment declines unless information and enforcement are well designed (Barrett, 2016; Eldakar & Wilson, 2008).
- *Network/spatial structure*: In spatial/networked games, punishment supports clusters of cooperators and punishers, allowing efficiency gains even when global parameters would make cooperation unstable in well-mixed settings (Perc et al., 2017; Helbing et al., 2010).
- *Second-order free-riding*: Sustained efficiency gains from punishment often require sanctioning of non-punishing cooperators (second-order free-riders); without this, punishment collapses in the long run (Perc, 2012; Sasaki & Uchida, 2013).
- *Ecological/resource context*: Effects of punishment on efficiency are moderated by resource dynamics (e.g., in common-pool resource games); in slow-renewal or high-variability environments, even high cooperation levels (induced by punishment) may not lead to higher efficiency (Chen & Szolnoki, 2018).
- *Existence of antisocial punishment*: Antisocial punishment (punishing high contributors) can reduce or eliminate efficiency gains, especially in the absence of normative constraints (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Sigmund et al., 2010).

**Empirical vs. Theoretical:**
- All findings above are based on theoretical models, agent-based simulations, or synthesis/generalization of results from experiments reported elsewhere.

**Disagreement and Ambiguity:**
- Some sources report that punishment almost always increases efficiency if implemented with favorable (not extreme) design parameters.
- Others report that, in typical lab conditions (moderate/large groups, costly punishment, or where punishment is not well-targeted), efficiency gains are rare; sometimes efficiency is lower with punishment than without.
- Effect direction and size are thus highly conditional on game parameters (especially punishment cost/effectiveness, information/reputation, group size, and presence of antisocial punishment).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**For Predicting Treatment Efficiency (Punishment Enabled) Given Control Efficiency and Design Dimensions:**

- **If the control efficiency is low (typical in standard PGGs without punishment) and the game parameters are favorable** (moderate cost, sufficient punishment magnitude, repeated rounds, reputation or information, and no major opportunity for antisocial punishment), *enabling punishment* should be expected to shift efficiency upwards, often dramatically—potentially to near the social optimum. This is a robust theoretical prediction (e.g., Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Perc et al., 2017).

- **If punishment is costly, punishment is misapplied, antisocial punishment is prevalent, or punishment is frequent due to lack of stable cooperation**, efficiency may increase only slightly, not at all, or may even decrease, despite large increases in cooperation rates (Guala, 2012; Sigmund et al., 2010; Kurzban et al., 2015).

- **Game designs with reputation/information feedback** (e.g., `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`) *amplify the positive effect* of punishment on efficiency. If these are absent (anonymous or nontransparent games), efficiency gains are unlikely (Sigmund et al., 2001; dos Santos et al., 2011).

- **Peer vs. institutional punishment:** Institutional (pool) punishment tends to provide greater stability but at greater cost—efficiency gains are only likely if institutional cost is not excessive and second-order free-riding is controlled (Perc, 2012; Sigmund et al., 2010).

- **Player count/group size:** Expect diminishing returns for enabling punishment in very large groups unless institutional design supports enforcement or information sharing (Barrett, 2016; Eldakar & Wilson, 2008).

- **Optional participation:** If `all_or_nothing` is set to allow opt-out (voluntary participation), punishment is much more efficient and less costly; if not, efficiency gains require higher punishment magnitude (Sasaki et al., 2012; Hauert et al., 2007).

- **Baseline (control) efficiency**: Absolute efficiency gains from punishment are highest when the control condition is inefficient (e.g., baseline free riding); marginal effects are smaller if baseline efficiency is already moderate or high due to other mechanisms (reputation, mobility, prestige effects, etc.).

- **Other design features:**
    - Communication or chat (`chat`): Indirectly increases the potential for efficient cooperation; punishment effects are magnified or may become unnecessary when communication is allowed (Ostrom, 2000).
    - Reward (`reward_exists`): Having reward in addition to punishment does not generally increase efficiency (Szolnoki & Perc, 2013).

- **Ambiguity must be preserved**: Even under idealized theory, model outcomes can be contingent on initial conditions, parameter thresholds, or can exhibit bistability/multistability (Brandt et al., 2006). Not all models agree on thresholds at which efficiency improvements occur.

**Caveats:** Outcomes are based on models and simulations, not on direct experimental measurements of group payoff; real-world settings may be more complex due to emotions, learning, or unmodeled behaviors.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions** (parameters systematically manipulated/analyzed with regard to efficiency and punishment effect):

- `player_count`: Most explicit models analyze group size effects.
- `num_rounds`: Repeated games and time horizon effects are well analyzed.
- `mpcr`: Central to almost all quantitative models; variations show effects on baseline cooperation and punishment's marginal effect.
- `punishment_cost`: Explicitly modeled in nearly all theoretical treatments; key moderator.
- `punishment_tech` (punishment effectiveness/magnitude): Directly manipulated.
- `all_or_nothing`: Binary vs. continuous choices are modeled; effect on ease of cooperation and effect of punishment.
- `reward_exists`: Included in models testing combinations of punishment and reward.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Directly mapped to observability/reputation/information dimensions in several models.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Where studies address reward.
- `show_n_rounds`: Sometimes relevant to repeated/intertemporal models.
- *Voluntary participation/opt-out* (some models map to `default_contrib`).

**Indirectly or contextually discussed (moderate to low direct evidence):**

- `chat`: Discussed as communication channels that facilitate cooperation and/or magnify punishment effect, but few formal models directly manipulate it.
- `default_contrib`: Framing and default effects are mentioned in some literature but are not systematically modeled in efficiency-focused theory.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Reward sometimes analyzed alongside punishment (esp. in hybrid or comparative models).
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Reputation/observability links are discussed, but implementation varies.

**Effectively missing or not systematically addressed:**
- Some micro-structural details (e.g., identity of punishers being visible, certain framing manipulations, or specific forms of chat) are rarely included in models with efficiency as an explicit outcome.
- Indirect references to context (institutional legitimacy, group heterogeneity) appear but are not always directly parameterized.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Theoretical/emulation basis:** No direct empirical or experimental tests—results depend on model assumptions. Laboratory and field studies referenced are usually cited, but not re-analyzed for efficiency endpoints.
- **Behavior vs. efficiency:** Many models focus primarily on cooperation/contribution frequency; efficiency (group payoff) effects are often asserted or inferred but not always quantified directly. The link between high cooperation and high efficiency is conditional (especially when punishment is costly).
- **Ambiguity and parameter sensitivity:** Models disagree about thresholds, stability, and size/direction of treatment effects when parameters approach critical values (e.g., very costly punishment can reduce efficiency).
- **Generalizability:** Most models assume rational, fully informed, or simplistic learning agents; real human subjects may deviate via bounded rationality, learning, emotion, or institutional context.
- **Reward and hybrid schemes:** The literature covers punishment much more extensively than reward or hybrid (punishment+reward) mechanisms. Guidance for hybrid environments is less developed.
- **Missing dimensions:** Some prediction dimensions (details of chat, group identity visibility, default options, etc.) lack sufficient direct modeling for efficient prediction; such features may matter in real designs but are mostly unaddressed in the theoretical base.
- **Realism of repeated games:** Many models assume repeatedly interacting, small groups, reputationally salient environments, and visible histories; modern online or field settings with high anonymity or extremely large groups may see different effects.
- **Second-order free-riding and antisocial punishment:** While recognized as critical, resolving these in practice (e.g., by always punishing non-punishers) may not be feasible or desirable outside theoretical models.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature provides strong, nuanced theoretical predictions that point prediction of treatment efficiency must be conditional on key game design dimensions—especially punishment cost and effectiveness, group size, reputation/information, presence of peer vs. institutional enforcement, and baseline (control) efficiency. Efficiency gains from enabling punishment are likely only when punishment is not excessively costly, is effective, is reputationally salient, and when antisocial punishment is not prevalent. In other settings—especially under high punishment cost, anonymity, lack of transparency, or institutional instability—punishment may make little difference or even decrease efficiency due to wasted resources. Quantitative effect sizes are often not available; phase diagrams and threshold-based predictions are the norm. Many behavioral-outcome-focused papers cannot be directly used for efficiency prediction but provide supporting mechanistic context. Real-world prediction should take these complex conditionalities and model limitations into account.
