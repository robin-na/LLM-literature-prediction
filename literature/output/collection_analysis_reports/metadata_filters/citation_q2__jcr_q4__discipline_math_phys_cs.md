# 1) Evidence Base

The literature set consists of 13 papers, spanning both **empirical (experimental and field)** and **theoretical** work. There is a moderate mix: several lab experiments (e.g., Castillo et al., 2021; Abbink et al., 2004), some field evidence (Noussair et al., 2015), and multiple theoretical papers with varying proximity to public goods settings (e.g., Voelkl, 2015; Forges et al., 2016). The **coverage is broad for norm enforcement and cooperation games**, but **narrow for directly matching, payoff-focused, peer-punishment-enabled public goods games**.

Empirical studies dominate the PGG and experimental economics domains, though direct measurement of group efficiency (total group payoff compared to the cooperative optimum) is less common than reporting on behavioral measures (e.g., cooperation/contribution rates, use of punishment). Several papers analyze adjacent game structures (ultimatum, CPR, two-stage, or principal-agent games), **reducing the directness of the evidence for PGG prediction**. Theoretical papers provide mechanisms and boundary conditions, but with limited empirical calibration.

**Conclusion**: The set is **rich for understanding the mechanisms and range of possible punishment effects**, but **sparse in direct, empirical, peer-punishment, efficiency-based outcome data for canonical PGGs**—the precise focus of the prediction task.

# 2) Task Relevance

Each dimension is rated using the relevance labels (`exact`, `close`, `adjacent`, `weak`, `none`):

### pgg_or_variant
- **exact**: Several papers work with standard linear PGGs or very close analogues (Castillo et al., 2021; Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- **close/adjacent**: Some are in related social dilemmas—common-pool resource (Noussair et al., 2015; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020), N-person PD (Castro & Toro, 2008).
- **adjacent/weak**: Others consider bargaining, two-person games, or principal-agent structures (Abbink et al., 2004; Forges et al., 2016; Chen, 2012).

### punishment_or_sanctions
- **exact**: Multiple papers specifically manipulate the existence, cost, and/or form of punishment via peer or institutional means (Castillo et al., 2021; Abbink et al., 2004; Zhang & Pei, 2022; Voelkl, 2015).
- **close/adjacent**: Some focus more on reward, redistribution, or social feedback as substitutes (Becchetti et al., 2018; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020).
- **adjacent/weak**: A subset examines self-regulation or honesty prompts, not explicit exogenous punishment (Madeo & Mocenni, 2021; Chen, 2012).

### efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome
- **exact**: Only a few papers directly measure **efficiency** or total group payoff as a primary outcome (Castillo et al., 2021; Castro & Toro, 2008; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020; Voelkl, 2015).
- **adjacent/close**: Many report behavioral measures with some implied relationship to efficiency, but not the payoff ratio versus full cooperation (Becchetti et al., 2018).
- **adjacent/weak**: A large portion measure only behavioral/strategic outcomes—contribution, cooperation, punishment rates (Davis & Holt, 1999; Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014).

**Summary**: The task-relevant evidence is **strongest for the existence and nature of punishment mechanisms**, less direct for **canonical PGG structures**, and **scarcest** for **quantitative efficiency/outcome shifts under enabled versus disabled peer punishment** within those settings.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

### Payoff-related outcomes  
- **Efficiency (group payoff relative to maximum)**: Measured directly in a small subset (Castillo et al., 2021—centralized punishment; Castro & Toro, 2008; Voelkl, 2015; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020—CPR).
- **Total earnings, welfare, surplus**: Sometimes reported or implied (Castillo et al., 2021; Voelkl, 2015), but often lacking direct cross-condition comparison.
- **Group-level profit or surplus**: Explicitly compared in rare cases (Castillo et al., 2021; Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020).

### Non-payoff behavioral outcomes  
- **Contribution and cooperation rates**: Frequently measured and compared (Becchetti et al., 2018; Davis & Holt, 1999; Zhang & Pei, 2022).
- **Punishment frequency and assignment**: Examined in terms of player strategies, not always group payoff (Abbink et al., 2004; Leibbrandt & López-Pérez, 2014).
- **Norm compliance, fairness, honesty/slack**: Studied as primary outcomes in some adjacent games (Chen, 2012; Davis & Holt, 1999).

**Important distinction**: Most papers that report increases in cooperation/contribution or reductions in free-riding **do not** directly report if this translates into **higher efficiency** due to the costs imposed by punishment or reward.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### Empirical findings:
- **Punishment increases efficiency—sometimes**: In centralized punishment PGGs, enabling punishment (regardless of selection mechanism or moderate cost) increases group efficiency versus the no-punishment baseline, and this holds across various cost/effectiveness regimes (Castillo et al., 2021).
- **Peer punishment may not always improve efficiency**: The peer punishment effect is less clear. Theory and field data caution that costs of punishment can offset gains from increased cooperation, sometimes yielding **lower efficiency** than in no-punishment conditions (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Abbink et al., 2004). Field implementations may produce **no improvement** or even harm (Noussair et al., 2015).
- **Punishment can reduce efficiency via cost**: When the punishment cost is high or used at intermediate rates, increased costly punishment can leave total earnings lower than in the baseline condition (Abbink et al., 2004).
- **Context and mechanism matter**: Institutional/centralized punishment tends to outperform peer punishment for improving efficiency (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Becchetti et al., 2018; Voelkl, 2015).

### Theoretical findings:
- **Efficiency depends on punishment effectiveness relative to temptation**: Punishment increases efficiency **only if** strong enough to deter defection but not so costly as to outweigh gains from cooperation (Voelkl, 2015; Forges et al., 2016).
- **Redistribution or reward can substitute for punishment, but are not always as effective**: Efficient group outcomes may also be achieved via mechanisms such as redistribution (Becchetti et al., 2018) or public social feedback (Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020).
- **Behavioral mechanism arguments**: Punishment increases cooperation but not always group payoff unless the design ensures that the cost of punishment is sufficiently low relative to the harm it deters (Zhang & Pei, 2022).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**The empirical-best-informed prediction** is that **enabling a punishment mechanism often increases group efficiency** (payoff ratio to max) in public-goods-like games, particularly when:
- **Punishment is centralized/institutional**, not peer-based (Castillo et al., 2021).
- **Punishment is ‘effective’**: its cost-to-impact ratio is such that it can reliably deter free-riding **without excessive use** (Voelkl, 2015).
- **Manager or ‘punisher’ identity or selection process does not matter**, within tested ranges (Castillo et al., 2021).

For **peer punishment**, the literature indicates:
- Positive efficiency effects are possible **if the costs of punishment are low or punishment is only rarely needed once deterrence is established**.
- However, **costly, frequent, or anti-social punishment can fully or partially reverse any gains** from increased cooperation, leading in some cases to no efficiency gain or even net loss (Zhang & Pei, 2022; Abbink et al., 2004).
- Field settings, or those with meaningful extra-game social context or uncertainty, may see **little-to-no efficiency benefit from enabling punishment** compared to controls (Noussair et al., 2015).

The effect of enabling peer punishment is likely to be **moderated by design features** (discussed below)—especially by punishment cost, visibility, and whether additional mechanisms such as social feedback or redistribution are present.

**If baseline efficiency is high without punishment**, the scope for improvement is limited; punishment may be unnecessary, or its cost may outweigh marginal gains.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

### Directly informed (frequently manipulated or reported, relevant for payoff outcomes):
- **player_count, num_rounds**: Almost all studies specify and manipulate these.
- **mpcr (marginal per-capita return)**: Often specified, matters for temptation to free-ride (Castillo et al., 2021; Castro & Toro, 2008).
- **all_or_nothing**: Some studies employ this format, especially to map binary vs. continuous cooperation (Becchetti et al., 2018).
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Explicitly varied in several papers; crucial for determining net effect on efficiency (Castillo et al., 2021; Voelkl, 2015).
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Frequently discussed, sometimes tested jointly with punishment.

### Indirectly informed:
- **chat**: Enabled in some studies (Castillo et al., 2021), but value with respect to efficiency payoff outcomes is uncertain due to confounding mechanisms.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Manipulated in select studies (Przepiorka & Diekmann, 2020), where visibility of feedback/social sanctions substitute for formal punishment.
- **default_contrib**: Framing effects sometimes manipulated, but evidence is mostly about behavior, not efficiency.

### Contextually discussed or sparse:
- **punishment_magnitude**: Few papers vary both cost and magnitude independently; ratio is often described, detailed mapping is sparse.
- **reward_magnitude**: Analogue holds for rewards—magnitude is sometimes discussed more as a boundary condition than systematically varied.

**Summary**: The **best-informed dimensions for payoff-based prediction from the literature** are: `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `all_or_nothing`, `punishment_cost`, and general presence/absence and nature of sanction and reward mechanisms (institutional vs. peer; monetary vs. non-monetary). **Punishment visibility and social feedback** are also highlighted as potentially strong moderators in adjacent games.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Sparse direct evidence on peer punishment and efficiency**: Most direct efficiency findings relate to centralized (institutional) punishment—not peer-imposed sanctions, which are the target for many public goods game designs.
- **Behavioral outcomes ≠ payoff outcomes**: Many studies report increased cooperation/contribution or norm compliance, but do not show whether group efficiency improves once punishment/reward costs are deducted.
- **Generality vs. context**: Robust lab-based PGG findings (punishment increases efficiency) may **not generalize to real-world or field settings** (cf. Noussair et al., 2015).
- **Design detail gaps**: Some design dimensions crucial for prediction (e.g., `punishment_magnitude`, social context, visibility, and framing) are not systematically or independently manipulated.
- **Ambiguity in theory**: Theoretical arguments often articulate **conditional effects**—punishment can either increase or decrease efficiency **depending on cost/effectiveness ratios and behavioral response**—making prediction uncertain without precise parameterization.
- **Limited joint manipulation**: Few papers address **interactions between multiple mechanisms** (e.g., simultaneous presence of peer punishment, reward, and feedback).
- **Mechanism differences**: Differences between peer vs. centralized punishment, or between monetary and non-monetary forms, may limit the direct transfer of results.

**Final caveat**: This literature set supports only **moderately confident, cautiously qualified predictions** for peer punishment’s effect on efficiency in standard PGGs. Where design or context differs (field vs. lab; centralized vs. peer; binary vs. continuous choices; cost/effect ratios), effects may diverge sharply or even reverse.
