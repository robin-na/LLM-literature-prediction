# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is comprised of seven papers with a mix of empirical (primarily laboratory experiments) and theoretical works, but is relatively narrow in its direct relevance to the downstream prediction task focused on efficiency changes in public-goods-game-like environments with and without peer punishment. Two papers (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Martinsson et al., 2015) are direct lab experiments with public goods games; two are theoretical or modeling analyses (Thöni, 2014; Blonski & Spagnolo, 2015) with varying degrees of proximity to the payoff focus; the remaining three are experimental or conceptual works examining punishment in adjacent (but not canonical PGG) settings or focusing on behavioral/attitudinal outcomes rather than realized payoffs. Among these, only Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) provides direct empirical results about efficiency (payoff outcomes) from enabling punishment in a PGG, including multiple punishment design variants. Several papers offer contextually relevant mechanism insights, but only one is highly aligned with the specific prediction of treatment efficiency.

# 2) Task Relevance

- **pgg_or_variant**:  
  - `exact`: Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015), Thöni (2014), Drouvelis et al. (2015), Martinsson et al. (2015) directly study public goods games.
  - `adjacent`: Blonski & Spagnolo (2015), Lopez (2017), Gordon & Lea (2016) connect via repeated PD, collective action, or norm contexts, but not PGG structure itself.

- **punishment_or_sanctions**:  
  - `exact`: Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015), Thöni (2014), Blonski & Spagnolo (2015), Lopez (2017), Gordon & Lea (2016) all focus on some form of punishment or sanctioning (though with variable design realism).
  - `none`: Drouvelis et al. (2015), Martinsson et al. (2015) do not include punishment or sanctions.

- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
  - `exact`: Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) reports group earnings and efficiency under control and punishment conditions.
  - `adjacent`: Thöni (2014), Blonski & Spagnolo (2015), and Lopez (2017) discuss theoretical or attitudinal outcomes relevant to efficiency, but report mainly behavior or mechanisms.
  - `none` / `weak`: Gordon & Lea (2016), Drouvelis et al. (2015), and Martinsson et al. (2015) do not provide payoff/efficiency data.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes**:
  - Only Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) measure and report _efficiency_ and group payout data under both control (no punishment) and two different punishment treatments.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**:
  - Contribution rates: Reported widely (e.g., Drouvelis et al., Martinsson et al., Thöni).
  - Punishment assignment/frequency, antisocial punishment behavior: Thöni (2014) and Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015).
  - Attitudinal/intentional measures: Lopez (2017), Gordon & Lea (2016) record self-reported norms, willingness, or perceptions.
  - Cooperation rates, norm compliance, social class or priming effects: Martinsson et al. (2015), Drouvelis et al. (2015).

It is important to note that, although non-payoff outcomes often show improvement (i.e., higher contributions with punishment), this does not always translate to higher efficiency due to the potential costs of punishment.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment Increases Contributions, but Efficiency Gains are Design-Sensitive**:  
  In Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015), standard peer punishment (anonymous, one stage per round) increases both contributions and efficiency relative to the no-punishment control over time. However, a "rich" punishment environment (multiple stages, fixed IDs, full transparency) results in high contributions but *no net gain in efficiency* due to costly feuding and retaliation; group earnings do not exceed control. Punishment costs can offset or even negate the cooperation benefit if the design allows for retaliation or high punishment frequency.

- **Antisocial Punishment Reduces Efficiency Gains**:  
  Thöni (2014) argues that antisocial punishment—punishment of cooperators or non-free riders—arises from factors beyond inequality aversion (such as spite or revenge), and, while not giving quantitative payoff results, claims that high antisocial punishment prevalence undermines the efficiency-enhancing role of peer punishment mechanisms.

- **Theory Emphasizes Risk and Strategic Considerations**:  
  Blonski & Spagnolo (2015) show in theoretical models structurally related to PGG that the effectiveness of punishment in supporting cooperation (and by extension, efficiency) increases with the harshness of punishment and the patience of players, but do not provide data or direct efficiency calculations.

- **Contextual and Normative Moderators**:  
  Papers such as Lopez (2017) and Gordon & Lea (2016) show that the perceived legitimacy and utilization of punishment are influenced by task framing (public good vs. private benefit), group needs, and punisher status, which may affect when and how punishment impacts behavior and, potentially, payoffs. However, these are indirect arguments without efficiency results.

- **No Evidence from Non-Punishment PGG Papers**:  
  Drouvelis et al. (2015) and Martinsson et al. (2015) are included as baseline or context but lack any treatment conditions with punishment or efficiency outcomes post-treatment.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct guidance for predicting treatment efficiency from control efficiency and design dimensions is only strongly backed by Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015):**

- **Efficiency gains from enabling peer punishment are not automatic**: They depend sensitively on punishment structure—specifically, whether the environment restricts opportunities for feud/retaliation and contains the costs of punishment actions. In simple, anonymous, one-stage punishment (standard peer punishment), expect *increased efficiency* over control, especially as the game progresses. In rich, multi-stage, identity-transparent punishment with fixed IDs, efficiency gains *may disappear* due to costly feuds, even if contributions rise.
- **Control efficiency is not sufficient to predict treatment efficiency**: The marginal effect of enabling punishment depends critically on game design features that modulate punitive behaviors and their associated costs.
- **High prevalence of antisocial punishment or complex social motivations can reduce or reverse efficiency gains**: When the environment or subject pool fosters punitive action against cooperators or random targets, net payoff benefits may not materialize.
- **Theory papers suggest greater effectiveness of punishment where it is enforceable and risk is minimized**, but without direct payoff evidence.

Thus: For new designs, if the punishment regime is structured simply and _contains_ punishment costs (limits retaliation, anonymity is maintained, one punishing stage per round), expect a **positive treatment-control efficiency difference**. If identities are exposed or punishment is unconstrained, be wary of net efficiency improvements, even if initial cooperation spikes.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

Most direct evidence exists for the following dimensions, as per Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015), Thöni (2014), and related works:

- **Directly Informed**:
  - `player_count`: Standard PGG experiments (typically 4 players).
  - `num_rounds`: Evidence for both one-shot and 30-round games.
  - `all_or_nothing`: Both continuous and dichotomous contributions seen.
  - `mpcr`: Explicitly manipulated/reported in empirical papers.
  - `punishment_cost`: Key moderator for net efficiency outcomes.
  - `punishment_tech` (i.e., the structure, timing, and anonymity of punishment): *Critical* for determining effect on efficiency.

- **Indirectly Informed**:
  - `show_punishment_id`: Identity/transparency is noted as a risk factor for feuds and efficiency breakdown (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
  - `show_n_rounds`, `chat`: Discussed as context or framing but not as direct moderators of efficiency effects with punishment.

- **Only Contextually Discussed**:
  - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Only Lopez (2017) mentions reward, but not in terms of behavioral economic payout; no payoff results.
  - `chat`, `default_contrib`: Discussed in baseline-pure PGG treatments but not in relation to moderation of punishment efficiency impacts.
  - `show_other_summaries`: Sometimes present or absent; not systematically varied with efficiency measures.

- **Effectively Missing**:
  - `reward_magnitude`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: No direct experimental manipulation in the relevant papers.
  - `default_contrib`: Mentioned only as framing, not analyzed for its effect on punishment efficiency.
  - `show_other_summaries`: No evidence for effect on efficiency when punishment is enabled.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Quantitative Prediction Limited to One Empirical Source**: Only Engelmann & Nikiforakis (2015) allows direct, quantitative predictions of efficiency change after enabling punishment, and only under a limited set of dimensions and parameterizations.
- **Sparse Coverage of Many Dimensions**: Many of the 14 prediction dimensions are either unmanipulated or implicitly held constant in extant studies, limiting generalizability.
- **Indirect or Mechanistic Evidence Dominates**: Much of the literature focuses on behavioral mechanisms, attitudes, or theoretical possibility rather than realized efficiency or group payoff changes.
- **Lack of Heterogeneity and Robustness Data**: The effect of individual or group characteristics (e.g., social class, status, subject pool), and the interactions with design features, remain unclear for payoff outcomes.
- **Non-payoff Outcomes Cannot Substitute**: Higher cooperation or contribution rates, as shown in several papers, do not necessarily imply higher efficiency due to possible offsetting by punishment costs (a result highlighted explicitly by Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Ambiguity in Rich or Complex Punishment Settings**: While some design features are identified as risks for efficiency loss, the boundary conditions for which specific combinations lead to efficiency breakdowns are not fully mapped.
- **No Evidence on Effects of Rewards or Mixed Incentives**: Although reward-related dimensions are included, no included paper experimentally manipulates or measures the impact of rewards in PGGs on efficiency, in isolation or in combination with punishment.
- **External Validity Unclear**: Most evidence comes from small-group, laboratory PGGs with student subjects; application to field settings or larger groups is not addressed.

---

**Summary**:  
Prediction of efficiency effects from enabling punishment in public goods games is only directly supported for some design environments, with evidence that design details (especially the structure of punishment opportunities and transparency) crucially moderate whether punishment increases or fails to increase efficiency. While several papers illuminate relevant mechanisms and contextual factors, robust quantitative prediction across all design dimensions is not yet possible with this literature set. Residual uncertainty is especially high where punishment can trigger antisocial punishment, retaliation, or persistent feuding.
