# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set is extensive and includes a broad mix of empirical and theoretical studies, with a substantial number of classic and recent lab experiments, supplemented by field and theory/simulation papers. Of the 199 papers, a significant subset provides direct, high-quality experimental results on standard public goods games (PGGs), including repeated, multi-player settings with and without costly punishment stages. Additional papers model more complex environments, address common-pool resources, institution-building, social norms, and explore mechanism design and psychological moderators. Many adjacency studies cover closely related paradigms (prisoner's dilemma, trust games) or contextual factors (reputation, culture, group selection), and a considerable number focus only on non-payoff behavioral outcomes or theoretical argument.

For the specific task—predicting the average *efficiency* effect of enabling peer punishment in PGG-like environments, conditional on game design and control efficiency—the empirical base is strongest in lab-experimental PGGs with standard (linear) payoff structures and peer or leader-enabled punishment modules. Theoretical papers often address repeated games, effects of monitoring, information, or population structure, sometimes providing comparative statics. However, many studies focus instead on cooperation rates, norm enforcement, or attitudes, without reporting efficiency. Overall, while the scope is broad, the proportion of studies delivering direct, design-dimension-specific, payoff-based findings for the prediction target is moderate.

# 2) Task Relevance

### a. PGG or Variant

- **exact**: Numerous empirical and theory papers are explicitly set in standard PGGs or their direct experimental variants (e.g., Fehr & Gächter, 2002; Gintis et al., 2003; Reuben & Riedl, 2009; Gürerk et al., 2009; Barclay 2004).
- **close**: Several papers use common-pool resource games, step-level (threshold) PGGs, or n-person dilemmas with analogous structure (e.g., Ostrom et al., 1992; Noailly et al., 2009; Tarui et al., 2008). Others test adjacent dyadic or population models with similar logic but not direct PGG payoffs.
- **adjacent/weak**: Many address prisoner's dilemma, trust games, dictator/ultimatum games, or social-exchange frameworks, focusing on analogous mechanisms but not the standard PGG structure.

### b. Punishment or Sanctions

- **exact**: Many key experiments manipulate peer or centralized punishment on/off, vary cost/impact, or compare punishment and reward (e.g., Reuben & Riedl, 2009; Gürerk et al., 2009; Fehr & Gächter, 2002).
- **close**: Some study ostracism/social exclusion, institutional/centralized sanctioning, alternative sanction types, or reputation as indirect punishment (e.g., Masclet, 2003; Fatas et al., 2010). A few focus on third-party or collective punishment mechanisms.
- **adjacent/weak**: Papers focusing on internalized norms, reputation cues, or symbolic sanctions are less directly relevant to the implementation of costly payoff-consuming punishment mechanisms.

### c. Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome

- **exact**: Only a moderate subset measure efficiency as the group's realized total payoff relative to the cooperative maximum; notable examples include Fehr et al. (2002), Gintis et al. (2003), Reuben & Riedl (2009), Masclet (2003), and others. Some theory papers derive equilibrium efficiency under various punishment schemes.
- **close/adjacent/weak**: Many report only on contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency, or attitudinal/psychological outcomes, and must be carefully separated from direct efficiency evidence.
- **none**: A substantial number of studies do not report any payoff-based outcome.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes (Relevant for Efficiency):**
    - Total group payoff/earnings, efficiency ratios, surplus (most direct for prediction task)
    - Provision success rates in threshold PGGs (proxy, less precise)
    - Welfare, net yield after punishment/reward costs, inequality (sometimes reported)
    - Some studies provide comparative statics or formulas mapping design to efficiency (theory)

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (Not Equivalent to Efficiency):**
    - Contribution or cooperation rates (common, but can diverge from efficiency if punishment is costly)
    - Frequency, size, and targeting of punishment
    - Norm compliance, sanction approval, willingness to pay for sanctions
    - Attitudinal variables (trust, fairness perceptions, satisfaction, emotion)
    - Voting for/against institutional change
    - Social status, psychological motives, emotional/moral reactions

**Distinction:**  
While high contribution rates often translate into high efficiency, this relation breaks down when punishment or rewards are costly, misapplied, or when cooperation peaks only due to anticipated but costly enforcement. Thus, direct measures of group payoff/efficiency after accounting for sanctioning costs are crucial for accurate prediction.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical Regularities and Empirical Ambiguities

- **Punishment increases efficiency when:**
    - The punishment is sufficiently effective (high fine-to-fee ratio) and not too costly—e.g., Fehr et al. (2002) observe a 40% increase in payoffs net of punishment costs in ten-round, four-player, linear PGGs, both with stranger and partner matching, and this has been replicated across cultures (Gintis et al., 2003).
    - The group is homogeneous and each player can both monitor and punish effectively (Reuben & Riedl, 2009; Guala, 2012).
    - Ostracism or exclusion threatens are present instead of, or alongside, monetary punishment (Masclet, 2003; Kerr et al., 2009).
    - The initial or baseline (control) efficiency is low—punishment has a large positive marginal effect (Fehr & Gintis, 2007).
    - The punishment system includes effective monitoring and/or reputation mechanisms (Bednar, 2006; Decker et al., 2003; Noailly et al., 2009; ABREU et al., 1991; Tarui et al., 2008).
    - Combined with communication and institution-building (Ostrom et al., 1992) or when endogenous choice of sanctioning mechanism is allowed.

- **Punishment can fail to increase efficiency or decrease it when:**
    - Punishment costs are high relative to benefits (Barclay, 2004: all-Mongolian groups; Decker et al., 2003; Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
    - Antisocial or emotional punishment is common, or punishment is misapplied or untargeted (Guala, 2012; Decker et al., 2003).
    - Group composition is heterogeneous or privileged (one high-benefit, others low-benefit; Reuben & Riedl, 2009).
    - Punishment is centralized/leader-administered with asymmetric benefit or cost structures, or if leader incentives diverge from group incentives (Gürerk et al., 2009).
    - Alternative more harmful defection options are available, or punishment can be evaded (Mulder et al., 2006; Mulder et al., 2009).
    - Monitoring is imperfect, noise is high, or institution for credible, severe (draconian) punishment is missing (Bednar, 2006; ABREU et al., 1991; Langlois & Langlois, 2004).
    - Group size is large without supporting institutions (Dubreuil, 2008; POLLOCK, 1988).

- **Reward or hybrid mechanisms sometimes outperform punishment:**
    - Reward almost always increases both contributions and efficiency, while punishment can reduce efficiency due to net resource destruction (Gürerk et al., 2009; Decker et al., 2003; Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
    - In some environments, especially when both are available, initial use and path dependence (leading with reward) results in higher efficiency (Gürerk et al., 2009).

- **Interaction effects:**
    - Communication, visibility, and institution-building *amplify* the positive effect of punishment on efficiency (Ostrom et al., 1992).
    - The effect is moderated by group composition, cultural background, and social context (Barclay, 2004; Reuben & Riedl, 2009).
    - Path dependence/history affects treatment outcomes (Gürerk et al., 2009).
    - Institutional design—targeted vs. collective punishment, the ability to share or shift punishment cost, etc.—critically shapes efficiency (Decker et al., 2003; Jaffe & Zaballa, 2010).

## Theoretical Results

- **Theory consistently finds that:**
    - Enabling sufficiently credible, severe, and not-too-costly punishment expands the parameter range over which high-efficiency equilibria can be sustained (Evans & Thomas, 2001; BOYD & RICHERSON, 1992; Bendor & Swistak, 2001).
    - Imperfect or costly monitoring reduces the achievable equilibrium efficiency (Bednar, 2006; ABREU et al., 1991).
    - Non-monotonic effects can arise: intermediate monitoring or punishment efficacy can produce bifurcations or catastrophic drops in efficiency if the system fails to coordinate on compliance (Whitmeyer, 2004).
    - The joint baselines—pre-punishment efficiency (control), monitoring/information structure, and sanction cost/impact—are strong predictors of treatment efficiency.

## Non-Payoff Outcomes

- While punishment *almost always* increases contributions and cooperation rates in the short run, these increases frequently outstrip the increase in realized efficiency due to the costliness of enforcement, targeting efficacy, or the appearance of antisocial punishment. Several experiments and meta-analyses highlight this distinction.

## Conflicting/Conditional Results

- Some experiments and theory show that under short repetition, centralized or costly/untargeted punishment, or high baseline cooperation, punishment has little or negative effect on efficiency (Guala, 2012; Mulder et al., 2006; Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
- The effect can be negative if the institution encourages excessive, spiteful, or antisocial punishment (Guala, 2012; Decker et al., 2003).
- In heterogenous or privileged groups, or with strong inequality, punishment may increase inequality and have little efficiency benefit (Reuben & Riedl, 2009).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Summary:**  
The literature supports a conditional expectation: enabling peer punishment in public-goods-game-like environments nearly always increases group efficiency when the punishment mechanism is both effective (i.e., sufficiently strong, credible, and correctly targeted) and *not* excessively costly, and when the baseline (control) efficiency is low due to unstable cooperation. However, if the punishment is poorly structured, costly, or subject to misuse (antisocial/emotional punishment), or when baseline cooperation is already high, the net effect on efficiency may be small or negative. Prediction of treatment efficiency from design dimensions and control efficiency should:

- Anchor on empirical control efficiency: the lower the baseline, the larger the likely efficiency gain if punishment is effective.
- Heavily weight punishment cost/impact parameters, MPCR, player count, and monitoring structure: punishment is more likely to be efficiency-improving when costs are low to moderate relative to the gain from increased cooperation, when MPCR is moderate to high (return to cooperation is worthwhile), and monitoring allows correct targeting.
- Consider the type of punishment (peer vs. centralized, targeted vs. collective, monetary vs. exclusion) and the opportunity for antisocial/emotional punishment, as these can moderate or reverse efficiency effects.
- Recognize that institutional features (communication, endogenous institution-building, visibility, reputation systems) strongly amplify positive efficiency effects (Ostrom et al., 1992).

**Dimension-specific prediction suggestions:**
- **High player_count (>6):** Increased risk of punishment dilution; efficiency gains from punishment only if supporting institutions exist.
- **num_rounds > 8:** Positive long-term effect more likely; in one-shot or very short games, punishment may not pay off (Balliet et al., 2011).
- **MPCR < 0.4:** Punishment may not yield net efficiency increases due to low return from cooperation (Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
- **High punishment_cost/low effectiveness:** Efficiency likely reduced; predicts resource wastefulness (Decker et al., 2003; Guala, 2012).
- **Reward_exists:** Reward may outperform or substitute for punishment (Gürerk et al., 2009).
- **chat and communication enabled:** Amplifies positive efficiency effects (Ostrom et al., 1992; Balliet et al., 2011).
- **Heterogeneous groups/privileged roles:** Punishment less effective for efficiency, risk of increased inequality (Reuben & Riedl, 2009).
- **Imperfect monitoring:** Expect incomplete efficiency gains (Bednar, 2006; ABREU et al., 1991).

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

- **Directly Informed:**  
    - `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (i.e., fine-to-fee ratio), and `all_or_nothing` are frequently manipulated or reported in high-quality experiments/theory, and are central to several key findings.
    - `chat` and `communication`–especially in combination with punishment–are highlighted as powerful moderators.
    - `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries` are addressed in studies on information feedback and monitoring, both in empirical and theoretical work.
    - Group composition (homogeneous vs. privileged/heterogeneous) is explored in a few studies (Reuben & Riedl, 2009; Barclay, 2004; Balliet et al., 2011), though not always as a formal dimension.

- **Indirectly Informed:**  
    - `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, and `reward_tech` are compared to punishment in some experimental studies (Gürerk et al., 2009; Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008), but less frequently.
    - `default_contrib` (opt-in/opt-out framing) is largely absent except in certain recent framing experiments or theory work.
    - `show_punishment_id` and aspects of punishment anonymity appear in studies of antisocial punishment and in some centralized/leader-administered punishment papers, but are not a primary focus.
    - `all_or_nothing` vs. continuous decisions receive treatment, especially in network and theoretical models.

- **Contextual or Sparse:**  
    - `chat` is directly manipulated in a few studies but often not systematically linked to efficiency outcomes.
    - Role of institution-building, endogenous group formation, or dynamic tie change (rare but highlighted in some simulation and field studies).
    - Information structure and monitoring frequency (e.g., individual vs. aggregate visibility) are central in theory, but only variably matched in empirical designs.

- **Effectively Missing:**  
    - Detailed analyses of the impact of `default_contrib` (contribution framing) are rare.
    - Nuanced manipulation of informational feedback (e.g., `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`) is sometimes present, but the direct impact on efficiency is not systematically quantified.
    - Dynamic or endogenous adjustment of punishment/reward levels during play (real institution building) is uncommon in lab environments.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Widespread reliance on indirect or adjacent outcome variables:**  
    Most studies report cooperation rates, punishment frequency, or attitudes rather than efficiency, necessitating inference and raising the risk of overestimating payoff-based improvements.

- **Generalizability limitations:**  
    Findings from standard, repeated, small-group PGGs with full information and salient punishment may not extend to large, heterogeneous, real-world or institutional settings unless supporting institution-building, communication, or centralized monitoring is available.

- **Complex interactions and path dependence:**  
    Efficiency effects of enabling punishment are highly conditional on parameter interactions, including baseline efficiency, game horizon, information structure, group composition, and punishment cost/technology. History dependence and learning effects are occasionally observed but rarely quantified for prediction.

- **Uncertainty about antisocial/emotional punishment:**  
    Studies disagree on the prevalence and impact of antisocial punishment; in some contexts it can substantially reduce efficiency, in others it is rare or self-limiting.

- **Few studies allow precise mapping from control efficiency, design dimensions, and punishment intervention to quantitative treatment efficiency:**  
    While qualitative directionality is clear for many design points, quantitative effect sizes and reliable prediction functions remain sparse except in well-studied, standard lab PGGs with 2–5 players, 8–10 rounds, and MPCR 0.4–0.5.

- **Cultural, contextual, and population-specific moderation:**  
    Cultural and group composition effects can strongly moderate both punishment assignment and efficiency outcomes, with ethnic/cultural background, legitimacy, and group identification emerging as important but incompletely understood factors.

- **Sparse attention to default framing, endogenous institution-building, and real-world communication constraints:**  
    While several theory papers highlight these as crucial, few empirical papers systematically vary or report these factors as part of the design matrix.

**In sum:**  
This paper set provides strong, directly relevant evidence for predicting efficiency effects of enabling punishment in standard repeated PGGs using central design dimensions. However, gaps remain in (a) documenting efficiency rather than just cooperation, (b) mapping under-explored design dimensions, and (c) providing comprehensive, quantitative prediction functions across broader real-world or complex institutional cases. Ambiguity, non-monotonicity, and context sensitivity are real and must be retained in predictive judgment.
