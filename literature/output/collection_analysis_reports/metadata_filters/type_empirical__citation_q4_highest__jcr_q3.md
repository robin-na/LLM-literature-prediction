# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set consists of **32 studies**—overwhelmingly **empirical, experimental lab experiments**—with a narrow-to-moderate topical focus relevant to predicting the effect of introducing punishment in public-goods-game-like (PGG) environments. Most papers directly employ PGGs or close linear variants (e.g., variant network structures, endowment heterogeneity), while a significant minority investigate related social dilemmas (trust games, sender-receiver games, dyadic exchanges, dictator/ultimatum games).

The **evidence base is broad on behavioral mechanisms (e.g., norm enforcement, conditional cooperation, role of status or feedback)**, and somewhat narrower on direct, within-study comparisons of group **efficiency (payoff relative to full cooperation) with and without peer punishment**. A subset of studies closely match the prediction scenario: randomized lab experiments in linear PGGs with explicit manipulation of peer punishment. Several studies additionally manipulate reward, information, group structure, or related sanction mechanisms.

Very few theory or purely observational/informal mechanism papers are present; most results are causal and derived from laboratory manipulations.

---

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) PGG or Variant**

- **Exact relevance:** About half the set directly uses linear PGGs or the voluntary contribution mechanism (VCM) (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Nikiforakis, 2010; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
- **Close relevance:** Some use trust games, public good variants with group formation or special networks, or structured intergroup games (e.g., Eckel et al., 2010; Ahn et al., 2008).
- **Adjacent to weak:** The rest employ dyadic social dilemmas (trust, dictator, investment games), gossip/reputation treatments, sender-receiver games, or study prosociality without a group dilemma structure.

**b) Punishment or Sanctions**

- **Exact relevance:** ~1/3 directly compare PGGs with and without peer (or legal) punishment (e.g., Anderson & Putterman, 2006; Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
- **Close relevance:** Many address sanctioning as a primary manipulation, but sometimes in forms distinct from standard PGG peer punishment (e.g., legal/sanction treatments, group formation as sanction, probabilistic punishment).
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Several focus on behavioral responses to sanctions, norms, or reputation, but not via a switchable punishment regime as per the prediction task.

**c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**

- **Exact relevance:** Core PGG punishment intervention papers generally report efficiency or group earnings as a central outcome (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Tyran & Feld, 2006; Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
- **Close to Adjacent:** Many others measure contributions, compliance, or frequency of efficient outcomes as proxies; some report earnings but do not centralize efficiency relative to the social optimum.
- **Weak/None:** A significant set is limited to prosocial behavior, cooperation rates, or norm-following, with little or no payoff reporting (e.g., punishment frequency, reputational perceptions).

**Summary:** The **most direct evidence** for the downstream task comes from a cluster of lab PGGs explicitly reporting group efficiency with/without peer punishment under clear design parameters. Additional relevant nuance is introduced through studies on information environments, group structure, and monitoring technology.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes** (Relevant for Prediction):
- **Group efficiency** (group earnings relative to the social optimum): measured in several PGG studies (e.g., Kube & Traxler, 2011; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
- **Total or average earnings/payoffs**: sometimes analyzed as net payoffs or surplus (Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
- **Welfare**: often appearing under "efficiency," usually synonymous with group payoff.
- **Frequency of efficient outcomes**: For some adjacent studies (e.g., McCabe et al., 1998) this is used as a qualitative analog to earnings.
- **No direct efficiency reporting**: In some papers, inferences must be drawn from reported contributions assuming standard payoff functions.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes** (Sometimes used as Proxies, but Not Equivalent):
- **Contribution or cooperation rate**: Majority of studies report these, often as central focus (e.g., Aoyagi & Fréchette, 2009; Carpenter, 2007).
- **Punishment frequency/amount**, **types of punished behavior**, **norm compliance**: Core to many studies, especially those examining psychological mechanisms or variations in punishment technology (e.g., Seip et al., 2014).
- **Reputational or trust outcomes**, **frequency of partner choice**, **perceptions after punishment**: Predominant in several adjacent papers, not directly convertible to efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the most relevant, empirical PGG experiments:

- **Enabling peer punishment often increases group efficiency**, but this effect is **conditional** and **not guaranteed**.
  - In standard, linear PGGs, **punishment increases efficiency substantially when free-riding is otherwise prevalent** (Kube & Traxler, 2011; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
  - The **magnitude and direction** of efficiency gains depend critically on game design:
    - **Punishment cost**: High punishment costs suppress use and minimize net efficiency effects; low-cost punishment increases both cooperation and punishment activity, sometimes resulting in *lower* net efficiency (due to cost drain) (Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
    - **Punishment technology**: Allowing **counterpunishment** (retaliation) or **multiple rounds** of punishment can cause efficiency to fall *below* baseline/no-punishment levels (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
    - **Information structure**: What is shown to players about others' behavior and payoffs can substantially moderate treatment effects. **Contribution feedback leads to higher efficiency** than earnings feedback; showing both can reduce efficiency below control (Nikiforakis, 2010).
    - **Group structure**: Network position/status, group size, and monitoring fractions are critical moderators (Eckel et al., 2010; Carpenter, 2007).
    - **Sanction source**: When mild punishment is *self-imposed* (via group vote), even non-deterrent regimes yield much higher efficiency than exogenous rules (Tyran & Feld, 2006).
  - **Positive efficiency effects are robust to heterogeneity** in endowment or marginal benefits, but the emergent contribution norm may differ (Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
  - **Effects are not always monotonic**: Increasing the scope or frequency of sanctioning (e.g., multiple punishment rounds or unrestricted retaliation) can decrease efficiency (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).

- **Non-PGG or adjacent-game studies** provide supporting evidence that reputation systems, social proximity, and endogenous norms can substitute for or amplify formal punishment effects. However, these are not direct evidence about efficiency changes due to peer punishment per se.

- When only **contribution rates** are measured (with no efficiency data), nearly all PGG punishment studies report large increases in cooperation, especially when punishment is well-targeted, but cannot resolve whether this translates to higher efficiency net of cost (Carpenter, 2007; Anderson & Stafford, 2003).

- **Absence or weakness of punishment**: In standard PGGs, groups without punishment rapidly converge to minimal contributions and low efficiency, regardless of group heterogeneity or normative framing (Reuben & Riedl, 2013).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

For **predicting treatment efficiency** in PGG-like games from the observed **control (no-punishment) efficiency and design dimensions**, the literature provides the following **guidance**:

- **Punishment increases group efficiency** in most standard linear PGGs with moderate punishment cost, sufficiently high marginal per-capita return (MPCR), and no counterpunishment opportunity. The margin by which efficiency increases is often substantial (Kube & Traxler, 2011; Reuben & Riedl, 2013).
  - **If control efficiency is low** and punishments are not overly cheap or prone to retaliation, the increases can be dramatic.
  - **If punishment cost is low and not well-targeted**, total group welfare may *decrease*, as the cost of punishment outweighs efficiency from increased contributions (Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
  - **If counterpunishment or extended cycles are possible**, enabling punishment can *reduce* efficiency relative to baseline (Denant-Boemont et al., 2007).
- **Key design dimensions** (see Section 6) **critically moderate** the direction and size of the effect. For example:
  - **Show_other_summaries**: The kind of feedback (contribution vs. earnings vs. both) is a strong moderator (Nikiforakis, 2010).
  - **Punishment_cost**: Optimal efficiency effect occurs at intermediate cost; too low costs raise harmful punishment activity.
  - **Punishment_tech**: Retaliation loops (e.g., multiple punishment rounds) are detrimental.
  - **Group structure**: If central, high-status players are present (star networks), efficiency gains from punishment only arise if the central figure is low-status (Eckel et al., 2010).
- **Indirect/contextual cues** (e.g., the ability to monitor/punish a large fraction of the group, endogenous group formation, communication) can amplify or suppress punishment efficacy.

**Caution:** Effects cannot be predicted solely from control efficiency and the binary presence of punishment—**interactions with detailed game design are decisive**. Strong design moderation means prediction is unreliable unless critical dimensions (punishment cost/tech, feedback, group structure) are specified.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count**: Many studies (typically 3–4), with some variation to 5–10; moderation by group size evidenced, especially via monitoring fraction (Carpenter, 2007).
- **num_rounds**: Repetition (typically 5–15 rounds) is standard; indefinite vs. finite repetition has been explored in adjacent games.
- **chat**: Typically disabled; limited evidence on its impact in these studies.
- **all_or_nothing**: Almost all use continuous contribution; exceptions noted.
- **mpcr**: Experimental variation in marginal per-capita return is common and directly impacts the effect of punishment (higher MPCR supports more cooperation).
- **punishment_cost**: **Central moderator**; directly varied and analyzed.
- **punishment_tech**: Coverage of feedback about punishment, anonymity, possibility of counterpunishment, and punishment round structure; critical for negative efficiency effects when misconfigured.
- **show_other_summaries**: **Highly salient**; the nature of feedback (contribution/earnings/both) is shown to affect efficiency outcomes (Nikiforakis, 2010).

**Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Explored in a minority of studies; less central to payoff prediction.
- **group formation mechanisms** (Ahn et al., 2008): Contextually discussed as a form of endogenous social sanctioning.
- **show_n_rounds, show_punishment_id**: Sometimes specified, rarely varied independently.
- **default_contrib**: Occasionally manipulated as "opt-in" vs. "opt-out," but not central in direct comparisons.

**Underrepresented/Missing:**
- **Full range of feedback mechanisms**: Some feedback types (e.g., only own vs. everyone’s outcomes) not comprehensively tested.
- **Reward symmetry**: The combined existence/relative cost/tech of rewards and punishments is not systematically explored.
- **Communication (chat)**: Effects of chat or explicit group discussion on punishment efficacy largely missing.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited coverage of rare design features**: Most studies use standard lab PGGs; evidence for all-or-nothing designs, non-anonymous or chat-enabled games, or dynamic group membership is sparse.
- **Efficiency effects depend on unobserved moderators**: Studies emphasize that **punishment's effect on efficiency is highly sensitive** to punishment cost, ability to retaliate, feedback type, and group structure—prediction without detailed knowledge of these is unreliable.
- **Many studies report behavioral but not efficiency outcomes**: For studies measuring only contribution/punishment rates, inferring efficiency is nontrivial, especially when punishment costs could offset cooperation gains.
- **Little evidence on very large group sizes or field PGGs**.
- **Heterogeneity in definitions of efficiency**: Although usually group payoff divided by maximum possible, some papers operationalize this differently.
- **External validity and generalization**: Most results are from lab settings with monetary incentives and undergraduate subjects.
- **Ambiguity and mixed effects**: Some studies find **no increase or even a decrease in efficiency** with punishment, especially when poorly calibrated or in presence of retaliation (e.g., Denant-Boemont et al., 2007; Anderson & Putterman, 2006).
- **Sparse evidence for reward-punishment combinations**.
- **Complexity of context**: Endogeneity (voting for rules), group heterogeneity, and status centrality all show large moderation effects difficult to encode in simple prediction rules.
- **Adjacent evidence with limited direct transfer**: Many adjacent studies on gossip, reputation, or trust games support similar mechanisms but are not directly about PGG efficiency with/without punishment.

---

**In summary:**  
A substantial core of laboratory experiments shows that the **effect of peer punishment on group efficiency is positive but highly conditional**—it can range from large increases to substantial losses depending on punishment cost, technology, information feedback, and group/network structure. **Prediction accuracy for efficiency effects from punishment therefore requires detailed measurement and encoding of the relevant game design dimensions, some of which (especially feedback modality, punishment symmetry, or retaliation possibility) are not always documented in prediction settings.** Careful attention must be applied to whether reported outcomes are efficiency/payoff versus behavioral (contribution/punishment), as the translation may not be monotonic or even positive across settings.
