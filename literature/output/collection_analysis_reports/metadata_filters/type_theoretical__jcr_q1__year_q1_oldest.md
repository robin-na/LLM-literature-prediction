# 1) Evidence Base

This evidence base consists of an extensive set of 151 papers—overwhelmingly theoretical or simulation-based models, including synthesis reviews, with almost no laboratory or field experimental studies focused primarily on payoff-based outcomes. The majority of papers explicitly model or discuss public goods games (PGGs) or very closely related collective-action environments. There is a very broad spectrum of punishment mechanisms represented (peer punishment, institutional/pool punishment, reputation-based, group sanctions, coordinated punishment, voluntary/mandatory regimes), as well as a diversity of payoff structures (linear, nonlinear, threshold games, asymmetric roles, reputation-driven, networked, or spatial environments).

Empirical findings (in the narrow sense of lab or field data) are referenced within some review/synthesis/theory papers (notably citing Fehr & Gächter’s experiments, and some field/ethnographic evidence), but primary data are rare; rather, simulated and analytical results dominate. Mechanism and theory discussion is rich, with many papers offering parameter sweeps, comparative statics, phase diagrams, and conditions for equilibrium selection.

Design dimension coverage is high for core PGG features (player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment details), but weaker or more variable for secondary features (chat/communication, default framing, reward features, info feedback, identity visibility).

Overall, the paper set is conceptually broad and moderator-sensitive but relatively narrow experimentally, and most direct evidence for downstream prediction of treatment efficiency comes from cross-paper theoretical synthesis and simulation rather than empirical effect sizes.

# 2) Task Relevance

**(a) PGG or Variant**
- **Relevance:** mostly `exact` (over half the papers), some `close`, many `adjacent` but conceptually similar (repeated PD, CPR, resource dilemma, indirect reciprocity).
- **Interpretation:** The core findings are strongly grounded in PGG or directly mapped variants.

**(b) Punishment or Sanctions**
- **Relevance:** primarily `exact` (costly peer, pool punishment, group sanctions), with some `close` (indirect, reputation-based, expulsion/exclusion, commitment, coordinated retaliation), and many `adjacent` (ostracism, withholding, reputation, social comparison, etc.).
- **Interpretation:** The treatment mechanism of peer punishment is well covered; other sanctioning mechanisms are also discussed.

**(c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**
- **Relevance:** About half of papers report efficiency or closely related outcomes (`exact` or `close`); others focus on contribution rates, cooperation, punishment frequency (`adjacent`/`weak` to payoff), or only mechanism discussion.
- **Interpretation:** Direct evidence for efficiency outcomes is common but often derived from theoretical or simulated models; less so from empirical measurement.

**Summary:**  
The literature is **highly relevant** to the task (many `exact` papers spanning all three target-relevance criteria), but direct, quantitative, empirical evidence on **efficiency** is sparser, with most precision coming from theory/simulation.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes (primary for task):**  
  Many theoretical studies and a smaller number of empirical reviews report average group payoff, welfare, surplus, or efficiency (ratio of observed to possible/maximum payoff), either directly or as analytically derived outcomes.  
  For example:  
  - "average efficiency or payoff" (Wang et al., 2011; Fehr & Gintis, 2007; Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Sasaki et al., 2012; Perc, 2012)
  - Group welfare/surplus (Kitts, 2006; Sigmund et al., 2010)
  - Total coins/earnings generated (various)

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes (important but distinct):**
  Many papers focus on:
  - Contribution or cooperation rate/fraction (Fehr & Fischbacher, 2004; Fehr & Gächter, reviewed in Guala, 2012)
  - Punishment frequency or targeting (Goette & Huffman, 2007; Fehr & Rockenbach, 2004)
  - Stability/invasion of cooperation/punishment (Brandt et al., 2006; Helbing et al., 2010)
  - Norm compliance, trust, or reputation measures (Sigmund et al., 2001; van der Weele, 2012)
  These are **not** efficiency outcomes but often precede or suggest possible efficiency changes.

**Distinction:**  
The most relevant findings for prediction relate to group efficiency or average payoff, complemented by clear acknowledgment of when only indirect indicators are available.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Pattern:**  
Theory and simulation consensus: **Enabling peer punishment in PGGs tends to raise group efficiency dramatically when the control (no-punishment) game is inefficient (dominated by free-riding),** provided certain key conditions are met.

**Key Moderators and Context Dependence:**

1. **Effectiveness and Cost of Punishment**  
   - Higher punishment impact (effect per unit cost) and lower cost of punishment consistently increase the positive effect on efficiency. If punishment is very costly, benefit may be negative (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999; Sigmund et al., 2010).

2. **Game Structure – Voluntary Participation/Entry**  
   - When participation is voluntary (optional), much smaller penalties are required for punishment to be effective, and efficiency gains are often larger (Sasaki et al., 2012; Hauert et al., 2007; Fowler, 2005).
   - In compulsory settings, effect of punishment is contingent on stronger punishment for incentive alignment (Mathew & Boyd, 2009).

3. **Information and Reputation**  
   - When some reputation or knowledge about punishment behavior is present, even small probabilities of observability can tip the system into high efficiency (Sigmund et al., 2001; dos Santos et al., 2011; Hilbe & Sigmund, 2010).
   - In anonymous/uninformed settings, punishment can be ineffective or even destructive to efficiency.

4. **Second-Order Free Rider Problem**  
   - If non-punishing cooperators are not sanctioned or if compensatory mechanisms for punishers are absent, group efficiency can be low or unstable even with punishment (Ye et al., 2011; Sigmund et al., 2010; Perc, 2012).

5. **Coordination and Peer Structure**  
   - Coordinated or cost-shared punishment is far more efficient than uncoordinated, individually costly punishment (Boyd et al., 2010; Jaffe & Zaballa, 2010).
   - Network, spatial, or group structure (reach/coverage of sanctions) can create sharp phase transitions in the impact of punishment (Bodnar & Salathé, 2012).

6. **Baseline (Control) Efficiency**  
   - If control (no-punishment) games already exhibit high efficiency (via social norms, trust, or strong reciprocators), introducing punishment may have no effect or decrease efficiency by crowding out intrinsic motivation (van der Weele, 2012).

7. **Time Horizon and Matching**  
   - In short games or with random strangers, costly punishment often reduces efficiency due to its cost (Guala, 2012; Frey & Rusch, 2012).
   - In long repeated games with stable groups, efficiency tends to increase over time as costly punishment is used less and cooperation stabilizes.

8. **Mutation/Exploration Structure**  
   - The evolutionary stability of cooperative/punishing equilibria and group efficiency is highly sensitive to the mutation structure; when certain transitions are restricted, punishment’s effect on efficiency can be minimal (García & Traulsen, 2012).

9. **Pool (Institutional) vs. Peer Punishment**  
   - Pool/institutional punishment can solve second-order freeriding but can be more costly and sometimes less efficient due to persistent institutional costs (Sigmund et al., 2010; Perc, 2012).

**Conflicting/Ambiguous Findings:**
- **Negative Effects:** Some reviews argue that punishment often reduces efficiency due to destroyed resources (punisher and punished both lose), especially in short games or when uncoordinated (Guala, 2012; Sigmund et al., 2010).
- **Positive Effects:** The bulk of models (including robust empirical simulations) find efficiency is dramatically increased by punishment in settings with low baseline efficiency and the right parameter regime.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should efficiency prediction be informed by this literature?**

- **Default Expectation:**  
  If a PGG or close variant features low control efficiency, is not anonymous, and allows even moderately effective costly peer punishment, **predict a significant increase in group efficiency (actual/maximum payoff ratio) when peer punishment is enabled**—up to, but not always attaining, the maximal (fully cooperative) group payoff.

- **Adjustment by Key Dimensions:**
  - **Time Horizon (num_rounds):**  
    Longer horizons, stable groups: larger long-run efficiency increase; short games: possibly no net gain.
  - **Punishment Cost & Magnitude (punishment_cost, punishment_tech):**  
    High cost/low impact: minimal/negative efficiency gain; moderate or low cost/high effectiveness: substantial gain.
  - **Information (show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id, reputation):**  
    If the technology enables reputation or even minimal observability, effect is more positive and robust.
  - **Participation (all_or_nothing, voluntary entry):**  
    Optional/voluntary entry: much easier to achieve high efficiency with smaller penalties.
  - **Second-Order Punishment or Reward (reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech):**  
    If some mechanism compensates punishers or punishes non-punishers, efficiency is more robust.
  - **Control Efficiency:**  
    If control is already high, adding punishment may have little or even negative marginal effect.
  - **Coordination & Communication (chat, peer/collective monitoring):**  
    Mechanisms that enable coordination or communication (even minimal) amplify the efficiency gain from punishment.

- **Qualifiers:**
  - **Anonymity/No Information:**  
    If the game is highly anonymous, or reputation cannot be built, punishment may not improve efficiency.
  - **Mutation/Population Structure:**  
    Prediction uncertainty is higher in evolutionary models with constrained mutation/exploration.
  - **Pool Punishment/Institutional Regime:**  
    Institutional punishment can be more stable but sometimes less efficient due to fixed institutional costs.

- **Special Cases:**
  - If compensatory mechanisms for punishers (sympathy/rewards) are present, efficiency gains may be larger than with punishment alone.
  - For spatial/structured/peer punishment with sufficient reach, expect possible threshold (nonlinear) increases in efficiency.

**Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes:**  
If the literature reports only increased contributions but not increased payoffs, one should **not** infer an increase in efficiency without further evidence on punishment costs and resource flows.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions (most robust):**
- `player_count` (group size): frequently analyzed as a moderator in theoretical models.
- `num_rounds` (game length): widely discussed with clear effects on efficiency gains.
- `mpcr` (Marginal Per Capita Return): almost universally used in model parameterizations.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (cost and technology): central, detailed in models as threshold parameters and in phase diagrams.
- `reward_exists` (and less often, `reward_cost`/`reward_tech`): considered in several models.
- `all_or_nothing` (binary vs. continuous contributions): frequently modeled.
- `voluntary participation` (entry/exit, abstention): critical moderator in several high-relevance models.
- `show_other_summaries`/`show_punishment_id` (information structure): key moderators of the efficiency effect in several models.

**Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
- `chat` (communication): less systematically analyzed, but noted in reviews (Ostrom, 2000; Kraak, 2011).
- `default_contrib` (framing): background factor, rarely explicitly analyzed.
- `show_n_rounds`: sometimes included, not a major moderator but sometimes relevant for horizon effects.

**Sparse or Largely Missing:**
- `show_punishment_id` (punisher identity): mostly discussed in reputation models; not always separable.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`, `reward_magnitude`: discussed in some reviews, much less systematically than punishment mechanics.
- Framing effects, psychological/belief updates, emotional context: present in commentary/review, not systematically parameterized.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Empirical Evidence Gap:**  
  Most predictions are supported by theory and simulation, not direct experimental effect sizes for specific game implementations.
- **Behavioral vs. Efficiency Outcomes:**  
  Many references to increased cooperation/contribution rates do not translate to increased efficiency when punishment is costly.
- **Parameter Sensitivity and Non-Monotonicity:**  
  Many effects are threshold-dependent, nonlinear, or exhibit discontinuous transitions; monotonic expectation is not justified for all design and context variations.
- **Context Dependence:**  
  Key moderators (reputation, group structure, information flow, time horizon) are not always explicitly described or easily mapped to PGG-like protocols.
- **Heterogeneity of Punishment Regimes:**  
  Pool punishment, peer punishment, exclusion/ostracism, and expulsion have distinct efficiency implications—findings for one do not always generalize to the others.
- **Limited Experimental Evidence for Rare Contexts:**  
  Some special regimes (e.g., voluntary entry + pool punishment, asymmetric information, mutation-limited evolutionary dynamics) are well modeled but lack broad empirical validation.
- **Effect Size Calibration:**  
  The theory provides directional and (sometimes) qualitative guidance; precise quantitative effect sizes for treatment efficiency are generally not available and require caution in translation.

---

**In summary:**  
- There is robust theoretical and simulation-based support for **predicting large efficiency gains from enabling peer punishment in PGGs with low control efficiency, provided punishment is not too costly and some minimal communication/reputation is present**.
- Many design dimensions can drastically moderate this effect, and there is substantial variation across mechanisms and contexts.
- **Payoff-based outcomes** are directly modeled in many studies, but quantitative experimental evidence is sparse.
- For downstream prediction, **the effect of punishment should be modeled as strongly positive in the canonical case, but sensitive to design features, especially cost, information, time horizon, and the presence or absence of second-order punishment or coordination mechanisms**.
- **Crucially, one cannot simply map increased cooperation to increased efficiency unless the cost structure is favorable**. Prediction models must reflect these moderator effects and the possibility, under some conditions, that punishment will not increase and may even decrease efficiency.
