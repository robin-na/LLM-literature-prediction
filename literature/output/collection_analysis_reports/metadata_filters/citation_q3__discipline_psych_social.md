# 1) Evidence Base

This paper set (~189 entries) is **broad** and includes a mix of **empirical experimental studies** (lab and field), **theoretical papers**, and **observational/ethnographic work**. For the core prediction task—forecasting the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments—coverage is:
- **Strong** for public goods games (PGGs) and close variants: Many lab experiments use exact or close analogs.
- **Dense** for punishment or sanctioning mechanisms: Both peer and central/institutional punishment systems are represented, with some comparison to rewards.
- **Substantial but divided** for efficiency or payoff-related outcomes: Several papers directly measure group efficiency or aggregate payoffs, but a large proportion report only behavioral outcomes (e.g., contributions, cooperation rates).
- Theoretical models provide comparative statics and mechanism explanations, but fewer quantitative predictions.

The **empirical evidence** is richer for lab PGGs with standard peer punishment than for highly complex or naturalistic contexts. Observational and ethnographic reports focus more on punishment norms and motivations but rarely provide payoff-based or group-level efficiency data.

# 2) Task Relevance

**Relevance for the three key target dimensions:**
- **pgg_or_variant:** *Exact* (many) to *close* (some) relevance. Most lab experiments and a number of models explicitly use repeated linear PGGs or step-level public goods games. Some close variants (team production, CPR, etc.) offer supplementary support.
- **punishment_or_sanctions:** *Exact* relevance for numerous studies—particularly for peer punishment (all-can-punish-all), centralized punishment, democratic punishment, and sometimes for reward/compensation as a contrasting intervention. Some studies address only the *expectation* of punishment, or institutional/third-party punishment (*close*).
- **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:** *Mixed.* While several key empirical papers (*exact* relevance) report group efficiency or direct payoff-based outcomes, many others focus on non-payoff behavior (contribution/cooperation rates). Some offer *adjacent* relevance by discussing group earnings, surplus, or welfare but not standardized efficiency as a ratio to the all-cooperate ideal.

**Overall:** The strongest evidence comes from lab experiments using repeated linear PGGs with exact measures of efficiency or group payoffs.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-based Outcomes:**
- *Directly measured in several studies*: Efficiency (group payoff relative to the full-cooperation optimum), total group earnings, welfare, surplus, total coins generated.
- *Frequently reported as group earnings or average payoff*, sometimes analyzed as “efficiency”.

**Non-payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- Contribution rate/cooperation rate: Most commonly reported behavioral measure; often, increases in contributions do not directly translate into improved efficiency due to punishment costs.
- Punishment frequency, assigned amounts, norm compliance: Useful for process understanding but **not predictors of efficiency unless mapped to cost structure**.
- Subjective outcomes: Reports on fairness, satisfaction, trust, psychological processes, etc.—useful for mechanisms but not direct predictors of efficiency.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis Across the Most Relevant Papers:**
- **Enabling punishment in PGGs almost never guarantees increased efficiency.** The effect is *highly conditional* on the design of the punishment institution, its cost/effectiveness, and the social context.
  - **Standard peer punishment** (all-can-punish-all) generally increases contributions but often fails to improve efficiency due to high punishment costs; in some cases, efficiency decreases (e.g., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Nockur et al., 2021; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
  - **Centralized or leader punishment** (especially when prosocial leaders) increases both contributions and efficiency, with lower punishment costs (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Gürerk et al., 2009).
  - **Democratic punishment** and *group-coordinated mechanisms* (e.g., democratic peer punishment) consistently yield higher efficiency than standard peer punishment by minimizing antisocial and excessive punishment (Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Nockur et al., 2021).
  - **Cost of punishment** is a critical moderator: High punishment cost can destroy efficiency gains from increased contributions (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017).
  - **Retaliation/antisocial punishment:** Environments allowing for retaliation (multiple punishment stages, open IDs) see efficiency gains nullified or reversed due to feuding and counter-punishment (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
  - **Social learning/history effects:** Providing detailed social information/history increases the efficiency of punishment relative to baseline, by reducing punishment use and improving initial cooperation (Gürerk, 2013).
  - **Outcome uncertainty/noisy monitoring:** When monitoring is imperfect or penalties are misapplied, the positive impact of punishment on efficiency is reduced or reversed (Bednar, 2006; Wu et al., 2020; Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
  - **Reward mechanisms:** Often produce higher efficiency than punishment for equivalent increases in cooperation, since rewards increase group surplus rather than dissipating resources (Gürerk et al., 2009).
  - **Interaction with control efficiency:** When the baseline (control, no-punishment) efficiency is already high, the net effect of enabling punishment is more likely to be negative or negligible. If control efficiency is low, punishment may have more room to increase efficiency, but only if costs are low and misuse minimized (Nair et al., 2018).

*Theory and Mechanism Support:*
- Theoretical models underscore that punishment can only increase efficiency if the cost-to-impact ratio (fine-to-fee) is favorable and sufficient coordination or institutional structure exists to prevent mutual costly punishment (Ye et al., 2011; Shinada & Yamagishi, 2008).
- Mechanism arguments emphasize the importance of monitoring quality, leader motivation, and the legitimacy/social acceptability of punishment regimes (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Kosfeld, 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **The main lesson:** **Do not simply predict a positive efficiency effect from enabling peer punishment in a PGG.** The effect is *positive, neutral, or negative* depending on:
  - The baseline (control) efficiency: If already high, punishment is unlikely to help and may reduce efficiency due to incurred costs (Nair et al., 2018).
  - **Punishment costs and effectiveness:** If the punishment cost is high relative to its deterrence effect, efficiency can decline.
  - **Punishment institution:** Peer punishment is prone to overuse and retaliation cycles unless features like democratic control or centralization are included.
  - **Game structure:** Games with explicit channels for both participation and compliance enforcement show stronger positive effects (Aakre et al., 2016).
  - **Availability of social or reward mechanisms:** When alternative positive incentives or social learning/history are available, the need for punishment and its marginal efficiency gain diminish or can even reverse.
  - **Information/monitoring:** Good monitoring and transparency (visibility of actions, income) amplify positive effects; noisy environments dampen them or produce perverse targeting (Hauser et al., 2021).
  - **Antisocial punishment:** If the design allows for significant antisocial punishment, net efficiency gains are less likely.

**Dimension-specific predictive guidance:**
- **Democratic/centralized/institutional punishment** → More likely positive or neutral efficiency effect.
- **Standard peer punishment** (all can punish all) → Efficiency often *declines* due to high punishment expenditures unless antisocial punishment is minimized.
- **Costless punishment** → Leads to excessive sanctioning and decreased efficiency compared to costly or regulated punishment (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017).
- **Designs with rich information (social history, feedback, visible income)** → Positive moderation of efficiency effect.
- **Retaliatory possibility, identity display, or multiple punishment rounds** → Negative moderation of efficiency effect.
- **Short games (few rounds)** → Less time for efficiency benefits to materialize, costs may dominate.
- **Low MPCR/all-or-nothing structure** → Punishment less able to compensate for coordination or incentive problems.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

The following 14 game design dimensions have varying levels of coverage:

**Directly informed by evidence (multiple empirical and theory papers):**
- `player_count` (group size): Multiple studies vary and specify effects, sometimes as a moderator (though generally less impactful for punishment effect unless very large/small).
- `num_rounds`: Most studies use repeated games (6–30 rounds). More rounds allow dynamic effects of punishment to emerge.
- `mpcr` (marginal per-capita return): Explicitly varied or reported. High MPCR strengthens positive efficiency effects of punishment.
- `all_or_nothing` (binary vs. continuous): Both types represented; effects of punishment comparable, but binary choices can make over-punishment more likely.
- `punishment_cost` & `punishment_tech` (cost-to-impact/fine-to-fee): Core moderating variables; repeatedly found to crucially determine whether efficiency rises or falls.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Covered in studies contrasting reward and punishment, showing rewards are often more efficient.
- `chat` (communication): Enabling chat/communication can sometimes substitute for or moderate need for punishment.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Informational changes are present in some studies and shown to affect efficiency, especially visibility of incomes and outcomes (Hauser et al., 2021).

**Indirectly or contextually discussed:**
- `default_contrib`: Present in a small number of studies as a framing device.
- `show_punishment_id`: Some evidence that identity display exacerbates retaliation/antisocial punishment and reduces efficiency.
- `punishment_tech` (mechanism, e.g., restricted vs. unrestricted): Important for moderate-to-strong effect on efficiency (Xiao & Kunreuther, 2016).

**Sparse or missing:**
- Several dimensions are underexplored: e.g., effects of default contribution framing, certain forms of information feedback, the interaction of chat with punishment regimes, or the precise effect of revealing the total number of rounds. Many studies report the dimension but do not systematically vary or analyze its moderating role.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavioral vs. payoff outcome confusion:** Many studies measure contributions/cooperation but not efficiency. Predicting efficiency purely from contribution rates is misleading, as overuse of punishment can erase/payoff gains.
- **Contextual and cultural effects:** Laboratory results may not generalize to field/natural settings, as real-world frequency and legitimacy of punishment are often lower; antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) or reputational motives can subvert intended efficiency gains.
- **Incomplete coverage of design space:** Some potentially important dimensions (e.g., impact of visibility, communication modality, endowment inequality) are only partly addressed.
- **Control efficiency not always reported:** Not all relevant studies provide baseline (no-punishment) efficiency, limiting direct calculation of efficiency change.
- **Lack of high-powered field experiments:** Most directly relevant findings are from lab games with student samples. Observational or field studies provide suggestive context but rarely measure group-level efficiency outcomes.
- **Ambiguity in direction and size:** Literature sometimes conflicts (e.g., some contexts show strong positive efficiency effect of punishment, others strong negative), and interaction effects (costs, institution, information) sometimes produce non-monotonic or threshold effects.
- **Few studies systematically model all 14 design dimensions:** Syntheses rarely report effect size variation across the full set—guidance is dimension-specific and often conditional, not comprehensive.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature provides *strong, directly relevant empirical and theoretical evidence* that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency in public-goods-game-like environments is *not universally positive*—it depends on how and by whom punishment is implemented, its cost/effectiveness, the baseline efficiency, and social/institutional context. Precise prediction from game design dimensions plus control efficiency is possible **only when these key moderators are known and mapped to prior study contexts**. When information is missing, ambiguity and risk of error increase. Behavioral outcome data should *never* be substituted for payoff-based efficiency outcomes in quantitative prediction.
