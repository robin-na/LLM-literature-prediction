# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 24 studies, predominantly empirical and experimental lab studies, with a few field experiments and observational papers. The empirical focus is broad, spanning classic laboratory public goods games (PGGs), common-pool resource (CPR) games, trust games, dictator/ultimatum games, and related social dilemma paradigms. A significant number of papers address psychological and neural mechanisms, often using neurostimulation or personality measures, while others present institutional design, social information, or communication manipulations. Only three papers include direct empirical analysis of efficiency or group payoff in the context of public goods games or close CPR analogues with punishment or sanctioning. The majority analyze behavioral outcomes (e.g., contributions, punishment decisions, cooperation rates), motivational processes, or contextual/cultural effects rather than direct payoff-based results. There are no pure theory-only papers in this set.

Overall, the evidence base is broad in terms of behavioral and psychological mechanisms in cooperation and punishment contexts, but narrow with respect to directly supporting the punishment-efficiency prediction task as defined (treatment vs. control efficiency in PGG-like settings as a function of design dimensions).

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- *Exact relevance*: Most experimental papers use standard or canonical linear PGGs (e.g., Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021; Molleman et al., 2019; Micheli et al., 2021; Li et al., 2018, 2020, 2018b; Jolly & Chang, 2021; Gianotti et al., 2019; Frey, 2019; Chen et al., 2021), or report their design and parameters in sufficient detail.
- *Close relevance*: Some studies employ CPR games with PGG-like structure (Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019) or one-shot, binary PGGs (Molleman et al., 2019).
- *Adjacent/weak relevance*: Several studies use trust games, dictator/ultimatum games, or other allocation paradigms (Fonseca & Peters, 2021; Lo Gerfo et al., 2019), or examine communication and gifting in pastoralist or social context games (Du et al., 2019; Gallier et al., 2018).

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- *Exact relevance*: Only a subset of papers directly test costly punishment or formal sanctioning in the experimental protocol (Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021; Molleman et al., 2019; Micheli et al., 2021; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Chen et al., 2019; Lo Gerfo et al., 2019; Baumgartner et al., 2021).
- *Adjacent*: Some analyze social sanctions, reputation mechanisms, or punishment motivation without real game-theoretic punishment options (Fonseca & Peters, 2021; Samu & Takács, 2021).
- *None/weak*: The remainder exclude punishment (most neural/tDCS studies, baseline CPR/PGG, communication, and observability studies).

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- *Exact relevance*: Only Wegmann & Musshoff (2019) and Fonseca & Peters (2021, though not a PGG) explicitly report efficiency or group payoff as a primary outcome.
- *Close relevance*: Burton-Chellew & Guérin (2021) and Burton-Chellew & D'Amico (2021) discuss efficiency/welfare as a secondary or interpretive outcome, often inferring from contribution and cost data rather than reporting efficiency directly.
- *Adjacent/weak*: Micheli et al. (2021) and several others infer potential payoff impacts from observed changes in contribution or cooperation, but without reporting actual efficiency or group surplus.
- *None*: Most other studies report only non-payoff behavioral outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-related outcomes:**  
- *Directly measured and reported*:  
    - Group efficiency and payoff: Wegmann & Musshoff (2019) (CPR field experiment, exact); Fonseca & Peters (2021) (trust game with gossip, exact for payoff, adjacent for PGG).
- *Interpreted or discussed*:  
    - Burton-Chellew & Guérin (2021) infer that "the costs of punishment are greater than the benefits from increased contributions, making punishment destructive to group welfare," indicating a negative effect on efficiency.
    - Burton-Chellew & D'Amico (2021) and related studies infer probable changes in efficiency from contribution patterns.
- *Not measured*:  
    - In most neural, communication, or behavioral mechanism papers, efficiency, total group payoff, or surplus are not measured or are omitted as primary outcomes.

**Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
- Nearly all studies report behavioral metrics:  
    - Individual or average contribution rates, cooperation choices, norm compliance, willingness to punish or reward, punishment/reward frequency, and information selection.
- Many explore neural or psychological mechanisms underlying these behaviors (e.g., tDCS studies on norm compliance, neural traits predicting prosociality).

**Distinction maintained:**  
- Direct, quantitative group-level efficiency or surplus effects are rare.
- Most findings are limited to indirect inference—e.g., increased/decreased cooperation presumed to impact efficiency without confirming actual payoff outcomes.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Empirical evidence with direct task relevance:**

- **Punishment can decrease efficiency in standard PGGs**:  
  In repeated linear PGGs with costly peer punishment (1 cost to reduce 3; small group size, anonymous, no chat), punishment does not reliably increase group efficiency. Indeed, the act of punishing is so costly that it is "ultimately destructive" to group welfare, especially where punishment is frequent and not well-targeted (Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021). Efficiency is lower with punishment enabled than in the control.

- **External/civic sanctioning increases efficiency in CPR analogues**:  
  In CPR field experiments with real monetary penalties for over-extraction and random monitoring, externally imposed sanctioning increases social efficiency (from 86% to 97%) and produces significant welfare gains over control. These effects are robust across urban/rural contexts and user types (Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019).

- **Contribution rates may increase with punishment or reward incentives, but payoff gains are not guaranteed**:  
  Enabling probabilistic, centralized financial or social sanctions (including rewards) increases average contribution *rates*, and both rewards and punishments have similar effects on cooperation behavior (Micheli et al., 2021). However, the study does not report efficiency or welfare—so impacts on group payoff are inferred only indirectly.

- **Punishment's impact on efficiency depends on structure and targeting**:  
  Evidence from behavioral and social information studies (e.g., Molleman et al., 2019; Fonseca & Peters, 2021) suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency is contingent on being (a) well-targeted (toward norm violators, not anti-social/hypocritical use), (b) coordinated, and (c) aligned with actual bad behavior. Where social or informal sanctions are weakly coupled to actual norm deviations or where punishment becomes anti-social, efficiency gains are limited or even reversed.

- **Communication and social information can sustain cooperation without formal punishment**:  
  Enabling gossip or chat increases contributions in repeated PGGs, especially under uncertainty, but does not guarantee higher efficiency—especially where social information is inaccurate or cannot be reliably used to coordinate against defectors (Jolly & Chang, 2021; Samu & Takács, 2021; Fonseca & Peters, 2021).

- **Baseline efficiency is lower in field/online PGGs without punishment**:  
  Real-world and online PGGs without punishment observe lower (often far lower) cooperation and efficiency than lab studies or games with enforcement options (Frey, 2019).

**Ambiguity and disagreement:**  
- Some studies (e.g., Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019) find strong positive efficiency impacts of externally imposed sanctions, contrasting sharply with lab PGGs with peer punishment showing destructive or negligible impact (Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021).
- The mechanism and target of punishment (peer vs. centralized, coordinated vs. anti-social, frequency, and cost) are critical in shaping payoff effects, but consensus on when efficiency gains occur is weak.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Efficiency effects of enabling punishment depend on punishment's cost, frequency, targeting, and the enforcement structure.**
    - *Peer punishment* (especially where costly and open to anti-social application) in standard lab PGGs may *reduce* efficiency compared to baseline—punishment costs can dominate any gains from increased cooperation, especially at typical MPCR values and without communication (Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021).
    - *Externally imposed, well-monitored punishment* mechanisms (with focused application against clear norm violations, as in CPR field experiments) can substantially *increase* efficiency, showing near-optimal group welfare gains in real-world settings (Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019).
- **In the absence of payoff data, non-payoff behavioral outcomes (e.g., increased contributions from enabling punishment) are only weak proxies for efficiency.**
    - Prediction models should not assume increased cooperation translates to higher efficiency unless the relative costs and frequency of punishment are known, and the specific form of the punishment (peer vs. institutional) is considered.
- **When control efficiency is much lower than the social optimum (i.e., baseline free-riding is high), the impact of adding effective punishment may be larger, but this is sensitive to enforcement type (peer vs. institutional), design specifics, and context.**

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- *player_count*: Frequently specified (especially in lab-based studies).
- *num_rounds*: Consistently reported; distinguishes one-shot from repeated games.
- *mpcr*: Explicitly given in most canonical PGG and CPR studies.
- *punishment_cost*: Included and varied in all papers involving real punishment.
- *punishment_tech*: Institutional vs. peer (centralized, probabilistic, externally imposed, vs. peer-invoked) is discussed in two key studies (Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Micheli et al., 2021).
- *all_or_nothing*: Several studies use binary-choice PGGs and specify this dimension.
- *chat*: Included in communication/gossip studies and sometimes in PGG setup.
- *show_n_rounds*, *show_other_summaries*: Occasionally included and inform information conditions.
- *default_contrib*: Included in a few PGG studies (e.g., Micheli et al., 2021).

**Indirectly Informed / Contextual:**
- *reward_exists*, *reward_cost*, *reward_tech*: Mentioned in reward vs. punishment comparison papers (Micheli et al., 2021; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019), but less detail overall.
- *show_punishment_id*: Addressed in a few adjacent papers focused on observability (Bradley et al., 2018; Baungartner et al., 2021), but not with efficiency outcomes.

**Missing or Weakly Informed:**
- Relative frequency, magnitude, and structure of punishment in context of *punishment_exists* and *punishment_magnitude* are only sometimes described in extractable detail.
- The conditions under which chat, communication, summary statistics, or identity visibility affect efficiency, and their interrelation with punishment, are not systematically reported in studies with efficiency outcomes.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Very few studies report both (a) efficiency or group payoff outcomes and (b) effects of enabling punishment in PGG-like environments using experimental controls.**
    - Most of the literature focuses on behavioral mechanisms rather than group efficiency metrics.
- **The mechanism of punishment in high-relevance studies is often very different (institutional/external vs. decentralized peer).**
    - Field CPR studies with institutional punishment show positive efficiency effects, but these may not generalize to peer punishment in canonical lab PGGs, where destructive and/or anti-social punishment is common.
- **Payoff-based findings are often inferred, not measured.**
    - Only two studies directly measure and report objective group-level efficiency as primary outcomes.
- **Game design dimensions relevant for prediction (e.g., punishment technology, magnitude, communication, information structure) are not always systematically varied or cross-validated across studies with efficiency outcomes.**
- **Ambiguity in generalizability:**
    - Contextual factors (setting, subject pool, real versus artificial stakes, monitoring) may drive substantial differences in punishment's efficiency effects.
- **Absence of theory papers or meta-analyses directly addressing efficiency effects of punishment across multiple designs.**
- **Risk of over-interpretation from limited direct evidence and reliance on behavioral proxies (e.g., contribution rate) for efficiency.**

---

**In summary:** The literature offers directly relevant efficiency evidence for institutional punishment (large efficiency gains in CPR field experiments), and for peer punishment in lab PGGs (often destructive or negative net efficiency impact). The majority of other studies focus on behavioral rather than payoff-based outcomes, providing only indirect or contextual support. Prediction modeling must carefully distinguish between peer and institutional punishment, control for costs and frequency, and avoid assuming cooperation gains always increase efficiency. Many design dimensions are well-reported, but few are strongly cross-validated with efficiency outcomes. The literature's utility for downstream prediction about efficiency under punishment is therefore valuable but fragmentary and highly sensitive to contextual specifics.
