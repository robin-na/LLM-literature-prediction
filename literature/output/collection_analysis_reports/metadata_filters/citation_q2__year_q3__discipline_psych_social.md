# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is broad in scope, with a heavy empirical focus and a substantial number of experimental (lab and field) studies directly manipulating punishment options in public-goods-game (PGG) environments or closely related social dilemmas. Several theory and modeling papers are included, as well as studies using natural field settings and large-sample observational data. The set covers classic linear PGGs, threshold and extraction games, PGG variants with centralized and peer punishment, and some adjacent contexts (Prisoner's Dilemma, common-pool resource, trust/lending games). 

The composition is strongest for lab/field experiment evidence in repeated PGGs with and without punishment, and less dense for CPR/resource dilemma games, market situations, and adjacent institutional/behavioral settings. Theory and mechanism discussions complement the empirical base, often drawing on, but not replicating, core experimental results.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) `pgg_or_variant`:**  
- *exact*: Many empirical and theoretical papers use the standard linear PGG or close variants (threshold PGG, resource extraction framed as PGG, etc.), with clear mapping to the stated prediction dimensions—e.g., (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).
- *close*: Some papers use CPR, extraction, or threshold games structurally similar to PGGs (Vollan et al., 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
- *adjacent/weak*: A number use PDG, dictator, trust, or market games to explore related concepts.

**b) `punishment_or_sanctions`:**  
- *exact*: Many studies manipulate the presence, cost, and technology of punishment or sanctions as a treatment variable (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Ertör-Akyazi, 2019).
- *adjacent*: Some focus on reward, reputation, or compensation instead (Toriumi et al., 2020; Stoop et al., 2018).
- *weak/none*: Several relevant studies do not include any punitive mechanism.

**c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`:**  
- *exact*: A smaller subset directly reports efficiency, total group payoff, or surplus (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; Vollan et al., 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
- *close/adjacent*: Many papers directly report behavioral outcomes (contributions, punishment rates) but allow strong inference to payoff/efficiency (Schlösser et al., 2018; Morgan et al., 2019).
- *weak/none*: Some report only non-payoff outcomes or focus on mechanism/psychological moderators with no efficiency analysis.

**Overall:** The literature delivers strong exact relevance on all three dimensions for a significant core, especially for repeated, small-group PGGs with lab-based peer or centralized punishment. Many adjacent/contextual studies discuss key mechanisms but lack efficiency outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff/Efficiency Outcomes**:  
  - *Directly measured*: Total group payoff, efficiency ratios (group payoff vs full cooperation), surplus, or total coins (e.g., Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Vollan et al., 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
  - *Indirectly inferred*: When high/low contributions map linearly to payoff and the punishment cost is accounted for, qualitative efficiency inferences are often possible though not always explicit.
  - *Partially analyzed*: Some studies discuss distributive outcomes or changes in inequality (e.g., Pansini et al., 2020), or welfare, but without always presenting normalized efficiency metrics.
- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**:
  - *Contribution/cooperation rates*: Almost ubiquitous (Schlösser et al., 2018; Otten et al., 2021).
  - *Punishment/reward rates*: Amount assigned, frequency, or behavioral correlates (Windmann et al., 2021).
  - *Norm compliance*: Direct or indirect, often as a driver for punishment or cooperation.
  - *Punishment motives*: Antisocial vs. prosocial punishment distinguished in some studies.
  - *Institutional variables*: Leadership selection, manager prosociality, etc.

**Distinction**: While high contribution rates generally map to higher efficiency in standard linear PGGs, this is not always true—especially with costly or antisocial punishment (Vollan et al., 2019), or in threshold/common-pool/resource games where payoff structure is nonlinear.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**General Direction of Effect**  
- *Classic repeated linear PGGs*: Enabling peer or centralized punishment substantially increases group efficiency over controls (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021; Schlösser et al., 2018; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).
    - The magnitude is large in standard, well-mixed lab PGGs—efficiency often nearly doubles (from ~45% to ~90%).

- *Threshold/CPR/extraction games*:  
    - Effects more heterogeneous; sometimes moderate or no efficiency gain (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019), sometimes negative (Vollan et al., 2019) due to high punishment costs or antisocial punishment undermining group payoff.

- *Framing & information*:  
    - Full information and universal accountability are key moderators; the mere threat of punishment keeps usage low and efficiency high (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).
    - Salient feedback that connects punishment to violation is necessary for efficiency gains (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019). Absence of such feedback can make efficiency gains disappear even if cooperation rises.
    - The framing of penalties vs. market-like mechanisms matters (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019).

- *Mechanism design variables*:  
    - Manager selection mechanism (democratic vs. imposed) in centralized punishment has little impact on efficiency (Castillo et al., 2021).
    - Endogenous shared punishment with low cost is especially efficient; full-scale or poorly targeted punishment can lower or erase efficiency gains (Kol'veková et al., 2021).

- *Exceptions and caveats*:
    - **Antisocial punishment/high punishment cost**: Where punishment cost is high or antisocial punishment is prevalent, efficiency gains may be reduced or eliminated (Vollan et al., 2019).
    - **Threshold games**: In environments where there are thresholds or non-linear payoffs (binary provision, extraction, or common pool), the presence of punishment does not guarantee higher efficiency and may, in some cases, lower it.
    - **Group composition**: Heterogeneity (e.g., status, ethnicity) can moderate both antisocial punishment and net efficiency.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **If the control game is a standard, repeated linear PGG (continuous contributions, 3–5 players, moderate MPCR, full information, no communication):**
    - Enabling peer punishment (even if costly and not strictly efficient at the transaction level) can be expected to yield a large positive shift in efficiency, often approaching full-cooperation levels (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Schlösser et al., 2018).
    - Robustness is highest when all players are subject to punishment, there is full/transparent information on behavior, and feedback clearly links punishment to norm deviations (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).
    - The level of punishment cost (within tested experimental ranges) may be less predictive than these institutional and informational moderators (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Castillo et al., 2021).
    - The control (no-punishment) efficiency is a strong predictor, and the main uplift is tied to the activation and proper targeting of the punishment technology.

- **If key design features are present that enable high punishment cost, limited accountability (e.g., anonymous or asymmetric punishment rights), or facilitate antisocial punishment:**
    - Efficiency gains can be much smaller, zero, or even negative, as direct and indirect punishment costs sometimes outweigh any cooperation benefits (Vollan et al., 2019; Pansini et al., 2020).
    - Threshold PGGs (all-or-nothing provision) can see smaller improvements, and improper targeting or group composition can make peer punishment inefficient or even harmful (Kol'veková et al., 2021).

- **Additional moderators (less well-quantified):**
    - The presence of communication may rival or exceed the positive impact of punishment on efficiency (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019).
    - Centralized punishment is generally as effective as peer punishment, with some evidence for fewer efficiency-reducing side-effects (Castillo et al., 2021; Vollan et al., 2019).
    - Effects of chat, leadership selection, information display are often on contribution rates or norm compliance and not directly mapped to payoff.

- **Where evidence is only behavioral (contributions, compliance):**
    - These generally imply higher efficiency in linear PGGs, but caution is needed, especially if punishment is costly or antisocial.
    - In threshold/CPR settings, increased contribution may not yield proportional efficiency gain, due to provision thresholds or resource depletion effects.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**  
- *player_count*: Manipulated in almost all papers; effects robust across n = 3–5; larger groups less studied.
- *num_rounds*: Most studies use repeated rounds; some examine end-game effects.
- *mpcr*: Directly manipulated in several studies (MPCR = 0.3–0.75 common).
- *punishment_cost*: Varies systematically in punishment studies; effects directly compared.
- *punishment_tech*: Cost:impact ratios, targeting ability, timing, delay, etc., all considered.
- *all_or_nothing/continuous*: Both types included; standard linear PGG predominates.
- *chat*: Included notably in field experiments; evidence that communication can outperform punishment.
- *show_n_rounds*, *show_other_summaries*, *show_punishment_id*: Some studies highlight information disclosure and salience as key moderators.

**Indirectly/Contextually Discussed:**  
- *default_contrib*: Few direct manipulations; sometimes discussed in framing.
- *reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech*: Several adjacent studies focus on reward; effects generally positive but not always included with punishment.
- *show_punishment_id*: Group visibility and accountability to punishment addressed as mechanism moderators (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019).

**Effectively Missing/Sparse:**  
- *larger group sizes* (beyond ~5), asymmetric/complex information, variation in default contributions.
- *Complex reward systems* or *combined reward/punishment regimes* less often studied together.
- *Institutionally complex or realistic, high-powered field studies* scarce relative to lab.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Population and Setting**: Most evidence is from student or WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic) samples in controlled lab settings; field and cross-cultural evidence are less common.
- **Payoff Reporting**: A considerable proportion of studies report only on contributions/cooperation, not normalized efficiency or net group payoff, especially for adjacent/variant games.
- **Heterogeneous Designs**: Findings on punishment cost, feedback, and group heterogeneity imply substantial moderators exist; thus, transferability across settings or to larger-scale or real-world groups is limited.
- **Antisocial Punishment and Side Effects**: Some environments—especially with peer punishment, high heterogeneity, or loose accountability—can foster costly retaliation or antisocial punishment that erode or reverse efficiency gains.
- **Threshold/Common-Pool/Extraction Games**: Efficiency effects of punishment may be smaller, absent, or negative in these, compared to linear PGGs.
- **Design Interactions**: Combined or interacting mechanisms (chat+punishment, centralized+peer punishment, endogenous vs. exogenous rule choice) are insufficiently covered for robust quantitative prediction.
- **External Validity**: Several theoretical and field studies caution against straightforward generalization to real-world institutions or larger, complex groups.

---

**Summary:**  
The literature base provides robust empirical evidence for predicting efficiency gains from enabling peer punishment in repeated, small-group standard linear PGGs with transparent information and universal accountability: enabling punishment almost always boosts efficiency, often dramatically. However, efficiency effects are highly sensitive to the specifics of the punishment technology, group structure, game framing, and informational environment. In threshold and extraction variants or in settings rife with antisocial punishment, efficiency gains may be small, absent, or even negative. Prediction models should incorporate moderators—such as the type of punishment regime, information available to punishers, the possibility of antisocial punishment, and the presence or absence of communication. Many design dimensions are directly informed in the literature, but some (notably large groups, rich institutional variation, and certain information conditions) are less well covered. Caution in generalization remains warranted due to limitations in external validity and the frequency of non-payoff behavioral outcome reporting.
