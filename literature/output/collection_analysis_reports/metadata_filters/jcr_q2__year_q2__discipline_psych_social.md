# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is predominantly **empirical experimental** in nature, with a smaller but meaningful subset of **theory/modeling** papers and several empirical observational studies. The set is relatively broad in scientific scope, spanning classic linear public goods games (PGGs), close game variants (such as common-pool resource games, Dictator/Ultimatum games with punishment, and team-production with incentives), and adjacent studies focused on social and evolutionary mechanisms of punishment and cooperation.  
   
Notably, the **experimental lab PGGs with explicit punishment and efficiency or payoff outcomes** are well-represented (e.g., Wang & Qin, 2015; Gürerk, 2013; Grieco et al., 2017; Qin & Wang, 2013). However, a substantial number of studies address variants or focus mainly on behavioral/contribution outcomes rather than group payoff or efficiency, and some are limited to theoretical mechanism arguments or indirect evidence.

Overall, the evidence base provides several strong empirical anchors for the prediction task, especially for the canonical PGG structures. However, the breadth of designs and outcomes means applicability to all prediction dimensions is inconsistent.

# 2) Task Relevance

### a) `pgg_or_variant`
- **exact:** Approximately half of the papers employ standard or textbook linear PGGs (e.g., 4-person, repeated, with typical MPCRs and explicit costly punishment options).
- **close:** A meaningful share uses close variants, notably common-pool resource games or trust/Ultimatum/DG-based punishment settings.
- **adjacent/weak/none:** Theory/anthropological and observational pieces are much less directly relevant, mostly providing context or psychological mechanism arguments.

### b) `punishment_or_sanctions`
- **exact:** Many key empirical papers mechanically enable or manipulate explicit punishment sanctions (peer or exogenous), with clear rules and quantifiable costs/impacts.
- **close/adjacent:** Several papers address reward, endogenous social sanctions, reputation/shaming, exclusion/exit, or discuss punishment only in adjacent game structures.
- **none:** A significant number of context or pure cooperation papers have no punishment manipulation.

### c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
- **exact:** A core set of PGG studies measure **group efficiency** (total payoff relative to the social optimum), or directly report total earnings.
- **close/adjacent:** Some report only contribution rates or indicate implied changes in group payoff, but don't quantify efficiency per se.
- **weak/none:** Mechanism, psychological, or anthropological studies largely lack efficiency or group-level payoff as an outcome.

#### Synthesis:
**Direct, high-relevance evidence exists for the canonical "PGG with payoff efficiency outcome" prediction,** but coverage thins for more complex game variants, alternative sanctioning structures, and when focusing strictly on efficiency as opposed to behavior.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (primary for prediction task):**
    - *Efficiency/group surplus/average group payoff (e.g., ratio to full cooperation):* Directly measured in a subset (Wang & Qin, 2015; Gürerk, 2013; Grieco et al., 2017; Qin & Wang, 2013).
    - *Total earnings, welfare, coins generated:* Used or discussed as close proxies in several works.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
    - *Contribution rate/cooperation rate/norm compliance:* Frequently measured, sometimes serving as proxies for welfare (but are not equivalent).
    - *Punishment frequency/assigned, antisocial punishment, norm salience, emotions, beliefs*: Widely studied.
    - *Voting, exclusion, shaming, communication, personal retribution, leadership selection:* Behavioral and process outcomes often used to infer mechanisms.

- **Distinction:** While increased cooperation (behavioral) often coincides with higher efficiency, substantial evidence points to cases where costly punishment reduces net payoff, or where increased contributions are offset by overuse or mis-targeting of punishment (see e.g., studies documenting antisocial punishment, ambiguous efficiency impacts of voting/disapproval, or identity-driven punishment).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## Empirical Findings

- **Consistent efficiency boost from punishment:** Studies with standard repeated PGGs show that introducing peer/exogenous costly punishment **increases efficiency (group earnings relative to the social optimum),** even accounting for punishment costs, compared to no-punishment controls, provided punishment is well-designed and not antisocial (Wang & Qin, 2015; Gürerk, 2013; Grieco et al., 2017; Qin & Wang, 2013).
- **Magnitude and structure of punishment matter:**
    - *Probability/severity tradeoff:* Efficiency follows an **inverse-U shape** relative to punishment probability/severity, with moderate, reasonably probable penalties maximizing efficiency. Low-probability/high-severity punishment may have no effect (Qin & Wang, 2013).
    - *Selection mechanism:* Assigning punishment rights to merit-based roles (e.g., top contributor as punisher) yields higher efficiency than random or unstructured assignment (Grieco et al., 2017).
    - *Exogenous vs. peer (endogenous) punishment:* Both improve efficiency, but peer-driven (social) punishment can be less efficient if poorly targeted.
- **Social learning and information disclosure:**
    - Enabling players to observe history, peer outcomes, or social norms can enhance the efficiency benefit of punishment, with social learning increasing both acceptance and payoff impact of sanctioning regimes (Gürerk, 2013).
    - Visibility (identity/summary) sometimes supports efficiency by improving targeting or deterring antisocial sanctioning.
- **Antisocial punishment as a limiter:** The presence of retaliation or antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators or as revenge) can partially or completely offset efficiency gains, especially in some cultural contexts or with unstable groups (Sylwester et al., 2013; Grieco et al., 2017; Xiao & Kunreuther, 2016).
- **Mechanism and contextual moderators:**
    - *Communication and leadership:* When combined with punishment, communication can further increase efficiency (Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013). Leadership structures with positive incentives (reward) can also boost efficiency but introduce equity tradeoffs (Drouvelis et al., 2017).
    - *Monitoring cost and visibility:* If punishment depends on costly monitoring/observation, efficiency gains are diminished (Goeschl & Jarke, 2016).
    - *Removal or sequencing of punishment:* Introducing and then removing punishment can lead to efficiency below the baseline, indicating path-dependence and potential negative spillovers (Romaniuc et al., 2016).
    - *Group identity:* Identity-homogeneous groups show less (and less intense) punishment; identity-heterogeneous groups show more, potentially reducing efficiency due to cost (Bicskei et al., 2016).

## Theory/Modeling
- **General theoretical predictions** support the expectation that, under most conditions, punishment expands the basin of attraction for cooperative/efficient equilibria (Nasrallah & Cheaib, 2016), but only if punishment is not too costly, not exempt for any subgroup (elites), and sufficiently universal in coverage.
- **Mechanism caveats:** Excessive punishment or monitoring cost, or opportunities for antisocial punishment due to status competition, can collapse, rather than enhance, efficiency.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Where control efficiency is low or moderate, enabling peer or exogenous punishment is highly likely to increase average efficiency in repeated standard PGGs**, provided punishment is not too costly, is not subject to high monitoring costs, and is not easily hijacked for antisocial ends.
- **Punishment mechanism structure is critical:** Predicted efficiency gains are larger when:
    - Punishment is **targeted to free-riders** (not random or antisocial).
    - Players have access to **full information** and social history.
    - There are **no large identity-based cleavages** or preexisting bases for out-group hostility.
    - **Monitoring is effectively free or low-cost.**
- **Design dimension implications:**
    - Lower **punishment cost** (to punisher) and higher **punishment magnitude** (penalty for target) increase deterrence, but excessive punishment or retaliation can reduce efficiency.
    - **Number of rounds** and presence of **social learning or information feedback** strengthen positive effects.
    - **Communication/chat** (especially coupled with punishment) can further enhance efficiency.
- **Contextually, if the control game already has high efficiency** (e.g., due to communication, trust, or other norm-promoting factors), marginal punishment may produce weaker or negative effects (crowding out or over-punishment).
- **Reward mechanisms** (as opposed to punishment) can increase efficiency but may be less robust to rematching or anonymous play; combining reward and punishment is sometimes more effective but results vary according to design.

**For predictions, the literature justifies:**
- Expecting a **statistically and practically significant increase in efficiency** when punishment is added, especially for classic, repeated, small-N PGGs with medium baseline efficiency and well-calibrated punishment.
- **Adjusting the magnitude of expected efficiency gains** based on design dimensions: lower when punishment is expensive, high monitoring costs exist, there is scope for antisocial retribution, or group identity is fragmentary.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Well-informed (directly, in multiple PGG studies):**
- `player_count` (mostly 2, 3, or 4)
- `num_rounds` (most studies use 10-20)
- `mpcr` (reported or specified in main quantitative papers)
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech` (many papers vary or specify these)
- `all_or_nothing` (most are continuous, but discrete treatments exist)
- `chat` (no or structured; mediated forms in some)
- `show_other_summaries`/`show_n_rounds` (widely discussed in the context of social info/results)
- `punishment_exists/punishment_magnitude`
- `reward_exists/reward_cost` (in fewer, but several direct tests)

**Indirectly informed/contextually discussed:**
- `show_punishment_id` (identity revealed vs. anonymous treatments; studied in the context of antisocial punishment or group identity but less often as a manipulated dimension)
- `default_contrib` (rarely a focus but sometimes in framing/priming studies)

**Effectively missing/underspecified:**
- **Some prediction dimensions (e.g., fine-grained settings for `default_contrib`, variations in framing, very large N, rare/extreme rounds or magnitude setups) remain underrepresented** or only discussed contextually/theoretically.
- **Reward-related mechanics** (`reward_cost`, `reward_tech`) are less frequently addressed than punishment, and when included, often as secondary analyses.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Coverage of prediction dimensions is uneven:** While classic design elements are well-represented, less common settings (e.g., large groups, very high or low MPCRs, long-run stranger matching, real-world communication) are underexplored or only discussed theoretically.
- **Efficiency vs. contributions ambiguity:** Many studies report only contribution behavior, making it hard to cleanly map their findings to efficiency—especially where punishment costs are high or targeting is poor.
- **Antisocial punishment and context sensitivity:** Efficiency benefits of punishment are highly context-dependent and can reverse in the presence of antisocial punishment, high monitoring costs, group identity conflicts, or poorly designed assignment of punishment rights—limitations that are underspecified in some experimental settings.
- **Sequencing/path dependence and removal effects:** Most studies only examine "punishment on" vs. "punishment off." When sequencing is addressed, removal of punishment can lower efficiency below the no-punishment baseline (Romaniuc et al., 2016), but generalizability is uncertain.
- **Limitations in generalizability:** Non-lab contexts, field environments, and long-run game-theoretic dynamics may yield different patterns than tightly controlled lab experiments.
- **Reward and hybrid mechanisms are underexplored** as direct comparators to punishment in many parameter spaces.
- **Theory papers aid mechanism understanding but lack quantitative predictive power.**
- **Cultural, ecological, or anthropological studies provide context but do not substitute for direct experimental or payoff outcomes for efficiency prediction.**

---

**Summary:**  
The literature strongly supports predicting higher average efficiency when enabling peer or exogenous punishment in standard PGG treatments with moderate control efficiency and well-calibrated punishment mechanisms. However, efficiency gains are moderated or reversed in the presence of antisocial punishment, high monitoring cost, group identity fragmentation, or poorly targeted punishment. Most canonical design dimensions are well-informed, but specific scenarios—large groups, unique framing, high identity salience, or alternate reward structures—require extrapolation or caution. The primary limitation is a relative scarcity of direct efficiency reporting (vs. contribution behavior), and context-specific moderators should be explicitly considered in quantitative predictions.
