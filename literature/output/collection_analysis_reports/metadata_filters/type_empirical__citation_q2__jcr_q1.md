# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 113 empirical studies, predominantly experimental lab and field experiments, with a smaller number of observational and review-type papers. The evidence base is broad in terms of the total number of papers and contexts represented, but narrows substantially when focused specifically on the prediction task: forecasting the impact of enabling peer punishment on efficiency (group payoff relative to optimum) in public-goods-game-like environments as a function of detailed game design. Roughly half the papers provide high or moderate relevance, with a strong core of studies employing standard or variant public goods game (PGG) designs with explicit peer punishment manipulations and direct payoff or efficiency outcomes. Alongside these, numerous adjacent studies cover related games (CPRs, trust/dictator games, threshold dilemmas, etc.), or focus on behavioral outcomes, motivational mechanisms, or psychological moderators.

The empirical literature is complemented by occasional theoretical or meta-analytic contributions, but direct formal theory is a minority voice in this set. The net result is a robust, mainly empirical body of evidence that allows for moderate-to-strong synthesis on core prediction dimensions, especially in canonical linear public goods games, but with notable sparsity or ambiguity in certain design permutations (e.g., noisy punishment, reward co-presence, collective decision rules, real-world or large-group CPRs).

There is extensive behavioral literature (contributions, punishment rates, emotional/psychological outcomes), but these cannot be translated uncritically into efficiency predictions.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Efficiency):**
- Many studies provide *direct efficiency* measures: group payoff as a proportion of the social optimum (e.g., (Kamijo et al., 2020; Eriksson & Strimling, 2012; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019)).
- Others report *group earnings*, *aggregate surplus*, or *net profits*—these can generally be mapped to efficiency given payoff structure details (e.g., (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Bravo & Squazzoni, 2013)).
- Some papers report *closely related* or *adjacent* outcomes (e.g., success rates at thresholds, or reduction in resource extraction in CPRs), which can sometimes be mapped to efficiency but with caveats (e.g., (Jiang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2022)).
- In a smaller but important subset, *punishment and/or reward costs* are carefully accounted for in reporting efficiency.

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**
- Many studies focus on *contribution rates*, *cooperation rates*, or *punishment frequency*, which, without explicit cost-of-sanctioning accounting, may not correspond to higher group efficiency—especially when punishment is frequent and costly (e.g., (Lefebvre & Stenger, 2020; Kubena et al., 2014; Paál & Bereczkei, 2015)).
- Additional reported outcomes include: *norm compliance rates*, *punishment targeting (pro-/antisocial)*, *perceptions/fairness*, *trust/signaling*, *partner selection*, and *psychological/neural correlates*.
- Some outcomes are purely reputational or attitudinal, not relating to group payoff.

**Distinction:**  
Care must be taken not to treat increased contributions as automatically yielding higher efficiency, since costs of punishment can offset or outweigh cooperation gains (e.g., (Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021; Grimalda et al., 2022)). Only studies explicitly accounting for both contributions and costs in payoff calculations should be considered directly informative for the efficiency prediction task.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

The empirical literature reveals substantial heterogeneity in the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in public goods games, with outcomes pivoting on both game design and contextual moderators:

- **Positive Efficiency Effects (common but not universal):**
    - *Standard repeated PGGs with deterministic peer punishment and full accountability* show robust increases in efficiency when punishment is enabled, particularly under the following conditions:
        - Punishment is not prohibitively costly or antisocial.
        - All players can be sanctioned (no immunity).
        - Full information/identifiability of actions is present (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).
    - *Externally imposed, strong, and automatic punishment* (higher fines, high thresholds, clear/visible rules) yields large and reliable efficiency gains, especially in low-cooperation groups (Eriksson & Strimling, 2012).
    - *Democratic or majority-vote punishment regimes* reduce antisocial punishment and increase efficiency compared to individual/dictator punishment (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Bahbouhi et al., 2024).
    - *Collective protection in appropriation/CPR games*, when mandatory, raises efficiency substantially (Ahn et al., 2016).

- **Mixed or Null Effects:**
    - In *already highly cooperative groups*, adding punishment can *reduce* efficiency due to unnecessary punishment costs (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).
    - *Weak, noisy, or rule-based collective punishment* shows minimal or no effect, and can sometimes harm efficiency: e.g., stochastic/noisy punishment, or “blind” group-level punishments (Salahshour et al., 2022; van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Koike et al., 2018).
    - The *presence of prior conflict*, *identifiability of past roles*, or *maladaptive punishment patterns* (e.g., antisocial punishment) reduce both cooperation and the efficiency benefit from punishment (Gross et al., 2022).

- **Negative Efficiency Effects:**
    - *Noisy or stochastic punishment tech* (high variance in punishment applied) can result in lower efficiency than control (no-punishment) due to wasted resources and increased antisocial punishment (Salahshour et al., 2022; van Miltenburg et al., 2017; Grimalda et al., 2022).
    - *Highly costly punishment*, frequent and not well-targeted, can result in net destruction of group welfare (Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021; Grimalda et al., 2022).
    - *In PGGs with MPCR < 1* (inefficient public goods), punishment often fails to raise efficiency, while rewards succeed (Kamijo et al., 2020).
    - *Punishment for competitive or status reasons* (rather than norm enforcement) can be wasteful, especially in rank-based or attack-allowed settings (Paál & Bereczkei, 2015; Romano et al., 2024).

Other key moderating findings:
- *Team/collective decision rules:* Unanimity or majority voting among teams reduces antisocial punishment and increases efficiency compared to individual punishment (Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).
- *Presence of reward mechanisms:* Rewards often outperform punishment for efficiency gains, especially in inefficient games (Kamijo et al., 2020; Stoop et al., 2018).
- *Social context, norm clarity, and framing:* Communication, information about group composition, and perceived legitimacy of sanctions all substantially impact the effectiveness of punishment (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; Dickson et al., 2022).
- *Punishment technology and cost-impact ratio:* Low-cost, high-impact punishment is generally more effective for raising efficiency—so long as it is not overly exploited for antisocial aims (Bond, 2019; Lo Iacono et al., 2023).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Approach:**
- The literature supports *using control (no-punishment) efficiency as a baseline*, then *adjusting the expected treatment (punishment-on) efficiency* based on key design and context moderators.
- Enabling peer punishment in a standard PGG with low baseline efficiency is, on average, likely to increase efficiency—but this effect can be negated or reversed under certain design conditions.

**Dimension- and Context-Specific Effects:**
- **Player Count (player_count):** Positive efficiency effects of punishment are robust across small and moderate group sizes (4-12), but may moderate for larger or more heterogeneous groups; larger groups may require stronger or more visible punishment to maintain efficiency (Lo Iacono et al., 2023; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).
- **Number of Rounds (num_rounds):** Effects of punishment can take several rounds to emerge; longer games better reveal sustained efficiency gains (Lo Iacono et al., 2023).
- **MPCR (mpcr):** Positive effects are stronger when MPCR is moderate/high (i.e., public good is not drastically inefficient); for MPCR < 1, punishment is largely ineffective and may even reduce efficiency (Kamijo et al., 2020; Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).
- **Punishment Cost / Technology (punishment_cost, punishment_tech):** Low- to moderate-cost, high-impact punishment is most effective for efficiency. High punishment cost and/or noisy punishment (punishment_tech=stochastic) can reduce or nullify efficiency gains (Salahshour et al., 2022; van Miltenburg et al., 2017).
- **Antisocial Punishment Risk:** High frequency or prevalence of antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) undermines efficiency benefit (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).
- **Information and Monitoring (show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id):** Punishment is more effective (and less antisocial) when individual actions, contributions, and identities are fully observable (Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).
- **Chat/Communication (chat):** Communication on its own can increase efficiency and sometimes matches or exceeds punishment’s effect, or can further enhance the benefit of punishment (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019; Dickson et al., 2022).
- **Reward/Incentives Co-Presence (reward_exists):** The addition of rewards can augment efficiency gains, sometimes more than punishment alone, especially in settings where over-contribution is not wasteful (Kamijo et al., 2020).
- **Decision Rule (all_or_nothing, default_contrib):** Team- or group-based punishment decisions (unanimity/majority rules) reduce wasteful punishment and boost efficiency over individual punishment (Bahbouhi et al., 2024; Ambrus & Greiner, 2019).
- **Immunity or Exclusion:** Granting immunity to punishment for some individuals sharply reduces group efficiency (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021).
- **Baseline Cooperativeness:** If control efficiency is already high, adding punishment may reduce net efficiency due to unnecessary punishment (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021).

**Design Dimension Use:**
- Directly adjust predicted efficiency upwards from control when punishment is enabled in standard conditions: repeated, moderate-sized groups, deterministic and not excessively costly punishment, no dominant antisocial punishment, and reasonable baseline efficiency.
- Adjust (reduce or nullify increase) for:
    - High punishment cost or noisy effectiveness,
    - Highly efficient or highly inefficient (MPCR <1) baseline games,
    - Environments prone to antisocial punishment, unclear norms, or competitive/attack options,
    - Designs with immunity, noisy monitoring, or collectivized/weakly enforced punishment.

**Uninformed Dimensions:** For design parameters not systematically manipulated in the literature (e.g., certain reward configurations, rare combinations of information treatments, etc.), prediction should fall back on broader findings or remain agnostic.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Heterogeneity and Context Dependence:** The efficiency impact of punishment is highly context-dependent—varying with group composition, baseline cooperation, norm clarity, social context (e.g., prior conflict, in-/out-group, local culture)—which often cannot be captured by the 14 prediction dimensions alone (Bühren & Dannenberg, 2021; Gross et al., 2022).
- **Sparse Data on Edge Cases:** Some design features (complex reward/punishment combos, extremely large groups, highly uneven cost-impact ratios, immunity settings, rare/novel information feedback) are sparsely addressed.
- **Behavioral vs. Payoff Outcomes:** Many studies only report contribution/cooperation rates or punishment frequency; mapping these to efficiency is nontrivial and can be misleading if punishment costs are high or anti-social.
- **Limited Long-Term/Field Data:** While there is some field and community-level work, the strongest causal evidence comes from lab experiments under controlled conditions. Generalizing to real-world, large-group, or longitudinal settings requires caution.
- **Ambiguity in Conflicting Results:** When results disagree (e.g., the efficiency effect is negative in the presence of noisy punishment, high antisocial punishment, or when punishment is too weak or too strong), prediction must preserve uncertainty; simple additive or uniform effect-size approaches will mislead (Salahshour et al., 2022; Grimalda et al., 2022).
- **Non-Standard Games/Outcomes:** Some close variants (threshold, contest, CPR) share mechanisms but have distinct efficiency and punishment dynamics, and results are not always transferable to standard PGGs.
- **No Direct Evidence for Some Dimensions:** Some design parameters are only contextually discussed, not systematically manipulated, so their interactions with punishment efficiency effects remain speculative.

In conclusion, **prediction of treatment efficiency from PGG design and control efficiency is well supported for canonical designs and standard parameter manipulations, but less so on the frontiers of complexity or in highly contextual environments.** Cautious, dimension-specific, and evidence-weighted adjustments to control efficiency are warranted, with careful attention to the scope and limitations of the underlying studies.
