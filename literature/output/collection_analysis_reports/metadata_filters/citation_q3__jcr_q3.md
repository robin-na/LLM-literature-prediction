# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set includes a broad and deep mixture of empirical and theoretical works, with strong representation of laboratory experiments and formal models directly focusing on public goods games (PGGs) with and without punishment. The sample size (93 papers) allows for extensive coverage of design dimensions and outcome measures. A substantial subset of these papers report empirical group payoff or efficiency outcomes in PGGs; many others provide closely linked payoff results from theory or experiment, while the remainder cover adjacent domains (e.g., repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma, trust games, resource dilemmas) or focus on behavioral (contribution, compliance) or contextual outcomes.

There is a relatively equal mix of theory and experiment among the most relevant and informative sources. Experiments tend to follow standard PGG lab designs, with controlled manipulation of punishment variables and direct reporting of efficiency or total earnings. Many theory papers provide explicit analytical or simulation results for group payoffs / efficiency as a function of game parameters, often sweeping key design dimensions. There is also a notable presence of empirical and theory studies contextualizing punishment in adjacent game structures or in naturalistic field settings.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a) PGG or Variant**

- **Exact relevance:** Many papers (approx. 20+) use standard or only slightly modified PGGs, with the canonical structure and parameter labels (`player_count`, `num_rounds`, etc.).
- **Close relevance:** Several theory and empirical works generalize to group-structured games, voluntary or spatial PGGs, or common-pool resource games. Some, though not all, maintain direct applicability.
- **Adjacent/Weak/None:** Numerous papers explore repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma, trust/dictator games, or resource dilemmas, which are structurally similar but not canonical PGGs. A minority address networked or field settings far removed from PGGs.

**b) Punishment or Sanctions**

- **Exact relevance:** Many studies directly manipulate or model punishment enablement, cost, targeting (peer/centralized/democratic), and related dimensions within the PGG environment.
- **Close relevance:** Some works focus on institutional or external (not peer) punishment, or on related mechanisms like reward, reputation, exclusion, or destruction.
- **Adjacent/Weak:** A subset examine indirect sanctions (gossip, ostracism, information), non-costly social sanctions, or non-material reputation penalties as forms of enforcement.

**c) Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**

- **Exact relevance:** A large suite of empirical and theoretical studies report group efficiency, group payoff, welfare, total earnings, or surplus—either as direct outcomes or analytically derivable from reported results.
- **Close relevance:** Several papers link contribution/cooperation rates to efficiency in standard linear PGGs where the mapping is unambiguous.
- **Adjacent/Weak/None:** Numerous works focus solely on behavioral or norm compliance outcomes, trust, reputation, punishment rates, or emotional/psychological responses; a small minority provide only theoretical discussion or network dynamics with no efficiency results.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes:**  
  - **Directly measured:** Group efficiency (ratio of actual to maximum group payoff), total group earnings, surplus, population mean payoff, welfare.
  - **Indirectly measurable:** Several studies provide contribution rates or equilibrium frequencies in linear PGGs where efficiency can be straightforwardly inferred.
  - **Close proxies:** Some agent-based and theoretical models sweep parameter regimes, mapping strategy prevalence to mean payoffs.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**  
  - Contribution/cooperation rates, punishment frequency, norm compliance, trust, retaliation, strategy prevalence, emotional responses, compliance with feedback, and meta-norm attitudes.

- **Distinction maintained:** Many papers discuss both (for example, noting that increased cooperation does not always translate into higher efficiency due to high punishment costs).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

### General Synthesis

- **Enabling costly peer punishment in PGGs tends to raise cooperation and, often, group efficiency compared to no punishment—but only under certain parameter ranges.**
    - *Optimal conditions*: Small to moderate group size, multiple rounds, non-excessive punishment cost, and effective punishment technology (high effectiveness per unit cost).
    - *Design moderation*: Mechanisms to reduce anti-social or excessive punishment (e.g., legitimacy constraints, democratic/deliberative targeting) increase the positive efficiency effect (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
    - *Risks*: Costless or poorly-targeted punishment, high anti-social punishment, or lack of feedback can lower efficiency relative to control (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Faillo et al., 2013; Powers et al., 2012).

- **Centralized, delegated, or democratic punishment systems—when honest and legitimate—yield the highest efficiency gains.**
    - Evidence from empirical and theory studies shows that centralized or democratically executed punishment curtails over-punishment and anti-social behavior (Kamijo et al., 2014; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
    - Corruption among enforcers, or lack of transparency, can render punishment ineffective or even harmful to efficiency (Lee et al., 2015, 2017; Huang et al., 2018).

- **Punishment cost and execution probability are crucial moderators.**
    - Moderate, nonzero costs signal legitimacy and prevent excessive, counterproductive punishment.
    - Theoretical results on probabilistic or graduated punishment show these schemes can achieve high efficiency even when costs are moderate to high, by optimizing deterrence while minimizing waste (Jiao et al., 2020; Couto et al., 2020; Iwasa & Lee, 2013).

- **Structural and contextual factors highly influence punishment's impact on efficiency:**
    - **Spatial structure**: Positive efficiency effects are most robust in spatially structured interactions; in well-mixed (random) settings, punishment can fail to promote cooperation or even reduce efficiency (Oya & Ohtsuki, 2017; Nakamaru & Dieckmann, 2009).
    - **Game length**: More rounds allow the positive effects of punishment on efficiency to accumulate (Eldakar et al., 2007; Leimar, 1997; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
    - **Group size**: Small to moderate player counts favor efficient punishment; larger groups magnify anti-social punishment and make cooperation harder (Powers et al., 2012; Dercole et al., 2013).

- **Monitoring and feedback are necessary conditions.**
    - Imperfect, noisy, or uninformative monitoring diminishes the ability of punishment to raise efficiency substantially (Bednar, 2006; Faillo et al., 2013).
    - Providing full feedback on contributions is necessary for legitimate punishment to realize its efficiency benefits (Faillo et al., 2013).

- **Baseline (control) efficiency matters.**
    - When control efficiency is already high (e.g., via communication, reputation, or high MPCR), punishment may offer little or no additional efficiency gain (Archetti & Scheuring, 2013; Milinski & Rockenbach, 2012; Takezawa & Price, 2010).
    - In low baseline efficiency settings, the proportional effect of punishment on efficiency is larger.

- **Risk of negative or null effects.**
    - When anti-social punishment is frequent, or when enforcement mechanisms are corrupt or nontransparent, efficiency may not increase and can even decrease relative to control (Faillo et al., 2013; Powers et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2015).
    - In one-shot or very short games, the costs of punishment may outweigh the cooperation gains (Eldakar et al., 2007; Kamijo et al., 2014).
    - When insurance or other avoidance mechanisms are available at low cost, punishment's efficacy in raising efficiency is reduced (Zhang et al., 2013).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Direct, empirical/theoretical evidence supports the following:**

- When predicting efficiency with peer punishment enabled, relative to known control efficiency, you should expect:
    - **Moderate to strong efficiency increase** if:  
        - Punishment is costly but not excessive,
        - Well-targeted (e.g., only high contributors can punish defectors, or punishment is democratic/delegated/centralized and transparent),
        - Monitoring is sufficiently informative,
        - Enforcement is either peer-based without anti-social punishment or institutionally constrained to legitimate cases,
        - Baseline efficiency is low (e.g., no chat, no reputation, no reward, moderate MPCR).

    - **Small or no efficiency gain, possible efficiency loss** if:
        - Punishment is costless or very cheap (in peer schemes, this leads to over-punishment),
        - High levels of anti-social or indiscriminate punishment are observed,
        - Feedback/monitoring is missing or uninformative,
        - Punishment institution is susceptible to corruption and no identification of punishers,  
        - Baseline efficiency is already high (due to communication, high MPCR, repeated play, or reputation mechanisms).

- **Key design interactions:**
    - The design of the punishment institution (peer, centralized, democratic), cost structure, and monitoring technology directly inform the magnitude and direction of the efficiency effect.
    - Population structure (spatial vs. well-mixed), group size, and round number modulate the effect—in general, smaller groups and longer games favor a more positive effect.
    - Legitimate, probabilistic, or graduated punishment outperforms unrestricted or rigid punishment in sustaining efficiency gains.
    - If the prediction task is in a domain with possible anti-social punishment, corrupted enforcers, or adjacent to social dilemmas (e.g., resource dilemmas), expect high context-dependence; prediction should be adjusted downward in these risk regimes.

- **Behavioral proxies vs. efficiency:**
    - Increased cooperation rates do not necessarily translate into higher efficiency if punishment is frequent and costly; always use direct efficiency data when available rather than inferring from contributions.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions (empirical and theoretical):**
- `player_count`: Small/moderate group sizes are linked to larger positive effects of punishment; large groups often show diminished efficacy or increased anti-social punishment.
- `num_rounds`: Longer repeated games allow positive punishment effects to accumulate.
- `mpcr`: Higher marginal per-capita return facilitates cooperation and reduces reliance on punishment; effect of punishment on efficiency is stronger at lower mpcr.
- `punishment_cost` and `punishment_tech`: Cost/impact of punishment are among the most-studied moderators; strong evidence for non-monotonic effects (i.e., both too low and too high costs reduce efficiency).
- `all_or_nothing`: Both continuous and binary contribution frameworks are examined; payoff effects are robust across both in linear games.
- `chat`: Some empirical studies explicitly compare with and without communication; baseline efficiency is much higher with chat, which moderates the additional effect of punishment.
- `reward_exists`: Less coverage, but a few key studies address combined punishment/reward designs and show additive or moderating interactions.
- `punishment_tech` (peer, delegated, democratic, probabilistic, graduated, centralized): Key differentiator in efficiency outcomes.

**Indirectly or contextually discussed dimensions:**
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_n_rounds`: Feedback and information structure are highlighted as critical for legitimate punishment effects.
- `show_punishment_id`: Central in corruption and anti-social punishment models.
- `default_contrib`: Covered less frequently; some framing effects discussed.
- `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: Some, less direct, evidence, often in combination with punishment.

**Sparse or missing in this literature set:**
- Fine-grained framing manipulations, visibility and anonymity effects beyond basic feedback or chat.
- Default contribution setting as an independent moderator.
- Interactions with elaborate reward structures not deeply covered.
- Systematic variation of multi-level or real-world institutional features (most centralized punishment is modeled, not field-tested).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **External validity:** Most strong evidence comes from laboratory PGGs with undergraduate samples and stylized parameters; generalization to large-scale, real-world or group-structured settings is less certain.

- **Design coverage gaps:** Some design dimensions (e.g., default contributions, nuanced reward structures) are not well explored across the sample.

- **Outcome translation:** In some theory and empirical papers, only behavioral outcomes (contribution, compliance, norm maintenance) are measured, not efficiency or group payoff. This limits direct quantitative guidance for the prediction task, even where qualitative directionality is clear.

- **Mechanism ambiguity:** The mechanisms underlying anti-social or excessive punishment, and their interaction with group size or population structure, are highlighted but not always resolved. In particular, the risk of efficiency declines due to anti-social punishment is present in well-mixed, larger, or culturally heterogeneous groups.

- **Institutional realism:** Several works demonstrate positive effects for idealized forms of punishment (e.g., honest centralized enforcers), but directly note that corruption or poor legitimacy can erase or reverse these gains. This may not be reliably observable from design variables alone.

- **Ambiguity for edge-case designs:** When punishment is enabled but highly constrained, indirect, or embedded in complex reward or communication frameworks, evidence is sparse or indirect.

- **Selection bias toward reporting positive effects:** Many review and theory papers caution that the literature may overstate the reliability and magnitude of efficiency gains from punishment, particularly where designs minimize anti-social punishment or control for culture/context.

---

**In summary:**  
The literature provides strong, consistent evidence that enabling well-designed, legitimate, and costly (but not extreme) punishment in standard PGG environments increases efficiency relative to no-punishment baseline, especially when the control (no-punishment) efficiency is low and structural features (monitoring, group size, feedback) are favorable. Poorly targeted, costless, anti-social, or corrupted punishment, or games with already high baseline efficiency, can yield little or even negative net efficiency gains. These findings are robust across a substantial portion of the available literature, which is directly relevant and quantitatively informative for predicting efficiency as a function of the specified 14 game design dimensions and control efficiency. However, significant caveats apply for generalizability, external settings, and edge-case institutional designs.
