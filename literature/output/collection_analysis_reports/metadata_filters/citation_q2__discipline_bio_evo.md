# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set combines a substantial range of **empirical experimental studies** (lab and field; both canonical PGGs and variants) and **theoretical models** (analytical and simulation-based). The empirical base includes controlled laboratory experiments with standard linear public goods games (PGGs), field experiments on real-world resource dilemmas (CPRs), and observational studies for broader context. The theoretical work offers models addressing evolutionary, institutional, and population-structural moderators of punishment's effects.

- **Coverage:** The evidence base is broad in scope for public-goods-game environments with punishment, but narrower for precise, parameterized guidance on efficiency changes due to punishment across the full 14 design dimensions.
- **Outcomes Studied:** Some papers report **payoff/efficiency outcomes directly**, others less so—many focus predominantly on **behavioral cooperation rates**, norms, or punishment behavior rather than explicit group efficiency.
- **Balance:** There is a healthy mix of empirical findings and theory; however, **the balance between payoff-related and non-payoff outcomes, and between direct and indirect relevance to the prediction task, is uneven**.

# 2) Task Relevance

Here, we assess the literature on three axes most salient for the efficiency prediction task:

### a) `pgg_or_variant`  
- **exact**: Many papers—empirical (e.g., Fischer et al., 2016; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021) and theoretical (Wolff, 2012; Wang & Lv, 2019)—directly implement standard or slightly modified PGGs.
- **close**: Numerous papers study close CPR or coordination variants (e.g., Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Javaid & Falk, 2015; Wang et al., 2024; Sääksvuori, 2014) with similar group payoff logic.
- **adjacent/weak/none**: Some focus on games lacking explicit group payoff interdependence (e.g., trust/ultimatum/donation, or real-world analogues).

### b) `punishment_or_sanctions`  
- **exact**: Studies with peer punishment, centralized punishment, or formal exclusion (e.g., Andrighetto et al., 2016; Fischer et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021), plus field/CPR experiments and theory.
- **close**: Papers using ostracism, exclusion, or sanctions closely analogous to punishment (e.g., Sääksvuori, 2014; Fang et al., 2020).
- **adjacent/weak/none**: Papers with reputation-based or indirect sanctions, or lacking punishment entirely.

### c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`  
- **exact**: Roughly half the most relevant papers measure group payoff/efficiency (e.g., Fischer et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Xu et al., 2022; Lee & Iwasa, 2014; Wang & Lv, 2019).
- **close**: Others report behavioral proxies (cooperation/contribution rates), sometimes mapping to payoff (e.g., Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021; Grimalda et al., 2022), or report efficiency in adjacent games.
- **adjacent/weak/none**: Several focus solely on non-payoff outcomes (e.g., norm enforcement, neural measures, evolutionary frequencies) or provide context rather than direct outcome data.

**Summary:**  
- **High direct relevance** for canonical and variant PGGs with payoff measurement of efficiency, moderate relevance for adjacent games/contexts.  
- **Punishment treatments** are well represented.  
- **Direct efficiency/outcome measurement** is present but outweighed by behavioral studies.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Group Efficiency/Payoff (exact):**  
  Directly measured in many empirical and theoretical PGG/CPR studies—group earnings, welfare, social efficiency (e.g., Wegmann & Musshoff, Fatas & Mateu, Andrighetto et al., Wang & Lv, Lee & Iwasa, Xu et al.)

- **Related Payoff Outcomes (close/adjacent):**  
  Welfare/surplus improvements, resource sustainability, collective risk (loss avoidance), or payoff in adjacent games (ultimatum, trust, coordination).

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes:**  
  Predominant in a large subset:  
    - **Contribution/Cooperation Rates:** Often used as a proxy but not identical to efficiency (e.g., punishment increasing cooperation but not payoff, as in Grimalda et al., 2022).
    - **Punishment Frequency/Severity:** Who punishes whom, anti-social punishment, counter-punishment (e.g., Espín et al., 2022).
    - **Norm Compliance, Reputational Effects, Signaling, Ostracism Behavior, Neural Responses.**
    - **Strategy frequencies/evolutionary stable states** (theory).

> **Explicit finding:** Many studies demonstrate that greater cooperation rates (from punishment) do not always equate to higher group efficiency due to direct punishment costs (e.g., Grimalda et al., 2022; Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the most directly relevant evidence, the following patterns and qualifiers emerge:

## a) **Punishment does not universally increase efficiency**
- In **standard linear PGGs**, enabling peer punishment *often fails to raise group efficiency* and may even decrease it if punishment is costly and overused/antisocial (Fischer et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021; Grimalda et al., 2022).
- Destruction of efficiency stems from **excess punishment cost**—when the cost of punishment outweighs the gains from increased contributions/cooperation.

## b) **Moderators of Positive Efficiency Effects**
- **Game Structure:**  
  - In *weakest-link/minimum* public goods games (i.e., complementarity), *punishment robustly increases efficiency* (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
  - In *coordination/threshold/CPR variants* (Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019; Lee & Iwasa, 2014), centralized or graduated punishment can strongly boost efficiency—especially when baseline provision is inefficient.
- **Communication:**  
  - *Enabling chat or messaging* alongside punishment reverses the negative impact of counter-punishment and increases efficiency (Andrighetto et al., 2016).
- **Information Environment & Structure:**  
  - *Centralization of punishment* eliminates perverse punishment but does not always improve efficiency compared to decentralized forms (Fischer et al., 2016).
  - *Antisocial punishment*, retaliation, or poor targeting often neutralize efficiency gains (Wolff, 2012; Fischer et al., 2016; Grimalda et al., 2022).
- **Punishment Cost and Technology:**  
  - *Low punishment costs* and high impact per cost are consistently linked to stronger efficiency effects in theory and simulation (Eldakar et al., 2013; Wang & Lv, 2019; Fang et al., 2020; Voelkl, 2015).
- **Baseline Efficiency:**  
  - If *control efficiency* is already high (e.g., due to strong local norms and visible feedback), *adding punishment can reduce efficiency* (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
  - If control efficiency is low (free-riding prevalent), punishment more often increases efficiency.

## c) **Variants & Extensions**
- **Ostracism/Exclusion as Punishment:**  
  - Social exclusion and ostracism can robustly increase efficiency, especially with migration/group formation dynamics (Sääksvuori, 2014).
- **Random or Probabilistic Monitoring:**  
  - Mixed evidence: When well designed, external (random) monitoring with monetary punishment improves efficiency (Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019), but ill-designed probabilistic punishment can decrease efficiency by introducing coordination problems (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
- **Reward:**  
  - Reward can equivalently or complementarily boost efficiency in some models (Wang et al., 2024; Lee & Iwasa, 2014).
- **Resource Dynamics:**  
  - For resource-based variants (dynamic CPRs), the natural resource growth rate and punishment tax size jointly moderate the efficiency effect (Wang et al., 2024).

## d) **Mechanism and Theoretical Results**
- **Punishment is effective only when costs are low, impact is high, and antisocial/retaliatory punishment is suppressed.**  
- **Efficiency gains from punishment are typically conditional**: High costs, poor implementation, or opportunities for antisocial punishment undermine net payoff benefits (Wolff, 2012; Fang et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2015).

## e) **Divergence**
- There is **ambiguous or conflicting evidence**: Some empirical studies find positive, others neutral or negative, effects of punishment on efficiency, often dependent on details of design and context (compare Fatas & Mateu, 2015, and Javaid & Falk, 2015, to Wegmann & Musshoff, 2019).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Synthesizing these findings, **the expected effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency depends on multiple interlocking design dimensions and the observed control (no-punishment) efficiency**:

- **Prediction from control efficiency:**  
  - **If control efficiency is low (free-riding dominates):** Adding well-designed punishment typically increases efficiency—**if** the punishment is well-targeted, not too costly, and antisocial punishment is minimal.
  - **If control efficiency is high (strong norms, public social information):** Adding punishment may yield *no effect* or *decrease* efficiency due to extra punitive costs (Javaid & Falk, 2015).

- **Most critical dimensions for prediction (with direct or strong indirect support):**
  - **mpcr**: Lower marginal returns make punishment less likely to recover costs (Xu et al., 2022; Wang & Lv, 2019).
  - **punishment_cost/punishment_tech:** Higher cost and lower effectiveness dampen or reverse gains (Eldakar et al., 2013; Fischer et al., 2016).
  - **player_count**: Some theory and empirical studies suggest positive effects of small group size for punishment efficacy.
  - **num_rounds**: Longer games permit reciprocation or retaliation; retaliation undermines punishment's efficiency effect at high continuation probabilities (Wolff, 2012).
  - **chat (communication):** Communication both enables norm setting and reduces efficiency-destroying counter-punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016).
  - **all_or_nothing**: Payoff impacts of punishment may be sharper with threshold/complementarity (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
  - **reward_exists/reward_tech**: The presence of reward as an alternative or adjunct can also increase efficiency.

- **Supporting Factors:**
  - **Information environment** (summary feedback, identification of punishers/rewarders) can modulate antisocial punishment and efficiency (Fischer et al., 2016).
  - **Institutional/centralized punishment** is less vulnerable to perverse effects but not always more efficient.
  - **Presence of antisocial punishment and retaliation**: If these are high, expect less or negative effect on efficiency.

> **Rule of thumb:** Enabling peer punishment does *not* guarantee an increase in group efficiency as measured by payoff relative to the fully cooperative benchmark; it may even decrease efficiency if control is high or punishment is misapplied. The most robust positive effects occur with low-cost, high-impact, well-monitored punishment in games with low control efficiency and vulnerable to free riding, *especially* when combined with communication or norm-setting opportunities.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

## **Directly Informed (with quantitative or qualitative efficiency data)**
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`, `chat`, `all_or_nothing`, `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (in select models), `show_other_summaries` (mainly via communication/information manipulation).

## **Indirectly Informed**
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id` (via communication/identification in select studies), `default_contrib` (framing, e.g., opt-in/opt-out, less studied re: efficiency, but contextually important).

## **Contextually Discussed**
- Group structure (e.g., migration, group formation), asymmetry, culture/gender/ethnicity—sometimes mapped onto game dimensions but more often addressed qualitatively or via behavior.

## **Effectively Missing**
- Very few papers directly vary or report effects of `default_contrib`, `show_punishment_id` (unless framing as identity/reputation), `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` (rewards receive (comparatively) less empirical coverage), and some subtleties of feedback mechanisms.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Payoff vs. Behavior:** Many studies measure cooperation, not efficiency; some positive effects on cooperation do not translate into efficiency gains due to punishment costs.
- **Specificity to Design:**  
  - Findings are **context-dependent**—effects of punishment depend crucially on the game structure (linear vs. threshold), group composition, and details of the punishment institution.
  - Some effect moderators (e.g., retaliation, antisocial punishment, communication) are themselves complex or hard to parameterize.
- **Adjacent and Close-Variant Evidence:** A substantial portion of the literature is on CPR, coordination, or evolutionary models not strictly matching the linear PGG efficiency task.
- **Empirical vs. Theoretical Gaps:**  
  - Theory offers strong mechanisms/qualitative predictions, but robust, parameterized empirical effect sizes are rare.
  - Field studies often lack experimental control or measurement of all design dimensions.
- **Generalizability:**  
  - Cross-cultural and ecological heterogeneity is highlighted contextually (e.g., punishment may work differently depending on local norms, existing baseline efficiency).
  - Long term/evolutionary dynamics may not map cleanly onto short-term experimental outcomes.
- **Sparse Data for Some Dimensions:**  
  - Several design elements, especially those related to visibility, default framing, or reward cost/tech, are poorly studied as efficiency moderators.

**Summary Limitation:**  
The literature provides strong qualitative and some quantitative guidance for prediction of efficiency outcomes as a function of design dimensions and control efficiency, but predictions will be **noisier, less reliable, and more ambiguous** in regions of design space where empirical evidence is thin, context is unusual, or payoffs are not measured directly. The efficiency effect of punishment is never universal and depends on nuanced interaction of multiple design dimensions and baseline conditions.
