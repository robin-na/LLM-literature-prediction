# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 65 scholarly articles, featuring a rich mix of empirical (lab and field experimental) and theoretical analyses. A substantial proportion directly tests interventions in public goods games (PGGs) or their close variants, often with explicit enabling/disabling of peer, centralized, or institutional punishment. Papers address a variety of outcome measures, but a notable number report efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes relevant to the prediction task. The evidence base spans both narrowly focused designs (near-standard PGGs) and broader social dilemma environments (CPR games, trust, bargaining, and labor markets).

Empirical papers constitute the majority, especially lab experiments closely matching the prediction context. Several theories and simulation models provide mechanistic insights and help generalize findings or explain divergent empirical effects. The evidence is neither exclusively narrow (not all papers are pure PGGs with on/off punishment and efficiency as primary outcome) nor diffuse: a strong core of highly relevant studies grounds the predictions, supported by valuable adjacent and context-setting work.

# 2) Task Relevance

## a) `pgg_or_variant`
- **exact relevance**: Numerous empirical and theoretical papers implement standard or near-standard linear PGGs with clear control and punishment-enabled treatments (e.g., Gürdal et al., 2021; Ramalingam et al., 2019; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Lim & Zhang, 2020; Nicklisch et al., 2021; Glöckner et al., 2018; Lippert & Tremewan, 2021).
- **close relevance**: A significant portion explore threshold PGGs, CPR games, trust games, or group-exchange settings structurally similar to PGGs but with distinct dynamics or institutional details (e.g., Selles et al., 2020; Chávez et al., 2021; Di Guida et al., 2021; Hajikhameneh et al., 2019).
- **adjacent/weak**: Several studies are further removed (e.g., bargaining, contest, partner choice, principal-agent games), providing supplementary context but offering less direct guidance for classic PGGs.

## b) `punishment_or_sanctions`
- **exact relevance**: A sizeable subset manipulates the presence and type (peer, authority, centralized, collective) of punishment or sanctions (e.g., endogenous institution selection, hired-gun mechanisms). Key studies match the prediction scenario of toggling peer punishment on/off.
- **close/adjacent**: Some papers analyze related phenomena such as enforcement, ostracism, reward, or information-based sanctions, which may substitute for or interact with punishment (e.g., exclusion, pledge-and-review, reputation systems).
- **weak/none**: A minority address only communication, group composition, or norms, without punishment manipulation—these provide contextual baseline outcomes.

## c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
- **exact relevance**: Many papers explicitly measure group efficiency (total payoff relative to full cooperation) or closely related payoff aggregates (e.g., total earnings, welfare, surplus)—directly matching the outcome requested for prediction.
- **close/adjacent**: Some studies infer efficiency from contribution rates (where full contributions map directly to payoffs, e.g., in linear PGGs), or focus on related welfare measures in slightly different environments (trust games, CPR).
- **weak/none**: Several papers only report non-payoff behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, cooperation frequency, norm compliance), limiting their direct predictive value for efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**: 
    - *Efficiency*—defined as total group payoff divided by the social optimum—is directly reported in many exact-match papers (e.g., Gürdal et al., 2021; Ramalingam et al., 2019; Nicklisch et al., 2021; Lim & Zhang, 2020).
    - *Group payoff/earnings, total coins, surplus, or welfare*—used in both experimental and theoretical studies as measures functionally identical or directly convertible to efficiency.
    - Some studies, especially in close/adjacent domains, use outcome measures like harvest, profit, or market surplus, interpreted analogously to efficiency.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**: 
    - *Contribution rates, cooperation levels*: Most studies also track these, but unless combined with punishment-cost data and explicit payoff mapping, they do not suffice for efficiency prediction.
    - *Punishment frequency/application*, *exclusion/ostracism*, and *norm compliance*: Common secondary outcomes, important for mechanism understanding, but not direct substitutes for efficiency.
    - *Communication, rating, or approval signals*: Occasionally measured, typically affecting behavior via non-monetary incentives.

Distinct separation between payoff and non-payoff outcomes is generally maintained, with explicit noting when inferences about efficiency are based on contribution rates alone.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

Synthesizing across the most relevant literature, the following findings emerge for the effect of enabling punishment in PGG-like environments:

- **Punishment often increases efficiency, but not always**. Most standard repeated PGGs report robust positive effects of enabling punishment on efficiency, sometimes with substantial improvements over control (Gürdal et al., 2021; Lim & Zhang, 2020; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Glöckner et al., 2018—both channels; Lippert & Tremewan, 2021—P&R as implicit punishment).
- **However, positive efficiency effects are highly contingent** on:
    - *Punishment cost-to-impact ratio*: If punishment is too costly or used excessively, the efficiency gains from increased contributions are offset or reversed by the resource drain of punishment itself (Ramalingam et al., 2019; Glöckner et al., 2018—single channel; Kanitsar, 2021).
    - *Institutional details*: Punishment works best when monitoring is cheap and accurate (Nicklisch et al., 2021; Mihm & Toth, 2020), when both observed and unobserved punishment channels are available (Glöckner et al., 2018), or when an authority/punisher can act credibly and efficiently (Lim & Zhang, 2020; Alventosa & Olcina, 2021). Centralized punishment’s efficiency effect may be tempered by how institution formation costs are borne (Safarzynska, 2020).
    - *Game framing and context*: Punishment’s efficiency effect can depend strongly on whether defection is framed as an act of omission or commission (Ramalingam et al., 2019), on the network/density of interaction (Kanitsar, 2021; Mihm & Toth, 2020), or on the presence of alternative institutions/competition (Chugunova et al., 2020).
    - *Group and societal composition*: Efficiency gains are largest in settings with more 'strong reciprocators' (pro-social punishers) and fewer 'norm keepers' (punishers of both high and low contributors) (Suleiman & Samid, 2021), and may be absent or negative if antisocial punishment or mis-targeting is prevalent (Robbett, 2019).
    - *Information availability*: Efficiency is maximized when punishment is well-informed—i.e., players know who has contributed and can target free-riders (Nicklisch et al., 2021); poorly informed or noise-prone punishment can reduce welfare (Tanimoto, 2018).
- **Efficiency gains do not always track increases in contributions**: Several papers show increases in cooperation/contribution rates with punishment, but no corresponding rise in efficiency due to the punishment cost (Ramalingam et al., 2019—Give frame; Robbett, 2019; Kanitsar, 2021—costly punishment).
- **Punishment can backfire or have null/negative effects on efficiency**: Some environments, especially those with ambiguous incentives, costly or misapplied punishment, or low information, exhibit either no improvement or reductions in efficiency when punishment is enabled (Robbett, 2019; Kanitsar, 2021; Safarzynska, 2020; Tanimoto, 2018). Extreme cases arise in generalized/exchange network structures (Kanitsar, 2021), or where punishment is behavioral rather than monetary and targets are unclear.

Overall, the literature strongly supports a predictive relationship whereby, in standard repeated PGGs under typical lab parameterizations (moderate group size, clearly observable contributions, moderate punishment cost/impact), enabling peer punishment usually increases efficiency, and the magnitude of the effect can be substantial. Yet, notable boundary conditions are identified.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

## For predicting efficiency with enabled peer punishment—given game design and control efficiency—the literature provides the following guidance:

- **Baseline (control) efficiency is predictive, but not sufficient**. The effect size of enabling punishment depends crucially on specific game dimensions and contextual moderators, not just on control group efficiency.
- **Dimension-wise guidance**:
    - *Punishment cost-to-benefit ratio*: Lower cost, higher impact punishment (e.g., 1:3 or better) predicts stronger efficiency gains (Gürdal et al., 2021; Ramalingam et al., 2019; Lim & Zhang, 2020). High-cost punishment may nullify payoffs or even reduce efficiency (Ramalingam et al., 2019—Give; Kanitsar, 2021; Safarzynska, 2020).
    - *Monitoring/information*: Punishment is most effective when monitoring is low-cost and accurate; lack of information undermines its benefit (Nicklisch et al., 2021; Mihm & Toth, 2020).
    - *Group size*: Efficiency gains from punishment can increase with group size in centralized authority designs (Lim & Zhang, 2020). In peer-punishment, standard group sizes (N=4) are best-studied; very large groups may see attenuation (Tanimoto, 2018).
    - *Game structure*: Standard linear PGGs show reliably positive efficiency effects; threshold or CPR games show more mixed effects depending on risk and loss structures (Bolle, 2021; Selles et al., 2020).
    - *Punishment technology*: The combination of punishment channels (immediate/observed and delayed/unobserved) can yield much larger efficiency increases than either channel alone (Glöckner et al., 2018).
    - *Cultural and behavioral context*: The effect of punishment on efficiency can vary cross-culturally—generally robust, but modulated by group norm types and proportion of reciprocators (Gürdal et al., 2021; Suleiman & Samid, 2021).
- **Where non-payoff behavioral outcomes are reported, inferences about efficiency should be made only when the payoff mapping is direct and the cost of punishment is low or absent**.
- **Ambiguity and exception cases**: Punishment may have no or even negative efficiency impact when it is:
    - Costly and overused;
    - Poorly targeted due to ambiguous norms or private information (Robbett, 2019);
    - Incorporated in complex or networked structures lacking clear identification or with high monitoring costs (Kanitsar, 2021; Mihm & Toth, 2020);
    - Contexts with high antisocial punishment, misapplied sanctions, or crosstalk between reward and punishment institutions (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Chugunova et al., 2020).

In summary: For standard PGGs under typical lab conditions with well-calibrated punishment (moderate cost-impact, accurate monitoring, clear feedback), enabling punishment typically increases efficiency relative to control, sometimes substantially; however, effects range from strongly positive to null or negative based on specific design parameters. Baseline (control) efficiency is informative, but full prediction accuracy requires consideration of the aforementioned moderators.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

Of the 14 key prediction dimensions, the literature coverage can be classified as follows:

## Directly informed:
- **player_count**: Most experiments report and vary group size (commonly N=4; Lim & Zhang, 2020 explore N=8).
- **num_rounds**: Number of rounds explicitly specified and linked to effect persistence or decay.
- **mpcr**: Marginal per-capita return varied and directly tied to incentives and efficiency effects (widely discussed).
- **punishment_cost**, **punishment_tech/punishment_magnitude**: Costs and technologies (including impact ratios, observed/unobserved channels, centralized/peer) are frequently manipulated and found to be major moderators.
- **all_or_nothing**: Both continuous and binary contribution settings are studied, with implications for efficiency effect size and mechanism.
- **show_n_rounds**, **show_other_summaries**: Feedback/monitoring conditions (visibility of rounds and contributions) highly salient in effect size and targeting accuracy.
- **chat**: Controlled for in many studies; direct communication is sometimes allowed, usually shown to increase baseline cooperation (i.e., increases control efficiency, sometimes narrows the relative impact of punishment).

## Indirectly or contextually discussed:
- **reward_exists**, **reward_cost**, **reward_tech**: Several studies examine the impact of combining reward and punishment or of competing institutions (e.g., Chugunova et al., 2020), showing interaction effects (rewards may outperform punishment alone under some conditions).
- **default_contrib**: Framing (opt-in/opt-out, take/give) is occasionally addressed (Martinsson et al., 2019; Ramalingam et al., 2019), influencing the context-dependence of punishment's effect.
- **show_punishment_id**: Specific feedback on punishment identity is uncommonly varied, but related constructs (information about who monitors or punishes) are discussed (Nicklisch et al., 2021).
  
## Effectively missing:
- No systematic or quantitative evidence on **show_punishment_id**, **default_contrib** as direct moderators for efficiency with punishment, though some studies address adjacent issues.
- Very few studies explicitly manipulate all dimensions jointly; coverage is strongest for foundational PGG/CPR design parameters.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Behavioral outcome/efficiency gap**: Several studies rely on cooperation/contribution, not efficiency, especially when punishment is present and costly—potentially overestimating efficiency improvements.
- **Parameter and context bounds**: Most strong-positive efficiency findings occur in standard lab settings (N=4, moderate MPCR, well-calibrated punishment cost/impact, no communication), limiting generalizability to large N, non-linear PGGs, or field environments.
- **Sparse coverage for less typical design features**: Dimensions like reward, punishment identity feedback, and contribution framing are not consistently manipulated.
- **Cultural/behavioral heterogeneity**: Substantial variation in punishment effectiveness by group norm composition or cultural background; predictions may fail where antisocial punishment, norm-keeper types, or conflicting fairness beliefs are prevalent.
- **Complex/joint institution effects**: Efficiency prediction in the presence of both reward and punishment, or under institutional competition (migration, endogenous voting), is less well specified.
- **Potential for backfire and negative effects**: Minority of robust studies document cases with null or negative efficiency impacts of punishment due to high cost, poor targeting, or systemic limitations (e.g., Kanitsar, 2021; Robbett, 2019; Safarzynska, 2020).
- **Adjacent results and external validity**: While adjacent domains (CPR, trust, labor, contests) offer lessons, their transferability to classic PGGs is always partial and context-dependent.
- **Few fully predictive models**: While some theory papers provide explicit mapping from game design to equilibrium efficiency (e.g., Botta et al., 2021), most empirical findings are context-specific and do not support fine-grained quantitative prediction without additional modeling.

---

**Summary**:  
The literature provides a strong empirical and theoretical basis for predicting that, in standard repeated PGGs, enabling well-calibrated peer or institutional punishment increases efficiency relative to the control condition without punishment. The size (and even sign) of the effect, however, is moderated by key design dimensions—most notably punishment cost/impact ratio, monitoring quality, game framing, and group context. Several design dimensions are well addressed; others are less covered or only indirectly discussed. Limitations arise from reliance on contribution rates (when not mapping to payoff), strong context dependency, and less coverage for complex institutions or rarely manipulated parameters. For robust accuracy in predicting treatment efficiency from design plus control efficiency, attention to these moderators and boundary conditions is critical.
