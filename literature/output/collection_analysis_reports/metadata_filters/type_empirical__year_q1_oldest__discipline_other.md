# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises four studies, largely empirical, with three laboratory experiments (Grechenig et al., 2010; Brick & Visser, 2010; Xiao & Bicchieri, 2012) and one observational survey-based study (Johansson et al., 2003). The experimental studies primarily involve economic games, although not all are standard public goods games (PGG), and not all address punishment or efficiency as defined for the prediction task. The papers vary in focus: one study (Grechenig et al., 2010) is directly on PGG with peer punishment and efficiency; another (Brick & Visser, 2010) is on sanction-based compliance in PGG-like emission games; the others are more peripheral, addressing policy attitudes (Johansson et al., 2003) or information seeking in dictator games (Xiao & Bicchieri, 2012). Overall, the literature base is narrow for prediction about peer-punishment efficiency effects in public goods games, with only one directly relevant study.

# 2) Task Relevance

**PGG or Variant:**
- **exact**: Grechenig et al. (2010) is a repeated PGG.
- **close**: Brick & Visser (2010) employ a related but more complex public good/emission game.
- **adjacent**: Johansson et al. (2003) discusses collective action dilemmas in policy, not in experimental or standard PGG format.
- **weak**: Xiao & Bicchieri (2012) uses only a dictator game.

**Punishment or Sanctions:**
- **exact**: Grechenig et al. (2010) studies peer punishment in PGG.
- **close**: Brick & Visser (2010) implements an institutional (tax-based, automatic) sanction for under-contribution, not peer punishment.
- **adjacent/none**: Johansson et al. (2003) discusses disincentive structures in policy (road pricing).
- **none**: Xiao & Bicchieri (2012) does not involve punishment or sanctions.

**Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome:**
- **exact**: Grechenig et al. (2010) reports on group efficiency (ratio of earnings to full cooperation).
- **adjacent**: Brick & Visser (2010) infers potential efficiency via compliance and earnings, but does not report efficiency directly.
- **adjacent/none**: Johansson et al. (2003) offers no quantitative payoff outcomes, only policy attitudes.
- **none**: Xiao & Bicchieri (2012) contains no payoff/efficiency data.

**Summary:** Only Grechenig et al. (2010) is exact for all three core relevance dimensions. Brick & Visser (2010) is close for the first two (PGG, sanctions), but lacks an efficiency outcome. The other two papers are largely irrelevant for the prediction task.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (group payoff, efficiency, welfare):**
    - Grechenig et al. (2010): Directly measures efficiency (primary outcome).
    - Brick & Visser (2010): Reports earnings and compliance with group targets, allowing inference about welfare but not efficiency in the strict sense.
    - Johansson et al. (2003): Does not report any group payoff or efficiency measures.
    - Xiao & Bicchieri (2012): Reports no payoff-based outcomes.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes:**
    - All include some non-payoff outcomes (e.g., contribution rate, norm compliance, information seeking).
    - Johansson et al. (2003) measures attitudes and stated preferences.
    - Xiao & Bicchieri (2012) measures information demand and allocation behavior in a dictator game.

**Distinction:** Only Grechenig et al. (2010) provides empirical payoff-based outcomes in PGG with peer punishment. Brick & Visser (2010) offers adjacent, group-compliance-based outcomes, but not efficiency per se.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Punishment in Standard PGG Environments (Grechenig et al., 2010):**
- When peers have accurate information about others' contributions, enabling punishment increases both cooperation and efficiency.
- When information about contributions is noisy or uncertain, punishment persists but is less targeted, leading to increased antisocial punishment, more frequent sanctioning, and a reduction in group efficiency—below the baseline with no punishment.
- The negative effect on efficiency under noise is substantial and not just short-lived.
- The study asserts the effectiveness of punishment depends critically on the information environment.

**Sanctions and Compliance in Target-based Public Good Game (Brick & Visser, 2010):**
- Automatic, rule-based sanctions (fines) for under-contribution substantially increase the rate of groups achieving target contributions, far exceeding the improvements gained from communication alone.
- Sanctions crowd out voluntary over-contribution but do not reduce compliance to targets.
- These results hold despite player heterogeneity.
- Actual efficiency (as defined in the prediction task) is not measured, but high compliance implies improved welfare.

**Non-empirical or Contextual Findings:**
- Johansson et al. (2003): Highlights importance of fairness and acceptability in social dilemmas and sanction acceptance, but offers no empirical efficiency or payoff results.
- Xiao & Bicchieri (2012): Focuses on information-seeking and norm compliance, not linked to punishment or efficiency.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

Overall, the literature offers guidance as follows:

- **Strong Empirical Evidence:** Grechenig et al. (2010) shows that enabling peer punishment in a standard PGG increases efficiency only when information is accurate. If information is noisy—meaning players cannot clearly identify defectors—punishment is misapplied and reduces efficiency below the no-punishment baseline.
- **Key Design Moderator: Information Accuracy:** The effect of punishment is not uniformly positive or negative; it is *conditional* on the accuracy of information about contributions. This variable is not one of the 14 listed prediction dimensions, but its presence or absence dominates the effect of punishment on efficiency in the setting studied.
- **Adjacent Support:** Brick & Visser (2010) suggests strong efficiency gains from automatic, institutional sanctions in achieving group targets, but these findings are for institutional, not peer, punishment and for target-based, not standard continuous, PGG.
- **Very Limited Direct Evidence for Other Dimensions:** None of the papers directly test modulations of the other design dimensions (player count, round number, MPCR, punishment cost, etc.) and their effect on efficiency difference between punishment and control conditions.

**Implication:** For games designed with accurate and transparent information about contributions, enabling peer punishment is likely to increase efficiency. If the game configures information to be noisy or ambiguous, enabling punishment is likely to decrease efficiency compared to the control game.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost (Grechenig et al., 2010):** Explored in designs or described as held constant, but only the information accuracy dimension was varied and found to determine punishment's effect.
- **punishment_tech (Brick & Visser, 2010):** Study employs automatic, rule-based punishment; findings pertain primarily to institutional—not peer—punishment.
  
**Indirectly or Contextually Discussed:**
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech:** None of the studies directly manipulate or empirically address reward conditions.
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id:** Xiao & Bicchieri (2012) varies some informational displays, but not in the context of punishment or efficiency outcomes.
- **default_contrib:** Not manipulated or measured in any included paper.
- **all_or_nothing, chat:** Both sometimes present as part of the design, but not directly analyzed as moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.

**Effectively Missing:**
- **Many design dimensions (e.g., reward structure, default contributions, peer-visibility features) are either not varied or not manipulated in connection with efficiency and punishment in any of the included papers.**

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Narrow Empirical Scope:** Only Grechenig et al. (2010) provides direct, empirical evidence on peer punishment's effect on efficiency in repeated public goods games. The other papers either lack payoff outcomes, lack relevant punishment mechanisms, or address only contextual issues.
- **Absence of Direct Manipulation Across Most Design Dimensions:** The key design moderators in the prediction task—player_count, num_rounds, punishment_cost, and others—are not empirically tested as efficacy moderators for punishment's effect on efficiency. Most are only held constant or present as background design features.
- **Dominance of Information Accuracy:** The single most important dimension identified is accuracy of information about others’ contributions—a factor not explicit among the 14 core predictor variables, but critical in determining whether enabling punishment raises or lowers efficiency.
- **Limited External Validity and Scope:** Evidence for effect of institutional punishment (Brick & Visser, 2010) may not transfer to peer punishment, and the efficiency construct in that study is not directly measured.
- **Sparse Evidence on Reward Mechanisms and Visibility Features:** No direct evidence for the impact of reward existence, reward cost, or features like showing punishment identities.
- **Fragmentation and Non-Payoff Focus:** Two papers are only contextually or adjacently relevant, and provide no empirical data on efficiency or payoff, further limiting the evidence base for detailed prediction across design dimensions.

**Summary:** The literature set provides strong, but narrow, evidence that the effect of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in public goods games is critically contingent on the accuracy of monitoring/observation. Most other game design dimensions are not empirically addressed as moderators in the available studies. For prediction, this means that without additional information about the monitoring environment, guidance must be limited and conditional.
