# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base comprises 48 academic papers, primarily empirical laboratory experiments, with a minority of observational or field studies. The majority of the experimental studies employ standard or variant public goods games (PGGs), using clear manipulations of punishment, communication, group structure, or institutions, and many directly measure efficiency or closely related payoff-based outcomes. The coverage is relatively broad for conventional PGG laboratory research but somewhat narrower for prediction tasks seeking evidence on all 14 nuanced design dimensions, particularly in more complex, field, or real-world environments. The evidence is overwhelmingly empirical (lab studies), with only rare contextual or real-world studies, and highly variable in terms of clarity and directness of payoff outcome reporting.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:  
- **exact**: Many papers use standard linear or threshold PGGs (e.g., Fischer et al., 2016; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Andrighetto et al., 2016; Kamei, 2019), yielding high direct relevance.  
- **close/adjacent**: Several use closely related common-pool resource (CPR) games, mutual insurance, or repeated PDs (e.g., Lévy-Garboua et al., 2017; Shreedhar et al., 2020), or PGGs with institutional twists (e.g., Lierl, 2016; Karakostas et al., 2023).  
- **weak/none**: Some context or observational papers are only weakly connected (e.g., Garfield et al., 2023; Holubcik et al., 2023).

**punishment_or_sanctions**:  
- **exact**: Over half the empirical PGG studies use direct peer or centralized punishment manipulations and report treatment effects (e.g., Fischer et al., 2016; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011).  
- **close/adjacent**: Several analyze sanction-like mechanisms (e.g., taxation as punishment in Brent et al., 2019, or ostracism as group punishment in Akpalu & Martinsson, 2012).  
- **adjacent or none**: A substantial minority report on reputation, communication, or behavioral punishment but lack institutional/explicit punishment interventions.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- **exact**: Roughly a third of the papers report on group efficiency, total earnings, or explicit payoff ratios to optimal (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Dannenberg, 2016; Zheng & Nie, 2013).  
- **close/adjacent**: Several report payoff-related outcomes in close variants or with less explicit connection to social optimum (e.g., welfare, mean coverage, profits).  
- **adjacent/weak/none**: Many only report behavioral cooperation measures, punishments assigned, or qualitative welfare implications, not efficiency metrics.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes (directly corresponding to "efficiency")**:  
  - Group efficiency (payoff as % of optimum), group earnings, total profits (e.g., Fischer et al., 2016; Zheng & Nie, 2013; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Kamei, 2019; Karakostas et al., 2023; Dannenberg, 2016).
  - Welfare, surplus, insurance coverage (Lévy-Garboua et al., 2017; Brent et al., 2019).
  - Some studies provide control and treatment group efficiency, permitting direct mapping to prediction task (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Dannenberg, 2016).

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:  
  - Contribution/cooperation rates, norm compliance, and punishment frequency (e.g., Espín et al., 2022; Mieth et al., 2017; Martinsson & Persson, 2019).
  - Retaliation and counter-punishment rates.
  - Communication content or style (e.g., Adams et al., 2022).

- **Other outcomes**:  
  - Social preference, trust and legitimacy, group identification, beliefs about institutions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## General Patterns

- **Punishment’s effect on efficiency is highly context-dependent**, moderated by group size, communication, production technology, information structure, cost and effectiveness of punishment, and the legitimacy and structure of the institution.
- **Enabling punishment does not always increase efficiency**; effects range from strongly positive (especially with supportive institutions or communication) to neutral, mixed, or even negative (when punishment is costly, antisocial, misdirected, or excessive).

## Key Moderators

- **Production technology**: In linear VCMs, punishment’s efficiency effect is often small or even negative; in weakest-link/complementary games, efficiency gains are large (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Karakostas et al., 2023).
- **Group size and structure**: Efficiency gains from punishment can increase with group size and network density (Harrell & Wolff, 2023) but may turn negative in large groups with anonymous punishment (Zheng & Nie, 2013).
- **Communication**: Allowing communication (chat, normative messages) strongly enhances the efficiency gains from punishment, sometimes eliminating losses from counter-punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Kamei, 2019; Adams et al., 2022).
- **Institutional design of punishment**:  
  - Centralized vs. decentralized punishment: No efficiency gain from centralization per se (Fischer et al., 2016; Chang et al., 2018).  
  - Legitimacy (selection by group/democracy) supports efficiency; wealth-based power assignment may undermine gains (Dorrough et al., 2017; Chang et al., 2018; Gatiso & Vollan, 2017).
  - Stability/equality in punishment power increases efficiency; inequality/instability can trigger competitive punishment and lower efficiency (Dorrough et al., 2017).

- **Noise/information structure**: Punishment improves efficiency mainly under perfect or intermediate information; with high noise, the gain vanishes or negative effects (Fischer et al., 2016; Gallo et al., 2022).
- **Types of punishment mechanisms**:
  - Costly peer punishment often erodes efficiency gains when overused or antisocial (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
  - Sanctioning/reward combined or via alternate mechanisms (e.g., taxes, allocators, or endogenous coverage) in some cases outperform or substitute for costly punishment (Colombier et al., 2011; Lévy-Garboua et al., 2017; Brent et al., 2019).

- **Interaction with agreements**: Efficiency gains from punishment are greatest when accompanied by clear, ambitious group agreements on cooperation (Dannenberg, 2016).

## Disagreement and Ambiguity

- Some experiments indicate positive efficiency impacts of punishment primarily under enriched contexts (communication, clear norms), while others find little or negative impact when punishment is anonymous, misdirected, or costly (Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Punishment’s efficiency impact cannot be assumed to be universally positive**; it depends on a combination of the control (no punishment) efficiency and several game design dimensions:

  - **Expect the largest efficiency gains**:
    - In weakest-link or complementary production games, or when punishment is highly legitimate and group size is moderate (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Zheng & Nie, 2013).
    - When communication/chat is available (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Kamei, 2019).
    - When punishment power is egalitarian and stable, and information is relatively noise-free (Dorrough et al., 2017; Fischer et al., 2016).
    - When punishment is paired with a clear, ambitious cooperation agreement (Dannenberg, 2016).

  - **Expect negligible or negative efficiency effects**:
    - In linear PGGs with cultures/contexts prone to antisocial or excessive punishment (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Rockenbach & Wolff, 2016).
    - With anonymous punishment in large groups (Zheng & Nie, 2013).
    - Under high noise, uncertainty, or when punishment power is highly unequal and unstable (Fischer et al., 2016; Gallo et al., 2022).
    - In nonlinear/CPR environments, unless accompanied by well-designed communication or close monitoring (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; Shreedhar et al., 2020).

  - **Control efficiency is a strong baseline predictor**, but the design dimensions above must be used to adjust upward or downward.

  - **Direct quantitative mappings** are available from experiments where control and treatment (punishment) efficiencies are reported across conditions for key dimensions: group size, production function, punishment cost/tech, anonymity, and communication (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Fatas & Mateu, 2015).

  - **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes** (contribution, cooperation rate) can suggest potential for efficiency changes, but cannot be substituted for payoff-based measures.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed**:  
- `player_count`: Many studies provide evidence across small (2–4) to large (>8) groups; group size is a robust moderator (Zheng & Nie, 2013; Harrell & Wolff, 2023).
- `num_rounds`: Present in nearly all studies; both one-shot and repeated games inform predictions about decay or sustainability of effects.
- `chat`: Strong moderator; communication increases efficiency gains from punishment (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Kamei, 2019).
- `mpcr`: Almost universally specified; higher MPCR often leads to more cooperation, but interacts with punishment effects.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Cost/effectiveness is directly manipulated in several papers (Fischer et al., 2016; Nicklisch & Wolff, 2011; Zheng & Nie, 2013).
- `all_or_nothing`: Binary or continuous contributions studied; some evidence of moderation by contribution framing (Park et al., 2013; Karakostas et al., 2023).
- `show_n_rounds`: Less commonly varied, but specified in many designs.
- `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Incorporated in studies differentiating punishment institution and information structure (Zheng & Nie, 2013).
- `punishment_tech` (central vs. peer, stable vs. unstable): Highly consequential (Fischer et al., 2016; Dorrough et al., 2017).

**Indirectly/contextually discussed**:  
- `default_contrib`: Mentioned in framing contexts but seldom manipulated directly.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech`: A few studies allow for combined punishment/reward, but systematic evidence on their joint effect is limited (Colombier et al., 2011).
- `show_punishment_id`: Varied in identity vs. anonymous punishment studies.
  
**Sparse/limited evidence**:  
- Multi-dimensional combinations (e.g., simultaneous manipulation of chat, reward, and punishment) are rarely explored together.
- Dimensions like default contribution and some detailed information-availability manipulations are underreported.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Gaps in coverage of all 14 dimensions**: Most studies address only a subset, focusing on classic variables like punishment cost, group size, and communication, while leaving other features (e.g., default contribution framing, summary displays) relatively unexamined.

- **Over-representation of lab settings**: The high degree of control in experiments aids internal validity but may limit external generalizability, especially regarding real-world heterogeneity in institutional and cultural contexts.

- **Ambiguity in efficiency outcomes**: While many studies measure efficiency, a substantial proportion focus on behavioral correlates (e.g., contributions, norm compliance) that cannot be directly mapped to the prediction task.

- **Mechanism ambiguity/disagreement**: There are mixed findings regarding the specific circumstances under which punishment increases versus decreases efficiency, with divergent results in otherwise similar design spaces (e.g., punishment increases efficiency only with agreements or communication in Dannenberg (2016) and Andrighetto et al. (2016), but not otherwise).

- **No direct meta-regression**: Prediction of efficiency with punishment enabled from all design dimensions plus control efficiency is not presented as a predictive model in any paper, and pooled effect size estimates are not available.

- **Cultural and institutional moderators not always explicit**: Where antisocial punishment or legitimacy is critical (e.g., Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Zheng & Nie, 2013), broader applicability may be limited.

- **Non-linear and threshold variants underexplored**: The effect of punishment in nonlinear, threshold, or best-shot PGGs is only intermittently addressed.

- **Confounding of efficiency and contribution effects**: Many studies report on contribution increases that do not translate into efficiency gains due to high punishment costs, emphasizing that increased cooperation is not equivalent to increased earnings or welfare.

---

**In sum**, the literature provides substantial empirical evidence about punishment’s effects on group efficiency in public goods games, but the predictiveness is highest when the focal game closely matches well-studied contexts (linear/weakest-link PGGs, with explicit design dimensions on group size, punishment institution, communication, and information). The importance of production technology, group size, communication, legitimacy, and punishment cost/structure is repeatedly emphasized as necessary conditioning variables. However, significant gaps, ambiguities, and underexplored dimensions remain, especially regarding institutional combinations and external validity.
