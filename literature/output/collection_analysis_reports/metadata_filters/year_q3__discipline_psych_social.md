# 1) Evidence Base

The evidence base comprises a large, diverse set (167) of primarily empirical laboratory and field experiments, with substantial representation of theoretical modeling and comprehensive reviews. The papers include both standard linear public goods games (PGG) and various close or adjacent social dilemma paradigms, with a core subset directly addressing peer/centralized punishment and efficiency outcomes. Most papers capture standard design features (e.g., player count, rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.), and a significant portion directly report on payoff-based outcomes such as group efficiency, profit, or welfare. There is, however, considerable breadth in study designs, subject pools (lab, field, cross-cultural), and variants of cooperation, punishment, and norm enforcement. Although the base is broad and multi-disciplinary, direct high-quality evidence for the precise prediction task—predicting the average efficiency of a PGG with peer punishment enabled from design dimensions and control efficiency—is concentrated in a smaller, but still sizeable, subset of the literature.

---

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:  
- **Exact:** The core evidence set includes many standard PGGs and close variants (e.g., threshold/extraction PGGs, linear and all-or-nothing forms), with well-documented mapping to prediction-relevant dimensions.
- **Close/Adjacent:** Some studies use adjacent paradigms (e.g., CPR/claim games, generalized exchange, team production, one-shot PDs with punishment), providing indirect but useful insights into punishment and efficiency under related incentives.

**punishment_or_sanctions**:  
- **Exact:** Numerous papers experimentally manipulate the presence/absence, cost, and structure of punishment (peer, central, democratic) and clearly separate baseline (no punishment) from punishment-enabled conditions.
- **Close/Adjacent:** A substantial additional set addresses reward, exclusion, redistribution, refusal to interact, gossip/reputation, or third-party punishment; these offer adjacent evidence on norm enforcement and efficiency but are less directly translatable.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:  
- **Exact:** Several studies report group efficiency, welfare, profit, or total earnings as primary outcomes, suitable for direct prediction.
- **Close:** Some measure aggregate contributions, allocation, or 'success rate' (e.g., threshold achieved, contest won) as proxies for efficiency.
- **Adjacent/Weak:** Many report only behavioral outcomes (contribution rate, frequency of punishment), psychological constructs, or proxy measures (inequality, compliance), which do not map directly to efficiency but inform underlying mechanisms.

In sum, the set is **strongly relevant** for PGG, punishment, and efficiency, but with variation especially in how efficiency is operationalized and measured.

---

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-based Outcomes (Efficient for Prediction):**
  - Group efficiency (total payoff relative to full cooperation)
  - Group profit/welfare/aggregate earnings (Benard & Barclay, 2020; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Nockur et al., 2021; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021; Billinger & Rosenbaum, 2019)
  - Surplus, success probability (e.g., in threshold or contest games)
  - Payoff distribution/inequality (Hauser et al., 2021; Pansini et al., 2020)
- **Behavioral/Process Outcomes (Not Efficient for Direct Prediction):**
  - Contribution/cooperation rates (most common; frequently, but not always, correlated with efficiency)
  - Frequency/type of punishment, norm compliance, antisocial punishment
  - Norm perceptions, satisfaction, perceptions of fairness
- **Other Adjacent Outcomes:**
  - Psychological/neurological measures (e.g., trust, fairness perception, affect, neural correlates)
  - Reputation, group solidarity, or social network structure

Crucially, **only payoff-based outcomes** should be used for direct prediction of efficiency effects, while behavioral and psychological outcomes are useful only as moderators or mechanistic explanations.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Key Empirical Regularities:**
- **Punishment can increase efficiency, but not always.**  
  In standard, repeated **linear PGGs with moderate MPCR and costly peer punishment**, enabling punishment usually increases cooperation and sometimes group efficiency, especially when punishment is normatively constrained, democratic, or centralized (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Castillo & Hamman, 2021; Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021).
- **The cost of punishment is decisive.**  
  When punishment is **costly or inefficient** (low cost-effectiveness ratio), or when antisocial punishment is prevalent, the increased contributions may be entirely offset by punishment expenditures, leading to no net efficiency gain or even efficiency loss (Nockur et al., 2021; Vollan et al., 2019; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Tanimoto, 2018; Ahmad & Loch, 2020).
- **Design of punishment institutions matters strongly.**
  - **Democratic/centralized punishment** is more likely to increase efficiency or be neutral; peer punishment often lowers efficiency due to high costs from uncoordinated/antisocial punishment (Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Benard & Barclay, 2020).
  - **Feedback, identification, and salience** (i.e., being able to connect punishment to previous behavior, having full information) are necessary for punishment to increase efficiency (Waichman & Stenzel, 2019; Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021).
- **Social/cultural context and group composition moderate effect size.**  
  The magnitude and direction of the efficiency effect depends on prevalence of prosocial punishers (strong reciprocators), group/societal norms, and the rate of antisocial or norm-keeper punishment (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- **Baseline (control) efficiency is predictive, but not determinative.**
  - The efficiency gain from enabling punishment is largest when control efficiency is low (Nair et al., 2018), and may be negative or negligible where baseline efficiency is already high (Nair et al., 2018; Ahmad & Loch, 2020; Vollan et al., 2019).
  - In threshold and contest games, or extraction CPRs, punishment rarely restores full efficiency and can sometimes worsen payoffs if punishment costs dominate (Chávez et al., 2018; Vollan et al., 2019).

**Complications and Mixed Results:**
- **Antisocial punishment and retaliation** can eliminate or reverse efficiency gains (Vollan et al., 2019; Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018).
- **Punishment structure**, such as the ability to target, coordinate, or immunize certain players, creates heterogeneity in effectiveness (Gordon & Puurtinen, 2021; Kol'veková et al., 2021).
- **Short horizon/small groups**: With few rounds and small player count, the positive efficiency effects are often muted or negative (Nockur et al., 2021; Ahmad & Loch, 2020).
- **Peer punishment often fails to improve efficiency, unless designed to minimize antisocial costs or to coordinate sanctioning** (Pfattheicher et al., 2018; Nockur et al., 2021).

**Additional Moderators:**
- **Endowment symmetry, transparency, and feedback on contributions** (Hauser et al., 2021)
- **Cost/impact ratio (punishment technology)** (Tanimoto, 2018; Nockur et al., 2021; Kol'veková et al., 2021)
- **Institutional legitimacy and group selection** (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Gavrilets & Shrestha, 2021)
- **Reward mechanisms** may complement or substitute for punishment, but effect sizes and efficiency gains are context-dependent (Hou et al., 2019; Kol'veková et al., 2021)

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**General Guidance:**
- **Do not assume punishment always increases efficiency**: The net effect of punishment depends on its **cost-effectiveness**, the potential for antisocial use, and the structure of the institution.
- **If predicting in standard linear PGGs with moderate group size (3-5), sufficient rounds (6+), and peer punishment with realistic (Fehr-Gaechter style) costs, expect:**
  - **Efficient or low-cost, prosocial, well-targeted punishment** (especially if democratic or centralized) to typically increase or at least not reduce efficiency compared to control. The effect is strongest when baseline efficiency is low (Suleiman & Samid, 2021; Kol'veková et al., 2021; Castillo et al., 2021).
  - **Uncoordinated or antisocial punishment, or high costs per unit impact,** to result in little or no gain, or even a drop in efficiency (Nockur et al., 2021; Vollan et al., 2019).
- **For threshold/CPR games and settings with high potential for retaliation or antisocial punishment**, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency unless mechanisms exist to mitigate these (Vollan et al., 2019; Tanimoto, 2018).
- **When baseline efficiency (no punishment) is high, enabling punishment offers little gain and may reduce efficiency** (Nair et al., 2018; Ahmad & Loch, 2020).
- **Democratic, centralized, and feedback-linked punishment systems outperform decentralized/peer punishment** unless the latter is explicitly prosocial and cost-effective (Nockur et al., 2021; Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
- **Moderators to account for**: Cost-to-impact ratio, information and feedback (ability to link punishment to prior behavior), group composition, endowment symmetry, ability to immunize/exclude certain players, presence of partner competition, and social context (e.g., cultural prevalence of norm-keepers vs. reciprocators).

**Dimension Mapping:**
- **Baseline (control) efficiency** is a valuable predictor, but adjustments must be made for cost of punishment, antisocial punishment prevalence, and institution structure.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed design dimensions** (many papers report or manipulate these, often with efficiency as outcome):
- **player_count**: Strong evidence (most studies use 3–5 players, but some scale to higher counts).
- **num_rounds**: Strong evidence (e.g., longer repeated games show larger efficiency changes due to sustained cooperation/punishment).
- **mpcr**: Well represented. Effects of marginal return on efficiency gain are frequently analyzed.
- **punishment_cost**/**punishment_tech** (cost per point, impact ratio): Directly reported and shown to moderate efficiency impact.
- **punishment_exists** (crucial treatment variable)
- **reward_exists** (in some designs; often compared as separate arms or combined with punishment)
- **all_or_nothing/continuous_contrib**: Clear in most studies; threshold vs. linear forms analyzed.
- **chat**/**communication**: Some studies show communication increases efficiency more than punishment (Ertör-Akyazi, 2019).
- **show_other_summaries/show_n_rounds**: Regularly described; visibility of contributions/punishment outcomes shown to affect prosocial norm enforcement and thus efficiency.
- **show_punishment_id**: Sometimes manipulated; anonymity vs. transparency effects noted.

**Indirectly/contextually discussed or occasionally reported:**
- **default_contrib** (framing, opt-in/opt-out): Seldom a primary focus but some mention.
- **reward_cost/reward_tech**: When rewards included, usually as fully specified additional arms.
- **show_punishment_id**: A moderator in some studies (anonymity of punisher/rewarder).

**Sparse or effectively missing:**
- No robust evidence for settings with **extremely large group sizes**, rare or irregular punishment opportunities, highly dynamic networked play, or simultaneous manipulation of multiple less-reported dimensions (e.g., default_contrib, rare reward structures).

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Antisocial punishment is a critical confounder:** Some environments with high rates of antisocial punishment or norm-keeper behavior experience efficiency drops or no improvement, despite higher average cooperation (Fehr & Schurtenberger, 2018; Vollan et al., 2019; Pleasant & Barclay, 2018).
- **Generalization beyond lab settings is risky:** Several reviews and field experiments find that lab-generated efficiency gains from punishment may not translate outside, particularly where social ties, social distance, or real-world retaliatory risks differ (Pedersen et al., 2020; Naar, 2020).
- **Behavioral outcome≠Efficiency:** Many studies (especially those focusing on cooperation rates, norm compliance, or punishment assigned) do not actually report efficiency or net group payoff; mapping contribution rate to efficiency can be misleading if punishment expenditures are high.
- **Heterogeneity across game types:** Results from standard PGGs may not translate to threshold, competitive, or CPR versions; in some cases punishment reduces efficiency in CPRs or competitive settings.
- **Interaction effects:**
  - Other design dimensions such as **chat**, **reward**, or **leader selection mechanisms** interact with punishment in non-additive ways.
  - Control efficiency is not always a sufficient predictor; context- and design-specific moderators must be considered.
- **Complexity of psychological/cultural moderators:** Strong variation in participant populations and cultural context makes prediction from lab findings uncertain for field or cross-cultural environments.
- **Sparse evidence on rarely varied dimensions:** For some design features (default contribution, reward cost, etc.), direct evidence is minimal, meaning predictions require extrapolation or must ignore these factors.
- **Temporal effects:** Short games may not realize positive efficiency effects due to insufficient time for cooperation to recover initial losses from punishment cycles; some treatments only become more efficient over time (Pfattheicher et al., 2018).
- **Limited coverage of dynamic network/reputation structures**: Very few studies address fully dynamic, endogenous network settings or fine-grained reputation-feedback loops.

---

**In conclusion:**  
While the literature is rich and provides solid ground for predicting the average efficiency effect of punishment in PGG-like environments as a function of key game design dimensions and control efficiency, successful prediction requires careful attention to institutional structure, punishment cost/tech, baseline efficiency, and known moderators like antisocial punishment and feedback salience. Where behavioral outcomes replace efficiency as the measured variable, results must be interpreted with caution and not treated as direct evidence for efficiency effects. The crucial missing areas are under-explored design dimensions, non-lab settings, and environments with non-standard group compositions or sanctioning technologies.
