# 1) Evidence Base

The supplied literature review contains a mix of **empirical (primarily lab experiments)** and **theoretical** papers, with the strongest representation by controlled laboratory experiments on repeated public goods games (PGGs). The evidence base is **broad and detailed** for the core prediction task of public goods games with and without punishment; adjacent literatures (e.g., trust games, dictator games, volunteer’s dilemmas, evolutionary models) are present but peripheral. The reviews also incorporate meta-analyses, cross-cultural studies, field experiments, and agent-based simulations. Overall, the papers provide a rich set of direct empirical findings, strong theoretical mechanisms, and a range of contextually adjacent or weaker sources. The coverage of payoff and efficiency outcomes in standard PGGs is **substantial**, though with notable gaps for some nuanced design dimensions.

# 2) Task Relevance

### a. **pgg_or_variant**  
- **Relevance:** Mostly **exact** – the majority of studies directly analyze standard or close variants of public goods games.
- Several papers extend to common-pool resource games and team production (close); a minority address only adjacent social dilemmas (adjacent), while field or naturalistic studies are often less precise with respect to experimental design.

### b. **punishment_or_sanctions**  
- **Relevance:** Largely **exact** – most studies carefully manipulate the presence/absence of punishment or sanctions (peer, centralized, exogenous, etc.).
- Some papers address adjacent concepts (e.g., ostracism, exclusion, or reward as alternatives to punishment), and a few use punishment mechanisms only peripherally.

### c. **efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**  
- **Relevance:** Core set provides **exact** or **close** efficiency outcomes (group earnings, welfare, surplus, etc.).
- Many studies, however, report only **adjacent** behavioral outcomes (contributions, cooperation rate, punishment frequency) without direct calculation of efficiency; some are purely behavioral.
- Efficiency outcomes are particularly well-covered in studies that vary punishment cost, technology, or institutional structure.

**Summary:** The literature is highly relevant to the core prediction task, with strongest coverage for PGGs with peer or institutional punishment, and for efficiency and related payoff outcomes.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff/Efficiency Related:**  
    - **Direct measures:** Group earnings (absolute or as % of social optimum), net payoff, welfare, surplus (e.g., Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Wang & Qin, 2015; Gächter et al., 2017; Harrell & Simpson, 2016).
    - **Indirect measures:** Some studies report contributions or public good level as proxies for efficiency.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral:**  
    - **Most common:** Contribution rates, frequency/intensity of punishment, cooperation decay, punishment assignment patterns, trust, reputation, ostracism, norm compliance.
    - **Usefulness:** While informative for mechanisms/moderators, behavioral outcomes are **not direct measures of efficiency** and cannot always be mapped to payoff improvements (explicitly noted as a limitation in several digests).
    - **Mechanisms:** Papers frequently discuss how punishment changes behaviors that ultimately (but not always) impact group payoffs.

- **Hybrid or Mechanism:**  
    - Studies combining efficiency with detailed behavioral process analysis provide the richest support for prediction (e.g., Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Fatas & Mateu, 2015).

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

## a. **General Effect of Punishment on Efficiency**
- **Positive Effects (Most Cases):**
  - **Standard lab PGGs**: Enabling peer or exogenous monetary punishment typically **increases group efficiency** relative to no-punishment baselines—often dramatically (Gächter et al., 2017; Wang & Qin, 2015; Qin & Wang, 2013; Andrighetto et al., 2016).
  - **Centralization, Cost, and Legitimacy:** Efficiency is **maximized when punishment is costly and/or centralized** (designated punisher, especially if prosocial/legitimate) and minimized when punishment is too cheap, uncoordinated, or allows excessive retaliation (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Grieco et al., 2017).

- **Negative/Null or Mixed Effects:**
  - **Costs of Punishment:** Sometimes, group losses from punishment expenditures, antisocial or retaliatory punishment outweigh cooperation gains—so enabling punishment **does not necessarily improve efficiency**, especially in peer-punishment regimes (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Fischer et al., 2016; Dorrough et al., 2017).
  - **Production Function Sensitivity:** Efficiency gains from punishment are **highly dependent on the PGG technology**; e.g., in weakest-link (complementary) games, punishment is more efficient than in linear games, where antisocial punishment is more problematic (Fatas & Mateu, 2015).
  - **Information and Communication:** **Communication** often **exceeds or complements** punishment in improving efficiency (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016).
  - **Structure and Context:** Formal institutional punishment (tax, centralized enforcement) usually provides larger efficiency gains than informal punishment; combinations can be complementary but are not always additive in efficiency (Cason & Gangadharan, 2013).

## b. **Moderator Effects and Design Dimension Insights**
- **Player Count / Group Size:** The effect of punishment can depend on group size; smaller groups generally show more positive efficiency effects, while large or heterogeneous groups may see diminished or negative effects (Boyd et al., 2014; Parks et al., 2013).
- **Rounds / Game Length:** Longer games with repeated interaction strengthen the positive effect of punishment on efficiency; short, one-shot, or few-round games yield weaker or negative efficiency effects (Kroupa, 2014; Kurzban et al., 2015; Barclay & Raihani, 2016).
- **Punishment Cost/Tech:** Punishment needs to be costly enough to signal legitimacy but not so costly it destroys value; **costless punishment often leads to excessive or antisocial punishment and reduced efficiency** (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Centralization/Institutional Structure:** Efficiency is higher with centralized, designated, or reputation-based punishment (often with prosocial leadership); peer punishment is less efficient, especially with opportunities for retaliation or lack of group coordination (Harrell & Simpson, 2016; Grieco et al., 2017; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
- **Antisocial and Counter-Punishment:** Antisocial punishment (punishing cooperators) and counter-punishment (retaliation) frequently **reduce or eliminate efficiency gains** from enabling punishment (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015; Dorrough et al., 2017).
- **MPCR (Marginal Per Capita Return):** The positive effect of punishment is largest when MPCR is moderate to low and baseline cooperation (control efficiency) is fragile (Farjam et al., 2015).
- **Communication and Feedback:** Enabling communication (chat, messages) either in addition to or instead of punishment typically produces very high efficiency, often making punishment non-essential (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013; Cason & Gangadharan, 2016). Feedback on others’ contributions can facilitate targeted punishment for maximum efficiency.
- **Information Environment:** Inefficiency arises when feedback is noisy, monitoring is costly/imperfect, or punishment power is unequal/unstable (Fischer et al., 2016; Goeschl & Jarke, 2016; Dorrough et al., 2017).

## c. **Special Cases and Cautions**
- **Nonlinear, Common-Pool, or Threshold Games:** Effects of punishment on efficiency can be absent or reversed in **nonlinear** or more complex resource games, or when the action space allows taking/undoing others' cooperation (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; van Soest et al., 2016; Freytag et al., 2014).
- **Adjacency and Exclusion:** Sanctioning mechanisms other than direct monetary punishment (ostracism, exclusion, gossip) can sometimes achieve high efficiency (Feinberg et al., 2014; Sääksvuori, 2014).
- **Culture and Context:** Efficiency results vary by country, trust level, and local social norms—especially regarding antisocial punishment (Balliet & Van Lange, 2013; Sylwester et al., 2013).
- **Reward availability:** Sometimes, offering both reward and punishment has ambiguous or negative effects on efficiency if reward isn’t targeted or is subject to second-order free-rider problems (Choi & Ahn, 2013; Rand & Nowak, 2013; Antoci & Zarri, 2015).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **Strongest Evidence for Prediction:**  
  - **In standard repeated lab PGGs with moderate group size (3–5), moderate-to-high MPCR (0.4–0.5), and no communication, enabling peer or institutional punishment almost always increases efficiency relative to control, especially if the baseline (control) efficiency is low** (Gächter et al., 2017; Kuwabara & Yu, 2017; Wang & Qin, 2015; Qin & Wang, 2013; Harrell & Simpson, 2016).
  - **Control Efficiency is a good predictor of how much efficiency gain punishment will yield:** The lower the control efficiency, the more room for improvement; high baseline efficiency implies smaller marginal gains from punishment (Gächter et al., 2017; Asgharpourmasouleh et al., 2017).
- **Key Moderators for Downstream Prediction:**
    - **Punishment Structure:**
      - **Peer-punishment**: Likely to build efficiency if punishment is neither too cheap nor too costly, retaliation/antisocial punishment is constrained, and feedback is transparent and reliable.
      - **Centralized/Designated Punisher**: Maximizes efficiency if legitimacy is clear and the punisher is prosocial or selected by performance (Grieco et al., 2017; Harrell & Simpson, 2016).
      - **Multiple or repeated stages, full transparency/IDs:** May invite counter-punishment feuds, eroding efficiency (Engelmann & Nikiforakis, 2015).
    - **Punishment Cost**:
      - **Costless or too-cheap punishment** can cause overuse, reducing efficiency (Kuwabara & Yu, 2017).
      - **High-cost punishment** dampens use, may undermine its deterrent effect unless offset by high effectiveness.
    - **Communication**:
      - **Enabling chat/communication** produces very high efficiency, often exceeding what punishment alone achieves (Andrighetto et al., 2016; Eisenkopf & Bächtiger, 2013).
    - **Game Structure**:
      - **Weakest-link (complementary) or threshold PGGs:** Punishment much more effective at increasing efficiency (Fatas & Mateu, 2015; Freytag et al., 2014).
      - **Nonlinear/common pool resource environments:** Punishment has little or negative effect on efficiency (Cason & Gangadharan, 2016; van Soest et al., 2016).
    - **Information Environment**:
      - **Perfect info, free monitoring:** Best for efficiency improvement (Goeschl & Jarke, 2016).
      - **High noise, costly monitoring, unstable power:** Adds risk of inefficiency or even negative returns to punishment (Fischer et al., 2016; Dorrough et al., 2017).
- **Predictive Use of Control Efficiency**:
    - **If control efficiency is low, enabling punishment in a well-designed peer or centralized regime with sufficient cost, effective targeting, and no strong antisocial punishment typically yields a big positive impact on efficiency.**
    - **If control efficiency is already high, or the main risks above are not well-controlled, marginal benefit of enabling punishment is small or even negative.**
- **Limitations:**
    - **Behavioral proxies** (contribution, cooperation) are commonly but not always linearly related to efficiency—always check if improved cooperation also outpaces the cost of punishment to yield real efficiency gains.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

### **Directly Informed Dimensions**
- **player_count:** Strong evidence on small N; moderate coverage of group size variation.
- **num_rounds:** Well-covered; longer duration usually improves gains from punishment.
- **mpcr**: Well-documented; lower MPCRs render punishment more valuable.
- **punishment_cost & punishment_tech:** Central to most results; high predictive value.
- **all_or_nothing:** Present in some studies; all-or-nothing mechanisms can change punishment's effect (e.g., weakest-link variants).
- **chat:** Frequently included as a moderator or treatment; major effect on efficiency.
- **show_other_summaries (feedback), show_n_rounds:** Regularly manipulated; full feedback facilitates targeting, sustaining high efficiency.

### **Indirectly Informed / Contextually Discussed**
- **default_contrib:** Infrequently explicitly stated, but some studies mention opt-in/opt-out framing or similar manipulations.
- **reward_exists/reward_cost/reward_tech:** Occasionally included as comparators or in combination with punishment.
- **show_punishment_id:** Studied in relation to anti/retaliatory punishment and reputation effects; less frequently isolated.

### **Effectively Missing/Sparse**
- **player heterogeneity, cultural context**: Sometimes discussed or controlled for, but rarely the main dimension in these designs.
- **Fine details of punishment technology (e.g., magnitude ratios; nonlinear punishment costs):** Less thoroughly explored except in a few focused studies.
- **Network structure, endogenous group formation:** Present in adjacent/agent-based work but less so in lab PGGs.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Payoff vs. Behavioral Outcomes**: Many studies infer efficiency from contributions; however, efficiency gains are not guaranteed if punishment costs exceed benefit or antisocial punishment is common.
- **Generality to Non-Lab Settings**: Many field and natural environment studies suggest that the positive efficiency effects of punishment found in lab PGGs may not generalize to more complex or realistic settings.
- **Parameter Gaps**: Some design dimensions, notably fine-grained punishment technology, player heterogeneity, and the precise form of communication, are underexplored or inconsistently reported, limiting high-fidelity prediction across arbitrary designs.
- **Antisocial and Counter-Punishment**: High rates of counter-punishment and antisocial punishment in some cultures or designs can completely eliminate or reverse efficiency gains from enabling punishment.
- **Overlapping Mechanisms**: When both punishment and communication/reputation-based solutions are present, their effects are often not additive and may even interact negatively.
- **Non-linear and Threshold Game Structures**: Results from standard linear PGGs may not transfer predictively to threshold or nonlinear resource games.
- **Control Efficiency Ceiling Effects:** If control efficiency is already near the cooperative optimum (e.g., due to framing, prior history, or a cooperative participant pool), enabling punishment may yield no improvement or might even hurt efficiency.

---

**Summary**:  
**Prediction of treatment efficiency from game design and control efficiency is well-supported by laboratory PGG evidence, particularly with respect to punishment cost, institutional structure, communication, group size, and baseline (control) efficiency.** However, careful attention must be paid to moderators and limitations, especially in cases of peer punishment, potential for antisocial use of punishment, and whether communication or non-monetary sanctions are present. Direct, payoff-based efficiency evidence should be prioritized over inferential evidence from contribution or cooperation rates. The literature base provides detailed, actionable gradients for prediction under most standard designs but has important gaps and sources of uncertainty for more exotic, hybrid, or real-world-mimicking cases.
