# 1) Evidence Base

This literature set comprises 23 papers with a mix of theoretical models (e.g., Eldakar et al., 2013; Sasaki & Uchida, 2014), laboratory experiments (e.g., Bone et al., 2014; dos Santos et al., 2013), field/lab experiments (e.g., Javaid & Falk, 2015; Gelcich et al., 2013), and a few observational or conceptual/theoretical reviews. The set spans a variety of domains—public goods games (PGG), common-pool resource (CPR) games, trust games, helping games, and real-world observations. However, the core prediction task—estimating the effect of peer punishment on efficiency in PGG-like environments based on design parameters—is addressed fully by only a subset of these papers. Some focus on mechanism or psychology, others on evolutionary or institutional context, with direct empirical reporting of efficiency outcomes in public goods or closely related games in a minority of cases.

Overall, the evidence base is:
- **Empirical papers:** Several laboratory/field experiments with variable relevance to efficiency in PGGs (e.g., Javaid & Falk, 2015; Gelcich et al., 2013; dos Santos et al., 2013).
- **Theory papers:** A significant number offer direct modeling of efficiency under punishment (e.g., Eldakar et al., 2013; Sasaki & Uchida, 2014), but some model adjacent domains.
- **Breadth:** The set is relatively broad and conceptually rich, but narrow in terms of high-relevance, directly empirical PGG efficiency outcomes with explicit punishment manipulations.

# 2) Task Relevance

**a. PGG or Variant**
- **exact:** A handful directly model or study standard PGGs (Eldakar et al., 2013; Bone et al., 2014; Broom et al., 2015).
- **close:** Several focus on CPR, helping, or reputation games with similar structure and incentives (Javaid & Falk, 2015; Gelcich et al., 2013; dos Santos et al., 2013; Perez et al., 2015).
- **adjacent/weak:** Many discuss cooperation or punishment in related paradigms—ultimatum games, trust games, biological systems, or real-world environments—without direct mapping to PGG structures (Güney & Newell, 2013; Raihani & Bshary, 2015; Boehm, 2014; Leighton, 2014; Wismer et al., 2014).

**b. Punishment or Sanctions**
- **exact:** Several studies explicitly manipulate or isolate punishment/sanction mechanisms and their costs (Eldakar et al., 2013; Javaid & Falk, 2015; Bone et al., 2014; Declerck et al., 2013).
- **adjacent:** Some address enforcement or exclusion mechanisms conceptually or observe informal enforcement behaviors (Perez et al., 2015; Sasaki et al., 2016).
- **none/weak:** A few papers focus exclusively on cooperation or reward, with minimal or theoretical treatment of punishment (Sasaki & Uchida, 2014; Smaldino et al., 2013).

**c. Efficiency or Related Payoff Outcome**
- **exact:** Only a subset report group efficiency, earnings, or other direct payoff metrics (Eldakar et al., 2013; Javaid & Falk, 2015; Gelcich et al., 2013; dos Santos et al., 2013).
- **close/adjacent:** Several focus on behavioral measures with partial payoff implications, or claim efficiency effects without quantitative output (dos Santos et al., 2013; dos Santos et al., 2014).
- **none/weak:** Many report only cooperation rates, punishment frequencies, or qualitative/cognitive/neural outcomes, not group efficiency (Bone et al., 2014; Güney & Newell, 2013; Du & Chang, 2015; Boehm, 2014).

**Summary:** Task relevance is high for a minority of the papers (particularly some theory and select empirical studies), but for the majority, the connection to the prediction task is indirect (adjacent) or contextually limited.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-Related Outcomes (Relevant for Prediction)**
- **Direct efficiency metrics:** A few papers report group efficiency (as percent of social optimum), group payoffs, or welfare (Eldakar et al., 2013; Javaid & Falk, 2015; Gelcich et al., 2013; dos Santos et al., 2013).
- **Aggregate group earnings:** Similarly, some studies use total group earnings, which are functionally equivalent for prediction (Javaid & Falk, 2015; dos Santos et al., 2013).
- **Indirect efficiency link:** Some theory papers model conditions under which punishment enhances or reduces group productivity or long-run payoff (Eldakar et al., 2013; Sasaki et al., 2016).

**Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**
- **Contribution or cooperation rates:** Frequent in experimental papers (Bone et al., 2014; Inglis et al., 2014) but not equivalent to efficiency for prediction.
- **Punishment/reward frequency:** Commonly reported (Konishi & Ohtsubo, 2015; Bone et al., 2014), sometimes with cost data but insufficient for direct efficiency inference.
- **Norm compliance, fairness, preference expressions:** These outcomes (Güney & Newell, 2013; Boehm, 2014; Du & Chang, 2015) are valuable for mechanistic understanding but not directly translatable to predictions of payoffs or efficiency.

The literature often conflates increased cooperation with higher efficiency, but this is not always accurate, especially where punishment is costly or has coordination side effects.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesized Findings (Empirical and Theoretical):**
- **Punishment generally increases efficiency when…**
  - Group size is small, punishment costs are low, and the game is repeated over many rounds (Eldakar et al., 2013).
  - Baseline (control) efficiency is low, so there is potential for gains from increased cooperation, and social information/feedback is not already strong (Gelcich et al., 2013; dos Santos et al., 2013).
  - Punishment is backed by reputation or is observable, enhancing its deterrent power (dos Santos et al., 2013).

- **Punishment does NOT always increase efficiency:**
  - In environments with already high baseline efficiency (near the social optimum), adding punishment can reduce efficiency—possibly due to coordination problems, misapplication, or wasted resources (Javaid & Falk, 2015; dos Santos et al., 2014).
  - Cognitive load or complex informational environments can undermine the efficiency gains from punishment; the beneficial effects depend on players’ cognitive capacity and the structure of the social information (dos Santos et al., 2014).
  - The structure and linkage of punishment with cooperation are critical—if punishment is not tightly linked to cooperative behavior (as in some biological or reputation-based systems), it may fail to improve or even worsen efficiency (Inglis et al., 2014).

- **Efficiency gains from reward mechanisms:** Theory suggests that under certain conditions, voluntary rewards can be as or more effective than punishment for increasing group efficiency, especially in large/threshhold-based groups (Sasaki & Uchida, 2014).

- **Design features moderate punishment's effect:**
  - Lower punishment costs and higher marginal per capita returns (MPCR) increase the effectiveness of punishment in raising efficiency (Eldakar et al., 2013).
  - Social context (group norms, capital, or cohesion) strongly moderates effectiveness of sanctions (Gelcich et al., 2013).

- **Absence of punishment, or the presence of only informal enforcement roles, does not guarantee low efficiency**; in some contexts, informal enforcement (via chat or emergent roles) is either unnecessary or symptomatic of preexisting conflict, not a driver of group success (Perez et al., 2015).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

For the specific task of predicting efficiency in peer punishment-enabled public-goods-game-like environments given game design dimensions plus control (no-punishment) efficiency, the following guidance emerges:

- **Punishment's effect is context-dependent.**
  - **When control efficiency is high** and/or social information is strong and baseline cooperation is near its maximum, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency, due to excessive or poorly targeted sanctions, coordination failure, or resource wastage (Javaid & Falk, 2015).
  - **When control efficiency is low** and there is room for improved cooperation, introducing punishment (especially with low costs and repeated interaction) often increases efficiency substantially (Eldakar et al., 2013; Gelcich et al., 2013).
  - **Punishment design matters:** The cost to punishers, whether punishment is peer vs. institutional, the observability of punishing actions/reputation, and the tightness of the link between punishment and cooperation all substantially impact efficiency outcomes (Eldakar et al., 2013; dos Santos et al., 2013; Inglis et al., 2014).
  - **Moderators such as group size, number of rounds, and MPCR** have empirically supported and theoretically justified effects on whether punishment will generate a net efficiency gain (Eldakar et al., 2013; Gelcich et al., 2013).

- **Prediction should not treat all punishment as efficiency-improving.** One must take the baseline efficiency (control game outcome) and design specifics—especially punishment cost, group size, number of rounds, and presence of strong social/reputational information—into account.

- **Evidence on cognitive/psychological moderators** suggests that environmental complexity and information overload can undermine expected gains from punishment and may even lead to counterproductive outcomes (dos Santos et al., 2014).

- **When applying this literature to make predictions:**
  - Use control efficiency as a crucial anchor: higher control efficiency leaves less room for improvement, and sometimes even means punishment reduces efficiency.
  - Factor in group size, punishment cost, number of rounds, and information structure as key moderators with both empirical and theoretical backing.
  - Be cautious in applying findings from non-PGG or single-shot games to repeated PGG settings unless the mapping is clearly justified in the paper.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions (with explicit empirical or theoretical conclusions):**
- `player_count`: Tested/theorized often—small group size frequently supports more effective punishment (Eldakar et al., 2013).
- `num_rounds`: Longer games favor greater benefit from punishment (Eldakar et al., 2013; Gelcich et al., 2013).
- `punishment_cost`: Lower costs support better efficiency returns (Eldakar et al., 2013; dos Santos et al., 2013).
- `mpcr`: Higher rates favor greater efficiency gains through punishment (Eldakar et al., 2013; Gelcich et al., 2013).
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`: Considered primarily in contrast to punishment and for adjacent reward predictions (Sasaki & Uchida, 2014).
- `show_other_summaries`: Used indirectly in studies involving public feedback or social information (Javaid & Falk, 2015; Gelcich et al., 2013).

**Indirectly or contextually discussed:**
- `all_or_nothing`: Present in several adjacent studies but with minimal isolated analysis of its effect.
- `chat`: Discussed more as a moderator (Perez et al., 2015) than as an isolated variable.
- `default_contrib`: Some mention in the context of framing but not directly tested for efficiency impact (Gelcich et al., 2013).
- `punishment_tech`: Mode of punishment (peer vs. institutional; peer-directed vs. probabilistic) differentiated in some theoretical works (Sasaki et al., 2016) and field-lab experiments.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_punishment_id`: Occasionally present, mainly in laboratory framing or real-world observation, with no direct test of their impact.
- `show_other_summaries`: Social feedback conditions are critical in some studies (Javaid & Falk, 2015).

**Sparsely informed or missing:**
- `reward_tech`, `reward_cost`: Only explicitly examined in contrast to punishment and mostly in theory papers (Sasaki & Uchida, 2014).
- `show_punishment_id`: Rarely isolated as a treatment; some relevance in studies of reputation and visibility (dos Santos et al., 2013).
- Other nuanced institutional or interface features are not systematically varied in most studies.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Limited focus on efficiency outcomes:** Many experimental and theoretical papers report on cooperation rates, punishment frequency, or norm compliance, but not on efficiency or aggregate payoffs—the outcome critical for prediction. This weakens the evidence base for payoff-focused prediction.
- **Sparse dimension-specific testing:** Not all 14 prediction dimensions are systematically manipulated or analyzed, and for several dimensions, effects on efficiency are only inferred or contextually discussed.
- **Varied punishment and game structures:** Studies use differing operationalizations of punishment (peer vs. third-party, institutional vs. probabilistic, with or without reputation), limiting direct comparability across results.
- **Mapping from adjacent domains is nontrivial:** Many findings come from games or natural systems only broadly analogous to PGGs (e.g., CPR games, trust games, reputation games, biological cooperation), requiring caution when transferring results.
- **Potential confounds and moderation:** The efficiency effect of punishment is highly sensitive to control efficiency, cognitive load, social context, and the presence or absence of communication or reputation. These are not always controlled or reported, complicating direct prediction.
- **Ambiguity and heterogeneity:** Some studies show negative or null effects of punishment (especially where baseline efficiency is high or in presence of information overload); others show strong positive effects under favorable conditions—making generalization difficult.
- **Lack of out-of-sample validation:** There is minimal evidence on how robust findings are to modest changes in game parameters or in different populations (lab vs. field vs. real-world).

----

**In summary:** The literature provides moderate, but highly context-dependent, empirical and theoretical support for predicting efficiency effects of punishment in public-goods-game-like environments. Strongest guidance comes from studies directly manipulating punishment and measuring efficiency under varying game parameters. However, many design dimensions are only indirectly informed, and the set's reliance on adjacent domains and non-payoff outcomes limits precision. Prediction should thus be anchored on studies with exact or close relevance, with appropriate caution about scope and generalizability.
