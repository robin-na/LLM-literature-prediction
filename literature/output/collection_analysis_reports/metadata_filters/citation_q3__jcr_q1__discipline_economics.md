# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 50 papers, containing a substantial mix of **highly relevant, empirical lab and field experiments** as well as **theoretical/theoretical-empirical papers**. The set covers a **broad range of designs**: canonical linear PGGs, repeated and one-shot games, public-bad variants, common-pool resource dilemmas, threshold games, and institutional or real-world analogues. **Most payoff-relevant papers are experimental lab studies** with direct evidence on group efficiency or related payoff outcomes, particularly under peer or centralized punishment. Several theory papers offer formal predictions about efficiency based on game design or institutional features, often via comparative statics or explicit equilibria.

However, there is substantial variability in reported outcomes: many papers (especially those from adjacent literatures or field experiments) emphasize **cooperation/contribution rates** or behavioral responses to punishment, rather than direct efficiency metrics. Several adjacent papers, particularly field or theory contributions, provide only indirect or contextual evidence for the core prediction task. Evidence is richest for standard PGGs with 3-5 players, lab environments, continuous contributions, and classical peer punishment; other dimensions (e.g., chat, show_punishment_id, non-anonymous punishment, show_other_summaries, field context) receive less concentrated attention.

**Summary:**
- **Empirical lab papers with direct PGG, punishment, and efficiency outcomes are present and strong.**
- **Empirical field, adjacent game types, and theory papers diversify mechanisms but dilute direct relevance.**
- **The evidence base is broad but deep only for certain core design conditions.**

# 2) Task Relevance

### a) `pgg_or_variant`
- **exact**: Numerous papers study canonical linear PGGs or very close variants (e.g., repeated contribution games with/without punishment: Dickinson et al., 2015; Nicklisch et al., 2016; Markussen et al., 2016; Kube et al., 2015; Dai et al., 2015).
- **close/adjacent**: Resource dilemma, threshold, or field analogues (Cason & Gangadharan, 2013; Chávez et al., 2018; Tarui et al., 2008) provide contextual breadth but depart in monitoring, structure, or incentives.
- **none/weak**: A minority of field and theory papers deal with settings fundamentally different from multi-player PGG dynamics.

### b) `punishment_or_sanctions`
- **exact/close**: Many core papers manipulate punishment or sanctions directly, either as endogenous peer punishment, centralized/third-party punishment, or formal rule-based sanctions. Some adjacent work uses exclusion, expulsion, or other deterrence, which aligns with sanctioning logic but differs in mechanism.
- **adjacent/weak**: Papers focusing on rewards, communication, or normative enforcement without punishment, as well as work on ostracism, reputation, or indirect social controls.
- **none**: Control-only studies or those focusing just on prosocial behavior without enforcement.

### c) `efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome`
- **exact**: Key empirical studies report group efficiency, group earnings, or total payoff as a ratio to maximum possible outcomes (Dickinson et al., 2015; Nicklisch et al., 2016; Markussen et al., 2016; Dai et al., 2015).
- **close/adjacent**: Many report only contributions or frequency of cooperation, requiring indirect mapping to efficiency; some only discuss payoffs in non-PGGs or through theoretical models.
- **weak/none**: Some report only behavioral/non-payoff outcomes or are explicit that efficiency/welfare is not measured.

**Overall:**  
The highest relevance is for empirical lab studies of PGGs with direct punishment manipulation and efficiency reporting. Several studies offer context or mechanism but with weaker direct payoff/efficiency outcome data.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-Related Outcomes**:  
    - *Efficiency*: Group payoff as a ratio to fully cooperative benchmark (Dickinson et al., 2015; Nicklisch et al., 2016; Markussen et al., 2016; Dai et al., 2015; Cason & Gangadharan, 2013; Bracht et al., 2008; Chávez et al., 2018).
    - *Group Earnings/Total Payoff/Surplus*: Often equivalent to or used as an operationalization of efficiency.
    - *Welfare*: Sometimes aggregated across group and normalized to cooperative or optimal level.

- **Non-Payoff Behavioral Outcomes**:  
    - *Contribution/Cooperation Rates*: Dominant in many adjacent papers; frequently correlated with efficiency but not identical.
    - *Punishment Frequency/Assigned Punishment*: Used to illuminate the mechanisms by which treatments might affect efficiency, but not efficiency themselves.
    - *Norm Compliance, Second-Order Punishment*: Discussed as compliance mechanisms or meta-norm supports.
    - *Free-Riding, Rule Breaking*: Sometimes mapped to payoff loss but not measured as efficiency per se.

- **Distinctions**:  
    - Where only contribution or punishment behavior is measured, **inferences about efficiency are indirect**—especially when punishment is costly, as higher contributions can (in rare cases) correspond to lower efficiency if punishment expenditures outweigh cooperation gains.
    - **True treatment efficiency data is available in a robust subset** of the most relevant studies.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

- **Punishment Generally Increases Efficiency in Standard PGGs**:  
    - **Strong and consistent positive effects** of enabling peer or centralized punishment on group efficiency relative to baseline, under information-rich, error-free, and deterrent punishment regimes (Dickinson et al., 2015; Kube et al., 2015; Dai et al., 2015).
    - **Magnitude is variable**, but robust direction: sanctions, when credible and deterrent, restore efficiency lost in control games due to free riding.

- **Efficiency Effects Are Moderated by Information, Cost, and Errors**:  
    - **Information environment**: Under perfect information, decentralized punishment produces greatest efficiency; with noise, both decentralized and centralized punishment weaken, with efficiency falling and possible misapplication of punishment (Nicklisch et al., 2016).
    - **Judicial error**: Type I errors (punishing cooperators) can make punishment treatments less efficient than no punishment; only error-free or low-error deterrent sanctions are reliably efficiency-enhancing (Markussen et al., 2016).
    - **Punishment cost and technology**: When cost to punishers is high relative to harm inflicted, efficiency gains are dampened (Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2020).

- **Rewards, Rules, and Communication**:  
    - **Rewards alone can increase efficiency**, sometimes less so than punishment; best results often from well-calibrated punishment-reward hybrids, not pure punishment (Dickinson et al., 2015; Bracht et al., 2008).
    - **Formal rules and institutions**: Strong, binding institutions with credible, deterrent punishment are highly effective; weak, non-deterrent, or ill-fitting rules (e.g., in heterogeneous or norm-mismatched groups) can have diminished or negative effects (Kube et al., 2015; Vollan et al., 2013; Dekel et al., 2017).
    - **Communication, chat, and transparency**: Open communication can independently raise efficiency or substitute for punishment, especially in homogeneous settings or smaller groups (Gangadharan et al., 2017). However, when punishment is present, communication may have little marginal efficiency effect (Leibbrandt & Sääksvuori, 2012).

- **Contextual and Population Moderators**:  
    - **Group size**: Small groups may sustain high efficiency gains with punishment; as group size increases, effects depend on other dimensions (Tarui et al., 2008; Saak, 2012; Bonroy et al., 2019).
    - **Baseline efficiency**: When control games already have high efficiency (due to social norms, trust, or context), punishment can provide little or even negative marginal gain (van der Weele, 2012).
    - **Cultural context and selection**: Cultural backgrounds, legitimacy, and selection into punishment regimes modulate cooperation and thus realized efficiency gains (Zhou et al., 2023; Vollan et al., 2017).

- **Possible Negative or Null Effects**:  
    - In environments where punishment is misapplied, poorly targeted, or supports norm conflict (e.g., harmed minorities, Cason & Gangadharan, 2013; Dekel et al., 2017), **enabling punishment can reduce efficiency** compared to no-punishment baseline.
    - Over-punishment or group-funded punishment can generate excessive social cost, reducing or reversing efficiency gains (Ouss & Peysakhovich, 2015).

- **Empirical-Theoretical Convergence**:  
    - Theory papers reinforce empirics: punishment's efficiency effects are strongest under credible deterrence, appropriate cost ratios, and group/member types that threaten baseline cooperation; crowding out, norm mismatches, and cost/externality structures can all reverse effects (van der Weele, 2012; Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2020).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

- **If the control (punishment-disabled) game has moderate to low efficiency**, enabling peer punishment under canonical lab settings is highly likely to raise efficiency—**sometimes dramatically**—as long as the punishment regime is credible, deterrent (i.e., high enough impact relative to cost), and misapplication of punishment is rare or minimal.

- **Prediction models should attend to:**
    - **Control group efficiency**: The lower the baseline, the more potential for improvement through punishment (van der Weele, 2012).
    - **Punishment regime properties**: Cost, magnitude, and whether punishment is peer/enforcer/centralized; high cost and weak impact reduce effect (Acemoglu & Wolitzky, 2020).
    - **Information environment**: Prediction is less certain if the environment involves noisy/mistaken identification of free riders (Nicklisch et al., 2016; Markussen et al., 2016).
    - **Group composition/heterogeneity**: Heterogeneous benefit/cost structures within the group, existence of harmed minorities, or high inequality aversion can blunt or reverse efficiency gains (Dekel et al., 2017; Gangadharan et al., 2017).
    - **Cultural/selection/context moderators**: Cultural fit, method of institution adoption (exogenous vs. endogenous), and the legitimacy of punishment mechanisms can all shift efficiency effects (Kube et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2023).

- **Predictions should be cautious when:**
    - Empirical data is limited to cooperation or contribution rates only (not actual payoffs/efficiency).
    - Punishment is likely to be applied to cooperators/type I error risk is high or when over-punishment is possible.
    - Reward, communication, or strong baseline pro-social context already achieves high efficiency.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed (measured, manipulated, or primary moderator):**
- `player_count`: Regularly manipulated/tested; strong evidence on group size as moderator.
- `num_rounds`: Most repeated-game studies cover from one-shot to 50+ rounds, allowing dynamic efficiency measures.
- `mpcr`: Systematically varied; central to calibrating efficiency and incentive compatibility.
- `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech`: Frequently manipulated; cost-to-magnitude ratios are critical in both experiment and theory.
- `all_or_nothing`: Both binary and continuous contribution games are present.
- `chat`/`communication`: Commonly manipulated or disabled; evidence on its independent and interactive effects.
- `reward_exists`/`reward_cost`/`reward_tech`: Discussed in several studies as alternative to or in combination with punishment.
- `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`: Occasionally varied or reported, especially in studies of transparency, anonymity, or monitoring structure.
- `punishment_exists`: Directly manipulated in all relevant papers.

**Indirectly Informed/Contextually Discussed:**
- `default_contrib`: Framing and default contribution status rarely explicitly manipulated, but context (opt-in vs. opt-out) appears in some studies.
- `show_punishment_id`: Studied in the context of anonymity and targeting; present in some design discussions (Mathew, 2017; Ouss & Peysakhovich, 2015), but not always as a manipulated variable.
- `show_other_summaries`: Sometimes reported; often implicit in feedback protocols.

**Sparse/Missing in This Set:**
- Some design facets, such as detailed technological parameters of punishment/reward delivery, are seldom explicitly compared across many studies.
- Certain fine-grained field dimensions (complex real-world institutions, multi-level governance, or endogenous population formation) are only peripherally represented.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Outcome Measures**: A considerable share of studies use **cooperation or contribution rate as the main outcome**; these cannot always be equated to payoff-based efficiency, especially if punishment is costly or misapplied.
- **Design Coverage**: Deepest evidence is for **standard linear PGGs with 3-5 players, perfect information, and symmetric payoffs**; results may not generalize to larger groups, highly asymmetric payoffs, more complex field environments, or non-canonical variants.
- **Mechanism Dependence**: **Efficiency effects are contingent on credible, well-calibrated, and justly applied punishment**. Settings with noise, error, or poorly designed punishment regimes frequently display reduced or negative efficiency effects.
- **Contextual Moderators**: Cultural background, group selection into treatments, legitimacy of institutions, and context-specific social norms **moderate both willingness to punish and its efficiency impact**, but are not comprehensively mapped or compared across all studies.
- **Reward and Communication Not Fully Controlled**: The independent and interactive effects of enabling rewards or communication with punishment are not always fully crossed, meaning their combined effects are only partially illuminated.
- **Theory-Empirics Gap**: Even when theories give strong comparative statics, many empirical studies cannot test boundary conditions (e.g., group size at scale, rare error types, heterogeneous populations).
- **Reporting Gaps**: Details on punishment/reward implementation, population characteristics, or manipulation of information feedback can be underspecified, limiting transferability to new prediction settings.
- **Limited Evidence on Some Dimensions**: Design features like `show_punishment_id`, default contribution framing, or explicit summary feedbacks are rarely focal, making predictions for games with these features more speculative.
- **Potential Publication Bias**: Positive results for punishment may be overrepresented (negative/null effects underexplored, see Ouss & Peysakhovich, Dekel et al.).

**Conclusion:**  
The literature provides **direct, robust evidence** for positive effects of enabling peer punishment on efficiency in standard PGGs—but **conditional on regime quality, information, and context**. For the specific task (predicting efficiency from game design and control efficiency after enabling punishment), **most design dimensions are at least indirectly informed**, but **predictions outside the best-covered settings, or where efficiency is not measured, should be made with caution**.
