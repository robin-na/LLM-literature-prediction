# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set comprises 167 experimental and field studies, almost exclusively empirical. The set is broad in both institutional design (from standard public goods games and close variants, to trust games, common-pool resource games, markets, and dyadic/contest games) and outcome measures (from direct efficiency measures to adjacent payoff-based proxies). However, only a subset of papers are directly relevant for predicting efficiency changes due to *peer punishment* in standard public goods games (PGGs). Most studies examine institutional, reputational, exclusionary, reward-based, or third-party punishment mechanisms, or adjacent outcomes such as social image, exclusion, and communication, without manipulating standard peer punishment with explicit cost/impact technologies. Direct PGG-with-peer-punishment evidence is thus sparse, and the literature provides more indirect or adjacent evidence for the primary prediction task.

There is a strong preponderance of experimental (lab and field) studies, with a rich set of design variations that allow for assessment of moderation by game parameters. Theory and mechanism arguments appear mostly in paper discussions but are not the primary contribution of the set. Overall, the evidence base is strong for basic payoff impacts of a wide variety of sanction and reputation mechanisms but is patchy for precisely predicting the effect of *peer punishment* in classic or modern PGGs, given specified game dimensions.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant**:
- *Exact*: A handful of studies focus on standard PGGs or voluntary contribution mechanisms (VCMs). Most other works use close or adjacent variants, such as trust games, minimum-effort/weakest-link games, common pool resources (CPRs), one-shot or repeated dyadic games, or real-effort team production games. Relevance to PGG is thus mostly *adjacent* to *close*.
  
**punishment_or_sanctions**:
- *Exact*: Some studies directly manipulate classic peer punishment (costly deduction of others’ payoffs at a personal cost), especially in PDs, trust games, or contest games. Most other works focus on adjacent mechanisms: exclusion/ostracism, reward, reputation, audit/fines, collective or institutionalized sanctions, or endogenous selection partner choice.
- Often, the “punishment” is non-material (social disapproval, peer rating), or operates via exclusion or partner choice, not direct costly destruction.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome**:
- *Exact or Close*: The bulk of papers measure group payoff, surplus, welfare, or efficiency, variously normalized (to Nash, to social optimum, to revenue, etc.), and are explicit about total group or aggregate payoffs, which aligns with the outcome definition for prediction.
- Some studies focus primarily on cooperation or contribution rates, norm adherence, or other behavioral outcomes, but typically include at least some payoff-based efficiency measures.

**Conclusion:**  
The set is broad and rich in adjacent evidence, but *only weakly populated by exact studies* that directly manipulate peer punishment in standard PGGs and report efficiency.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

**Payoff-based outcomes (directly relevant)**:
- Group efficiency (total payoff normalized to social optimum)
- Group earnings, welfare, surplus, or coins generated
- Employer payoffs, worker payoffs, social surplus (in principal-agent or firm-worker settings)
- Market prices/trade, resource/goods harvested or produced

**Non-payoff (behavioral) outcomes**:
- Contribution/cooperation rates, extraction rates, group stability, selection/exclusion behavior
- Norm adherence, compliance, punishment/reward frequency, third-party evaluations
- Reputation/status, fairness perceptions, emotional signaling, exclusion rates

**Distinction**:  
Many studies that report increased cooperation/contributions under punishment do NOT always see increases in efficiency—because increased costs of punishment, antisocial punishment, or retaliation can more than offset gains.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis highlights**:

- **Enabling punishment can, but does not always, increase efficiency**. The effect of peer punishment on efficiency is *highly context-dependent*, often positive but sometimes neutral or negative, especially when punishment is overused, misdirected (antisocial), or when punishment costs outweigh cooperation gains [(Wu et al., 2009); (Dreber et al., 2008); (Abbink et al., 2010)].
  
- **Mechanisms other than peer punishment (e.g., reputation, exclusion, communication)** generally produce larger, more robust, or more consistent efficiency gains than peer punishment alone in many PGG-type and adjacent environments [(Milinski et al., 2002); (Semmann et al., 2004); (Charness & Yang, 2014); (Nosenzo & Tufano, 2017)].

- **The technology and cost structure of punishment (punishment_tech, punishment_cost)** are critical:  
    - High-impact or leveraged punishment (large-to-cost ratio) is more likely to improve efficiency than low-tech (1:1) punishment, but at the risk of over-punishment and efficiency loss in large groups or poorly coordinated settings [(Kamei, 2020); (Rigdon, 2009); (Roux & Thöni, 2015)].
    - High punishment costs or retaliatory cycles can nullify or reverse the gains from increased cooperation [(Wu et al., 2009); (Hopfensitz & Reuben, 2009); (Abbink et al., 2010)].

- **Group size, repeated interaction (num_rounds, player_count), and matching structure** strongly moderate efficiency effects:
    - Small groups, longer horizon games, and random matching tend to support (modest) positive effects [(Camera & Casari, 2009); (McCabe et al., 1996)].
    - Large groups or settings with uncoordinated multiple punishers can see over-punishment and efficiency loss [(Kamei, 2020); (Li et al., 2018)].
  
- **Contextual and cultural moderators** (e.g., social norms, prior cooperation, context of contest or conflict, group composition) can override design variables:
    - Antisocial or spiteful punishment undermines or reverses efficiency gains [(Fehr et al., 2008); (Abbink et al., 2010); (Praxmarer et al., 2024)].
    - Norm uncertainty can amplify efficiency gains from punishment, but only if punishment is responsive and well-targeted [(Zong et al., 2025)].
    - Prevalence of third-party interventions, in-group/out-group dynamics, and legitimacy of institutional rules all affect outcomes.

- **Enabling communication or endogenous partner/group formation (chat, exclusion)** typically yields strong positive effects on efficiency—sometimes rivaling or exceeding those of peer punishment alone [(Gangadharan et al., 2017); (Charness & Yang, 2014); (Riedl et al., 2016, 2021); (Wang et al., 2012)].

- **Control efficiency is imperfectly predictive**: In some settings, the effect of enabling punishment is small or negative, especially when baseline efficiency is already high, and the scope for gain is limited. In others (especially with low control efficiency and well-calibrated punishment), the efficiency gain can be substantial—but only if the negative side-effects (punishment cost, antisocial punishment) are contained.

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**How should this literature inform prediction of treatment efficiency for PGGs with peer punishment, given design dimensions and control efficiency?**

- **Expect average efficiency to increase only when certain conditions are met**. Peer punishment is most likely to increase group efficiency above control when:
    - The punishment cost/effect tradeoff is well-calibrated (moderate cost, high impact, minimal risk of over-punishment or antisocial punishment).
    - Group size is small-to-moderate and coordination among punishers is feasible, avoiding excessive aggregate punishment.
    - The baseline (control) efficiency is low—there is “room” for improvement.
    - Norms favor punishment of defectors and not cooperators, and antisocial punishment is rare.
    - Cultural or contextual moderators (e.g., high trust, group homogeneity) do not undermine sanctioning incentives.

- **In many environments, especially with large groups, high punishment costs, norm conflict, or risk of antisocial punishment, efficiency gains may be absent or negative**. Enablement of peer punishment may reduce efficiency, especially when the cost of punishment and retaliation outweighs cooperation gains.

- **The presence of alternative or complementary incentive mechanisms often supersedes or substitutes for peer punishment in boosting efficiency**. If reputation, exclusion, communication, centralized sanctions, or democratic delegation are available, these may generate efficiency gains with fewer risks of resource destruction or equity losses.

- **Take account of “adjacent” evidence cautiously**: When predicting for standard PGGs from studies on weak-link or contest games, CPRs, trust games, or dyadic PDs, be explicit about differences. Most adjacent evidence suggests the net efficiency effect of punishment is highly context-specific; mechanisms and magnitudes may not transfer directly.

- **Dimension-specific guidance**: See next section.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly Informed Dimensions:**
- **player_count**: Effects are well-documented. Larger groups face greater risks of over-punishment, coordination failure, and efficiency loss [(Kamei, 2020); (Li et al., 2018); (Wu et al., 2009)].
- **num_rounds**: Longer repeated games typically see stronger (positive or negative) punishment effects, as strategies can evolve and reputations can influence behavior [(Camera & Casari, 2009); (McCabe et al., 1996)].
- **punishment_cost, punishment_tech**: Heavily studied as moderators of efficiency effect. High-impact, low-cost punishment promotes efficiency but risks overuse. High-cost or low-impact punishment usually fails to improve efficiency [(Rigdon, 2009); (Kamei, 2020); (Roux & Thöni, 2015)].
- **chat**: Consistently shown to boost efficiency, sometimes more than punishment. Communication enables norm coordination and trust [(Gangadharan et al., 2017); (Ahn et al., 2018)].
- **reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Many studies compare or combine reward and punishment. Rewards often increase efficiency, sometimes more reliably or with lower risk than punishment [(Vyrastekova & van Soest, 2008); (Bigoni et al., 2014)].

**Indirect or Contextual Dimensions:**
- **all_or_nothing, default_contrib**: Some studies address discrete vs. continuous contributions or framing effects. Discrete (binary) contribution games may see different punishment dynamics but are generally less central [(Arai et al., 2023); (Bruttel & Eisenkopf, 2012)].
- **mpcr**: Reported in many studies; higher MPCR increases the scope for efficiency gains and can strengthen punishment’s effect [(Roux & Thöni, 2015); (Gangadharan et al., 2017)].
- **show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id**: Information conditions (e.g., round horizon, transparency of contributions/outcomes, visibility of punishment identity) are discussed in relation to norm enforcement and reputation, and are key in many adjacent studies [(Milinski et al., 2002); (Kamei, 2018); (Diekmann et al., 2014)].

**Sparse or Mostly Missing Dimensions:**
- **default_contrib**: Not systematically varied in most studies.
- **reward_cost, reward_tech**: Fewer direct studies manipulating both reward and punishment side-by-side and reporting efficiency (vs. behavioral) effects.
- **show_punishment_id**: While some studies manipulate anonymity, few vary explicit visibility of punishment in peer punishment phases of standard PGGs.
- **Contribution framing (opt-in/opt-out), detailed group structure (e.g., centrality, nesting), and heterogeneity**: Often described but not always directly varied for causal inference.

# 7) Important Limitations

**Scope of applicability:**
- *Few “exact” public goods games with toggled peer punishment and direct efficiency measurement*: Most evidence is from adjacent or modified designs (trust, contest, exclusion, third-party, or institutional punishment).
- *Scarce direct evidence on all 14 design dimensions combined*: Most studies vary one or a few dimensions at a time; systematic joint variation is rare, hindering comprehensive, fully-parametric prediction.
- *Efficiency is often confounded by externalities of punishment (e.g., retaliation, antisocial use, equity consequences)*: Many studies caution that increased cooperation does NOT always equal greater efficiency—punishment costs, retaliation, and targeting must be accounted for.
- *Contextual moderators (norms, culture, baseline cooperation)* limit transferability: Substantial evidence shows effect heterogeneity by country, group norms, experimental framing, and prior group experience.
- *Over-representation of laboratory experiments*: Results may not generalize fully to real-world, high-stakes or field settings (some field/lab-in-the-field studies exist, but most are lab-based).

**Adjacency vs. transferability:**
- Adjacent findings (from contests, trust games, CPRs, etc.) are *informative* about mechanisms and boundary conditions, but *quantitative transfer to PGGs with peer punishment requires caution*.
- Some design dimensions (e.g., exclusion, communication, or third-party punishment) are fundamentally different in their practical and strategic consequences from peer-to-peer costly punishment.

**Unresolved disagreements and ambiguity:**
- Significant conflicting findings regarding the net effect of peer punishment on efficiency—especially in repeated dyadic games, large groups, contexts with high antisocial punishment, or where punishment is used emotionally or for social competition rather than norm enforcement.
- In many cases, *increased cooperation/contribution is offset or overcompensated by the direct costs of punishment*, yielding zero or negative net treatment effects on efficiency.

---

**Summary:**  
Based on this literature, *enabling peer punishment in public goods games does not guarantee an increase in group efficiency*. The treatment effect’s direction and magnitude depend strongly on punishment cost/effectiveness, group size, coordination, baseline efficiency, information structure, and context/norms. When predicting treatment efficiency from game dimensions and control efficiency, include all available design moderators, and carefully consider whether punishment is likely to be pro-social, well-targeted, and cost-effective in the given context. When design features or context risk the overuse or misdirection of punishment, expect weak or negative efficiency gains, despite possible increases in cooperation rates.
