# 1) Evidence Base

The paper set is dominated by empirical laboratory experiments, with a small subset of field and observational studies. Out of 198 papers, nearly all focus on public goods games (PGGs) or close variants, and most introduce and manipulate punishment or sanctioning systems. The majority of outcomes measured are behavioral—especially contribution and cooperation rates, punishment frequency, and norm enforcement—rather than direct, payoff-based results like efficiency, group payoff, welfare, or surplus.

Only a minority of papers report efficiency or closely related payoff outcomes directly; most discuss such outcomes indirectly by inference from behavioral data. Several meta-analyses and reviews synthesize evidence about punishment's effect on cooperation, with some referencing effects on group payoff or welfare. The evidence base is therefore broad and robust for behavioral outcomes and the mechanisms of punishment in PGG-like environments, but considerably narrower regarding direct prediction of treatment efficiency.

# 2) Task Relevance

**pgg_or_variant:**  
- [Exact] The paper set overwhelmingly consists of studies with exact mapping to PGGs or their core variants (linear, threshold, step-level, CPR, team production with analogous payoff structure).
- [Close] Some subset of papers extends to variations like CPR games, team production, and n-person Prisoner's Dilemmas; these are "close" due to analogous incentive structures.

**punishment_or_sanctions:**  
- [Exact] Nearly all studies explicitly introduce, manipulate, or examine punishment or sanctioning regimes, including institutional punishment (centralized/automatic), peer punishment, social exclusion/ostracism, and group-level enforcement.
- [Close] A handful of studies measure "weak" punishment (e.g., costless emoticons, social sanctions, or ostracism) as a secondary or adjacent mechanism.

**efficiency_or_related_payoff_outcome:**  
- [Exact] Direct reporting of efficiency (i.e., realized group payoff as a fraction of social optimum) is limited.  
- [Close]/[Adjacent] Most reported results are contribution/cooperation rates; often, efficiency is inferred from these via structural mapping (in linear PGGs, higher average contribution generally implies higher efficiency, unless offset by extensive punishment cost). Several papers explicitly caution against assuming equivalence between the two, noting that costly punishment or antisocial punishment can decrease group welfare even as contributions increase.  
- [Weak/None] Few papers offer systematic, quantitative data on efficiency effects across a range of game design parameters.

# 3) Outcomes Measured In The Literature

- **Payoff-related outcomes**:  
    - Group payoff, group profit, earnings, success/provision rates (especially in threshold games), welfare, total coins generated, surplus.  
    - *Direct efficiency* (ratio: group payoff/maximal cooperative payoff) is rarely reported; more often, earnings or provision rates serve as proxies.

- **Non-payoff behavioral outcomes**:  
    - Contribution rates, cooperation rates, compliance with norms/minimum requirements, punishment frequency/type/target, free-riding rates, trust and belief shifts, norm enforcement patterns, ostracism/voting, group identification, psychological mechanisms (anger, legitimacy, fairness perceptions), and emotional or cognitive correlates of punishment use.
    - Effects of context (e.g., culture, group composition), institutional features (centralized vs. decentralized punishment), game framing, information structures, and communication interventions.

# 4) Main Findings Relevant To Prediction

**Synthesis of Empirical Evidence for the Prediction Task:**
- **Punishment usually increases average contributions/cooperation** in repeated, linear PGGs (Fehr & Gächter-style): adding (peer or institutional) punishment often sustains high contribution rates relative to baseline games without punishment (e.g., Gächter & Thöni, 2005; Dong et al., 2016; Engel & Zhurakhovska, 2017), especially with repeated interactions, moderate group size (3-4), standard incentive structure, and costly punishment.

- **Effect on efficiency is context-dependent:**  
    - **Positive effects** on efficiency emerge reliably only when:  
        - The cost of punishment is moderate (cost-to-impact reasonable: e.g., 1:3), punishment is not abused (minimal antisocial punishment), and groups are sufficiently heterogeneous or randomly composed (Gächter & Thöni, 2005).
        - The institution is complete (covers the whole group), credible, and/or combined with rewards (Bühren et al., 2025).
        - Cultural context supports prosocial punishment and norm compliance (Gächter, Herrmann, & Thöni, 2010; Bortolotti et al., 2015).
    - **Negative or null effects** are found:
        - When punishment is frequent and/or antisocial, costs can outweigh gains from increased cooperation—punishment may become "destructive" (Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021; Bortolotti et al., 2015).
        - In some cultures or with certain population samples, punishment increases antisocial sanctions or fails to boost contributions, even reducing efficiency (Gächter, Herrmann, & Thöni, 2010; Bühren et al., 2025).
        - If baseline (control) efficiency is already high, enabling punishment often does not improve—and sometimes reduces—efficiency.
        - Weak, non-monetary, or purely expressive punishments (e.g., smilies/frownies) have little or no effect on contributions or payoffs (Peeters & Vorsatz, 2013).
        - Centralized punishment can be abused by authorities, decreasing group welfare (Hoeft & Mill, 2024).

- **Moderators and Mechanisms:**
    - **Group size:** Little to no consistent moderation in standard PGGs (Carpenter, 2007), but social exclusion's deterrent effect fades in larger groups (Kerr et al., 2009).
    - **MPCR (marginal per capita return):** Higher MPCRs make punishment more effective and beneficial for efficiency; lower MPCRs reduce both cooperation and the net effect of punishment (Carpenter et al., 2009).
    - **Punishment cost and effectiveness:** Low-cost, high-impact sanctions can sustain cooperation but may invite antisocial punishment; free or cheap punishment sometimes has perverse efficiency consequences (Bond, 2019; Chen et al., 2025).
    - **Institutional features:** Complete and credible punishment institutions are highly effective and efficient (Bühren et al., 2025); incomplete, ambiguous, or weak punishment systems can reduce efficiency by enabling strategic or antisocial punishment (Burton-Chellew & Guérin, 2021; Yang et al., 2020).
    - **Cultural/contextual factors:** Culture, social background, and identity moderate both the use and effects of punishment on efficiency—e.g., high trust societies gain more from punishment (Balliet & Van Lange, 2013), while those with high antisocial punishment can see net welfare losses (Gächter, Herrmann, & Thöni, 2010; Bortolotti et al., 2015).
    - **Reward/punishment interplay:** Rewards can have similar or more persistent positive effects on contributions, especially when punishment is removed, but cost-benefit tradeoffs differ (Dong et al., 2016; Kumakawa, 2013; Balliet, Mulder, & Van Lange, 2011).
    - **Persistence:** Efficiency gains from punishment are usually not persistent post-intervention; upon removal, cooperation and efficiency tend to drop, sometimes to below-control levels (Nakagawa et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2009; Mulder et al., 2006).
    - **Information, monitoring, and transparency:** Punishment is far more effective when individual-level behavior is observable (Eisenkopf & Walter, 2022; Carpenter, 2007). High transparency or detailed feedback can undermine cooperation (Engel, 2019).
    - **Individual heterogeneity:** Outcomes are moderated by group composition, personality, subject pool, and local norms. Groups with more pro-social punishers (but few perverse punishers) achieve higher efficiency (Albrecht et al., 2018; Ones & Putterman, 2007).

# 5) Prediction Guidance

**Takeaways for Predicting Treatment Efficiency:**
- **Enabling punishment generally increases efficiency relative to control when baseline cooperation/efficiency is low, antisocial punishment is rare, and punishment is reasonably costly with high effectiveness.** The expected efficiency gain is larger in repeated, small-group, linear PGGs with random group formation, moderate MPCR (e.g., 0.5–0.7), partner matching, and absence of negative cultural or social moderators.

- **Magnitude and direction of effect depend on design dimensions:**
    - **Player count, num_rounds, MPCR, punishment_cost, and punishment_tech** are the most directly informative dimensions in the literature: higher MPCR and lower punishment cost generally increase the positive efficiency impact of punishment.
    - **Reward_exists, reward_cost, reward_tech**: Where reward is present, its interaction with punishment can enhance or sometimes substitute efficiency gains.
    - **Institutional form and coverage** (centralized/complete vs. peer/incomplete): complete, costless institutions are more effective (Bühren et al., 2025).
    - **Show_other_summaries, show_punishment_id, show_n_rounds, chat, default_contrib**: Contextual evidence suggests these dimensions influence trust, targeting of punishment, and perception of legitimacy, which can all impact efficiency through their effect on cooperation and the rational use of sanctions.
    - **Antisocial punishment and group composition/identity/culture are key moderators** not always captured by game design parameters. In their absence, efficiency predictions carry more uncertainty.

- **If control efficiency is already high**, enabling punishment often produces little or no gain—and may reduce efficiency if punishment is overused or misdirected.

- **In some contexts or with certain subject pools, enabling punishment can reduce efficiency due to costly, non-strategic, or antisocial punishment patterns.** This is more likely when antisocial punishment is culturally or situationally prevalent, the institution is incomplete, or monitoring is limited.

- **Efficiency must not be inferred from contribution rates in cases where punishment is frequent and costly, or antisocial punishment is common.** Several papers directly caution against this assumption.

- **Where only contribution/cooperation rates are reported, and punishment is infrequent, or its cost is small relative to the gain in cooperation, higher contribution is a valid proxy for increased efficiency.** Otherwise, predictions should note ambiguity and possible efficiency loss.

# 6) Design Dimensions Highlighted Across Papers

**Directly informed dimensions:**  
- `player_count`, `num_rounds`, `mpcr`, `punishment_cost`, `punishment_tech` (e.g., cost/impact ratio, centralized vs. peer, institutional coverage) are directly manipulated and analyzed in many papers.
- `reward_exists`, `reward_cost`, `reward_tech` are less common, but addressed in some studies.
- `all_or_nothing` (binary vs. incremental contribution), `default_contrib` (framing), and `show_n_rounds` are present in experimental manipulations and analysis.

**Indirectly/contextually informed:**  
- `chat` (communication), `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`—contextual, mainly discussed as moderators or in robustness checks.
- Cultural and social context, group composition, identity, subject pool, and social norms—repeatedly highlighted as critical, but not always formalized as design dimensions.

**Sparse or missing:**  
- Most papers do not manipulate or systematically report `default_contrib`, `show_n_rounds`, `show_other_summaries`, `show_punishment_id`, or group chat as core prediction variables, though several mention their qualitative influence.
- **Efficiency outcomes** as required by the prediction task are sparse; only a handful of studies directly report group efficiency or total payoff.

# 7) Important Limitations

- **Direct efficiency data are rare**: The vast majority of studies report behavioral proxies (contributions, cooperation rates) rather than group-level efficiency or payoff outcomes.
- **Empirical findings are primarily for repeated, small-group, linear, lab-based public goods games;** reliability of predictions in field, large-group, or structurally modified settings is less clear.
- **Effect direction and magnitude are critically mediated by social and cultural context** (e.g., antisocial punishment, group composition, environmental trust, subject pool, cultural background) not always captured in game design variables; this introduces substantial ambiguity and potential for prediction error.
- **Punishment cost, magnitude, and technology are variably defined and not always comparable;** effectiveness of punishment regimes is highly sensitive to these structural parameters.
- **Repeated versus one-shot games:** Most strong efficiency gains from punishment are in repeated games; in one-shot or short games, effects are small or ambiguous, especially for efficiency.
- **Persistence:** Efficiency gains from punishment do not persist after punishment is removed—and may reverse.
- **Reporting bias and lack of standardized efficiency metrics:** Lack of direct, standardized reporting of efficiency limits the calibration and cross-study synthesis required for precise quantitative prediction.
- **Many relevant moderators (norm perception, identity, leadership, legitimacy, transparency, monitoring detail, reward mechanisms) are not always encoded in game design dimensions, making consistent prediction challenging.**
- **Potential for negative effects:** In specific contexts (e.g., high antisocial punishment, weak institutions, cultural mismatch), enabling punishment can reduce efficiency below baseline.

---

**Summary:** The literature set robustly supports the expectation that, in standard repeated PGGs with costly, well-targeted punishment and low antisocial punishment, enabling punishment increases group efficiency relative to control—especially when control efficiency is below the cooperative optimum. However, the effect is strongly moderated by punishment cost/tech, MPCR, institution coverage, group composition, and cultural context. Where control efficiency is already high, or the environment enables antisocial or ineffective punishment, efficiency could stagnate or decline. Most studies report only behavioral proxies, and direct efficiency results are sparse. Prediction based strictly on design dimensions and control efficiency—without accounting for these nuanced moderators—should be made with caution.
