- source: Cressman, R., Song, J. W., Zhang, B. Y., & Tao, Y. (2012). Cooperation and evolutionary dynamics in the public goods game with institutional incentives. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that institutional incentives (reward, punishment, or both) can fundamentally alter the Nash equilibrium and evolutionary outcomes of the public goods game. Without incentives, the only stable outcome is full defection (zero contribution, zero efficiency). With sufficient institutional reward, partial cooperation emerges and group efficiency increases, but full cooperation is not stable. With sufficient punishment, full cooperation can be maintained, but it cannot invade a population of defectors. The combination of reward and punishment leads to full cooperation and maximal efficiency as the unique globally stable outcome. Theoretical results are supported by simulations of replicator dynamics, showing that the mean contribution (and thus efficiency) increases with the strength of incentives, and that the combined scheme is most robust.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling institutional punishment (and/or reward) in a standard public goods game increases group efficiency relative to the no-incentive baseline. The effect size and stability depend on the magnitude of the incentive, the group size, and the specific incentive scheme. For the canonical parameter set (n=4, r=1.6, E=20, A=20), institutional punishment alone can maintain full cooperation if established, but cannot invade defection; reward alone increases cooperation but not to full efficiency; the combination achieves full efficiency. The results are directly relevant for predicting efficiency in games with similar design dimensions, especially for continuous contribution, no chat, known group size, and institutional (not peer) incentives. The findings are less directly applicable to repeated games or peer-sanctioning settings.

  findings: The paper finds that institutionally coordinated punishment and reward mechanisms can stabilize cooperation and increase group efficiency in public goods games, but only if sufficient resources are allocated to these mechanisms. In structured populations with hard selection, there is a risk that groups will underinvest in punishment/reward, leading to a collapse of cooperation and group efficiency. This risk is mitigated if the benefits of public good investment experience diminishing returns, which stabilizes both cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that individuals tend to prefer rewards over punishments, even when punishment would be more efficient. The findings are based on simulation results, not empirical data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical and simulation-based evidence that the presence and design of institutional punishment and reward mechanisms can have a strong, but context-dependent, effect on group efficiency in public goods games. The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive when sufficient resources are allocated to it, but negative or null if groups underinvest due to individual incentives. The model highlights the importance of game structure (e.g., group size, migration, diminishing returns) in moderating these effects. For prediction tasks, the paper suggests that enabling punishment can increase efficiency, but only if the institutional context ensures adequate investment in enforcement; otherwise, efficiency may not improve or may even decline.

- source: Wu, Z. W., Xu, Z. J., & Zhang, L. Z. (2014). Punishment Mechanism with Self-Adjusting Rules in Spatial Voluntary Public Goods Games. *Communications in Theoretical Physics*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The introduction of a punishment mechanism in a spatial voluntary PGG, where players only know their own payoff and update strategies via a self-adjusting rule, can significantly increase average payoff (efficiency) when the cost of punishment is low. This effect is especially pronounced at low values of the multiplication factor r (mpcr), where, without punishment, cooperation and payoff would otherwise be low. The model demonstrates that as punishment cost decreases, the region of high average payoff expands. Memory length also affects outcomes: moderate memory can further improve average payoff, but very high memory may reduce it. The results are robust across initial conditions and show that punishment can transform low-efficiency environments into high-efficiency ones under the right parameter settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in spatial voluntary PGGs, enabling punishment (especially with low punishment cost and fixed punishment effectiveness) can substantially increase average efficiency compared to the control (no punishment) case, particularly when the mpcr is low. The findings suggest that the effect of punishment on efficiency is highly sensitive to the cost of punishment and the value of mpcr. For prediction tasks, this paper supports the expectation that lowering punishment cost or enabling punishment in low-mpcr environments will yield large efficiency gains. The model also highlights the importance of memory length as a moderator. The results are most directly applicable to spatial, voluntary, mixed-strategy PGGs with local information and self-adjusting update rules.

- source: Sun, X. P., Liu, X. Z., Kang, H. W., Shen, Y., & Chen, Q. Y. (2025). The impact of reputation-driven locally variable synergy factors on public goods games. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study finds that introducing manipulators—who both punish defectors and reward cooperators—into a spatial public goods game can sustain cooperation and increase average group payoff, even when the synergy factor (r) is low and cooperation would otherwise fail. The model shows that for a range of parameter values (punishment/reward efficiency, manipulation cost), manipulators and cooperators can coexist, and the group achieves higher payoffs than in the absence of punishment/reward. When punishment is sufficiently efficient (beta/gamma high), the system can reach the all-cooperator state, maximizing group payoff. If the cost of manipulation is too high or punishment/reward is inefficient, cooperation collapses and payoffs drop. Reward withdrawal (manipulators reducing rewards when surrounded by cooperators) reduces the average payoff of cooperators and narrows the payoff gap between manipulators and cooperators. The model demonstrates that the combined effect of punishment and reward is robust but sensitive to parameter settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct, high-signal evidence that enabling punishment (and reward) in a spatial public goods game increases group efficiency and average payoff, especially when the control (no punishment/reward) condition would result in low cooperation and low payoff. The effect is moderated by the efficiency of punishment (punishment impact per cost), the cost to punishers/rewarders, and the synergy factor (mpcr equivalent). The findings suggest that, for a given control efficiency, enabling punishment (especially when combined with reward) will generally increase efficiency, provided the punishment is not too costly and is sufficiently effective. The model also highlights that the presence of defectors and the structure of the population (spatial lattice) are important, but the main prediction is robust across a range of parameter values. This paper should be weighted heavily when predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in similar spatial PGG designs.

The effect of punishment on efficiency is positive, but smaller than that of reward, for a given cost. The findings are most relevant for settings with a small fraction of influential individuals, low MPCR, and where the cost of punishment/reward is paid by society. The results support the prediction that introducing punishment will increase efficiency relative to control, but that reward may be an even more effective intervention. - source: Hwang, S. H., & Bowles, S. (2012). Is altruism bad for cooperation?. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper models a public goods game with costly punishment, where individuals have both unconditional altruism and reciprocity motives. It finds that increasing unconditional altruism can reduce the willingness to punish free riders, which in turn lowers the threat of punishment and thus reduces equilibrium contributions and group surplus (efficiency) over a substantial range of parameter values. The negative effect of altruism on efficiency is larger when the reciprocity motive is strong or when the fraction of reciprocal altruists in the population is high. For very high levels of altruism, contributions and efficiency rise again, as altruism alone is sufficient to sustain cooperation. The model provides explicit equilibrium expressions for contributions and surplus, and characterizes the parameter ranges where altruism is 'bad for cooperation' and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games depends critically on the interplay between social preferences (altruism, reciprocity) and the punishment mechanism. Specifically, it shows that punishment can increase efficiency, but this effect is weakened or even reversed if group members are highly altruistic, because altruism reduces the willingness to punish defectors. The negative effect is amplified when the reciprocity motive is strong. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the efficiency gain from enabling punishment is not monotonic in social preference parameters and may be lower (or even negative) in more altruistic or more reciprocal groups. The model also provides explicit formulas for equilibrium contributions and surplus as a function of game design parameters (player count, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, etc.), which can inform quantitative prediction.

- source: Sun, Z. B., Chen, X. J., & Szolnoki, A. (2023). State-Dependent Optimal Incentive Allocation Protocols for Cooperation in Public Goods Games on Regular Networks. *Ieee Transactions on Network Science and Engineering*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that in structured populations (regular networks), the optimal allocation of a fixed institutional incentive budget between reward and punishment depends critically on the efficiency ratio of reward to punishment. When punishment is more efficient, pure punishment is optimal for maximizing cooperation and minimizing the payoff advantage of defectors. When reward is more efficient, pure reward is optimal. For intermediate efficiency ratios, an adaptive hybrid protocol—rewarding when cooperation is low, punishing when it is high—is optimal. These results are derived analytically and verified numerically. The findings differ from well-mixed populations, where the adaptive hybrid protocol is always optimal regardless of efficiency ratio. The main outcome is the expected payoff difference between defectors and cooperators, which is minimized under the optimal protocol, leading to higher group efficiency and cooperation.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct theoretical guidance for predicting the effect of punishment (and reward) on efficiency in public goods games with institutional incentives on regular networks. The key moderator is the efficiency ratio of reward to punishment: if punishment is more efficient, enabling punishment (with a fixed incentive budget) increases efficiency and cooperation more than reward; if reward is more efficient, the reverse holds. For intermediate ratios, a state-dependent switch is optimal. The results are most applicable to games with institutional (not peer) incentives, fixed budgets, and structured (not well-mixed) populations. The findings can be used to predict the efficiency impact of enabling punishment (vs. reward or hybrid) given knowledge of the incentive budget, the efficiency of each incentive, and the network structure.

The results suggest that, if the game allows for voluntary entry and a small entry fee, enabling punishment will substantially increase efficiency, regardless of group size, population size, or moderate changes in punishment cost. The paper's results are most applicable to settings with anonymous, memoryless, and dynamic group formation, and may not generalize to repeated games with persistent identities.
