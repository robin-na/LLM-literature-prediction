- source: Faillo, M., Grieco, D., & Zarri, L. (2020). The impact of peer ratings on cooperation: The role of information and cost of rating. *Journal of Public Economic Theory*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: In a repeated public goods game with four players and 20 rounds, adding a mandatory, costless peer rating stage (where players rate each other's contributions on a 0-10 scale, with no monetary consequences) increases group contributions and efficiency relative to a baseline with no rating. This effect is robust to whether rating information is private (only the recipient sees their ratings) or public (all see everyone's ratings). When a small cost (1 token per peer rated) is introduced for assigning ratings, most players stop rating, and the effect on contributions and efficiency vanishes. The study provides direct evidence that non-monetary, payoff-irrelevant peer approval can foster cooperation and efficiency in public goods games, but only if it is costless to provide.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a costless, non-monetary peer approval mechanism (rating) in a standard repeated public goods game increases efficiency, even when ratings have no direct payoff consequences and are anonymous. The effect is robust to whether rating information is private or public. However, even a small cost to rating eliminates the effect. For prediction, the presence of a costless, mandatory, non-monetary peer approval mechanism should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control. If rating is costly, no efficiency gain should be expected. The findings are directly relevant for settings where the only sanction/reward is social approval, not monetary punishment or reward.

- source: Milinski, M., Semmann, D., & Krambeck, H. J. (2002). Reputation helps solve the 'tragedy of the commons'. *Nature*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The experiment demonstrates that when public goods games are alternated with indirect reciprocity games (where players' reputations are visible and affect future help), cooperation in the public goods game remains high and group payoffs are significantly higher than in treatments where public goods games are played in blocks without reputation incentives. When the possibility of future indirect reciprocity is removed (by announcing only public goods rounds remain), cooperation and efficiency quickly decline. Thus, reputation-based incentives can substitute for direct punishment in maintaining cooperation and efficiency in public goods settings.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that mechanisms which link reputation to future rewards (indirect reciprocity) can sustain high efficiency in public goods games, even in the absence of explicit punishment. For prediction tasks, it suggests that the presence of reputation/reward mechanisms (even without punishment) can lead to high efficiency, especially when players expect future interactions where their reputation matters. The findings are most relevant for settings where reputation is salient and can affect future payoffs, and less directly informative for games with explicit costly punishment but no reputation/reward linkage.

- source: Carpenter, J., & Seki, E. (2011). DO SOCIAL PREFERENCES INCREASE PRODUCTIVITY? FIELD EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE FROM FISHERMEN IN TOYAMA BAY. *Economic Inquiry*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=field_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study uses a field experiment with fishermen and students, employing a repeated public goods game (VCM) with a costly, non-material 'social disapproval' mechanism (unhappy face) in the last five rounds. Pooling fishermen (who share catch and income) are more productive in real life, and this is linked to higher social preference measures from the experiment. The social disapproval mechanism increases contributions among poolers, and social preference measures (conditional cooperation, propensity to disapprove, response to disapproval) are significant predictors of both experimental contributions and real-world fishing productivity. The effect of social preferences on productivity is substantial, and the productivity advantage of pooling boats is largely explained by these preferences. The 'punishment' in the experiment is not material, so the findings are most directly about social (rather than monetary) sanctions.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that social preference mechanisms, even when implemented as costly but non-material signals (social disapproval), can increase both experimental and real-world efficiency in public-goods-like environments. However, the 'punishment' here is not material: it is a costly signal with no direct monetary penalty. The findings suggest that enabling even non-material, costly social sanctions can increase efficiency, especially among groups with strong social ties or cooperative institutions. For prediction tasks, the presence of a social disapproval mechanism (even without material punishment) is likely to increase efficiency, particularly in groups with pre-existing cooperative norms. The results are most applicable to settings where punishment is non-material but salient, and less directly informative about material punishment effects.

- source: Gangadharan, L., Nikiforakis, N., & Villeval, M. C. (2017). Normative conflict and the limits of self-governance in heterogeneous populations. *European Economic Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: Allowing communication and rewards in a public goods game leads to near-maximal efficiency in homogeneous groups, but only partial efficiency in heterogeneous groups where returns from the public good differ. In homogeneous groups, communication increases group earnings to 94% of the maximum, while in heterogeneous groups, the increase is only 80% of the maximum. The main reason is that most heterogeneous groups prioritize equality or compromise between equality and efficiency, rather than maximizing group earnings. Rewards are not systematically used to offset inequality. The loss in efficiency in heterogeneous groups is due both to lower contributions and to under-use of rewards. The study demonstrates that normative conflict can limit the effectiveness of self-governance mechanisms in promoting efficiency in heterogeneous populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling communication and rewards in public goods games depend critically on group homogeneity. In homogeneous groups, such mechanisms can achieve near-maximal efficiency. In heterogeneous groups, even with communication and rewards, efficiency gains are much smaller due to normative conflict between equality and efficiency. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence of heterogeneity in returns (mpcr differences) is a strong moderator that limits the efficiency benefit of enabling communication and rewards. The absence of punishment means the results are most directly applicable to reward-based or communication-based interventions, but the findings on normative conflict are likely relevant for predicting the limits of punishment as well, especially in heterogeneous settings.

- source: Semmann, D., Krambeck, H. J., & Milinski, M. (2004). Strategic investment in reputation. *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: This experiment demonstrates that the possibility of reputation transfer between a public goods game and an indirect reciprocity game leads to higher cooperation and significantly higher average payoffs in the public goods game. When players' identities are consistent across both games (transferable name), they invest more in cooperation, resulting in higher group earnings. When identities are not transferable, cooperation and payoffs decline. The manipulation of reputation transferability is the key treatment; no punishment or sanctioning mechanism is present. The findings show that reputation incentives can substitute for punishment in sustaining cooperation and efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that enabling reputation transfer between a public goods game and another social context (indirect reciprocity game) increases efficiency, as measured by average payoff per player. While the study does not manipulate punishment, it is highly informative for prediction tasks involving reputation mechanisms as an alternative to punishment. The design dimensions (e.g., player count, MPCR, number of rounds, information structure) are clearly specified, and the efficiency outcomes are directly reported. For prediction, this paper supports the view that mechanisms increasing the salience or transferability of reputation can have a strong positive effect on efficiency, even in the absence of punishment.

- source: Falvey, R., Lane, T., & Luckraz, S. (2025). On a mechanism that improves efficiency and reduces inequality in voluntary contribution games. *Games and Economic Behavior*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: The Galbraith Mechanism (GM) leads to higher efficiency (measured by average group contributions and payoffs) and lower inequality (measured by Gini coefficients) than the standard equal-sharing mechanism in voluntary contribution games with heterogeneous endowments. The GM's effect is robust across different levels of endowment inequality and marginal per-capita return (beta), though the effect is stronger when beta is higher. The mechanism works by allowing players to allocate the public good among each other based on observed contributions, incentivizing higher contributions without explicit punishment or reward costs. The GM outperforms the control in both efficiency and equity, especially benefiting the less-endowed players.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that mechanisms allowing endogenous allocation of the public good based on observed contributions (like the GM) can substantially increase efficiency compared to equal-sharing controls, even in the absence of explicit costly punishment or reward. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that introducing a mechanism where players can allocate payoffs based on contributions (even without explicit punishment) can yield efficiency gains similar to or greater than those seen with standard punishment mechanisms, especially when the marginal per-capita return is sufficiently high. The results are most directly relevant for games with 3 players, 15 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and full information about others' contributions and allocations. The GM is not a punishment mechanism per se, but its allocation stage can serve a similar incentive function. The paper does not test standard punishment (costly reduction of others' payoffs), so direct quantitative transfer to punishment-enabled PGGs should be cautious, but the qualitative lesson is that endogenous, contribution-based allocation can strongly boost efficiency.

- source: Ozono, H., Kamijo, Y., & Shimizu, K. (2016). Institutionalize Reciprocity to Overcome the Public Goods Provision Problem. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: In a repeated four-person public goods game, embedding a reciprocal strategy as an institution (institutionalized reciprocity, IR) leads to significantly higher group efficiency (total profit) than a standard control game. The effect is strongest when the institution enforces a strict upper bound on contributions (S-IR), which enables reciprocators to coordinate against free riders and prevents excessive cooperation. In S-IR, group profits are significantly higher than in both the control and a less restrictive IR variant (NL-IR). The IR mechanism operates as a collective punishment system: if any player fails to meet the target, the group's future benefit is reduced for all. The study does not implement standard peer punishment, but the IR mechanism provides a collective sanction that is effective at increasing efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that institutionalized, rule-based collective punishment (via reduction of future group benefit after a deviation) can substantially increase efficiency in repeated public goods games, especially when the institution strictly limits contributions to a target. While the study does not test standard peer punishment, it shows that collective, institutionally enforced sanctions can outperform standard repeated games without punishment. For prediction tasks, the findings suggest that introducing a credible, enforced institutional sanction (even if not peer punishment) can raise efficiency well above control, particularly when the rules are clear and binding. The results are most directly applicable to games with four players, 20 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions (in increments), and full feedback on others' contributions and payoffs.

- source: Hamman, J. R., Weber, R. A., & Woon, J. (2011). An Experimental Investigation of Electoral Delegation and the Provision of Public Goods. *American Journal of Political Science*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: Electoral delegation, where a group elects a member to allocate contributions in a linear public goods game, results in sustained high efficiency and group payoffs, with most groups achieving or nearly achieving the social optimum. In contrast, standard voluntary contribution games (with or without communication) see contributions and efficiency decline to near zero. Communication boosts efficiency, but only when paired with delegation does it sustain high contributions. When delegation is endogenous (players can opt in), high efficiency is only achieved if communication is allowed, as it facilitates coordination and trust. The main mechanism is the selection of prosocial allocators and the ability to sanction selfish ones. Majority tyranny (where a coalition exploits a minority) is observed but infrequent. The results show that democratic delegation can overcome the free-rider problem and lead to efficient public goods provision.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games with a low MPCR and large groups, introducing a democratic delegation mechanism (electoral selection of a central allocator) dramatically increases efficiency compared to standard voluntary contribution mechanisms. Communication further enhances this effect, especially when delegation is endogenous. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of electoral delegation (even without explicit punishment) can substitute for or outperform punishment in achieving high efficiency, especially when communication is allowed. The control efficiency (voluntary contribution) is very low in this design, but efficiency with delegation is near the social optimum. The results are most applicable to settings with large groups, low MPCR, and centralized decision-making via election.

- source: Gërxhani, K., Volker, B., & Van Breemen, J. (2021). Who Will Do It? Volunteering to Change Cooperation Rules in a Heterogeneous Population. *European Sociological Review*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds
  findings: Changing the minimum contribution rule from low to high in a public goods game increases both individual and collective earnings for all group members, regardless of their MPCR. The effect is robust and statistically significant. The likelihood of attempting a rule change depends on both social value orientation and benefit level: high-earning proselfs are most likely to initiate a change, while prosocials' behavior depends on whether they are high or low earners. The rule change also increases inequality between high and low earners. The experiment does not include peer punishment or reward mechanisms; the only 'cost' is the fee to initiate a rule change vote.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides direct evidence that increasing the minimum required contribution in a public goods game (via a formal rule change) increases group efficiency and total earnings, even in the presence of heterogeneity in MPCR. The findings are highly relevant for predicting the effect of institutional changes that increase minimum cooperation requirements, but less directly relevant for peer punishment interventions. The absence of peer punishment or reward means the results are most applicable to settings where institutional rules, rather than decentralized sanctions, are the main intervention. The efficiency gain from raising the minimum contribution is clear and robust, suggesting that, in similar designs, enabling a mechanism to increase minimum contributions can substantially improve efficiency.

- source: Charness, G., & Yang, C. L. (2014). Starting small toward voluntary formation of efficient large groups in public goods provision. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The paper finds that endogenous group formation with exit, exclusion, and merger options produces very high contribution rates and group efficiency in public goods games. In the main treatment, where the marginal per-capita return (MPCR) increases with group size, groups tend to grow large and stable, and efficiency is maximized. When the efficiency gain from larger groups is capped, groups remain smaller and less stable, and efficiency is lower, but still substantially higher than in fixed-group baselines. Exclusion (a form of sanction) is used to remove low contributors, which protects cooperators and sustains high efficiency. Redemption (the ability for excluded individuals to rejoin) further boosts efficiency. The results suggest that voluntary group formation with exclusion and merger mechanisms can solve the free-rider problem and achieve high efficiency in public goods provision.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in public goods games, mechanisms allowing voluntary group formation, exit, exclusion (as a sanction), and merger can dramatically increase efficiency compared to fixed groups. The effect is especially strong when economies of scale are present (i.e., when MPCR increases with group size). The exclusion mechanism functions as a sanction, but is not a direct costly punishment; rather, it is a group-level sanction that removes low contributors. The findings suggest that, for prediction tasks, the presence of exclusion/exit/merger mechanisms and increasing MPCR with group size are strong positive moderators of the efficiency effect of sanction-like mechanisms. However, the effect is not directly about costly punishment, but about exclusion as a sanction. The results are less informative for standard costly punishment, but highly informative for exclusion-based sanctions and endogenous group formation. Control efficiency (fixed group) is much lower than in the endogenous group treatments.

- source: Otto, P. E., & Bolle, F. (2016). The advantage of hierarchy: Inducing responsibility and selecting ability?. *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment compares three regimes: standard PGG (voluntary contributions), AG (randomly assigned allocator determines all contributions), and PAG (principal selects allocator each round). Both AG and PAG significantly increase group production and average incomes relative to PGG. PAG (principal selection) yields the highest efficiency, with further increases in satisfaction and fairness ratings. The effect is not due to learning or order effects. The mechanism works by centralizing responsibility and leveraging selection, not by explicit punishment. In treatments with heterogeneous productivity, the most productive worker is more likely to be appointed allocator, but does not exploit the position to reduce own effort. The findings show that hierarchy can substitute for punishment in sustaining high efficiency in public goods provision.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing hierarchical control (an allocator or principal-allocator structure) in a public goods game can substantially increase efficiency, even in the absence of explicit punishment mechanisms. For prediction tasks, the presence of a central authority who can determine or influence contributions should be expected to yield efficiency gains similar to or greater than those typically attributed to punishment. The results are robust across group size (n=3), number of rounds (10 per regime), and both homogeneous and heterogeneous productivity. The findings suggest that, in environments where punishment is not available but hierarchy is, efficiency can be high. For prediction, if a game design includes a mechanism where one player can set others' contributions (with or without selection by a principal), expect efficiency to be much higher than in standard PGG, even if punishment is absent.

- source: Nosenzo, D., & Tufano, F. (2017). The effect of voluntary participation on cooperation. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, show_n_rounds, show_other_summaries
  findings: The experiment compares three main treatments: Baseline (forced participation), Unconditional (voluntary participation before knowing partner's contribution), and Conditional (voluntary participation after seeing partner's contribution). In Baseline and Unconditional, average contributions and efficiency are nearly identical (about 22% of endowment contributed; efficiency ~22%). In Conditional, average contributions and efficiency are much higher (47% of endowment; efficiency up to 81.9% among those who agree to play, and 46.6% overall). The positive effect of voluntary participation on efficiency is only observed when participation can be used as a threat against free-riding. There is little evidence that unconditional voluntary participation leads to assortative matching of cooperators, even when the outside option is increased. The main mechanism for increased efficiency is the threat of non-participation disciplining free-riders, not selection. However, frequent withdrawals can reduce realized efficiency gains.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-person one-shot PGG, enabling voluntary participation only increases efficiency if players can condition their participation on knowledge of the partner's intended contribution (i.e., if non-participation is a credible threat against free-riding). Unconditional voluntary participation (without information) does not improve efficiency, even with a higher outside option. For prediction, the presence of a conditional participation mechanism (a form of endogenous partner exclusion or exit threat) is a strong moderator of punishment-like effects on efficiency, even in the absence of explicit costly punishment. The results suggest that, in similar game designs, enabling conditional exit can substitute for explicit punishment in raising efficiency, but unconditional exit does not. Control efficiency (forced participation) is a good predictor of efficiency in unconditional exit games, but not in conditional exit games, where efficiency is much higher.

- source: van der Heijden, E., Potters, J., & Sefton, M. (2009). Hierarchy and opportunism in teams. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.

  findings: The experiment compares two institutions: equal revenue-sharing (control) and leader-determined shares (treatment, where one member can allocate output). In the control, free-riding leads to declining effort and efficiency, with teams achieving on average 61% of maximum possible earnings. In the leader treatment, most leaders reward contributors and exclude shirkers, resulting in average team earnings of 83% of maximum possible. The presence of a leader thus leads to a significant and substantial improvement in efficiency and group payoff. However, the benefit depends on the leader's behavior: teams with unfair or opportunistic leaders perform poorly. The results are explained by a reputation model where leaders may act fairly to sustain cooperation, especially in repeated games.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing a central authority with reward power (akin to a 'punishment' or sanctioning mechanism, though framed as reward) can substantially increase efficiency in public-goods-like environments, provided the leader uses their power to reward contributors and punish shirkers. The effect is robust in a repeated, all-or-nothing contribution game with four players and perfect monitoring. For prediction, the presence of a leader with discretionary allocation power should be expected to increase efficiency relative to equal sharing, especially when the leader can observe and condition rewards on individual contributions. However, the effect depends on the leader's willingness to use their power to enforce cooperation. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where the 'punishment' is implemented via exclusion from rewards rather than explicit costly punishment, but the mechanism is closely related.

- source: Billinger, S., & Rosenbaum, S. M. (2019). Discretionary mechanisms and cooperation in hierarchies: An experimental study. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=close | punishment=adjacent | payoff=exact
  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists
  findings: The experiment compares a baseline PGG (no hierarchy) to four HPGG treatments varying in whether managers can contribute (discretionary contribution: present/absent) and whether managers can allocate returns (discretionary return: static/dynamic). All HPGG treatments yield higher average contributions and accumulated earnings than the baseline, indicating that hierarchy and managerial discretion can sustain cooperation and improve efficiency.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing hierarchical mechanisms (managerial discretion over inputs and/or outputs) in a PGG-like environment increases group efficiency relative to a standard PGG baseline. However, the efficiency gains depend on the specific design: allowing managers to contribute and/or restricting their ability to appropriate returns leads to higher and more sustained efficiency. If managers can appropriate returns without contributing, efficiency and worker payoffs suffer. For prediction, the presence of hierarchy and the structure of managerial discretion are key moderators: enabling managerial contribution and limiting appropriation both support higher efficiency. The findings are directly relevant for predicting treatment efficiency from game design dimensions, especially in settings with hierarchical or leader-follower structures.

- source: Drouvelis, M., Nosenzo, D., & Sefton, M. (2017). Team incentives and leadership. *Journal of Economic Psychology*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment

  outcomes: primary=efficiency_or_payoff | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: Introducing a leader with discretionary reward power in a repeated team production game (a close variant of the public goods game) increases group efficiency from about 30-33% to 72-76% of the maximum possible. Leaders use their power to reward high contributors and withhold rewards from low contributors, effectively incentivizing effort. This effect holds for both homogeneous and heterogeneous teams. However, the distribution of gains is highly unequal, with leaders capturing most of the surplus.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in VCM-like team production games, introducing a central authority with discretionary reward power (a form of positive sanctioning) can dramatically increase group efficiency, even when the leader is self-interested. The effect is robust to team heterogeneity. However, the efficiency gains are distributed unequally, and when delegation is endogenous, reluctance to empower the leader limits the realized gains. For prediction tasks, the presence of a leader with reward power (as opposed to equal sharing) should be expected to increase efficiency substantially, but the effect may be moderated if the leader's power is limited or if workers can restrict delegation. The findings are most directly relevant to settings where the 'punishment' dimension is implemented as positive reward power rather than costly punishment, but the mechanism (centralized sanctioning) is closely related.
