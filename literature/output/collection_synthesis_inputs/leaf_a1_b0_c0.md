- source: Chen, X. J., & Perc, M. (2014). Optimal distribution of incentives for public cooperation in heterogeneous interaction environments. *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in public goods games on heterogeneous (scale-free) networks, the optimal way to distribute limited institutional punishment (and reward) depends on the network structure, the enhancement factor (r), and whether payoffs are absolute or degree-normalized. For low enhancement factors, distributing punishment/reward equally is optimal for maximizing cooperation. For high enhancement factors, targeting high-degree nodes (punishing or rewarding them more) is optimal, but the direction depends on whether payoffs are absolute or normalized. The main outcome is the stationary fraction of cooperators, not group efficiency or total payoff.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect support for predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in PGG-like games, especially in heterogeneous network settings. It suggests that the effectiveness of punishment in promoting cooperation (and thus potentially efficiency) depends on the distribution of punishment, the enhancement factor (analogous to MPCR), and the network structure. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, its value for predicting efficiency outcomes is limited to inferring that higher cooperation rates (under optimal punishment schemes) may translate to higher efficiency, especially when the enhancement factor is high. The findings are most relevant for settings with network heterogeneity and institutional (not peer) punishment.

- source: Wang, S. X., Liu, L. J., & Chen, X. J. (2021). Tax-based pure punishment and reward in the public goods game. *Physics Letters A*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that both decentralized punishment and reward can drive a networked public goods game to high cooperation, but the minimal cumulative cost required depends on the initial cooperation level. If the initial fraction of cooperators is low, reward is more cost-effective; if it is high, punishment is more cost-effective. The study provides analytical and simulation results for the optimal incentive protocols and the conditions under which full cooperation is stable. The main outcome is the cumulative cost of incentives needed to reach a target cooperation level, not group efficiency or total payoff per se.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides indirect but informative evidence for predicting the efficiency impact of punishment in networked public goods games. While it does not report group efficiency directly, it analyzes the cost (incentives spent) required to achieve high cooperation, which is closely related to efficiency when incentive costs are subtracted from group payoffs. The findings suggest that the effectiveness of punishment (in terms of cost to achieve cooperation) depends on initial conditions and the structure of the game (network, group size, synergy factor). For prediction tasks, the results imply that punishment is not always the most efficient intervention; its relative efficiency compared to reward depends on the starting state of the group. The detailed modeling of incentive cost and cooperation dynamics can inform parameterizations of efficiency in similar networked PGG environments.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: The paper finds that introducing punishment into spatial public goods games can enable the survival and dominance of cooperative strategies under harsher conditions (lower multiplication factors) than in the absence of punishment. The effectiveness of punishment depends on the fine and cost parameters, with phase transitions (sometimes discontinuous) between different strategy compositions. The addition of reward and the interplay of positive and negative reciprocity further enrich the dynamics, leading to complex patterns such as cyclic dominance. However, the paper does not report explicit efficiency or group payoff outcomes; instead, it focuses on the prevalence of cooperation and the conditions under which cooperation can persist.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that punishment can promote cooperation in spatial public goods games, especially when the punishment fine is sufficiently large and the cost is not prohibitive. The models specify how game design parameters (e.g., group size, multiplication factor, punishment fine and cost) affect the prevalence of cooperation. However, because the main outcomes are strategy frequencies and phase transitions rather than explicit efficiency or group payoff, the paper offers only indirect support for predicting efficiency effects of punishment. It is most useful for understanding the qualitative impact of punishment-related parameters and for mapping the conditions under which cooperation (and thus higher efficiency) is likely to emerge, but does not provide quantitative efficiency data for direct prediction.

- source: Han, D., & He, Y. X. (2023). The impact of labor subsidy, taxation and corruption on individual behavior. *Applied Mathematics and Computation*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists
  findings: Theoretical analysis and numerical simulations show that: (1) Taxation, when used to fund both labor subsidies for executors and the costs of reward/punishment, can promote and stabilize cooperation in public goods games. (2) Moderate labor subsidies for executors (who implement reward/punishment) are optimal for forming stable alliances of cooperators and executors, excluding defectors. (3) Excessive reward (relative to punishment) or excessive subsidies can destabilize cooperation and allow defectors to invade. (4) The presence of a corruption (bribery) mechanism has little effect on cooperation levels when subsidies are high, but can slow the increase of cooperation as taxation increases. (5) The main outcomes are the steady-state fractions of each strategy; average payoffs are derived but not the focus. (6) The model is entirely theoretical and does not provide empirical or experimental data.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical, mechanistic evidence about how the structure of punishment, reward, taxation, and executor subsidies affect the evolutionary stability of cooperation in public goods games. It is directly relevant for understanding how punishment and reward mechanisms (and their funding) interact to affect cooperation, but does not provide direct evidence on efficiency or group payoff as a fraction of the social optimum. The results suggest that moderate, well-funded punishment (and reward) mechanisms, especially when executors are compensated, can stabilize cooperation, but excessive reward or subsidy can backfire. The presence of corruption can reduce the effectiveness of punishment, but this effect is mitigated by higher subsidies. For prediction tasks, this paper informs the expected direction and possible moderators of punishment's effect on cooperation, but not directly on efficiency.

- source: Baker, F., & Rachlin, H. (2002). Teaching and learning in a probabilistic prisoner's dilemma. *Behavioural Processes*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech, reward_exists, reward_cost
  findings: The study finds that introducing punishment or reward mechanisms—whether applied individually (peer-to-peer) or collectively (by group consensus)—substantially increases contributions in public goods games. The effect is stronger as the penalty/reward size or effectiveness increases, and as the proportion of punishers/rewarders in the population rises. Both mechanisms are effective, but punishment is found to be marginally more effective than reward in promoting contributions. The model also shows that collective mechanisms (pool punishment/reward) can be more effective than individual ones under certain parameter regimes. Although efficiency is not directly measured, the payoff structure implies that higher contributions lead to higher group payoffs, except for the initial cost to punishers/rewarders. The findings are robust across a range of parameter values and network structures.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical support for the prediction that enabling punishment in a public goods game will increase group contributions and, by implication, group efficiency/payoff, relative to a control without punishment. The model explores a wide range of parameter values for punishment cost, effectiveness, and the proportion of punishers, showing that higher punishment effectiveness and more punishers lead to higher contributions. While the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio, the payoff equations and discussion make it clear that group payoff increases with contribution. The results are most directly applicable to networked, repeated PGGs with binary (cooperate/defect) choices and both individual and collective punishment/reward options. The findings should inform predictions that, for similar game designs, enabling punishment will yield higher efficiency, especially when punishment is effective and not prohibitively costly.

  findings: The simulation model, based on the structure of standard public goods games with punishment (n=4, mpcr=0.4, punishment cost: 1 MU spent removes 3 MUs from target), shows that when agents evolve under disadvantageous inequity aversion, they develop a stable, empirically-matched propensity to punish defectors (k≈0.25). The model finds that disadvantageous inequity aversion is evolutionarily stable and dominates other preference types, leading to higher average wealth, lower volatility, and longer agent lifetimes. While the model does not report efficiency in the strict sense (group payoff as a fraction of maximum possible), it provides strong evidence that punishment under disadvantageous inequity aversion increases group welfare and evolutionary success compared to self-regarding or non-punishing populations.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical and simulation-based evidence that, in a standard public goods game with punishment (n=4, mpcr=0.4, punishment cost 1:3), enabling punishment leads to higher group welfare and evolutionary success, provided agents have disadvantageous inequity aversion. The model is calibrated to match empirical punishment levels and shows that these levels are sufficient to sustain high cooperation and maximize group fitness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that, in similar game designs, enabling punishment is likely to increase efficiency (group payoff/welfare) relative to the control. However, the paper does not provide direct quantitative efficiency ratios, so the evidence is most useful for qualitative or directional prediction, especially in settings matching the model's design parameters.

- source: Podder, S., Righi, S., & Pancotto, F. (2021). Reputation and punishment sustain cooperation in the optional public goods game. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that in the OPGG, punishment alone (including anti-social punishment) does not increase cooperation or group fitness, and can even reduce them by increasing the prevalence of loners. Reputation alone can increase cooperation and fitness, but only under specific social norms (e.g., when defectors are assigned a strictly worse reputation than loners). The combination of reputation and punishment leads to the highest levels of cooperation and population fitness, with most agents adopting conditional cooperation strategies and rarely using punishment. The results are robust to variations in group size, synergy factor, punishment cost and effectiveness, and other parameters. The findings suggest that reputation and punishment interact synergistically to sustain cooperation and improve group outcomes, but that reputation can substitute for costly punishment in many cases.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that, in optional public goods games, the addition of punishment alone (especially when anti-social punishment is possible) does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and may even reduce it. However, when reputation mechanisms are also present—especially those that allow agents to distinguish defectors from loners—punishment and reputation together can synergistically increase cooperation and group fitness. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of enabling punishment on efficiency depends critically on the presence and nature of reputation mechanisms and social norms. In the absence of reputation, enabling punishment may not increase efficiency; with reputation, especially under certain norms, enabling punishment can have a more positive effect. The model's robustness checks indicate these findings are not highly sensitive to group size, rounds, or punishment cost within the tested ranges.

- source: Huang, F., Chen, X. J., & Wang, L. (2018). Conditional punishment is a double-edged sword in promoting cooperation. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: theory | empirical=N/A | experimental=N/A
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=mixed
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that introducing conditional punishment into a public goods game can have both positive and negative effects on cooperation, depending on how easy it is for conditional punishers to join in (the threshold H). When H is low, conditional punishment increases the basin of attraction for cooperative outcomes and raises the average frequency of cooperation and punishment, compared to the case without conditional punishment. When H is high, conditional punishment reduces cooperation and makes defection more likely. These effects are observed in both well-mixed and structured populations, and are robust to changes in punishment cost, punishment effectiveness, and observation cost. The model tracks average payoffs for each strategy, but the main reported outcomes are behavioral (strategy frequencies, basins of attraction).
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the effect of punishment on efficiency (or related payoff outcomes) in public goods games depends critically on the design of the punishment mechanism, specifically whether punishment is conditional and how easy it is to coordinate punishment. If conditional punishment is easy to trigger (low threshold), it can increase cooperation and, by implication, group payoff/efficiency. If it is hard to trigger (high threshold), it can reduce cooperation and efficiency. The model also shows that the effect is robust to changes in punishment cost, effectiveness, and observation cost. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the presence and design of conditional punishment (threshold H) are key moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency, and that simply enabling punishment is not always sufficient to increase efficiency.

- source: Janssen, M. A., & Bushman, C. (2008). Evolution of cooperation and altruistic punishment when retaliation is possible. *Journal of Theoretical Biology*.

  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The paper finds that when agents in a public goods game can retaliate against those who punish them, the effectiveness of punishment in sustaining cooperation is greatly reduced. In the absence of retaliation, punishment can support high levels of cooperation (and thus higher group payoffs/efficiency). When retaliation is possible, cooperation and punishment both decline, especially in larger groups or when the cost of retaliation is low relative to the cost of punishment. The visibility of the punisher (i.e., whether punished agents can identify who punished them) is also important: if punishers are less visible, retaliation is less likely and cooperation can be higher. The model also shows that spiteful punishment (defectors punishing others unconditionally) does not undermine the main result: retaliation is the key factor that reduces cooperation and, by implication, efficiency.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong theoretical evidence that the efficiency gains from enabling punishment in public goods games are highly contingent on whether retaliation is possible and on the relative costs of punishment and retaliation. In environments where punished agents can easily identify and retaliate against punishers, the positive effect of punishment on efficiency is likely to be much smaller or even negative, especially in larger groups. The model also highlights the importance of punisher anonymity: making it harder to identify punishers can help preserve the efficiency benefits of punishment. For prediction tasks, this paper suggests that the presence of retaliation and punisher identifiability are critical moderators of punishment's effect on efficiency.

  findings: The paper finds that introducing a threshold-based resource allocation regime in a PGG with peer punishment can effectively solve the second-order free-rider problem and promote the dominance of punishment. The evolutionary dynamics depend on the threshold parameter (lambda), reward weights for cooperators and defectors, and punishment parameters (fine and cost). For a wide range of threshold and reward parameters, the system converges to full punishment or bistable states between punishment and defection. The basin of attraction for full punishment shrinks as more resources are allocated equally (higher lambda), but only a small portion of resources need to be used for reward to promote punishment. The model suggests that proper adjustment of threshold and reward weights is key to promoting punishment and resisting second-order free-riders. However, the paper does not directly report efficiency or group payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides theoretical evidence that the structure of resource allocation and the presence of peer punishment can strongly affect the evolutionary stability of cooperation and punishment in PGG-like environments. While it does not report efficiency or group payoff directly, it suggests that regimes which reward punishers (and to a lesser extent cooperators) based on contribution, especially when only a small portion of the public good is used for reward, can promote the dominance of punishment and suppress second-order free-riding. For prediction tasks, the paper supports the idea that enabling punishment and introducing targeted reward mechanisms can shift the system toward higher cooperation and punishment prevalence, which is typically associated with higher efficiency in empirical PGGs. However, the lack of explicit efficiency or payoff reporting means the findings should be used as indirect, mechanistic support rather than direct quantitative evidence.

While the paper does not report efficiency as a direct ratio of actual to maximal group payoff, the shift in r_c is a strong proxy for increased group welfare. The findings are most applicable to spatial, all-or-nothing, three-player, repeated games with binary contribution and punishment choices, and may be less directly transferable to well-mixed or continuous-contribution settings.
