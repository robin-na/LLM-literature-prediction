- source: Dong, Y. L., Zhang, B. Y., & Tao, Y. (2016). The dynamics of human behavior in the public goods game with institutional incentives. *Scientific Reports*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: In repeated four-player PGGs, institutional punishment and combined reward/punishment significantly increase average contributions and group payoffs compared to control and reward-only. The effect is not due to direct behavioral response to being punished or rewarded, but to a change in self-regarding preferences (b1). The group average payoff is strongly positively correlated with b1 (self-regarding preference) in punishment and combined conditions. The effect of varying the intensity of incentives (Up, Const, Down) is minimal. The presence of institutional punishment is the key driver of higher cooperation and payoffs.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a standard repeated PGG with four players, 50 rounds, no chat, continuous contributions, and known MPCR, adding institutional punishment (with moderate cost and effectiveness) increases group payoffs and average contributions compared to control. The effect is robust to variations in punishment intensity. Reward alone does not significantly increase payoffs. For prediction, the presence of institutional punishment should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, especially when combined with reward. The effect is mediated by changes in self-regarding preferences, not by direct response to being punished.

- source: Burton-Chellew, M. N., & Guérin, C. (2021). Decoupling cooperation and punishment in humans shows that punishment is not an altruistic trait. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B-biological Sciences*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=less_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The study shows that in a standard repeated public goods game with punishment, contributions remain stable when all can punish and be punished. However, when some individuals are made immune to punishment, these immune individuals (even those classified as conditional cooperators) learn to contribute less over time, despite being able to punish others and being surrounded by high contributors. Punishment is frequent but often hypocritical or anti-social among immune individuals, and overall, the costs of punishment are greater than the benefits from increased contributions, making punishment destructive to group welfare. The results indicate that punishment does not reliably increase efficiency or group payoff, and that the link between cooperation and punishment is not altruistic but may be driven by confusion and learning.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the effect of punishment on efficiency in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that punishment can be destructive: the costs of punishment can outweigh any gains from increased cooperation, leading to lower overall efficiency. The design closely matches standard PGGs with linear returns, four players, repeated rounds, and costly punishment (1 MU cost to reduce 3 MU from another). The paper does not report efficiency directly, but states that punishment is 'ultimately destructive' in terms of group welfare. Therefore, when using game design dimensions similar to this study (small groups, linear PGG, costly punishment, no communication, anonymous punishment), one should expect that enabling punishment may not increase, and may even decrease, group efficiency relative to the control. The findings are especially relevant for settings where punishment is frequent and costly, and where the marginal per capita return is less than one.

- source: Engel, C., & Zhurakhovska, L. (2017). You Are in Charge: Experimentally Testing the Motivating Power of Holding a Judicial Office. *Journal of Legal Studies*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Authorities (randomly assigned, with no personal stake in group outcomes) are willing to incur costs to punish free riders in a repeated linear public goods game. This punishment leads to higher contributions and higher payoffs for active participants than the Nash equilibrium (no contribution, no punishment). The effect is robust to framing the authority as a public official or judge, which further reduces the influence of personal preferences on punishment. Announcing punishment policies, election of judges, and experience all modulate the targeting and sensitivity of punishment, but the basic effect—punishment increases cooperation and group payoffs—remains. The main outcomes are behavioral (contribution, punishment), but group profit is reported and is higher than in the absence of punishment.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that introducing centralized punishment (by a non-beneficiary authority) in a repeated linear public goods game increases group payoffs and efficiency relative to the no-punishment baseline. The effect is robust across variations in authority framing, policy announcement, election, and experience. The design dimensions (player count, number of rounds, MPCR, punishment cost/tech, no chat, continuous contributions, no rewards, full information on others' actions and punishment) are all specified, making the results directly relevant for predicting efficiency in similar lab PGGs with centralized punishment. However, the paper does not report efficiency as a ratio to the social optimum, so the effect must be inferred from reported mean earnings and profit.

- source: Bühren, C., Dannenberg, A., & Händel, P. (2025). The demand for complete and incomplete punishment institutions to promote cooperation. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The paper finds that in a public goods game with endogenous, costless, formal punishment institutions, complete punishment institutions (where all group members are covered) reliably lead to the implementation of deterrent punishment when cooperation is profitable, resulting in high contributions and, by implication, high efficiency. In contrast, incomplete punishment institutions (where only a subgroup is covered, or where subgroups can independently implement punishment) lead to less consistent implementation of deterrent punishment, lower average punishment rates, and lower contributions, even when cooperation is profitable. The marginal group return (MGR) is a strong predictor of punishment demand and contributions in complete institutions, but much less so in incomplete institutions, where individual cooperativeness and subgroup coordination become more important. Coordination failures between subgroups often prevent the realization of efficient outcomes in incomplete institutions.
  prediction_guidance: For predicting the efficiency effect of punishment in public goods games, this paper provides strong evidence that the structure of the punishment institution (complete vs. incomplete) is a critical moderator. When punishment is costless and covers all players, enabling punishment leads to near-maximal efficiency if cooperation is profitable. When punishment is incomplete (applies only to a subgroup or is implemented separately by subgroups), enabling punishment leads to less reliable efficiency gains, with outcomes depending more on group composition and coordination. The marginal per-capita return (mpcr) is a strong predictor of efficiency gains from punishment in complete institutions, but much weaker in incomplete ones. Control efficiency (without punishment) is not directly reported, but the design implies low efficiency in the absence of punishment when cooperation is not profitable. The findings suggest that, for prediction, the presence of a complete, costless, formal punishment institution is a strong positive moderator of efficiency, while incomplete institutions introduce substantial uncertainty and lower average efficiency.

- source: Gächter, S., & Thöni, C. (2005). Social learning and voluntary cooperation among like-minded people. *Journal of the European Economic Association*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=close
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=more_positive
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost, punishment_tech
  findings: The study uses a repeated linear public goods game (MPCR=0.6, 3 players, 10 rounds) to examine how group composition and the availability of punishment affect cooperation. Groups are either sorted by initial cooperative attitude (measured in a one-shot game) or randomly composed. In the no-punishment condition, sorted groups of high cooperators maintain very high contributions, while randomly composed groups show lower and declining cooperation. When punishment is available, average contributions increase substantially in both sorted and random groups, but the effect is strongest in randomly composed groups. In sorted groups of high cooperators, punishment is rarely used and does not increase contributions further. In sorted groups of low cooperators, punishment is used frequently but does not substantially raise contributions. The paper reports average contributions and income reduction due to punishment, but not efficiency per se. The findings suggest that punishment is most effective at raising cooperation (and likely efficiency) in heterogeneous groups, while homogeneous groups of cooperators do not need punishment to achieve high outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in standard repeated linear public goods games, enabling punishment increases average group contributions, especially in randomly composed (heterogeneous) groups. The effect on efficiency is positive, but the net gain depends on the magnitude of punishment costs. In homogeneous groups of high cooperators, punishment is unnecessary and does not improve outcomes. For prediction tasks, the presence of punishment should be expected to increase efficiency relative to control, particularly when group composition is random. The specific game parameters (3 players, 10 rounds, MPCR=0.6, punishment cost 1:3) are informative for parameter-matched predictions. However, efficiency gains may be offset by punishment costs, especially if punishment is used frequently. The paper does not provide direct efficiency ratios, so predictions should be based on the reported contribution and punishment patterns.

- source: Nakagawa, M., Lefebvre, M., & Stenger, A. (2022). Long-lasting effects of incentives and social preference: A public goods experiment. *Plos One*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: The experiment finds that all three incentive mechanisms (monetary punishment, non-monetary reward, recommendation) significantly increase contributions to the public good during the policy phase. Monetary punishment is most effective in raising contributions, but this effect is not persistent: contributions drop sharply after the punishment phase ends. Non-monetary reward and recommendation also increase contributions, with some evidence of longer-lasting effects, especially for recommendation among certain types (e.g., free-riders). However, the main outcome is contribution rate, not efficiency or group payoff. The study also finds that the effect of incentives varies by social preference type: monetary punishment increases contributions across all types, while non-monetary reward and recommendation mainly affect strong conditional cooperators.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that enabling monetary punishment in a repeated linear PGG increases contribution rates during the intervention, but the effect is not persistent after removal. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so direct prediction of efficiency outcomes is not possible. The design dimensions (player count, rounds, punishment cost/tech, etc.) are well specified and match standard PGG lab experiments. The findings support the expectation that punishment increases cooperation (and likely efficiency) during the intervention, but the lack of payoff/efficiency data means the magnitude of efficiency gains cannot be directly inferred. The results are most useful for predicting contribution rate changes, not efficiency per se.

Monetary punishment and non-monetary reward are the most effective. After incentives are removed, contributions remain higher than baseline for several rounds, indicating some long-lasting effect, but eventually decline. The effect of punishment and reward on later contributions depends on whether individuals were high or low contributors and how many points they received. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict the effect of punishment on efficiency. The results are highly informative for predicting contribution behavior, but less so for efficiency unless one assumes a direct mapping from contribution to efficiency (which is not always valid, especially when punishment is costly). The design dimensions are well specified, so the paper is useful for understanding the behavioral response to punishment and reward mechanisms in similar lab PGGs.

- source: Peysakhovich, A., & Rand, D. G. (2016). Habits of Virtue: Creating Norms of Cooperation and Defection in the Laboratory. *Management Science*.

In a second experiment, exposure to cooperative environments increases third-party punishment of selfishness and reduces antisocial punishment in public goods games with punishment.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that institutional environments can shape prosocial and punishment behaviors in public-goods-game-like settings, and that these behaviors spill over into new contexts. However, because the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, it does not directly inform the prediction of treatment efficiency from game design dimensions plus control efficiency. The findings are most useful for predicting changes in cooperation and punishment rates, not efficiency. The design details (e.g., group size, punishment cost/tech, MPCR) are reported and can inform mapping to other studies, but the lack of efficiency outcomes limits direct predictive value for the downstream task.

- source: Chen, X. P., Pillutla, M. M., & Yao, X. (2009). Unintended Consequences of Cooperation Inducing and Maintaining Mechanisms in Public Goods Dilemmas: Sanctions and Moral Appeals. *Group Processes & Intergroup Relations*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Sanctions (punishment or reward) in public goods games increase cooperation while present, but when removed, cooperation drops sharply—sometimes to levels below those in groups that never had sanctions. Moral appeals also increase cooperation, but this effect is more persistent after the appeal is removed. The drop in cooperation after removing sanctions is mediated by reduced trust in others' cooperation. The study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so findings are based on contribution rates, not direct payoff outcomes.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that the presence of punishment (and reward) increases cooperation in the short term, but this effect is not sustained after removal, and may even backfire. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, it cannot be used directly to predict efficiency outcomes for games with punishment enabled versus disabled. The findings are most useful for predicting contribution rates and for understanding the psychological mechanisms (trust) that may moderate the effect of punishment on cooperation. For efficiency prediction, the results suggest caution: increased cooperation under punishment may not translate to sustained efficiency gains, especially if punishment is later removed.

- source: Irwin, K., Mulder, L., & Simpson, B. (2014). The Detrimental Effects of Sanctions on Intragroup Trust: Comparing Punishments and Rewards. *Social Psychology Quarterly*.
  type: empirical | empirical=experimental | experimental=lab_experiment
  relevance: pgg=exact | punishment=exact | payoff=adjacent
  outcomes: primary=non_payoff_behavior | overall_effect=N/A
  dimensions: player_count, num_rounds, chat, all_or_nothing, mpcr, punishment_cost
  findings: Both punishment and reward systems, when present, can increase short-term cooperation (especially when sanctions are moderate rather than extreme), but their removal leads to a significant decline in trust, and in some cases, a decline in cooperation. Strong sanctions do not increase cooperation above control, and their removal reduces cooperation. Weaker sanctions increase cooperation while present, but their removal does not immediately reduce cooperation, though trust is still undermined. The negative effects on trust are similar for both punishment and reward. The paper does not report efficiency or group payoff, so findings must be interpreted through the lens of contribution/cooperation rates.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides evidence that the presence and removal of punishment (and reward) systems can have complex effects on cooperation, which is a behavioral proxy for efficiency but not a direct measure. Strong punishment does not increase cooperation above control, and its removal reduces cooperation. Weaker punishment increases cooperation while present, but its removal does not immediately reduce cooperation. The undermining of trust by sanctions is robust, regardless of sanction strength. For prediction tasks, this suggests that the effect of punishment on efficiency may depend on the strength of the sanction and the baseline level of cooperation, but direct efficiency outcomes are not reported. Use caution when extrapolating to efficiency, as only contribution rates are measured.

- source: Kumakawa, T. (2013). Social Rewards and Sanctions: A Voluntary Contribution Mechanism Experiment. *Journal of Neuroscience Psychology and Economics*.

  findings: The experiment finds that in a two-player public goods game, the opportunity to send social (free-form) or monetary rewards/sanctions does not increase first-round contributions compared to a baseline with no such opportunity. However, when participants actually experience social sanctions (i.e., receive a negative message from their partner), their contributions in the next round increase significantly. The study does not report group efficiency or payoff outcomes, so the effect of punishment on efficiency cannot be directly assessed.
  prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in a two-player public goods game with a game-theoretic payoff table and no prior knowledge of a second round, social sanctions (blame via free-form messages) can increase subsequent contributions, but only after they are actually experienced. The mere opportunity for sanctions or rewards does not affect cooperation. However, the study does not report efficiency or group payoff, so it cannot directly inform predictions about the effect of punishment on efficiency. The findings are most relevant for predicting behavioral (contribution) responses to social sanctions, not efficiency outcomes. The design (two players, two rounds, no chat except for the sanction/reward stage, full information about partner's contribution, and a linear MPCR of 0.7) should be considered when generalizing to other settings.

First-order punishment of defectors occurs, but second-order punishment of nonpunishers is rare or absent, and punishers are not socially endorsed.   prediction_guidance: This paper provides strong evidence that, in one-shot, four-player public goods games with binary contribution and surprise sanctioning opportunities, punishment is not socially supported at the second order, and reward is more likely to be used and socially endorsed. However, because the paper does not report efficiency or group payoff outcomes, it does not directly inform the prediction of efficiency changes when punishment is enabled.
