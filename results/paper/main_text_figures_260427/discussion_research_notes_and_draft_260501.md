## Operating frame

- Do not frame LLMs as a neutral test of whether the literature "transfers."
- Frame the evidence more narrowly: LLMs update their predictions in convergent ways when given the same input; aligned inputs can improve prediction; broader literature often moves predictions without improving them.
- The Discussion should connect prediction, literature organization, and AI-assisted synthesis without making the paper about LLM synthesis itself.
- Avoid database terms such as `row` in main-text prose. Prefer `experimental condition`, `treatment arm`, or `extracted condition`.

## Suggested Discussion structure

1. Main finding: relevant literature does not uniformly improve LLM prediction; alignment matters.
2. What we learn about LLMs: models can use supplied scientific content; convergence is evidence of shared response to input, not evidence of accuracy.
3. What we learn about the literature: empirical evidence can be abundant but organized in ways that are misaligned with a high-dimensional prediction target.
4. Relation to predictive social science and integrative experiments: prediction helps reveal whether accumulated explanations support generalization across conditions.
5. Practical implication: AI-assisted synthesis should be evaluated on downstream predictive tasks and paired with better evidence infrastructure, reporting standards, and integrative designs.
6. Limitations: one domain, one benchmark/outcome, prompting/report choices, full-text rather than SI/raw data, model versions, extraction uncertainty, and benchmark design-space limits.
7. Close: LLMs may help make the predictive utility of literature more visible, but only if scientific evidence is organized for prediction.

## Draft v0

Our results show that providing LLMs with scientific literature can change their predictions of behavioral experiments, but that these changes are not reliably beneficial. When models were given the benchmark paper itself, which was written around the same experimental program and summarized evidence from the same design space, predictions improved substantially and converged across models. When the same models were given papers from the broader literature, predictions also often became more similar across models, but not necessarily more accurate. This distinction is central. Agreement among LLMs after reading the same paper suggests that the models are responding to shared content in the input. It does not imply that the input contains the information needed to predict the target outcomes.

This finding adds a qualification to recent optimism about using LLMs for scientific prediction. Prior work has shown that LLMs can forecast social and behavioral outcomes in some settings, sometimes matching or exceeding human forecasters. Our results are consistent with that possibility: off-the-shelf LLMs performed competitively with human crowds, and benchmark-paper augmentation pushed performance close to the estimated noise ceiling. But they also show that adding scientific content is not a generic route to better prediction. The value of a paper depends on how well its evidence is aligned with the prediction target, not simply on whether it is topically relevant, empirical, or highly visible in the literature.

The results also speak to the growing use of LLMs for literature search and synthesis. Retrieval-augmented systems can improve factuality, citation accuracy, and coverage in scientific question answering, and LLMs can support structured extraction from research articles. These capabilities are important, and our study relies on them. However, literature synthesis and outcome prediction are not the same task. A model can accurately summarize a paper and still be moved in the wrong direction if the paper reports outcomes, design choices, or empirical contrasts that are only partially aligned with the target experiment. In this sense, our findings suggest that evaluation of AI-assisted scientific synthesis should include downstream tests of whether synthesized evidence improves prediction, not only whether the synthesis is faithful to the source text.

Why did empirical papers show consistently lower predictive utility in this benchmark? Our analysis suggests that the issue is not a lack of empirical work. The PGG punishment literature contains hundreds of laboratory studies and thousands of experimental conditions. Instead, the limitation appears to be structural. Many studies vary one factor at a time, report outcomes other than efficiency or welfare, omit design details needed for comparison, or concentrate on a narrow subset of parameter values. These patterns are understandable within an explanatory research tradition, where studies are often designed to test whether a theoretically motivated factor matters under controlled conditions. But knowing that many factors matter is different from knowing how much they matter relative to one another and how they combine across settings. That latter problem is precisely what prediction across a multidimensional design space requires.

This interpretation is consistent with arguments for integrative experiment design and for a closer relationship between explanation and prediction in behavioral science. The benchmark used here was built to vary many design parameters within a common experimental framework, making outcomes commensurable across conditions. That structure allowed the benchmark paper to give LLMs information that generalized to the held-out target experiments. The broader literature, by contrast, contains many useful local findings but does not necessarily organize them in a way that supports the same predictive task. The point is therefore not that empirical papers are uninformative or inferior to theory. Rather, empirical findings can be locally informative while still being difficult to combine into accurate predictions for new, systematically varied conditions.

A practical implication is that AI systems will not remove the need for better scientific infrastructure. If the relevant outcome is not reported, if design parameters are missing, or if the literature sparsely covers the parts of the design space that matter most for prediction, an LLM cannot recover that information by synthesis alone. LLMs may help identify these gaps at scale by extracting design features, comparing coverage across studies, and testing whether different evidence sources improve prospective predictions. But improving predictive utility will also require more deliberate reporting of experimental design parameters, more direct measurement of comparable outcomes, and more studies that sample combinations of theoretically important factors rather than only isolated contrasts.

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. Our benchmark is drawn from one domain: peer punishment in public goods games. This domain is useful because it has a large literature, a clear prediction target, and a systematic held-out benchmark, but it does not represent all behavioral science. The 14 design parameters in the benchmark are also not exhaustive of every factor that may moderate punishment, and the target outcome, efficiency, is not the main outcome reported in many studies. Our augmentation strategy used structured reports generated from full texts rather than all possible source material, such as raw data, appendices, and supplementary analyses. More specialized retrieval, filtering, or reasoning pipelines may improve performance. Finally, LLMs are moving targets: model behavior, training data, and tool access change over time, so claims about absolute performance should be interpreted as tied to the models and prompts evaluated here.

These limitations point to a broader agenda. Prediction tasks can help reveal not only which models perform well, but also which forms of scientific evidence support generalization to new settings. In our case, LLMs were useful partly because they made the gap visible: they responded to the literature, but the response often failed to improve prediction. Scientific literature may therefore become more useful for both humans and AI systems when it is built with prediction in mind: clear targets, comparable measurements, systematic design coverage, and prospective tests of generalization. Explanation remains essential, but without prediction we may not know whether accumulated explanations combine into usable knowledge about what will happen next.
