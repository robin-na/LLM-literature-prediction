# Figure 1 Results Section Draft

We first evaluated whether LLMs could predict treatment outcomes in the 20 held-out public-goods-game experiments without any literature augmentation. For each prediction approach, performance was defined as the Pearson correlation between the vector of true treatment outcomes and the vector of predicted treatment outcomes across the 20 experiments, \(Corr(y_{\mathrm{true}}, y_{\mathrm{pred}})\). Human wisdom-of-the-crowd benchmarks were computed by averaging predictions across participants who completed all 20 forecasts, separately for laypeople and experts, and then correlating those average predictions with the true outcomes. This produced similar crowd-level performance for laypeople (\(r = .604\), 95% bootstrap CI [.234, .825]; \(n = 468\)) and experts (\(r = .606\), 95% CI [.233, .842]; \(n = 38\)).

Unaugmented LLMs performed in the same broad range as the human crowds, but with substantial variation across models. Baseline correlations ranged from \(r = .399\) for Gemini 2.5 Pro to \(r = .664\) for Claude Sonnet 4.6. By paired bootstrap over the 20 experiments, no unaugmented LLM differed significantly from either the layperson or expert wisdom-of-the-crowd benchmark at the 95% level. Pairwise differences among unaugmented LLMs were also limited: only Claude Sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.1 significantly exceeded Gemini 2.5 Pro, and the remaining unaugmented model comparisons did not exclude zero. Relative to individual human forecasters, however, several unaugmented LLMs were already competitive: Claude Sonnet 4.6 exceeded 95.3% of individual laypeople and 97.4% of individual experts, whereas Gemini 2.5 Pro exceeded 34.4% of laypeople and 15.8% of experts.

We then asked whether prediction improved when the same models were augmented with the benchmark paper. The paper was converted into a structured report and added to the prompt. This report did not include the exact treatment outcome values for the 20 target experiments, but it did summarize aggregate empirical patterns from the same benchmark study, including the target experiment set and 150 additional experiments sampled across the same 14 design dimensions. This is therefore an intentionally favorable test of whether LLM predictions improve when given a highly aligned source, rather than a claim that arbitrary papers would produce the same gain in a fully out-of-distribution setting.

Benchmark-paper augmentation improved performance across all eight displayed LLMs. The average correlation across models increased from \(r = .578\) without augmentation to \(r = .750\) with benchmark augmentation. The largest gain was observed for Gemini 2.5 Pro, which increased from \(r = .399\) to \(r = .843\). Using paired bootstrap confidence intervals for the difference in correlation between augmented and unaugmented predictions, the improvement was significant at the 95% level for six of the eight models: Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-4.1 Mini, GPT-4.1, GPT-5 Nano, GPT-5 Mini, and Gemini 2.5 Pro. The gains for GPT-5.1 and GPT-4.1 Nano were positive but did not exclude zero at the 95% level.

The augmented models also moved beyond the human crowd benchmarks. Six benchmark-augmented LLMs significantly exceeded both layperson and expert wisdom-of-the-crowd performance at the 95% level: Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-4.1 Mini, GPT-4.1, GPT-5 Nano, GPT-5 Mini, and Gemini 2.5 Pro. GPT-5.1 and GPT-4.1 Nano did not significantly exceed the human crowds under this paired-bootstrap criterion. Relative to individual forecasters, all benchmark-augmented models exceeded at least 90.0% of laypeople and 84.2% of experts; all except GPT-4.1 Nano exceeded at least 98.5% of laypeople and all individual experts.

Finally, we compared these correlations to two reference levels. The no-treatment outcome baseline, which correlates the observed control outcome with the treatment outcome, was \(r = .541\). We also estimated a noise ceiling of \(r = .777\), reflecting the fact that the measured treatment outcomes are themselves noisy estimates based on finite samples/trials in each experiment (8 to 12 trials per experiment). Exact details of this calculation are reported in the Supplementary Information. Benchmark-paper augmentation brought model performance close to this ceiling: the strongest augmented models matched or exceeded the point estimate, and the bootstrap confidence intervals for the augmented LLMs overlapped the estimated ceiling. Thus, under this favorable aligned-paper condition, LLM forecasts improved consistently and approached the maximum correlation expected given experimental measurement uncertainty.
